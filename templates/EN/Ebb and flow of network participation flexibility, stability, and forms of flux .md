Abstract
The flexibility/stability tension is a key challenge for purpose-oriented networks, especially salient with network participation. Because of the
voluntary nature of networks, it is common for network participation to fluctuate, with participants entering, leaving, and returning over time for
a variety of reasons. This fluctuation may challenge the stability that is key to network effectiveness. Yet, despite the salience of this tension, we
know little about managing the ebb and flow of network participation. Driven by phenomenon-based theorizing, we draw on longitudinal partic­
ipatory action research to examine participant attendance and contribution in monthly workgroup meetings over a four-year period of an early
child education network. Combining interviews (n = 5), meeting attendance tracking (n = 37), and meeting observations (n = 30), we identify
six types of flux stemming from individual, organizational, and system forces. We find these forces of flux support both flexibility and stability.
Highlighting the duality of flexibility and stability, we explain how flexibility at one level may result in stability at another and vice versa. Our
findings contribute to a greater understanding of how stability and flexibility are both valuable for networks and thus, the need to embrace the
ebb and flow of participation.
Key words: flexibility/stability tension; purpose-oriented networks; longitudinal case study; network participation.

Introduction
Addressing a complex problem takes time and stability
(Provan and Kenis 2008), and at the same time, flexibility
and adaptability (Head and Alford 2015). This is why a ten­
sion between flexibility and stability was one of the inherent
network governance tensions noted by Provan and Kenis
(2008). Interorganizational networks are meant to be flex­
ible structures (Powell 1990), but the fluidity of collaborative
structures can hamper momentum.
Nowhere is this tension more salient than when it comes
to the issue of individual and organizational participation in
interorganizational networks. Because of the voluntary nature
of networks, it is common for network participation to fluc­
tuate, with participants entering, leaving, and returning over
time for a variety of reasons. In addition to people coming
and going, the priorities of member organizations also shift
over time, thus creating multiple potential sources of partici­
pant flux for networks. This ebb and flow of participation is
an integral part of the flexibility of the network form, but also
makes it difficult to sustain collaborative momentum.
Not only is sustaining momentum an issue, but participant
fluctuation may also have an impact on performance, for any
type of network but especially for purpose-oriented networks
(PONs), the focus of this article. PONs are interorganizational
networks formally organized to address complex problems
(Nowell and Kenis 2019) and stability is often found to sup­
port the long-term work necessary to make a difference at
the community level (Provan and Lemaire 2012; Provan and
Milward 1995; Siciliano, Carr, and Hugg 2021).
However, focusing on achieving network stability to the
detriment of flexibility can often result in rigidity, like efforts
to formalize and institutionalize network processes and mem­
bership. Rigidity would render the networks less adaptable
and more bureaucratic, defeating the reason for using a net­
work approach in the first place (Wachhaus 2012). Like with
organizations, turnover can never be completely avoided; in­
stead, the focus is on managing the turnover, and the resulting
tensions between flexibility and stability, in a way that
minimizes the negative impact on performance.
Recognizing that the tension between flexibility and sta­
bility arises in PONs because both are needed and seemingly
contradictory, we illuminate the forces behind the ebb and
flow of participation to advance knowledge that managers
can use to manage flexibility and stability simultaneously.
Our interest is not to explain why individual participants
come and go, but rather to focus on the various forces
explaining change in participation at the network level, which
we refer to as the ebb and flow. We do not aim to identify
causal antecedents to explain change, but rather to disen­
tangle the lower-level dynamics contributing to network-level
dynamics. Specifically, we are interested in the following re­
search questions: (1) What are the types of flux that constitute
the ebb and flow of network participation and (2) what are

Our research motivations are driven by phenomenon-
based theorizing, which “entails the development of new
theoretical insights and perspectives based on the observa­
tion of real-world phenomenon” (Fisher, Mayer, and Morris
2021, 632); thus, we purport to build upon existing theory
by garnering insight from problems identified from practice
rather than academic theory alone (Bhardwaj, Mahoney, and
Nickerson 2025; Ployhart and Bartunek 2019). Our research
questions emanate from a longitudinal mixed-methods study
of First Steps, a child education and development PON. While
studying the initiation and development of First Steps, we
observed participant fluctuation over time and the challenges
of that fluctuation for sustaining network progress. Thus, the
First Steps case is a revelatory case study (Yin 2014) as it re­
vealed a phenomenon important to investigate and theorize
on to inform practice.
As PONs are complex systems of organizing, to under­
stand the ebb and flow, we identify categories of flux. We
draw on observations of network meetings over a four-year
time-period, supplemented by a small number of partici­
pant interviews. Examining attendance and contribution of
“network members,” we find that individuals, organizations,
and systems are the forces behind the ebb and flow of net­
work participation. Analyzing these different forces, we ex­
tract six types of flux that we label using musical analogies:
One-Timers (and One-Hit Wonders), Fade-Aways1, Lineup
Changes, Rockstars, Redirections, and Hidden Hit Makers.
Though we offer a typology of types of flux, the implications
of our research are how these types of flux act as stabilizing
forces and also support both network flexibility and stability
simultaneously to sustain momentum towards reaching PON
goals.
PON stability and flexibility
Networks comprised of public, nonprofit, and for-profit or­
ganizations working collectively to address an issue have be­
come commonplace in governance systems and communities
(Voets, Koliba, and Keast 2019). As a particular form of
interorganizational network, the PON model emphasized here
are networks consciously formed to achieve an aim greater
than any one organization or collaborative arrangement could
lay claim, which often involves tackling “wicked” (Weber and
Khademian 2008) or especially complex problems (Mandell,
Keast, and Chamberlain 2017). The rationale for networks
as an organizing approach is because they are adaptable,
flexible, and dynamic forms (Huxham 2003; Powell 1990).
Stability, though, has been found to impact network effective­
ness, especially at the community level (Provan and Milward
1995; Raab, Mannak, and Cambré 2015; Siciliano, Carr, and
Hugg 2021).
Farjoun (2010) argues for conceptualizing stability and
flexibility not as dualism, but rather as a duality, meaning
they are distinct but not rigid antagonistic concepts. Similar
to others, he defines stability as associated with “continuity,
reliability, predictability, low variance and regularity” (204)
and considers flexibility as associated with change, along with
variation and adaptability. Despite discussion about stability
and flexibility in the interorganizational networks literature,
“what” exactly comprises PON flexibility and stability are
less well defined. For example, Provan and Kenis (2008, 245)
discuss flexibility and stability in multiple ways, including
responses to environment, relationships, and governance. The
various ways of conceptualizing network flexibility and sta­
bility make it difficult to integrate research findings, especially
when considering the additional complexity brought on by
the nested levels of PONs (Lemaire et al. 2019). For instance,
Blanken et al. (2022, 7) found that relationships between
organizations in child welfare networks do change consid­
erably over time, but that “the overall connectedness of the
networks is relatively stable.” This finding suggests that sta­
bility may exist at one level, like at the network level, despite
flexibility at another, such as at the dyadic level. Thus, one
of the challenges of managing the tension between flexibility
and stability is that there are various dimensions and levels of
analysis of flexibility and stability in organizational networks.
Actor composition and the ebb and flow of
network participation
For this article, we focus on one dimension of flexibility and
stability, actor composition. Whereas the concept of net­
work churn (Koka, Madhavan, and Prescott 2006) focuses
on network change due to changes in ties between actors, we
focus on changes in the composition of actors rather than
ties. Actor composition can influence network effectiveness
(Siciliano, Carr, and Hugg 2021) and in turn, the loss or addi­
tion of an actor (node) affects tie structure (Borgatti, Carley,
and Krackhardt 2006) as well as the resources of a network
(Auschra and Sydow 2022).
Majchrzak, Jarvenpaa, and Bagherzadeh (2015, 1348)
define network actor composition as membership of organ­
izations and key individuals in an interorganizational collab­
oration. While the stability/flexibility tension clearly stems
from many aspects of network characteristics, Majchrzak,
Jarvenpaa, and Bagherzadeh (2015) note that actor compo­
sition is an important aspect of interorganizational collab­
oration that can change over time. The dynamics of actor
composition include adding new actors or actors leaving, but
what has not been examined is the variety of ways in which
actors coming and going contribute to both network stability
and flexibility.
Turnover is challenging in any organizational context and
a lack of continuous and consistent participation is espe­
cially challenging in PONs because of the interdependence
between participating organizations to achieve the common
purpose. Achieving the purpose is dependent on the organ­
izational members and the individuals representing those
member organizations to carry out the network work.
Member interactions aid the development of trust (Vangen
and Huxham 2003) and build the structure and processes of a
network (Sedgwick et al. 2022) but are dependent on member
attendance to occur. Engagement in a PON is often voluntary
(Lemaire and Provan 2018) and usually an additional work
task of participants. The needs of their organization will often
be a priority over network commitments. When attendance
is inconsistent, network momentum can be impacted. For in­
stance, network discussions and decisions are often revisited
from one meeting to the next due to fluctuating attendance.

Another example is when a strategy is decided upon, but then

Individual turnover is not the only dynamic behind changes
in actor composition. Membership ambiguity, complexity,
and dynamism (Huxham and Vangen 2000) stem not only
from individuals, but from organizations as well. Vangen and
Huxham (2003) note, in describing the transitory nature of
collaborative arrangements, the coming and going of indi­
vidual representatives as well as the coming and going of or­
ganizations due to changing purposes, internal changes, or
organizational demise. PONs are reliant on organizational
members and their resources to achieve the common purpose
and shifts in organizational purposes, priorities or capacity
may impact PON momentum as much or even more than
losing only individual representative participation. Thus, the
dynamics of actor composition in PONs includes both indi­
vidual and organizational dimensions.
Our observations of actor composition dynamics also
pointed to a system dimension as well. Networks are often
referred to as systems, but it is important to distinguish
between PONs and the larger service systems in which
PONs are embedded. Nowell and Milward (2022) distin­
guish between system-oriented networks, analyst-imposed
delineations of relevant populations of actors, and PONs,
bounded and self-referencing collectives of actors that con­
sciously affiliate with a shared purpose. Multiple classes of
networks can occur within the same context simultaneously;
for example, when an analyst identifies one or more PONs
as relevant to a broader system-oriented network of interest
or vice versa. In their classic study of network effectiveness,
Provan and Milward (1995) found system stability a strong
predictor of effectiveness. Thus, it is important to recognize
that the dynamics of actor composition in PONs are also
influenced by changes in the broader systems in which they
are embedded.
The ebb and flow at the network level as a result of changes
at the individual, organizational, and system levels is the
phenomenon of interest for this study. We conceptualize ebb
and flow at the network level and define it as the dynamics of
individual and organizational participants coming, going, and
returning to the PON over time. The ebb and flow is shaped
by the flux that occurs within organizations and systems, de­
fined as the continuous transition of people, ideas, and re­
sources (Panitz and Glückler 2020). The flux and resulting
ebb and flow are observable through changes in individual
participation, which we refer to as fluctuation. Figure 1
depicts how we are relating these various concepts to one an­
other in the context of PONs. We draw on the case of First
Steps to examine fluctuation and categorize the types of flux
and the forces behind similar fluctuation patterns. We tie how
these categories of flux connect to participant contribution
and PON flexibility and stability. By understanding what the
flexibility and stability forces are and how they manifest in
the ebb and flow of networks, public managers will be better
prepared to support the duality of flexibility and stability.
Research methods
We utilized a longitudinal participatory action research ap­
proach (Kidd and Kral 2005) to study a child education and
development (CED) PON.2 Network participants simultane­
ously recognized us as network participants and researchers,
providing us with insider information about the happenings
and impressions of the group (Coghlan 2003). This in-depth
participatory study approach to a revelatory case (Yin
2014) allows for context-sensitive insights that facilitate our
phenomenon-based theorizing (Fisher, Mayer, and Morris
2021).

region of Virginia in the United States. To strengthen CED

collective of actors that consciously affiliate with a shared pur­
pose (Nowell and Milward 2022). While its formation is in­
tentional, it does not have a formal governance structure or
formal membership. Its governance is a mix between shared
and lead-agency (Provan and Kenis 2008), as it is convened
by a community foundation, but decisions are made by the
entire group. Despite not having formalized membership,
individuals and organizations refer to themselves and others as
First Steps members. Therefore, the network is consistent with
the membership condition of PONs as proposed by Nowell
and Milward in that members endorse their affiliation and the
affiliation of others with the shared identify of the network.
Data collection and analysis
For the analysis, we draw on Info Hub workgroup meeting
observations and interviews.3 The specific timeframe of this
data analysis is from the launching of the Info Hub workgroup,
January 2017 to the end of 2020, when we ended our system­
atic data collection on the First Steps network. The interviews
document the phenomenon of interest, the ebb and flow of
participation, from the perspective of a sample of network
participants. Meeting attendance data provides observational
evidence of this phenomenon (Alvesson and Sandberg 2023),
depicting the ebb and flow of network participation and the
stability and flexibility it represents. We then analyze the var­
ious streams of the ebb and flow using our observational and
context knowledge to identify the types of flux contributing
to the ebb and flow. Last, we draw on meeting contribution
from the observations to examine how the types of flux con­
tribute to PON flexibility and stability. Examining these var­
ious data from our case study approach provides us a way to
address why participant fluctuation matters, what flux types
affect fluctuation and contribution, and how these elements
contribute to stability and flexibility for the network.
Interviews
To triangulate field observations with participant impressions,
one author conducted five, semi-structured in-person
interviews with Info Hub participants between March 2018
and August 2018. Interviews averaged 1.5 hours and were
digitally recorded and then transcribed. The interviewees
represented public schools, nonprofit service providers, and
nonprofit CED advocacy groups and consisted of a mix of reg­
ular participants that varied in their involvement. Interview
questions (included as Supplementary Appendix A) prompted
interviewees to discuss their own participation as well as First
Steps recruitment.
Responses were systematically coded using structured codes
(Saldaña 2009) to identify interviewees’ discussions of partic­
ipation, recruitment, flexibility, and stability. During a second
round of coding (Bailey 2017), “stress of flux” emerged as a
significant theme. To conduct our analysis, we used a “general
inductive approach” to derive meaning and categories from
observed data (Thomas 2006), which provided insight re­
garding the challenges of participant fluctuation and the
overall ebb and flow of network participation.
Meeting observations and attendance rosters
The Info Hub workgroup met for a total of 41 meetings over
the 48-month period covered in this analysis. From these
meetings, we extracted three different types of data: attend­
ance rosters, field notes, and observation coding sheets. We
triangulated these data for our analyses discussed in further
detail below.
Attendance at meetings was compiled using attendance
rosters completed by Info Hub participants for each meeting,
supplemented by our meeting observations when rosters
were unavailable. Rosters consisted of both the names of
the participants and the organizations they represented.
Attendance was compiled for 37 of the 41 meetings of the
Info Hub workgroup between 2017 and 2020; we had in­
complete data for four of the meetings. During the observed
time frame, individual meeting attendance ranged from six
to 16 individuals, with an average of 10 participants at
each meeting. Anonymized attendance data is included as
Supplementary Appendix B.
Meeting observations were first conducted through field
notes before we transitioned to observation coding sheets.
One author took detailed field notes of meeting dialogue
for 21 of 25 possible meetings between January 2017 and
April 2019. The author typed the notes as meeting transcripts
indicating which person spoke and their corresponding dia­
logue, whether paraphrased or direct quoting (Bailey 2017).4

To examine attendance fluctuation over the four-year
period, three-mode matrices were compiled linking individuals
and the organizations they represented and their attendance
or absence at each of the 37 meetings. For the quantitative
analysis presented below, we aggregated the total meeting at­
tendance per year by the represented organizations. However,
because individual representatives change as well as their
affiliated organizations, we kept track of which individuals
were representing which organizations for each meeting be­

field notes in relation to attendance patterns. Given the in­
sider knowledge garnered by researchers as participant ac­
tion researchers (Coghlan 2003; Kidd and Kral 2005), our
observations of different attendees across the four years were
also noted.
Our observations of meetings also included data on
members’ contribution to the dialogue during meetings,
which we call “meeting contribution.” Of the 37 meetings
coded for attendance, 30 were also analyzed for dialogue
contribution. To examine meeting contribution, we first
tallied the total number of dialogue codes assigned to an
attendee per meeting. This tally estimated the number of
times an attendee contributed to the conversation during
each meeting. We then added up the total number of dia­
logue codes for each attendee resulting in a total contribu­
tion measure. Next, we tallied the total number of meetings
attended (out of the 30 being analyzed for contribution)
for each person using the attendance data discussed above.
After developing our flux types (discussed in detail below),
we either analyzed a person individually or we aggregated
their dialogue code and attendance totals per the organiza­
tions they represented. We analyze contribution this way
because some flux types have a one-to-one correspondence
to a person, while some represent multiple people who rep­
resent one organization. We created an average contribution
per meeting value by dividing total contribution by total
meetings attended.
Findings
In this section, we present findings from our interviews, at­
tendance tracking, and meeting observations. We first dis­
cuss interviewee reflections on network participation and the
impact on network momentum.7 We then present the overall
ebb and flow of network participation based on organiza­
tional attendance and then identify the various types of flux
and the underlying forces behind the ebb and flow. Last, we
discuss meeting contribution and connect how the types of
flux contribute to PON stability and flexibility.
Reflections on network participation
The momentum behind the launching of First Steps was
generated from a series of community conversations in the
fall of 2016 that attracted over 200 people, but as work­
group participants continually commented, much of those
participants never engaged in the Info Hub workgroup. “And
many of the faces, we’ve started with what two-hundred
people in those first couple of sessions… It’s [now] kind of
the Info Hub, so I do worry a little that we might’ve lost those
other groups” (Interview 1). Instead, most of the action of the
network was the result of the Info Hub workgroup: “In the
beginning it was a larger group of people. Some people were
just there to listen, not really there to work. And I feel like it
got drilled down to the hub or the workers” (Interview 2).
The result of the Info Hub workgroup’s efforts exceeded
expectations. Between the launching of the workgroup until
the end of 2020, First Steps held 17 Meet Yourself Events
that attracted well over 200 different providers. The progress
and success of the working group was surprising even to the
participants:
From a group that was just throwing ideas out there of
what we wanted to see happen to a fine-oiled machine
right now really making it happen. And to think where we
were and where we’ve come…going to these communities
and having such successful groups our first go around. I
think it has been tremendous. (Interview 3)
I feel like we have a good group that’s making decisions
and moving things along and it’s working well. (Interview
1)
These perspectives are consistent with our observations as
researchers, as over time the Info Hub workgroup displayed
how quickly they could make and execute decisions, despite
the coming and going of individuals and organizations each
month. In fact, many of the new participants to the Info Hub
workgroup attended because of the awareness and value they
gained from participating in a Meet Yourself Event. The sta­
bility generated by the work of the Info Hub (Meet Yourself
Events) contributed to flexibility in participation.
Participation flexibility was not without its challenges as
fluctuating attendance was considered by interviewees as
the only major challenge this group faced. The interviewees
discussed the stress of sustaining participation, not due
to lack of desire to attend meetings, but rather due to the
challenges of making it work alongside their commitments
to their organizations. For instance, in response to an inter­
view question about whether they are happy with their own

makes. Missing meetings can limit engagement or interrupt

characterized some meetings as two steps backwards be­
cause someone who had not been at the meetings had to
be brought up to speed and that prevented the group from
tackling the agenda item for the day. At times this rehashing
of conversations was not due to missing meetings, but rather
to the entry of new people. “I feel like in some of our network
meetings sometimes we hash out the same things again…
but I think it was needed because sometimes different people
were at the table. 1 mean some people stepped in the hub
group that I had never even seen come to any of the actual
network meeting groups. We had new people to the table”
(Interview 3). Therefore, attendance fluctuations, whether a
result of consistent participants missing a meeting or new
participants entering, slow momentum due to the need to
bring participants up to speed.
Turnover is also one of the challenges interviewees
perceived. The stress on the network due to turnover is illus­
trated by one of the active participants of the Info Hub work­
group: “I think probably the biggest challenge is when people
change and we’re seeing that now with [CARES]. It’s like she
was one of the drivers of the whole process and suddenly she’s
gone” (Interview 5). At the same time, this particular example
refers to the loss of an individual representing a key organiza­
tion and even though this person left, she made sure someone
else from the organization would sustain organizational in­
volvement in the workgroup. Through this continued organ­
izational participation, the new individual eventually became
a new driver for the group. This last example demonstrates
that the ebb and flow is not a simple story of individuals
coming and going. Because interorganizational networks are
nested structures involving individuals and organizations,
what drives the ebb and flow of participation is actually var­
ious types of flux.
Ebb and flow
As participant fluctuation and the stress on network dy­
namics were voiced by the Info Hub interviewees, we set out
to analyze what organizational and individual attendance
looked like over a period of time. Figure 2 is a Sankey di­
agram that illustrates the ebb and flow of attendance in
the Info Hub workgroup between 2017 and 2020. Sankey
diagrams are a type of flowchart originally developed to
analyze the thermal efficiency of steam engines but have
since become tools for visualizing energy or material flows
in complex systems (Schmidt 2008; Soundararajan, Ho, and
Su 2014). These diagrams clarify input and output flows
so that they may be more efficiently managed, such as by
identifying energy efficiency improvements (Soundararajan,
Ho, and Su 2014). We appropriated the Sankey diagram
for depicting attendance flows of the Info Hub workgroup
as it illustrates the variation among participants across the
four-year period.
Specifically, the Sankey diagram shows each organization’s
proportionate attendance in the Info Hub workgroup
for the years 2017, 2018, 2019, and 2020, and how each
organization’s level of attendance compares with the other
participants. The organizational participants are listed
along the left side of the graph in an order that minimizes
overlapping streams to make for easier readability. The right
side of the graph depicts the years. For each year an or­
ganization attended workgroup meetings, there is a “flow”
from the left column to the year. The height of these flows
conveys the percentage of the total meetings that organization
attended for each year. For instance, if one were to examine
the “MRFL” flow (dark blue, fifth organization from the top),
it would become apparent that they were strong attendees in
2017, but their attendance dropped off for the years 2018,
2019, and 2020 (their flows for those years are far narrower
than the flow for 2017).
The organizations at the center of the graph are those or­
ganizations with relatively consistent attendance across the
years.8 Those at the top of the graph are those organizations
with higher attendance in the early years and with a drop
off in attendance in the later years. The organizations at
the bottom of the graph are those with greater attendance
in the later years. The ones at the very bottom are recent
participants that began attending workgroup meetings in
only the last year.
In addition to comparing across organizations, one can
compare the flows for each year on the right side of the graph
to see changes in workgroup composition. For instance,
comparing the colors of the 2017 flow with the 2020 flow,
there are some consistent organizational flows, like the light
tan CHIP (19th organization from top or bottom). However,
there is also a noticeable difference in width of some organ­
izational flows as well as overall different composition of
colors. Comparing the overall color compositions for each
year on the right side of the graph suggests a balance of sta­
bility flexibility. The stability is represented by the consistent
colors across the years and the flexibility by the differences of
colors in each year set.

Discussion
Driven by a phenomenon-based approach, we analyzed the
ebb and flow of PON member participation, one of the few
network challenges noted by participants of First Steps, a
child education and development PON. The data we present
in this article demonstrates that even for PONs, the ability to
“reliably identify other members and distinguish them from
nonmembers” (Nowell and Milward 2022, 10) undermines
the dynamism of the network form. Instead, as argued
by Kilduff, Tsai, and Hanke (2006, 1037), “individuals’
cognitions of networks reflect not just the current state of
the network but also ghost ties—that is, memories of actors
who are no longer physically present.” Because PONs involve
individuals and organizations which are embedded in various
systems, all of which are sources of flux, our study depicts
how actor or “member” composition changes over time and
that contribution to the network may endure for longer than
“membership.”
Network participation fluctuation is not as simple as people
coming and going. Rather, the participation fluctuation ex­
perienced by networks is comparable to industries, like the
music industry we used as an analogy in this article. Using a
Sankey diagram to depict the overall ebb and flow of partici­
pation in the PON, we then identified variation in the types of
flux by examining both individual and organizational partici­
pation, uncovering six types of flux in our study: One-Timers
(and One-Hit Wonders), Fade-Aways, Lineup Changes,

Rockstars, Redirections, and Hidden Hit Makers. Our study
contributes to the literature by explaining how individuals, or­
ganizations, and systems are the forces behind these different
flux types and how these forces support both flexibility and
stability. This contribution supports earlier network theory
by illustrating how, at least in terms of actor composition,
stability and flexibility are both valuable for networks and
should be managed, not eliminated. Highlighting the duality
of flexibility and stability, we progress our understanding of
the importance of both by unpacking how flexibility at one
level may result in stability at another and vice versa.
Our contributions reinforce the caution offered by
Wachhaus (2012) that rigidity is not the solution. The
frustrations of participant fluctuation led one interviewee in
our study to reflect on the need for more rigidity, “I don’t
know that it’s going to be successful if you have too many
people that aren’t actively attending the meetings. I think eve­
rybody has to attend every single network meeting.” The case
of First Steps suggests otherwise, as First Steps continued to
make an impact in the community despite maintaining flexi­
bility around participation. Ultimately, a network approach is
about being adaptive, open, and flexible. Implementing rigid
participation rules would eliminate the flexibility that is a key
component of the network approach and would likely deter
individuals and organizations from participating. As some of
our other interviewees noted, their network participation was
dependent on the ebb and flow within their own organiza­
tions. Additionally, our analysis of member dialogue revealed
that both flexibility and stability in participation contributed
to the network momentum and activities. Rigid participation
rules could limit the activation of new participants resulting
in the loss of additional resources for the network (Auschra
and Sydow 2022) that could help stabilize the network de­
spite the flux that will likely occur in the systems in which
they are embedded.
In fact, our typology of flux uncovers that embracing flex­
ibility can result in stability. Flux at one level is sometimes
stability at another level. For example, turnover at the in­
dividual level can result in institutionalized organizational
participation by ensuring sustained organizational repre­
sentation despite the departure of individuals. Alternatively,
though individuals leave organizational positions, that does
not mean they will leave the network and as we found, they
may instead bring new organizations to the table with their
continued participation. Given 21st-century workforce dy­
namics and the propensity for job-switching, supporting in­
dividual commitment to the network may be as important
as supporting organizational commitment. Considering flex­
ibility and stability as a duality allows for recognizing that
“flexibility does not have to be seen in opposition to sta­
bility but as a requirement for higher-level stability” (Sus and
Puszko 2020, 461). Our research suggests that managing the
flexibility/stability tension, at least in terms of actor composi­
tion, means recognizing that there is stability as well as flexi­
bility in fluctuating participation.
By embracing the flux, research and practice can focus on
how flexibility can be managed in order to strengthen the
network. Network participant fluctuation confers certain
benefits, including the inclusion of new perspectives that can
help ensure the adaptability of the network. New participants
can bring new and exciting ideas, even if they only show up
one time like with our One-Hit Wonder. In addition, even if
there is not sustained participation, with every new partici­
pant, there will be broader awareness of the network. This
broader awareness includes individuals, but it also includes
entire organizations that may learn of the network through
their new employees, like we found with the movement of
the individual Rockstars across organizations. And finally,
there can be benefits to losing individual or organizational
participants. Though participant exit may afflict the network,
it may also benefit the group, for example, losing difficult
participants can often benefit the progress of the network.
Therefore, embracing the ebb and flow of participation might
indeed improve network stability and effectiveness. These
benefits of supporting participant flexibility are consistent
with the implications of Mosley and Wong (2021, 343): “in­
stability that is well-managed rather than stability that is
maintained is perhaps what can strengthen the internal dy­
namics of the network.” Rather than solving the flexibility/
stability tension, the solution may be to strive for a balance
in the various types of flux that contribute to the overall ebb
and flow of participation.
Thus, despite creating challenges to momentum, embracing
the ebb and flow will also result in stabilizing forces for the net­
work. The implications of our research suggest that network
managers need to be encouraged to embrace fluctuating net­
work participation by recruiting and supporting participation
of individual champions as well as organizational champions.
Future research should unpack how that can be done without
sacrificing stability. For instance, how can network managers
leverage the excitement or new ideas generated by One-Hit
Wonders? How can ghost ties or Fade-Aways be reengaged?
What processes can help the network move forward while
also carving space for new participants? How can individual
or organizational commitment be supported despite indi­
vidual or organizational flux? And finally, how can the en­
gagement of shadow participants be enhanced?
More research into the processes behind supporting the du­
ality of flexibility and stability would also address the limitations
of our research. One strength of this research is the longitudinal
analysis, but studying a PON is complex, especially over time.
Our analysis of the ebb and flow is based only on participation
in one workgroup. PON participation goes beyond workgroup
participation; however, analyzing the core group behind the
PON is likely where the greatest stability of a network will be
(Nowell et al. 2018). Understanding how to manage the ebb
and flow even at the stable core means supporting flexibility at
the periphery (Provan and Lemaire 2012).
In the network we studied we observed minimal conflict
which may be attributable to the strong facilitation skills
demonstrated by the network convenor. We did not observe
significant power dynamics and the silencing of some voices
over others. In fact, we usually observed instead the effort
to hear from everyone or even to consider those voices not
present at the table, which led to us including the Hidden
Hit Makers as a type of flux. Thus, future research should
examine how more power dynamics or the dynamics created
by the convenor results in a different relationship between
flexibility and stability.
Finally, as with any case study, generalizability to other
PONs may be limited and our typology should be examined
in other network contexts. The network we studied was not
a mandated network, rather it was a grassroots approach
to building a collaborative network by starting small, with

561
one workgroup. The network aims to be inclusive and has
no formal membership or rules for participating. Therefore,
the types of flux we uncovered may differ greatly from other
networks formed under different contexts or with different
governance approaches. However, we believe the implications
of our findings are analytically generalizable (Yin 2014), that
uncovering the benefits of embracing fluctuation and the sta­
bility it can generate may be important in other PON con­
text, such as for more formal or rule-based collaborative
approaches. Our phenomenon-based approach and revelatory
case (Yin 2014) reveal the challenges but also the benefits of
flexible participation that challenge the notion that collabo­
rative networks should have rigid participation rules. Future
research should examine under which contextual conditions
emphasizing rigid versus flexible participation is more effective.
We also recognize that another force behind the var­
ious types of flux is the PON itself. As Chen and Sullivan
(2023) found, collaborative performance, processes, and
composition influence participant exit. However, the aim of
this study was not to uncover whether and how the charac­
teristics of the PON explain the coming and going of indi­
vidual participants as others have done and as participatory
researchers, we likely were not in the position to capture this
potential flux force. Instead, our goal was to unpack the com­
plexity behind participant fluctuation so future research can
disentangle forces within and beyond the control of the PON.
For instance, what drives Rockstars to keep coming back and
how does the PON contribute to organizations being Lineup
Changes versus Fade-Aways? Future research should there­
fore examine how the PON itself influences the types of flux
and the relationship between flexibility and stability.
Despite the limitations of our study, our aim is to prog­
ress understanding of how to maintain one form of net­
work flexibility while still achieving stability in the hopes of
stimulating more research to guide managing the flexibility/
stability tension. Networks are meant to be fluid structures
and maintaining that fluidity and flexibility is essential to
preserving the strengths of the network approach even for
more formalized PONs. But for PONs to balance that flexi­
bility with enough stability to address the complex problem
that is the reason for their existence, participant ebb and flow
needs to be embraced and harnessed. The success of PONs
may rely on how well we are able to manage the ebb and flow,
rather than see it as a problem to be solved.

Abstract
Drawing on the literature on bureaucratic discretion, this study conducts a large-
scale, nationwide causal analysis of the effects of body-worn cameras (BWC) in the
United States (US). It employs a staggered difference-in-differences (DiD) approach
using 12 years of panel data (2008–2019) covering 697 local police agencies. The
findings indicate that BWC adoption had no significant effect on White arrests but
led to an approximately 5.5% decrease in Black arrests. Importantly, the reduction
in Black arrests was concentrated in alcohol-related cases, which involve consider-
able officer discretion, but did not extend to cases categorized as more serious
public safety threats, such as violent or property crimes, where officer discretion
may be more limited. Meanwhile, there was also a 7.7% increase in White arrests
related to weapon offenses, aligning with prior studies that suggest BWCs may
serve as a tool for evidence collection. Our findings provide insight into how BWCs
have influenced police arrests in the US, advancing beyond previous single-case
evaluations.
Evidence for practice
• Technology-based oversight mechanisms, like BWCs, can constrain street-level
bureaucrats’ discretionary behavior, but these effects are limited.
• BWC adoption led to a percentage decrease in Black arrests (most pronounced
for alcohol-related offenses) and a percentage increase in White weapon-related
arrests.
• BWC adoption did not significantly reduce police arrests for violent, property,
drug, or public disorder crimes.
INTRODUCTION
Repeated incidents of police violence and misconduct
threaten the legitimacy of police organizations. These
incidents disproportionately affect residents from com-
munities of color, leading to deficits in perceptions of
police fairness and legitimacy (Edwards et al., 2019) and
consistent calls for police reforms and accountability
(Crabtree, 2020). To counteract threats to legitimacy and
accountability, police organizations have explored a vari-
ety of strategies, including adopting body-worn cam-
eras (BWCs).
BWC technology enables closer monitoring of front-
line officers’ interactions with civilians. BWCs are theo-
rized to make officers more cognizant of aligning their
behavior with evaluative expectations to demonstrate
compliance or avoid penalties for their actions. BWC poli-
cies purport to improve officer behaviors and enhance
the transparency of interactions with community mem-
bers, particularly interactions involving coercion and force
(Gaub & White, 2020; Lum et al., 2019). Proponents of
BWC policies also suggest that they may improve officer
safety, increase the likelihood of accurate resolutions of
community
complaints
brought
against
the
police
(Cubukcu et al., 2023), and introduce training opportuni-
ties for officers. In 2015, BWCs were presented as a critical
policy recommendation supported by federal funding for

To

total number of arrests, but subgroup analyses show that

decrease

discretion. Comparatively, there was no impact of BWC
adoption on Black arrests for offenses that are typically
considered more serious crimes, such as violent and prop-
erty crimes—indicated by their categorization as Part
1 Crimes, according to the Federal Bureau of Investigation
(FBI). Furthermore, BWCs had a positive and significant
effect on arrests of White individuals in cases involving
weapons—such as the unlawful sale, purchase, carrying,
possession, or use of weapons—suggesting that BWCs
may increase police arrests in situations where a BWC
may serve as a tool for evidence collection.
Our findings demonstrate the influence of BWCs on
police arrests and their limits in the US, advancing
beyond previous single-agency case studies that were
sensitive to various agency-specific factors affecting the
implementation of BWCs. Findings indicate that BWCs
have functioned as a technology-based oversight mecha-
nism, reducing racial disparities in police arrests in situa-
tions involving greater discretionary interpretation and
decision-making. Importantly, BWC oversight also seemed
to serve as a tool for evidence collection in weapon-
related cases. This aligns with theoretical insights from
previous studies (Kang, 2023) that the impact of BWCs
can result in increases or decreases in arrests, depending
on the specific role the device plays in an agency and sit-
uational context.
BODY-WORN CAMERAS: OVERSIGHT ON
FRONTLINE POLICE ENCOUNTER
The alignment of bureaucratic operations with public
interest is a significant issue in modern democracies, con-
sidering the dominant influence wielded by bureaucrats
through discretionary power. As nonelected institutional
representatives, bureaucrats exercise discretion within the
bounds of a wide range of rules and regulations estab-
lished by elected authorities with democratic legitimacy
(McCubbins et al., 1987; Pollack, 1997). Bureaucratic dis-
cretion is challenging to regulate in advance due to inher-
ent
uncertainties
and
unforeseen
circumstances
in
bureaucrat-citizen interactions (Lipsky, 2010). Lipsky first
posited in 1980 that public servants who interact continu-
ously with the public on the frontlines need discretion in
implementing policies and programs, meaning they cre-
ate policies in action. Owing to the amount of discretion
provided to street-level bureaucrats, concerns exist about
how they wield their discretion and whether the public is
treated fairly and equitably (Lipsky, 2010).
It is well known that street-level bureaucrats often work
in environments where they manage competing demands,
constrained resources, and high workloads; thus, they must
decide which members of the public to prioritize, why and
how (Jilke & Tummers, 2018). While some posit that bureau-
crats are neutral implementers of public policies, others
argue
that
such
neutrality
does
not
exist
(Portillo
et al., 2022). For instance, street-level bureaucrats may show
preferential treatment to members of the public who they
perceive to be deserving (Schneider & Ingram, 1993), in
need (Jilke & Tummers, 2018), or who share similar sociode-
mographic characteristics as them (Bradbury & Kellough,
2008). As it pertains to race and bureaucratic discretion spe-
cifically, a body of research in public administration has
argued that discretion is an important prerequisite for actu-
alizing the positive effects associated with a representative
bureaucracy (Meier & Bohte, 2001; Sowa & Selden, 2003;
Wilkins & Wenger, 2015). On the contrary, other research
has also shown that legal or organizational constraints on
discretion can reduce bias, reliance on stereotypes, and dis-
crimination (Glaser 2024; Pfaff et al., 2021; Raaphorst &
Groeneveld, 2019).

The insights from the literature on bureaucratic discre-
tion and control underlie various public accountability
mechanisms, including BWCs that have gained popularity
in policing. Over the past decade, BWCs diffused rapidly
across democratic nations as a promising answer to calls
for improved police–public interactions. For context,
BWCs quickly gained momentum in the aftermath of
high-profile incidents of police brutality and unwarranted
use of force during the early 2010s. BWCs are widely
viewed as a tool for improving procedural accountability
in frontline policing and are purported to be an ex-post
bureaucratic control, which refers to “authorities monitor-
ing and reacting to what bureaucrats do in the perfor-
mance of their jobs” (Moe, 2012, p. 2). Ex-post controls
use rewards and sanctions to modify the incentive struc-
ture for bureaucrats, which externally regulates the moti-
vation behind bureaucratic behavior. When cameras
record police–civilian encounters, these interactions are
subject to potential subsequent evaluation. This mecha-
nism is combined with public self-awareness theory,
which suggests that, while under observation, individuals
are more likely to comply with established rules and
exhibit behaviors that align with external expectations
(Duval & Wicklund, 1972; Farrar, 2013). As individual offi-
cers are more aware that their actions could come under
public attention and scrutiny, officer behaviors might be
more likely to align with public expectations (Froming
et al., 1982).
Part of the motivation for close observation of police
behavior stems from how frequently police, as street-level
bureaucrats, utilize discretion when making decisions that
affect the public. Police officers, in cases where an action
is not mandated by departmental policy or law, exercise
discretion in choosing which action to take (Huff, 2021;
Walker, 1993). They consider legal and extralegal factors
when taking intrusive measures, such as arrests, against
individuals. Legal factors include the evidence available
to justify an arrest, the presence or cooperation of a vic-
tim,
or
the
seriousness
of
an
offense
(Mastrofski
et al., 1995). Research shows, for example, that offense
severity, a legal factor, is a consistent predictor of arrest
(Brown & Frank, 2006). Regarding legal factors, officers’
discretion may play a limited role. In other instances,
however, officers may rely on extralegal factors, including
a person’s age, sex, race/ethnicity, neighborhood compo-
sition, or perception of threat (Mastrofski et al., 1995;
Sierra-Arévalo, 2024). Extralegal factors are often com-
bined with officer-specific factors, including officer race
(Brown
&
Frank,
2006;
Mbuba,
2018),
sex
(Novak
et al., 2011), education level (Rosenfeld et al., 2020), or
tenure (Mastrofski et al., 2000), and shape arrest decisions
jointly.
The effects of BWCs on police actions are likely more
pronounced when police action is driven by an officer’s
discretionary interpretation of the situation and the civil-
ian. We contend that arrests related to violent crimes or
property crimes, such as gang violence, armed robbery,
or theft, are unlikely to be affected drastically by BWC
adoption. For these types of crimes—also considered Part
1 Crimes according to the FBI due to their seriousness—
there may be limited room for officer discretion in making
sense of the scene, as the cases have a victim, and arrests
are primarily a reaction to legal factors directly relating to
the criminality of the individual’s conduct. By contrast,
there are other cases where officers have a more compre-
hensive range of behavioral choices based on their discre-
tion. For instance, calls for service that are categorized as
public disorder are often relatively less severe and more
likely subject to an officer’s discretionary judgment. Offi-
cers may choose the course of action based on the discre-
tion afforded to them rather than abiding by formal rules
and procedures that guide more serious scenarios where
evidence of criminality or danger is most apparent. Officer
behavioral choices may range from showing lenience,
such as loosely interpreting any rules to meet the individ-
uals’ needs (Tummers et al., 2015), or based on their
moral judgments (Maynard-Moody & Musheno, 2000), to
being overly aggressive to maintain control of the situa-
tion (Sierra-Arévalo, 2024). However, after BWC adoption,
officers may be discouraged from relying solely on their
discretionary latitude and, instead, incentivized to adhere
to official protocols and prevalent norms. The change in
officer behavior may be driven by the potential scrutiny
that cameras bring from the public or from supervisory
reviews of recorded police-civilian interactions. Based on
this perspective, the conventional expectation for BWC
programs in the US is that the device will lead officers to
practice more caution in their actions against individuals,
resulting in closer adherence to organizational policy and
norms, and a potential decline in coercive measures such
as arrests or the use of force (Ariel et al., 2015).
On the other hand, there has been reasonable specu-
lation that BWCs may increase, rather than decrease
police arrest and use of coercion. Kang (2023) stated: “the
device may encourage coercive police actions when it is
used to enhance officer performance, such as by protect-
ing officers from misleading complaints or discouraging
them from stretching rules” (p. 972). Contrary to past
cases where officers may have used their discretion to be
more lenient, the presence of BWCs may encourage a
stricter adherence to policy or performance standards—
particularly in cases where arrests are encouraged or
mandatory. Thus, a cautionary note is warranted against
automatic assumptions about BWCs reducing police per-
formance or authority because they constrained discre-
tion. The technology itself is value-neutral; its impact on
bureaucratic behavior depends on why it is adopted and
how it is implemented. Indeed, the body of empirical evi-
dence remains mixed with respect to the direction of the
effects of BWCs on police behaviors. While there are stud-
ies suggesting that BWCs led to fewer arrests (Braga,
Barao, et al., 2020; Braga, Coldren Jr, et al., 2020;
MacDonald et al., 2016), fewer fatalities stemming from
police–civilian encounters (Miller & Chillar, 2022) and

Inconsistent research evidence, mainly conducted
within single agencies, is attributable to various agency-
specific political or bureaucratic factors, such as the agen-

example, some agencies might have leaders who priori-
tize enhanced officer performance and safety outcomes
through BWCs. By contrast, other agencies might face
stronger demands from local community leaders to priori-
tize procedural and accountability values. The details of
the BWC policy, such as how the device should be used,
or how recorded video footage should be disclosed (Lum
et al., 2019), will vary greatly across agencies, resulting in
variations in how officers change behaviors once the
cameras are implemented. Therefore, while single-unit
case studies may be meaningful within their context, they
do not necessarily produce generalizable evidence that
captures the overall patterns of how BWCs affect coercive
police actions.
BODY-WORN CAMERAS, OFFICER
DISCRETION AND RACIAL DISPARITIES
The literature on bureaucratic discretion and control
extends to the discussion of BWCs’ potential to mitigate
racial disparities in policing, namely for disparities that
may result from officers wielding elevated levels of discre-
tion. Thus, BWC policies have been discussed as one tool
that may help remedy consistent findings that Black indi-
viduals are more likely to be stopped, searched, frisked
and arrested than their White counterparts (e.g., Epp
et al., 2014; Headley, 2020; Headley & Wright, 2020;
Pierson et al., 2020). While reasons attributed to racial dis-
parities in police outcomes vary, research has consistently
shown disparities in arrests
(Black, 1970; Brown
&
Frank, 2006; Kochel et al., 2011; Smith & Visher, 1981).
An individual’s race is an extralegal factor that should
not be used as an evaluative factor when considering the
legality of behavior; however, due to officers’ subjective
heuristics and biases as well as structural policy decisions,
race tends to be associated with coercive measures
(e.g., Pierson et al., 2020). Once police-civilian interactions
are video recorded through BWCs, the consequence, one
hopes, is a higher likelihood that officers refrain from
using discretion in racially biased ways that could lead to
civilian sanctions. Here, BWCs would serve as an oversight
mechanism that reduces officers’ idiosyncratic discretion-
ary judgments based on individuals’ race.
Despite the attention given to BWCs as a path to
enhancing racial justice in policing, empirical evidence is
still needed. Even the few studies that explored the issue
alluded to a tenuous link between BWCs and racial justice
in policing. For instance, Pyo (2021) and Kang (2023)
found null results for BWCs on racial disparities in intru-
sive police actions. Several explanations have been
offered regarding BWC policies’ sufficiency to guard
against racial biases in policing. For instance, racial bias as
a matter of human intent that is difficult to prove entirely,
even with video footage (Birzer & Birzer, 2006; Coleman &
Kocher, 2019; Garrett, 2000).
Additionally, there may be cases where racial biases
are deeply embedded in the agency’s culture and struc-
ture, affecting frontline officers, mid- or upper-level man-
agers, or other policy decisions (such as the deployment
of police). Consequently, racial equity might not be fully
incorporated into the program’s implementation process,
which ranges from reviewing the footage to enforcing
disciplinary measures. Considering the ongoing scholarly
debates and the scarcity of evidence on the benefits of
BWCs for promoting racial equity in policing, our empiri-
cal analysis seeks to utilize more comprehensive data
from major local law enforcement agencies in the US
between 2008 and 2019, a period that marked a signifi-
cant return to demands for racial justice in policing.

Dependent variables
Our dependent variables capture the number of arrests,
categorized into two subcategories: 1) crime types, and 2)
crime types broken down by the race of an arrested per-
son. There are seven categories of arrests broken down
by the following crime types: total crimes, violent crimes,
property crimes, drug crimes, weapon crimes, alcohol
crimes, and public disorder crimes. We categorized arrests
for violent crime (e.g., murder, rape, robbery, assault) and
property
crime (e.g., burglary, larceny, theft, arson)
according to the FBI’s classifications. The types of crimes
that make up violent and property crime categorizations
are often considered Part 1 Crimes by the FBI because
they are more serious infractions, occur regularly across
the US, and have a higher likelihood of being reported to
the police (e.g., 911 call). Drug crimes refer to the total
number of arrests for drug abuse incidents, including the
manufacture, distribution, possession, use, or sale of
drugs. Weapon crimes include arrests for the unlawful
sale, purchase, carry, possession, or use of weapons. Alco-
hol crimes include arrests for driving under the influence,
liquor law violations (such as unprohibited sale, purchase,
transportation, or use), and drunkenness that inhibits and
impairs mental and physical capabilities. Finally, public
disorder crimes include arrests made for disorderly con-
duct, vagrancy, suspicion and juvenile curfew and loiter-
ing violations. Arrests for drugs, alcohol and public
disorder crimes may have more variation in the level of
discretion an officer uses to identify the violation and for-
mally act on the violation (e.g., proactive pedestrian or
traffic stop). However, weapon arrests may entail less dis-
cretion to the extent the presence of a weapon is
recorded on camera as evidence. The number of total
arrests, as well as arrests of each crime type, were also
measured separately for White and Black populations.

DISCUSSION
BWC policies have been proposed by communities calling
for police reform and their adoption has rapidly expanded
across the US (Huff, 2021). BWCs provide a way to record
and review police-civilian interactions (Lum et al., 2019).
As such, drawing on insights from the literature on
bureaucratic discretion and control, we conceptualize
BWCs as a technology-based oversight mechanism. We
posit that BWCs should constrain discretionary judgments
of officers, potentially altering patterns of arrest, particu-
larly in scenarios where officers have relatively higher
opportunities to use their discretion. Focusing on arrest
outcomes, our staggered difference-in-difference analysis
examines how adopting BWCs affected police arrest deci-
sions at the agency level.
This study’s large-scale nationwide analysis confirms
an aggregate null effect of BWCs on arrests, despite
mixed results from existing studies focused on single
police departments—e.g., some studies indicated an
increase
in
arrests
post-BWC
adoption
(see
Braga
et al., 2018), while others suggest a reduction in arrests
post-adoption (Ariel, 2016). As previously discussed, varia-
tion in empirical findings across single-case studies could
be influenced by agency-specific contextual factors, such
as the intention behind adopting BWCs (e.g., to support
law enforcement crime-fighting activities versus to aid in
accountability and curb police misconduct), leadership
style,
organizational
culture,
and
other
factors.
For
instance, research has suggested that while BWCs were
initially introduced in the US as a tool for public transpar-
ency and procedural accountability, their role gradually
shifted towards agency-centric purposes such as improv-
ing officer safety, enhancing evidence quality, and reduc-
ing agency liability (Hyland, 2018; Merola et al., 2016;
Robertson et al., 2022). In addition, it is possible that

tion from officers, where officers in some departments
may feel elevated pressure to be active, and officers in
other settings may feel pressure to be cautious with their

Beyond our interest in understanding the effects of
BWC adoption on overall arrest patterns, we assess the
effect of adoption on arrest patterns by civilian race and
crime type. Calls for BWC adoption as a means for police
reform, in addition to the hope for more effective reviews
of police misconduct, stem from the expectation that this
technological oversight will constrain officer discretionary
behavior and limit, to an extent, arbitrary subjectivities in
their decisions. Our results suggest that BWCs helped mit-
igate racial disparities in police arrests, to an extent,
highlighted by an overall statistically significant decrease
in total arrests of Black individuals post-BWC adoption.
This effect was concentrated primarily in alcohol-related
arrests that involve higher levels of officer discretion. Vio-
lations related to alcohol regulation (e.g., liquor law viola-
tions, public drunkenness, or DUI tests following a traffic
stop) are more likely to be susceptible to officer discretion
due partially to the nature of the offense and to the rela-
tively lower severity of the offense. These offenses carry a
higher likelihood that officers have leeway in decision-
making compared with violent crimes or property crimes
that may be more serious, have evidence documented via
BWC, or may have a clear victim who is seeking police
assistance.

In our study, arrests for property, violent, drug and
public disorder crimes remain relatively steady pre-
and post-BWC adoption and across both White and Black
individuals. Put differently, there are many sub-categories
of arrests where we do not observe statistically significant
changes post-BWC adoption (according to the use of Call-
away and Sant’Anna’s more conservative estimator). This
aligns with prior studies of the general effects of BWCs on
arrests (see Pyo, 2021) and may not be surprising, given
mixed results from prior studies and BWC implementation
occurring within varying organizational contexts across a
large sample of departments. This reinforces our sugges-
tion that BWCs may increase or decrease arrests, depend-
ing on their purpose and use post-adoption. For example,
our findings indicate an increase in arrests of White indi-
viduals for weapons offenses post-adoption. In these
cases, it may be that BWCs are constraining discretion
when clear documentation of crime is evident, such as
the presence of a weapon being recorded on camera, in
that the officer has a stronger incentive to arrest as
opposed to letting someone go.
A broader implication is that across nearly 700 local
police agencies, BWC adoption did not necessarily lead to
officers pulling back from arrest activities involving sub-
stantive public safety threats out of concern that their
behavior is now being monitored. Therefore, police
leaders should not worry that BWC adoption leads officers
to reduce their use of authority. Simultaneously, our find-
ings also mean that police leaders cannot assume that
BWC adoption solves issues of police misuse of authority
(especially where misuse of authority is hard to discern
from the data used herein). BWCs may be an effective
oversight mechanism to dampen some negative effects of
bureaucratic discretion in specific types of cases, particu-
larly by constraining bureaucratic discretion in scenarios
where police traditionally wield relatively more discretion
over outcomes. Ultimately, whether and how BWCs and
similar technological oversight mechanisms influence
bureaucratic discretion depends on the purpose of adop-
tion and how it is implemented post-adoption. The com-
bined effects found in this analysis may allow police and
community leaders to better weigh the strengths and lim-
itations of BWCs as an oversight mechanism that con-
strains officer discretion.
To understand whether officers become more or less
responsive to the effects of monitoring over time, we pre-
sent event study plots (see Figure 1) that illustrate the
gradual growth of the initial effects of BWC adoption.
Indeed, we observe that patterns of relatively fewer
arrests of Black individuals, particularly for discretionary
offenses, seem slightly stronger as we get further from
adoption. This may indicate that changes to officer
behavior stem from departmental changes beyond BWC
adoption; however, it also aligns with our expectations
that new policies require time to be adopted, particularly
in organizations that are rigid and resistant to change
(Fernandez & Rainey, 2017). As bureaucrats adopt, adjust
to, and use innovative technologies, there are often learn-
ing curves, ramp-ups in buy-in, and various departmental
timelines for updating related policies, which may have
led to a lag in the effects of BWCs. The rapid adoption of
BWC policies in the US followed a series of high-profile
incidents of police misconduct and abuse of power
against racial minorities. Notably, after the police killing of
Michael Brown in Ferguson, Missouri, in 2014, then-
President Obama endorsed BWCs as a necessary measure
to enhance racial equity in policing. As such, agencies
adopting BWCs likely faced ongoing pressure to imple-
ment additional written policies to enhance racial equity
(e.g., implementing implicit bias training) and reduce
harm (e.g., de-escalation training). Given this background,
we speculate that the effect of BWCs on racial disparities
in arrests may not have occurred immediately after their
adoption but could have developed through additional
organizational policies introduced at a later time. Alterna-
tively, it may be that BWC adoption—an actual mecha-
nism to monitor police action and follow up on police
decisions—serves best as a technological oversight that
assists other reform-oriented policies in constraining
officer behavior, as other research has shown accountabil-
ity
mechanisms
can
work
best
when
combined
(Headley, 2022). Future research may consider whether
oversight is enough or if oversight paired with additional
policies leads to stronger effects in the desired direction.
CONCLUSION
This study adds to our understanding of technology over-
sight mechanisms, discretion in street-level bureaucracies
and coercive interactions with the public. Drawing on the
literature on bureaucratic control and discretion as well as
prior empirical evaluations of BWCs in policing, we exam-
ine whether BWCs affect police arrests in the US, particu-
larly arrests that involve varying levels of discretion.
Existing empirical studies have primarily relied on single-
case analyses. As such, it was unclear whether BWCs
increase or decrease arrests and whether BWCs mitigate
racial disparities in arrests. We performed a large-N rigor-
ous causal design with over 600 police departments
across a 12-year period. We found BWCs decreased the
total number of Black arrests, albeit by a small margin,
and this effect was concentrated in alcohol-related crime
arrests, which, arguably, are one type of offense with
greater levels of officer discretion compared to violent or
property crime. We did not find any effects of BWCs on
Black arrests with other subcategories of arrests, such vio-
lent, property, or drug-related crimes. Taken together, this
demonstrates that the effects of BWCs are limited, at best,
in constraining officer proactivity and mitigating racial
disparities more broadly.
The contributions of this research should be consid-
ered alongside some limitations of our study. First, as dis-
cussed above, we acknowledge that BWCs have been

implemented against the backdrop of larger calls for
police reform in the US. It is plausible that agencies imple-
mented multiple reforms in their organizations alongside
BWC programs. As such, there is a possibility that our
measure of BWC adoption may be one indicator of an
accountability mechanism coupled with many other polit-
ical and bureaucratic factors—leading to a potential bun-
dled treatment effect. As such, our results should be
interpreted cautiously, and we encourage future studies
to employ empirical approaches capable of distinguishing
BWC adoption from other relevant reform initiatives that
may coincide during the same period.
Inconsistencies in findings from BWC evaluations on
similar outcomes cannot solely be resolved by improving
methodological rigor. Instead, such variation is often
attributable to a range of administrative and managerial
factors that affect the process of how organizations
implement public policies. This is where impact analysis
studies could benefit from incorporating theoretical
insights from public administration and management
research. Future research could respond to this concern
by considering the details of the policies that have been
adopted. Particularly when there is a widespread rise in
adoption, researchers could examine differential effects
on officer behavior within departments that adopt a more
stringent version of the policy versus an adoption with
fewer initial rules or restrictions. In this context, greater
stringency could mean requirements to record at all
times, lengthier timelines on keeping recorded footage,
or clear sanctions if BWC policy is not followed.
Second, scholars argue that a “Ferguson effect” in
police agencies occurred following the 2014 protests
in response to the killing of Michael Brown in Ferguson.
This effect refers to officers engaging in less proactive
policing, such as police stops and searches (Shjarback
et al., 2017), which could increase crime. That said, the
evidence of this effect is limited (Wallace et al., 2018).
Granted, if officers changed the way they policed more
broadly, then it is plausible that a decline in arrests
occurred due to reduced enforcement or fewer officers
proactively patrolling; however, we would expect this
change to happen across more crime types and in all
police departments alike (those with and without BWC
adoption)—especially since one of the primary reasons
why police departments reported not adopting was due
to funding and resource constraints (not necessarily lack
of desire, will, or need; see Hyland, 2018). Nonetheless,
there is mixed empirical evidence regarding the factors
that lead to BWC adoption or resistance. For instance,
Andreescu and Kim (2022) found that police departments
with more complaints and excessive use of force inci-
dents were more likely to resist BWC adoption. On the
other hand, Pyo (2022) reported that police departments
with heightened conflict between the police and commu-
nities of color were more likely to implement BWCs.
Third, questions exist in the literature regarding mea-
surement approaches and appropriate benchmarks con-
cerning racial disparities (Knox & Mummolo, 2020), which
is particularly important when explaining biased or dis-
criminatory police behavior (Neil & Winship, 2019; Tregle
et al., 2021). Any conclusions drawn from our findings
about the effects of BWCs on racial equity in police
behavior should be interpreted with caution, as we can-
not causally attribute discriminatory motives of individual
actors as the reason for changes in racial patterns of
arrests.
Relatedly,
our
organizational-level
quasi-
experimental approach does not allow us to unpack
individual-level mechanisms between BWC adoption and
police arrest decisions. Scholars suggest that BWC effects
might be driven by changes in police behavior, civilian
behavior, or a combination of both (see Kim, 2019); how-
ever, we cannot deduce the key drivers herein.
Limitations aside, this study contributes to our
understanding of constraints on bureaucratic discretion
by providing more generalizable evidence on how this
technological
oversight
mechanism
affected
police
arrests in the US for the police departments in our sam-
ple. This contribution to the literature complements and
clarifies previous studies aimed at understanding the
effects of BWC adoption that predominantly focused on
a single agency within a unique context or multiple
agencies within a more limited time frame. We acknowl-
edge that BWCs alone are not a panacea to improving
police behavior and police–civilian interactions, nor to
effectively reducing racial disparities. This study high-
lights the benefits and limitations of constraining officer
discretion in scenarios where we traditionally observe
disparate outcomes while also amplifying our need to
understand the influence of varying accountability con-
texts that inform the effects of major policy adoptions.
Oversight mechanisms depend on rewards and sanc-
tions to regulate behavior extrinsically; however, their
effectiveness may also depend on cultural reform, lead-
ership changes, or training and education initiatives nec-
essary to alter officers’ intrinsic willingness and ability to
meet public needs.

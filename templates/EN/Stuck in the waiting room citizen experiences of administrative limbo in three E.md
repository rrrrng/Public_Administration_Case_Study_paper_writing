Abstract: In recent years, there have been numerous cases where citizens have found themselves
stuck in highly protracted administrative processes affecting outcomes vital to their lives and
livelihoods. However, systematic inquiries into the experience of being trapped by bureaucratic
procedures or state inaction for sometimes multiple years are still lacking. To study the
interactions between citizens and the state when long waiting times and significant outcomes are
involved, we introduce the concept of administrative limbo. We examine administrative limbo
using primary and secondary interview data from the Irish defective concrete block crisis, the
Dutch childcare benefits affair, and the Dutch Groningen gas crisis. Findings suggest that
experiences of administrative limbo are marked by prolonged and extensive uncertainty,
accumulating strain, and negatively impacted life perspectives. We find that the effects of being
stuck in administrative limbo are profound, indicating that temporal experiences of inaction and
action should be further explored in public administration.

1. Introduction:
Government decisions are crucial in people’s lives. However, some decisions involve lengthy
waiting times and procedures that can take years. Examples include compensation and redress
applications (Gill & Picou, 2008; De Togni, 2022, p. 171), asylum and migration procedures (Moynihan
et al., 2022), and judicial or administrative appeals on decisions and sanctions (McClean, 2019).
Occasionally, citizens find themselves in a state of uncertainty because no applicable procedure is
available at all (Ceballos et al., 2021; Binder et al., 2020). From the state's perspective, meticulous
decision-making processes can be justified to ensure due process or the legality of decisions. However,
from an individual’s perspective, prolonged waiting times for vital outcomes can lead to extreme
uncertainty (Hartonen et al., 2021), resulting in increased levels of strain and negative outlooks on life
(De Togni, 2022; Binder et al., 2020). Lengthy waiting periods can make individuals feel trapped in a
bureaucratic no-man’s-land with no clear way out. We propose a new conceptualization of
administrative limbo to capture this experience of being stuck in administrative procedures that concern
vital decisions affecting life and livelihood without any prospect of resolution.
This article develops the concept of administrative limbo through in-depth research of cases
involving prolonged procedures. We examine cases in the Netherlands and Ireland. For the Netherlands,
we analyze the childcare benefits scandal and the Groningen gas crisis. The childcare benefits scandal
occurred from 2004 onwards and centered on improperly (and often discriminatorily) revoked childcare
benefits (Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 2020). Affected citizens frequently accumulated high
debt as the Dutch tax administration, responsible for childcare benefits, actively obstructed citizens'
appeals (Fenger & Simonse, 2024). Redress procedures are expected to continue until 2026-2027. The
Groningen gas crisis involves manmade earthquakes caused by gas extraction in the province of
Groningen. In 2012, a severe earthquake triggered protests and thousands of damage claims.
Nevertheless, gas extraction continued for several years. Meanwhile, redress procedures and a public
sector program to reinforce houses faced multiyear delays (Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 2023).
In Ireland, we examine the so-called ‘defective concrete blocks’ crisis. Around 2012, campaigners raised
awareness that building blocks for housing contained excessive levels of muscovite and iron sulfides,
causing blocks to lose their inner strength. Severe cracking, structural issues, and moisture infiltration
have impacted thousands of homes in Ireland, particularly in County Donegal in the far northwest of the
country. In some cases, the only solution is to demolish and rebuild the house. The damages often exceed
the financial capacity of the affected families (Expert Panel Concrete Blocks, 2017). A 2021 government
scheme was introduced but had to be revised, causing further delays and frustration for homeowners
who had been aware of the problem for years.
The case studies demonstrate how citizens experience living in administrative limbo as feeling
‘stuck,’ ‘trapped,’ ‘abandoned,’ or ‘ground down.’ Using an abductive approach (Timmermans &
Tavory, 2012), we identify three core elements of the administrative limbo experience: (1) a negatively

2. Theoretical framework
The administrative limbo concept is not entirely new; various fields use terms like legal,
bureaucratic, or administrative limbo to describe lengthy waiting processes. First, in migration studies,
limbo encapsulates the idea of lives being put on hold by indefinite asylum and migration procedures
(e.g., Haas, 2017; Vrăbiescu, 2019; Doná, 2015; Nimführ & Sesay, 2016; Cabot, 2012). A first meta-
analysis of limbo situations in migration conceptualizes limbo as a ‘betwixt-and-between state’
(Hartonen et al., 2021): refugees’ limbo experiences are marked by outcomes beyond their control “and
in which there is no improvement or progress in sight” (Hartonen et al., 2021, p. 1136). In migration,
limbo may arise both in transit and in the host state. In transit, limbo is often defined by a lack of formal
legal status and its associated rights. In host states, individuals face undetermined waiting for the
potential grant of legal statuses (e.g., asylum or temporary protection status) in migration or detention
facilities, with these facilities symbolizing their disempowerment (Hartonen et al., 2021). Such limbo
experiences can be linked to severe stress, feelings of powerlessness, and uncertainty (Cabot, 2012;
Hartonen et al., 2021; Abrego & Lakhani, 2015). Secondly, legal scholarship uses the concept of limbo
to describe situations where litigation is impossible, such as when a person lacks standing before a court.
This leads to a paralyzing and inescapable situation, without prospect of resolution (Ceballos et al.,
2021). Finally, scholars in disaster studies have used the term limbo to describe the experiences of
victims who are ineligible for post-hurricane government buyout programs (Binder et al., 2020)
However, the few existing systematic definitions originate from migration studies, where the
concept is specifically aimed at addressing entrapment in the home state, the migration routes, and the
acquisition of refugee status at the destination. These definitions are less generalizable to prolonged
administrative procedures outside the migration context (Hartonen et al., 2021). A more broadly
applicable definition is necessary to establish the concept for public administration research. We intend
to develop such a broader concept abductively based on observations from our case study analysis (see
sections 3-5), existing insights into limbo and administrative burdens, and theoretical insights from
migration, disaster response, stress, and administrative burden studies (Hartonen et al., 2021; Halling et
al., 2024; Timmermans & Tavory, 2012).

We contend that administrative limbo comprises both objective and experienced components
(Haas, 2017; Binder et al., 2020; Nimführ & Sesay, 2019; Moynihan et al., 2015). This distinction is
based on the administrative burden literature, where state action is distinguished from subjective
experiences thereof. Administrative burdens scholars argue that experiences of burdens are subjective,
being experienced differently by differing individuals (Moynihan et al., 2015; Baekgaard & Tankink,
2022; Bell et al., 2024). As such, burdens scholars argue that citizen-state experiences should be
separated from the objective components of government decision-making (Tiggelaar & George, 2023).
What is more, burdensome experiences are often distributed unequally across society, differing based
on factors such as individual-level resources and socio-economic position (Halling & Baekgaard, 2024;
Herd & Moynihan, 2025; Monrad, 2024; Bell et al., 2024; Tiggelaar & George, 2023).1 These insights
on experiences of state action are similarly relevant in our context of state inaction, and the way that
waiting for state action is experienced. We therefore define administrative limbo as: being stuck in an
uncertain, prolonged, and complex period of waiting for state action, where state action is essential to
resolve a salient issue, and where being stuck waiting leads to experiences of loss of control, loss of
perspective, and an accumulating strain on life. The definition encompasses both an objective state of
waiting and limbo as a lived experience, both of which we argue are necessary to call an individual-
level experience administrative limbo. The definition also recognizes that both actions taken by the state
and instances in which the state fails to act can entrap individuals in limbo. We discuss each of these
elements in turn. We subsequently conclude the theory section with a discussion of how administrative
burdens and limbo are distinct yet complementary perspectives for understanding citizen-state
interactions.
2.1. Waiting for resolution – objective elements of limbo
Administrative limbo has two objective elements. First, issues are typically unresolvable until
some form of state action is undertaken. Examples include disaster relief exceeding victims’ means (De
Togni, 2022, p.116; Binder et al., 2020), acquiring refugee status (Hartonen et al., 2021), or lifting an
unjustly imposed sentence. Secondly, limbo entails extended waiting periods, sometimes lasting years
(Hartonen et al., 2021; Cabot, 2012). The urgency of state action would not pose a problem if decisions
could be made rapidly. However, situations of limbo also involve protracted waiting. Individuals may
follow multiple, sometimes mutually conflicting procedures (e.g., one application cannot be completed
due to the need to obtain documents, proofs, or certificates through another stalled procedure (De Togni,
2022, p.115; Nisar, 2018)). Waiting can be prolonged by administrative appeals against potentially
unlawful decisions, mandatory applications for extensions, or litigation in court (Cabot, 2012; Hartonen

an individual’s situation may truly become unresolvable (De Togni, 2022; Binder et al., 2020).
2.2. Feeling stuck - Experiences of administrative limbo
Three elements are central to the experience of administrative limbo. First, experiences of
administrative limbo often manifest as a lack of perspective or an altered outlook towards the future, as
individuals perceive themselves to be stuck with stressors so severe that these must be resolved before
they can move on (e.g., high debt, condemned housing) (De Togni, 2022; Binder et al., 2020). Thus,
time is perceived as suspended (Nimführ & Sesay, 2019; Hartonen et al., 2012). Individuals may, for
example, report feeling trapped in their situations without seeing light at the end of the tunnel or believe
they will lose years of their lives to their focal issue—years they had envisioned spending differently.
Such experiences were reported among nuclear refugees displaced after the 2011 Fukushima incident,
where many individuals lacked a sense of future perspective as their financial aid was set to end in 2017
(De Togni, 2022 p.101-102 p.116), as well as among labor migrants and asylum seekers awaiting the
outcomes of their applications (Hartonen et al., 2021). We expand on these findings by arguing that
negatively impacted life perspectives are not only relevant to physically displaced individuals but also
to others caught in prolonged, significant, and/or stalling government procedures
Secondly, individuals experiencing administrative limbo often report low levels of control and
feelings of powerlessness as they confront ambiguous and worsening personal situations while awaiting
administrative action (Binder et al., 2020). For instance, they may express uncertainty regarding their
personal circumstances or when the state will finally take action (Hartonen et al., 2021), or they may
feel helpless in the face of an unresponsive bureaucracy (Nisar, 2018; De Togni, 2022: 138; Binder et
al., 2020). Recent research in public administration has already indicated that uncertainty is almost
inherently present before, during, and after citizen-state interactions (Cecchini, 2024). In cases of limbo,
we argue that as (unsuccessful) interactions intertwine over extended periods, uncertainty and a
perceived lack of control may similarly become a continuous experience (Hartonen et al., 2021).
Alternatively, individuals in limbo may face a lack of state action and bureaucratic interactions. This
also induces perceived lack of control, as individuals feel they have nowhere to turn. Perceived lack of
control contributes to a prolonged sense of entrapment in limbo (Belloni, 2019 p.134)
Thirdly, the ongoing, grinding nature of individuals’ limbo experiences produces escalating
levels of strain, which often manifest as chronic stress, emotional exhaustion, or fatigue (De Togni, 2022

p.127-128; Binder et al., 2020). Conservation of resources (COR) theory posits that stress arises as
individuals strive to acquire and maintain the resources they value, offering a compelling perspective
on how stress develops when individuals find themselves in administrative limbo (Hobfoll, 2016;
Döring & Madsen, 2022). A salient issue such as fraud accusations or obtaining disaster relief is likely
to threaten valued resources. Therefore, individuals, may need to invest other resources—including
energy, finances, and time—in an effort to mitigate the threat and achieve a positive outcome. However,
when stuck in administrative limbo, these investments prove futile, even as the primary threat of
resource loss looms over the individual. The net result is that multiple types of resources are lost
simultaneously. These so-called ‘resource loss bandwagons’ lead to persistent and increasing stress
levels (Hobfoll, 2016). Prolonged stress as a response to being stuck in limbo has been previously
described by migration scholars (Hartonen et al., 2021) and in disaster studies (Binder et al., 2020).
However, we propose that related sources of strain, such as emotional exhaustion and fatigue, should
also be taken into account (De Togni, 2022 p.127-128), thereby justifying the use of the broader term
strain.
2.3. Moderators and mediators: how does waiting lead to experiences of administrative limbo?
Simply having to wait for the state is insufficient to produce experiences of administrative
limbo. We argue, drawing on insights in administrative burden theory (e.g. Baekgaard & Tankink, 2022;
Halling et al., 2024), disaster studies (e.g. Binder, 2020), stress theory (e.g. Hobfoll et al., 2016) and our
own observations, that several linking variables must be considered to fully understand when waiting
leads to experiences of limbo.
First, the issue needing resolution must be highly salient to an individual. The term salient refers
to outcomes of significant consequence, such as determining someone’s income, life savings, health,
housing, legal liability, or innocence in criminal law (Moynihan et al., 2022; Binder, 2020; De Togni,
2020) – that is, matters impacting essential needs like security, health, or income. While a lengthy appeal
process for an unfairly issued parking ticket can be frustrating, such matters are less likely to result in a
feeling of being stuck in limbo compared to a protracted appeal against a government sanction that cuts
off the majority of one’s income or against an order that displaces your family (De Togni, 2022: p.1-4).
When an issue is not of critical importance, individuals can choose exit options by not pursuing further
appeals. Conversely, situations that are likely to evoke experiences of administrative limbo involve
outcomes so important that conceding is simply not feasible (e.g. Belloni, 2019 p.133-134).
Second, personal resources likely moderate experiences of administrative limbo. This moderator
builds on the argument in administrative burden theory that burdens are often distributive (Moynihan et
al., 2015; Nisar, 2018; Martin et al., 2023; Halling et al., 2024), and the argument in psychological stress
theories that individuals with more resources frequently experience less strain from a stressor compared

7
to their counterparts with fewer resources (Biggs et al., 2017; Hobfoll et al., 2016; Binder et al., 2020).
Resources such as individual coping styles, personality traits, expertise, financial resources,
administrative & emotional capital, and social networks can all be crucial in determining how objective
states of administrative limbo influence the experience of being in limbo (Binder et al., 2020; Nimführ
& Sesay, 2016; Moynihan et al., 2015; De Togni, 2022 p.102; Monrad, 2024). For example, extensive
social networks may provide an applicant with the necessary information to navigate the system more
effectively while also offering emotional support, thus reducing the extent to which an objective state
of limbo manifests as administrative limbo in lived experience (Hobfoll et al., 2016; Masood & Nisar,
2021). Emotional capital may allow some citizens to express emotions at the right time (such as anger),
allowing individuals to cope with burdensome citizen-state interactions (Monrad, 2024).
2.4. Feedback loops
Loss of or altered perspective, low control, and accumulating strain will create a feedback loop
through which an individual’s personal resources – particularly non-tangible resources such as self-
esteem and energy – are gradually diminished (Halling et al., 2024; & Hobfoll et al., 2016). Given that
these resources are expected to serve as a moderator between objective and experienced limbo,
experienced limbo may gradually erode the resilience of individuals, which in turn may increase the
extent to which the individual feels stuck in experienced limbo. Prolonged administrative limbo may
thus produce a resource loss spiral, further worsening experiences of limbo (Hobfoll et al. 2016).

2.5. Distinguishing between types of administrative limbo based on the relevance of administrative
burdens
The discussion to this point raises the question how administrative limbo relates to other theories
describing citizen-state interactions, in particular the administrative burdens literature (Moynihan et al.,
2015; Halling et al., 2024). Administrative burden scholars have convincingly shown that state action
may be perceived as onerous, resulting in learning, compliance, and psychological costs (Moynihan et
al., 2022; Halling et al., 2024; Baekgaard & Tankink, 2022; Döring & Madsen, 2022; Tiggelaar &
George, 2023). We argue that limbo can be considered analytically distinct but that the two frameworks
can serve as complementary tools. Administrative limbo sets itself apart for several reasons. First, limbo
explicitly focuses on suspended time and temporal dynamics rather than the immediate experiences
elicited by state action. Second, in an ideal-typical case of ‘pure’ administrative limbo (Nimführ &
Sesay, 2019), there is no requirement for prior state action to take place—as is typically assumed in the
burdens framework (Baekgaard & Tankink, 2022). A lack of state action implies that learning and
compliance costs are limited, as there is no government scheme or program to learn about or comply
with. Thus, individuals have nowhere to go, leaving them with nothing to do but wait in suspended time
(Nimführ & Sesay, 2019; Hartonen et al., 2021). Later, we will observe that the ‘defective concrete
block’ crisis in Ireland exhibits elements of such ‘pure limbo.’
Beyond situations of pure administrative limbo, instances of ‘burdensome limbo’ may also arise.
Here, limbo, learning costs, and compliance costs are all pertinent, and collectively lead to psychological
outcomes such as stress. Such situations typically involve government programs that, for various
reasons—whether intentional or unintentional—have come to a standstill (Moynihan et al., 2022).
Citizens navigating these procedures may face prolonged waiting times and incur significant compliance
and learning costs to determine the status of their applications, expedite decision-making, or appeal
previous decisions (Moynihan et al., 2022; Holler et al., 2024). Applicants in these burdensome limbos
might find hope in intermediate ‘successes’ (e.g., an appeal to a prior decision has been submitted), only
to have their expectations dashed again (e.g., the application is deemed inadmissible over a technicality).
After years of such difficulties, time will again become a source of stress, low control and loss of
perspective. Simultaneously, sporadic peaks in learning and compliance costs may emerge as major
stressors—indicating that administrative burdens and limbo are complementary in such cases. These
peaks will occur whenever a new bureaucratic interaction in the broader waiting process is necessary,
and are interspersed with low-intensity but independently grinding waiting times.
When integrating the burdens and limbo paradigms, two types of limbo arguably emerge. First,
a type of ‘pure limbo’ that revolves almost entirely around waiting based on state inaction. Second, a
type of ‘burdensome limbo’ where waiting for state inaction is interspersed with burdensome
experiences of state action. Table 1 summarizes this discussion, presenting administrative burdens in

3. Data & methodology
We empirically examine the Dutch Groninger gas and childcare benefits affair, as well as the
Irish defective concrete blocks case. These are purposefully selected extreme cases. All feature severe
outcomes and long waiting times. While Groninger gas and defective concrete blocks relate to housing
issues, the childcare benefits affair involves unduly revoked benefits, enhancing our ability to generalize
across policy fields. Both the Groninger gas and childcare benefits affair align with the ‘burdensome
limbo’ concept introduced in section 2. Thus, in addition to lengthy processing times and entrapment,
citizens endured repeated and onerous interactions with the state. By contrast, citizens had no recourse
to government procedures until 2021 in the defective concrete blocks crisis. This situation brings
defective concrete blocks closer to a case of pure limbo (although post-2021 stressful interactions with
the state are also commonly reported). The selection of cases from the Netherlands and Ireland is based
on the researchers’ familiarity with both contexts, allowing us to conduct face-to-face interviews.
We conducted an abductive qualitative study that begins with an unexpected observation that
does not entirely align with the existing theory (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012). Following abductive
logic, we approached the cases with prior theoretical knowledge of administrative burden theory. We
initiated phase 1 of the empirical process by utilizing secondary data consisting of 12 interviews from
parliamentary inquiries. Through this analysis, the impact of waiting in suspended time over highly
salient outcomes emerged as a distinct dimension not fully captured by existing contributions to

4. Overview of cases
4.1. Groninger gas crisis
The Groninger gas crisis involves manmade earthquakes, damage redress, and home
reinforcement due to gas extraction in Groningen, the Netherlands. The issue gained national attention
in 2012, when the largest earthquake yet occurred (Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 2023). Despite
thousands of damage claims, the government and extraction company (NAM) continued to view gas
extraction as sufficiently safe. As the problems became clear, damage redress shifted to the public sector,
first to Centrum Veilig Wonen (CVW, private), then replaced by Instituut Mijnbouwschade (IMG,
public). By 2016, over 100,000 homeowners faced lengthy damage redress procedures (NOS, 2016). It
became clear that many houses needed reinforcement or demolition due to unsuitable designs for
earthquake zones. This initiated a separate reinforcement operation separate from damage reparations
(Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 2023). Citizens’ homes would be inspected, leading to a report
with recommendations on what reinforcement measures are necessary. Then, negotiations between
homeowners and the Nationaal Coordinator Groningen (NCG), the competent body for the
reinforcement operation, would start. Once this process was complete, the actual reinforcement or – in
some cases - ‘demolish-rebuild’ phase could start, often requiring homeowners to move to temporary
accommodation (Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 2023). As with damage redress, the
reinforcement operation was plagued by delays, and individual procedures could last years (Dückers et
al., 2023).

4.2. Childcare benefits affair
The childcare benefits affair centers on revoked benefits. Since 2008, the Dutch tax service
(Belastingdienst) had been tasked with rigorously enforcing childcare benefits fraud. Minor errors were
harshly penalized, often forcing citizens to repay all their childcare benefits (Fenger & Simonse, 2024).
The Tax Service particularly structurally targeted parents with double nationality, suggesting
discriminatory revocations (Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 2020). Appeals were met with tax
officials instructed not to consider the fairness of revocation decisions. Parents often lacked necessary
information for effective appeals (Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 2020), with repayment orders
sometimes nearing or surpassing their earnings. Victimized parents accumulated debts, frequently
leading to negative experiences with debt restructuring government services. Additionally, as labeled
fraudsters, they faced harsher treatment from public services beyond the Tax Service (Parliamentary
Committee of Inquiry, 2020). Childcare benefits enforcement issues were long denied by both the Tax
Service and the government. However, rising parliamentary and media scrutiny around 2017-2018
revealed that the Tax Service knew its actions were discriminatory and illegal, leading to a major scandal
(Fenger & Simonse, 2024). In 2021, the Dutch government resigned, prompting the next administration
to initiate redress procedures (Fenger & Simonse, 2024). Victims initially received 30,000 Euros and
local government support (Rijksoverheid, 2023), but many parents remain caught in redress processes.
A new Tax Service division, Uitvoeringsorganisatie Herstel Toeslagen (UHT), handles cases needing
more than the initial 30,000 Euro. However, it is expected that some parents, often waiting since 2012,
may not see outcomes until 2026 (Rijksoverheid, 2023).
4.3. Defective concrete blocks crisis
The Irish defective concrete blocks crisis involves blocks with high levels of harmful minerals
that weaken their strength. During the 2000s building boom, blocks containing excessive iron sulfides
(pyrite and pyrrhotite) and muscovite were used, allegedly without sufficient government oversight.
This reduces the bonding strength of the concrete, making it porous and prone to water absorption.
Consequently, severe cracking and crumbling occur, rendering some homes unsafe and requiring
complete rebuilding (Expert Panel Concrete Blocks, 2017). Thousands of homes across various counties
are currently affected, especially in County Donegal.  The Irish government became involved for several
reasons. First, many homeowners lack the financial resources to resolve the issue, as resolution may
require replacing walls or complete demolition. Second, the government was seen as partially at fault
due to insufficient oversight of building regulations. Public calls for redress began around 2011-2012
due to widespread cracking and decay, yet a scheme was not introduced until 2021. Although an initial
plan was adopted in 2021 at the County Council level, it was deemed inadequate, covering only 90% of
costs and excluding foundations. Individuals also faced extensive engineering costs to prove eligibility


## Page 13

13
for the scheme. A revised scheme emerged in July 2023, but campaigners argue it still suffers from
delays and shortfalls – including the exclusion of several counties where houses were affected (Deering,
2024). Demonstrating its political relevance, four candidates of 100% Redress were elected in 2024
local elections to Donegal County Council, and a 100% Redress candidate won a seat in the 2024
parliamentary elections (Ryan, 2024).
4.4. Critical comparison of cases
Integrating all three crises allows for an examination of limbo across cases that exhibit
commonalities as well as analytically relevant divergences (see Table 3). All cases involve extensive
waiting for citizens, as well as harms that emerged around 2012 and continue today. All three also
necessitated extensive reform to citizen compensation schemes, which itself was a cause for further
delay. However, some divergences could influence the way that waiting plays out. In the childcare
benefits affair, revoking benefits over minor mistakes (and among individuals with a migration
background) was active and intentional policy supported by parliament, government and the tax
administration. This intentionality implies that waiting for the state will often revolve around appeals to
undue punitive government action and its resultant harms. For Groningen and defective concrete blocks,
harms are indirectly caused by the public sector. Nonetheless, the public-private partnership
constellation of the Dutch gas extraction system (in which the extraction company is co-owned by the
Dutch government and determinations on annual levels of extracted natural gas are made at the
ministerial level) quickly implied strong government involvement in compensation. Redress options
were already available in 2012, only to become bogged down in high caseloads and uncertainty. For
homeowners in Groningen, waiting for the state frequently involves waiting for the outcomes of
applications or waiting on appeals to earlier applications. Defective concrete blocks Ireland is the case
with the least government involvement in the crisis’ initial stages. The government initially saw private
actors as responsible for defective building material. As resolution remained forthcoming, the Irish
government was eventually convinced to step in with a compensation scheme in 2021.
Thus, for Irish homeowners, waiting for the state revolved around a lack of government action
until 2021, combined with throughput times of procedures post-2021. Therefore, it is likely that the two
Dutch crises will feature a type of burdensome limbo, i.e. waiting for the state interspersed with
administrative burdens. For defective concrete blocks, we should find similar dynamics post-2021.
Before 2021, however, the case represents a type of pure limbo, in which homeowners had nowhere to
turn. By selecting these three cases, we thus differentiate the level of government involvement and the
type of limbo induced (burdensome and pure limbo).

5.1. Waiting for resolution (objective state)
Primary and secondary data from all three crises provide extensive evidence of individuals stuck
in an unresolvable situation and facing protracted waiting – the factors comprising objective states of
waiting for resolution. For Groninger gas, both redress for earthquake damage and reinforcement to
make homes resistant to future tremors produced waiting over unresolvable outcomes. Reinforcement
provides a clear example. Despite large groups of homeowners in the earthquake zone being informed
that their houses would need reinforcement to make them earthquake-resistant, the process leading up
to actual reinforcement was highly protracted. The first stage (i.e. obtaining an advisory report on the
needed reinforcement works) resulted in extensive waiting times, with some individuals having been
stuck at this point for 5-7 years. Due to the complex nature of the required works, as well as the extensive
cost of rebuilding or substantially redesigning a house, staying in the proverbial ‘waiting room’ is the
only option open for homeowners to resolve their problems. Those who do eventually get their
reinforcement advice may still find their procedures bogged down in negotiations further down the line,
as recounted by a civil society actor and victim:
R: And then the fight over the money begins. Is there a budget? The reports are often years old,
there is a preliminary design, and a budget has been drawn up. Prices for building materials
are rising enormously. So if it [the process] then stops for six months or a year, that adds
another 10% ... And then the cost expert from the NCG [competent public authority] goes
through it again: ‘Well, should this specifically be like this? That might also be possible, it
would be cheaper.’ Well, then you get into a fight with the NCG as a resident.
This quote illustrates how, after years of waiting, individuals may eventually see light at the end
of the tunnel, only for that perspective to be taken away by another stage of limbo – all the while facing
an increasingly problematic situation due to concurrent spikes in material costs.
Similarly, early in the childcare benefits affair, many affected individuals were stuck waiting
for the tax administration to clear up reasons for fraud accusations or to arrange bearable levels of
periodical debt repayments. Obtaining resolution was essential for many individuals to resolve extensive
debts but was not forthcoming. Attempts by parents to move the case forward (e.g. by calling call centers
or sending letters) were usually futile, doing little to speed up resolution. Parents were (unduly and often
discriminatorily) labelled as fraudsters, with civil servants having received the instruction to apply zero-
tolerance towards these parents. Thus, over time, addressing the issue at hand begins to feel futile:
R: Every time I got in touch, it was not a good phone call, not a good conversation: "You have
to pay!" Because the moment I gave them my social security number, they knew right away: no
mercy.”


## Page 18

18
Although the Dutch government set up compensation schemes in 2020-2021, the process to
compensate parents has proven protracted not unlike redress for earthquake problems. Thus, despite
differing subject matters, many parents affected by childcare benefits experienced being stuck in the
waiting room in a way similar to homeowners in the Groninger gas crisis.
Narratives from homeowners affected by defective concrete blocks in Ireland are similar but
with a notable twist. Whereas individuals in the Netherlands were trapped in existing procedures, some
homeowners with defective concrete blocks have been trapped without a redress scheme altogether. As
insurance companies were unwilling to provide remediation, homeowners in this crisis had nowhere to
turn to until the introduction of the first compensation scheme in 2021 – making this perhaps the clearest
example of being stuck waiting. Some homeowners were already aware of the adverse impact of
deleterious materials as early as 2012. Moreover, some counties with cases of defective concrete blocks
remained excluded from the scheme for several more years. One County Clare-based respondent only
became eligible in 2023, while a County Sligo-based respondent was still waiting for her county to be
included during our interview (Sligo was added to the scheme in October 2024 (Deering, 2024)). Even
if eligible, the barriers to submitting an application are frequently too high for homeowners to consider.
The scheme is, for instance, designed on a payback basis, requiring homeowners to pay costs before
declaring them for compensation to the County Council. With service costs for professionals (e.g.,
engineering costs) and costs for individual elements of a home frequently running in the thousands,
providing such advances is not feasible for many homeowners (see also subsection 5.3 on resources).
Foundations have also been excluded under both versions of the scheme, even though the deleterious
minerals may be found there as well. This leaves homeowners in ambiguity, as they are forced to choose
between rebuilding on possibly compromised foundations or paying new foundations themselves –
adding thousands to their bills. As an interviewed homeowner recounts:
R: …but then they’re not including foundations. Our house is particularly deep. Our house,
because we’re on boggy ground, they’re particularly deep here. You’re talking 30-, 40,000 for
foundations. That’s before I, before I even put a tile, a brick down. That’s what we’re facing.
In a post-interview e-mail exchange the respondent mentions that a new quote brought the cost
of replacing foundations down to an estimated 25.000 euros. Even then, her account illustrates that the
scheme’s design can result in substantial shortfalls and ambiguity for homeowners.
5.2. Lived experiences of administrative limbo
The core of the administrative limbo concept lies in how objective waiting generates lived
experiences of limbo. When trapped in administrative limbo, individual experiences are comprised at


## Page 19

19
least of 1) low levels of perceived control over their own situation and over the state action needed to
improve their situation, 2) an adversely affected sense of perspective and 3) accumulating strain.
Low control
Low control manifests in different ways. Data from all three crises describe extensive
uncertainty stemming from a lack of control over their issues. Childcare benefits affair victims were
often unaware why they were labeled as fraudsters, how they could prove their innocence, why their
benefits were revoked, or how they would be able to repay the amount they allegedly owed to the tax
administration:
R: There was only the concern: behind this door, we have a mega debt, which I don’t know
whether I will pay it or you... Because my mind kept spinning all the time: when I come back
home, will my things still be there, and will I still have a house? I assumed it wasn’t €125,000,-
but €125,-, until I was informed at work: [name], that is not €125,- but €125,000,-.
Similar perceptions of low control and uncertainty abound in the Groninger gas and defective
concrete blocks crises. Among other things, homeowners faced prolonged uncertainty over how
earthquake damage and deleterious materials in concrete might affect the safety of their houses. As a
homeowner in Groningen notes after inspections revealed that her farmhouse was unsafe to live in:
R: That was a big shock. Because you live there with two small children. They were 7 and 9
when it started, so when we heard this I think they were both two years older. But at that moment
you feel a huge responsibility: yes, and what does this mean, can we continue to live here?
Moreover, for defective concrete blocks specifically, uncertainty over financial shortfalls was
strongly pronounced, which is surprisingly similar to the financial uncertainty expressed by victims of
the childcare benefits affair.
Low control also manifests as experienced powerlessness against the actions or inaction of
impassive state bureaucracies. As repeated attempts at obtaining resolution prove futile, and as
resolution continues being delayed, individuals increasingly feel that the matter is out of their control
and is solely in the hands of a disinterested, inconsistent or hostile government. This manifestation of
low perceived control seemed particularly prevalent in Groninger gas. When discussing how competent
authorities for earthquake damage redress repeatedly cast doubt on the causality between earthquakes
and damage, a civil society leader/victim emphasizes how such distrust-based approaches generate
experiences of powerlessness among genuinely affected homeowners:
R: That feeling of powerlessness is really something. I find it a very intense feeling. You think:
it’s not right, it’s no good, those people don’t deserve it. All in all, they are all such exceptionally


## Page 20

20
reasonable people who would be the first to say: well, I think that crack was already there. They
are the people who are then approached about things that they really couldn’t do anything
about, didn’t ask for and are suffering from … Those people are also becoming less and less
reasonable and increasingly difficult.
Lack of or altered perspective
Although many individuals initially seem to be hopeful, the combination of protracted waiting
and the unresolvable nature of their situation alters individuals’ perspectives over time. Such changes
signify the transition from simply being stuck in a procedure to also feeling stuck in your life. As
administrative limbo drags on and there is no perspective toward resolution, an individual’s outlook on
life can become adversely affected. Some individuals report spending years in a situation they never
asked for, and feeling like these are “lost years” of their life. Instead of fulfilling the plans they had for
life (e.g. moving to a new place, taking up a new job), they are stuck with a stressor that ensnares them
(e.g. due to financial pressures associated with the issue). One defective concrete blocks respondent
describes the experience as looking at life with a ‘tainted lens’:
R: I would say the lens is completely tainted by it. It's just simple things like trying to plan a
family holiday or a family trip. It's like, oh, if this doesn't work out this scheme, we potentially
know, I know family holidays aren't the be all and end all, but you know, it's just every area that
potentially you might have financial drainage. It's like, are we taking this away from potentially
what we need further down the line?
Even once individuals do obtain some form of redress, the impact of adversely affected
perspectives remains. One childcare benefits affair victim explained in a parliamentary inquiry interview
how it altered her personality, implying how it will continue to affect her life, even post-limbo:
R: They took almost nine years away. That will never come back. You can’t make up for that
with any money. You can’t describe that with a pen. I can’t even describe my feelings. Some
people say: you just got tough.
The son of another childcare benefits affair victim explains how revoked benefits exceeding
100.000 have affected his perspective toward life from there. During those years, instead of enrolling in
his preferred study, he chose to take up as much part-time work as possible to provide the family with
some money:
R: Yes, I often thought: imagine if I hadn't gotten into this situation or if my brother hadn't
gotten into it, then we would have had a completely normal life. Maybe I would have become a
[ship] captain after all.

Simultaneously, many directly affected respondents indicate that hope and perspective are up-
and-down experiences during administrative limbo. Some choose to create their own perspective by
engaging in activism, which provides a sense that things can be changed for the better. Others gain hope
from the few successes they achieve in moving their case forward. However, such newfound hope can
easily regress into despair when the next unexpected delay sets in. One homeowner in the Groninger
gas parliamentary inquiry recounts how, after spending years waiting, builders had just started repairing
damage to her home. It was at the start of these works that a supervisor sent the builders home as they
did not comply with safety requirements, leading to months of delays as the builders’ schedule was full.
In addition to inducing feelings of powerlessness, this episode again alters her life perspective: hope is
replaced by the realization that she will be consigned to another period stuck in limbo. In her words:
R: “Then work finally starts and something is finally being repaired that you have been fighting
for for years, and then it crumbles in your hands again. That was very intense.”
Cumulative strain
Individuals experiencing administrative limbo face prolonged and increasing strain.
Respondents’ metaphors to describe this aspect of the experience include facing a “grinding” situation,
“being kicked when you are down,” continuously “being taxed to the limit” of your abilities, and
becoming severely “fatigued.” This is reflected in the fact that individuals are continuously confronted
with their stressors, with seemingly no escape. Several defective concrete blocks respondents emphasize
how difficult it is to return home to a house that you used to be proud of, only to be reminded of its
deteriorating state. Such patterns repeat across the childcare benefits affair and Groninger gas crisis.
Parliamentary inquiry interviews describe the stressful nature of continued battles against government
while procedures remain stuck, while parliamentary inquiry interviews for the childcare benefits affair
emphasize stress driven by continuing powerlessness. One Groninger gas homeowner invited to the
parliamentary inquiry describes the lopsided nature of such impacts, observing that officials can go
home in the evening without worrying, whereas victims are permanently confronted with their stressors.
Thus, during states of administrative limbo, time is not on the victim's side. Officials can delay without
issue, whereas victims feel a strong sense of urgency to resolve their problems. In her words:
R: “They can simply go on until they weigh an ounce, so to speak. They go back to their safe
house in the evening, sleep in a nice bed. And if someone in front of them completely breaks
down? Yeah…”
In addition to stress, the cumulative strain produced by limbo manifests in several related ways,
including severe exhaustion. Such exhaustion can become intertwined with loss of perspective, as
individuals no longer have the energy to engage in activities that would help to resolve their situations,

or even in unrelated activities that might somehow benefit them. In the words of one civil servant
supporting childcare benefits affair victims:
R: “You see a lot of clients or victims who, yes, that apathy, who just think, I can’t expect things
from the government. I really want as little as possible, I would still apply for a passport … I
really can't get around that. But for everything else, you know, the government is, of course,
coming up with a lot of new schemes, we're going to have energy surcharges - well, just think
of something. But those people are not going to take advantage of those [schemes], because they
are already completely exhausted.”
Similarly, a homeowner from Groningen is forced to cope with the accumulated strain of her
multiannual reinforcement process by avoiding optional activities in her personal life:
R: “We do talk to people in the neighborhood, but the energy, the time and the desire, so to
speak, to do really nice extra things, is not really there. At least, it was not really there anymore
with us. That purely has to do with "you can only handle so much", and we could not handle so
much anymore.”
There are indications that individuals may even choose to abandon attaining the outcome they
are in limbo on, preferring to reduce strain in the short-term by remaining in their unresolvable situation.
Governments risk losing sight of these severe cases of individuals stuck in limbo, implying that those in
need of support face the greatest risk of becoming a blind spot. As indicated by a Groninger expert
interview respondent:
R: You get people who eventually say: I'm going to take off that backpack and sit down here…
a nurse or a general practitioner enters the home, and they say: but you can't live here like this.
“Yes, I live here and this is where I live.”… Where people have to leave their homes
immediately. Where they themselves had not seen, they are so exhausted that they no longer pay
attention to their own safety. That can’t be added on top anymore. And then the government
says: yes, but we don’t hear from those people.
5.3. Moderators: the salience of outcome and resources
Why is it important to distinguish between objective waiting and experiences of administrative
limbo? Our data suggests that the two are strongly related, but that waiting is not a sufficient condition
to experience administrative limbo. Two factors seem to influence the relationship: issue salience and
the resources of the individual experiencing burdens.
Although all three crises studied have strongly affected thousands of lives, there are still groups
of affected individuals who sustained damage that was notable but not life-altering. Despite experiencing


## Page 23

23
the same protracted wait over an unresolvable situation, there is a notable difference in experiences
between those individuals for whom the outcome is moderately important, and those individuals whose
lives and livelihoods are on the line. One defective concrete blocks respondent, for instance, noted that,
although he has notable wall cracking, other elements of the house are still holding up reasonably well.
He suspected the state of his house would not be critical for several years to come, implying he could
wait before entering the compensation scheme. His narrative mainly discusses others’ stress, uncertainty
and perspective, while explicitly noting his own situation is less severely affected. Correspondingly, his
tone is notably less intense than that of other, more severely affected respondents. Thus, although such
individuals could also face highly protracted and unresolvable outcomes, they did not experience
cumulative strain, uncertainty and loss of or altered perspective to the same degree as some of their peers
did. Similarly, a civil society respondent from Groninger gas indicated that although he has had some
earthquake-related damage and associated frustration in obtaining redress for them, his own experiences
did not seem extremely severe. Instead, he referred to other board members of his civil society
organizations who endured worse, and who seemed to display greater exhaustion. Although most of our
respondents thus discussed severe outcomes, contributing to severe experiences of limbo – there are
indications that manageable outcomes may help mediate these experiences.
Second, resources can moderate how individuals experience administrative limbo. In line with
stress theory and administrative burden theory (Christensen et al., 2020; Moynihan et al., 2015; Biggs
et al., 2017), we observe that various resource types may help buffer the impact of unresolvable
situations and waiting times. Hobfoll (1989)’s seminal work on resources distinguishes between four
categories: object (material objects), conditions (statuses such as marriage, work, social ties, etc.),
personal characteristics (e.g. stress resistance) and energies (e.g. time, money and knowledge). Our data
suggests that at least conditions, personal characteristics and energies play a role in reducing the impact
of objective waiting on experiences of limbo.
In the conditions class, social support seems to be particularly important. Some individuals in
the defective concrete blocks crisis indicate how their spouse’s experience as accountants helped them
engage with the scheme. Similarly, Irish homeowners with family in construction could leverage their
relatives' expertise, facing reduced financial shortfalls as less work would need to be contracted out. A
childcare benefits affair expert notes an example of financial support being provided by a victims’
parents, making harms less unsurmountable. Another homeowner from Groningen indicates how they
could support the applications of their grandfather, who did not have the required administrative capital
himself:
R: “Procedures, all the legal red tape, conversations with authorities: that kind of thing.
Because he, at least, did not want to and could not do it alone. The man was approaching 80 at
the time.”


## Page 24

24
Second, personal characteristics play a role. Those with assertive character traits emphasized
how they could navigate the system due to their tenacity, while individuals with a different
predisposition may not be heard. An expert supporting individuals in the Groninger gas crisis notes how
some individuals “have a pessimistic or negative image that it will amount to nothing,” while an action
group member from the defective concrete blocks crisis notes that her proactive character sometimes
even makes her battle against the state fulfilling.
Third, some individuals’ own energies may be sizeable enough to avoid experiences of limbo
altogether, as was suggested by several experts in the childcare benefits affair – an affair that
disproportionately affected lower-income families, but did affect wealthier families as well. While
severely affected lower-income families would be thrust into crippling debt, wealthier families were
able to buffer some impacts. Irish respondents note how the elderly may disengage from the defective
concrete blocks grant scheme altogether, citing lower energy, insufficient digital skills to navigate the
online-only application forms, and an unwillingness to be stuck in protracted stress at the end of their
lives. Various types of expertise, ranging from construction to legal and project management skills, were
also emphasized as important. In the words of one professional supporting defective concrete blocks
applications:
R: “So it's a lot of people can't access the scheme because, because you have to have some
ability, you know, you have to engage engineers or architects, you have to engage builders, you
have to organize a project. So, and so there is, there is a lot in that in terms of people's ability.”
In sum, there are important nuances in individual situations that may increase or decrease the
likelihood that someone’s protracted entrapment turns into a lived experience of limbo.
5.4. Pure limbo and burdensome limbo
Although the data suggests that waiting over irresolvable outcomes produces a common core of
strain, lack of control and altered life perspectives, there is a notable divergence between the two Dutch
cases and the Irish case. That is, both Dutch cases seem to be explained well by a combination of
administrative limbo and administrative burdens, whereas the initial stages of the defective concrete
blocks crisis lack extensive burdens. In the defective concrete blocks case, we, therefore, see how the
impact of limbo can develop even in – or more accurately due to – the absence of substantial interactions
with the state. When states leave citizens in the cold, the result seems to be a form of ‘pure limbo’ that
revolves almost entirely around waiting in ambiguity. One respondent who was “diagnosed” with
defective concrete blocks in 2014 emphasizes the ambiguity and individual-level strain imposed by such
a state of administrative limbo, as well as the severity of limbo’s impact at the community level.


## Page 25

25
Although there were some (unsuccessful) attempts to engage with private parties through insurers and
through a legal case, ultimately she had nowhere to turn:
“… That was actually a horrendous time as we didn’t have a scheme, we didn't know where to
go to, whereas now at least there is support in place. We spent years trying to fix our home with
nobody listening, people had no idea what it was like having your family home crumble away.
We went through [insurance company] and a legal case, but both were a futile journey that
achieved nothing but a bad debt and unimaginable stress. Mentally, this crisis presented huge
health and safety concerns which no money or scheme will ever retrieve. It was a very long,
tough, exhausting, stressful journey, with no process, it was a very difficult place to be.”
And, later in her account:
“… unfortunately many people are genuinely unable to deal with this pressure, unable to see
their family home deteriorate and crumble away, with many experiencing thoughts of suicide
since being affected by defective concrete and sadly there was a suicide locally. Overall there
were different traumas that people endured because they were not able to deal with this crisis
as they had nowhere to turn, it has affected marriages, families, people's physical and mental
health.”
In the Dutch cases, as well as the Irish defective concrete blocks case post-2021, waiting and
experiences of onerous state action instead seem to be jointly relevant. Periods of extensive compliance
and learning costs, e.g. when submitting applications for redress or compensation or when filing appeals,
are interspersed with prolonged periods of waiting. Together, waiting in limbo and administrative
burdens seem to drive psychological outcomes such as uncertainty and strain, producing a never-ending
stage of ‘burdensome limbo.’ In such situations, experienced burdens become intertwined with the
temporally never-ending experience of limbo that individuals remain stuck in. This is clearly
summarized by a Groninger homeowner when mentioning an example of how another earthquake
damaged her house even as builders were repairing old earthquake damage, and how she had to convince
the competent damage redress organization to allow the builders to repair her new damage as well (as
the protocol required that this damage would be examined separately):
R:  I emailed those photos to the Centre Safe Living, after which the contact person answered
the phone and said that this was not allowed, because an inspector would have to come first.
But they [the builders] were already working. Then we said: no way, they are just going to
repair it now. And that is what happened. But you are standing there in the hallway and you
really think: guys, there is no point in what we are doing; you turn around and it is broken again
… It keeps going on and on.


## Page 26

26
Whereas administrative burdens captures bursts of compliance costs on individuals’ timelines
well, administrative limbo is better suited to capturing the separate impact that the never-ending nature
of individuals’ situations have. As such, future studies may benefit from a combined approach.
6. Discussion & conclusion
Administrative limbo can be highly impactful for individuals, as they become trapped in
administrative quagmires during which important issues remain unresolved for prolonged periods of
time (Haas, 2017; Binder et al., 2020; Hartonen et al., 2021). In addition to providing a definition and
conceptualization of administrative limbo applicable to public administration, this article contributes
three exploratory case studies. In all three cases, we find consistent reports of objective waiting and
experienced administrative limbo that helped abductively inform our definition. Our results show how
‘being stuck in the waiting room’ over an important and unresolvable outcome can generate experiences
defined by loss of or limited perspectives, low perceived levels of control and accumulations of strain.
Exit is normally not a viable option for individuals as outcomes are simply too important – consigning
them to the waiting room. In rare cases, strain becomes so severe that individuals choose an exit option
by giving up, which instead consigns them to their unresolved issues (such as a broken home or severe
debt). Our analysis also points to the value of distinguishing between objective waiting and subjective
experiences of administrative limbo (Tiggelaar & George, 2023), and how this link may be mediated by
the relative importance of the outcome to an individual and moderated by that person’s available
resources. The sheer psychosocial impact of limbo alone makes this a topic worthy of further inquiry. It
illustrates how severely issues with public services can upend individuals’ lives.
We see administrative limbo as a concept that can complement existing theoretical approaches,
including the administrative burden paradigm (Moynihan et al., 2015; Moynihan et al., 2022). Whereas
the relative strength of administrative burden-based research lies in analyzing learning and compliance
costs of the various interactions citizens have with the government (Moynihan et al., 2015), the strength
of administrative limbo lies in its emphasis on the experience of being trapped for prolonged periods of
time (Cabot, 2012; Hartonen et al., 2021). Many situations of ‘burdensome limbo’ may benefit from a
combined burden/limbo-based approach. This is apparent in the childcare benefits and Groninger gas
cases, where prolonged limbo is interspersed by ‘bursts’ of administrative burdens once a government
decision comes in. At that stage, individuals have to make compliance and learning costs to appeal to
said decision (Moynihan et al., 2015; Baekgaard & Tankink, 2022; Halling et al., 2024), before another
period of waiting initiates. Simultaneously, it is useful to point out that not all situations featuring limbo
will necessarily feature burdens. Individuals stuck in situations where no government programs exist at
all, or who have been geographically excluded from compensation schemes (which occurred for two of


## Page 27

27
our defective concrete blocks respondents), may feel stuck in ‘pure’ administrative limbo without
necessarily experiencing extensive learning and compliance costs.
Although our study is limited to three crises in The Netherlands and Ireland, the concept is
likely also applicable in other crises, including a variety of disasters and the (often slow) relief or redress
programs that follow (Gill & Picou, 1998). Binder et al. (2020) describe similar phenomena in the
context of post-Hurricane Sandy buyout programs (which excluded some victims, leading to similar
limbo-like situations). De Togni (2022) describes the stressful, protracted experiences of voluntary
evacuees displaced following the Fukushima nuclear disaster, who felt that their designation as
voluntary evacuees (and associated lower levels of support) was undue. Manmade disasters can also
include policy failures, similar to the childcare benefits affair. For instance, in Michigan (US), thousands
of unemployment welfare beneficiaries were wrongfully labeled as fraudsters by the MIDAS algorithm,
leading to years of limbo not unlike the Dutch experience described in this article (De La Garza, 2022).
Exploring why some crises generate administrative limbo crises may, therefore, be an important avenue
for future research.
The relevance of the administrative limbo concept is not solely tied to crises, however.
Administrative limbo could emerge in non-crisis, individual-level procedures. The obvious candidate is
migration, an area where studies on protracted entrapment already exist (e.g. Nimführ & Sesay, 2016;
Doná, 2015; Belloni, 2019; Cabot, 2012). It is unsurprising that authors in migration studies specifically
have an interest in the subject, as migration procedures are almost by definition protracted and life-
altering (Haas, 2016), and leaving migrants in limbo is sometimes a conscious deterrence strategy
(Moynihan et al., 2022). Areas such as social policy may also be susceptible when throughput times are
extensive and welfare recipients’ livelihoods are on the line. Moreover, although the childcare benefits
affair revolves around unduly revoked benefits and the associated fallout, there may be other (non-crisis)
situations where individuals have to appeal or litigate against government decisions they believe are
unduly imposed, and which may similarly take years. Finally, there are situations in which individuals
are unable to access any procedure, resulting in administrative limbo tied to state inaction. Ceballos et
al. (2021) mention the US example of minorities that were unable to apply for redress where policies
have disproportionately large negative impacts on them, primarily due to the inadvertent effect of a
complex line of Supreme Court case law. This raises the question of how individual-level experiences
evolve when individuals are indefinitely stuck.
We suggest a number of avenues for further research. A first question for future quantitative
approaches would be examining the prevalence of limbo-like experiences, both in crisis and non-crisis
situations. For instance, given a larger and more representative sample of victims in any of the three
crises studied here, how many individuals would not only be stuck in the objective ‘waiting room,’ but
would also be confronted with the associated experiences of loss of perspective, low control, and


## Page 28

28
cumulative strain? Second, public administration scholars could focus on antecedents of administrative
limbo in crisis situations. Our cases tentatively suggest a diverse array of causes, ranging from policy-
making by other means (the Dutch childcare benefits affair) (Moynihan et al., 2015) to severe failures
in the agenda-setting process (defective concrete blocks in Ireland) (Fenger & Simonse, 2024). Third,
we may expect limbo to contribute to a number of outcomes. As individuals feel abandoned by
governments, they may exhibit increasing levels of anger, feelings of injustice and distrust (Monrad,
2024). In line with administrative burden studies, this may mean that some citizens give up and choose
exit options instead (Brodkin & Majmundar, 2010; Heinrich, 2016). For individuals in limbo, this
implies giving up on an outcome as important as the safety of your home or severe debt induced by
government action. Investigating such outcomes is vital to ascertain the scope of administrative limbo’s
impacts and vital for practitioners to assess how to engage with individuals who have begun to actively
avoid government due to their limbo experiences (see e.g., Tiggelaar & George, 2023). Fourth, the
childcare benefits affair suggests that out-groups with limited political power (in this case, individuals
with a migration background) may be particularly susceptible to governance systems that generate
administrative limbo. Addressing how limbo relates to discrimination and intersectionality is, therefore,
an important topic for further inquiry. Finally, and as mentioned previously, although the analyses
presented here focused on three major crises, migration studies already show that ‘business as usual’
procedures may also generate experiences of limbo (Cabot, 2012; Hartonen et al., 2021). An open
question is, therefore, whether we may also see experiences of limbo emerging in other non-crisis
contexts, such as individual welfare or taxation procedures that have become bogged down.
The aim of this study was to introduce, define and empirically describe an administrative limbo
concept that is widely applicable in public administration. However, an exhaustive and conclusive test
of the causal pathways involved is beyond the scope of our (non-representative) study. The authors hope
this contribution provides a useful starting point for a discussion on limbo and temporality in citizen-
state interactions, and welcome future research that refines its conceptualization.  The findings presented
here suggest that administrative limbo has a wider application and hopes to point the way to the
development of remedies for citizens stuck in the waiting room.

Langslepende procedures
Een belangrijk element in ons project is het tijdsaspect van dossiers als de [dossier] en hoe burgers
daar mee omgaan. Ik weet dat [dossier] al sinds […] loopt, maar in uw ervaring, hoelang zijn burgers

Is dit over de tijd heen veranderd?

-
Zijn er naast de overheid nog actoren relevant (bijvoorbeeld verzekeraars, werkgevers,
rechters)?
-

bezwaar- en beroep werkt, wat de diversiteit aan betrokken actoren is, etc.

vinden?

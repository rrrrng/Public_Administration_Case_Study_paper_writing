ABSTRACT
Based on archival data, this study utilizes the theoretical framework of policy transfer to conduct a case analysis of the history of
PACH's transnational dissemination of its unique networking ideas, institutional models, and practices in the mid-­20th century.
The findings indicate that during the two stages before and after World War II, PACH's transnational transfer activities demon-
strated different features in terms of motives, actors, content, routes, degrees, constraints, and facilitating factors for transfer.
Our analysis shows the transnational transfer capabilities possessed by professional organizations as a special type of non-­state
actor, as well as the dual influence of professional mission and geopolitical factors on its transfer processes. This study provides
a complementary perspective and empirical evidence for explaining the network formation in the field of public administration.
The findings may help contemporary public administrative organizations consider how to adopt more effective strategies to par-
ticipate in global governance through transnational cooperation.
1   |   Introduction
Networks refer to “structures of interdependence involving
multiple organizations or parts thereof, where one unit is not
merely the formal subordinate of the others in some larger hi-
erarchical arrangement” (O'Toole Jr 1997, 45). Since the 1980s,
interorganizational cooperation networks have become a nota-
ble phenomenon in the field of public administration worldwide,
and have received increasing attention from scholars (Oliver and
Ebers 1998; Berry et al. 2004; Isett et al. 2011; Kapucu et al. 2014).
Given that organizations in a network are usually considered au-
tonomous actors with noncoercive participation, the question of
why different public administrative organizations would unite
to establish and maintain a certain horizontal cooperative inter-
organizational network relationship, especially in the absence
of legal authority ties, has naturally become an issue that needs
to be explained. Extant literature has emphasized the impor-
tance of factors such as trust, shared belief or common purpose,
mutual dependency based on resource exchanges, cost reduc-
tion, as well as leadership and managerial ability (Agranoff and
McGuire 2001, 311–314; Ebers 1997). However, most previous
research, while considering these factors within a single multi-
unit structure, discusses network formation in the context of a
particular nation or area, largely overlooking the role of trans-
national factors.
This study aims to provide a complementary perspective for
understanding the formation of interorganizational networks
in public administration by introducing the policy transfer
analysis, which can help public administration scholars and
practitioners better understand the network phenomena in the
real world. Networks develop and function not only within the
boundaries of individual countries but also transcend national
borders. They exist among professionals and experts who en-
gage in information exchange or the dissemination of techni-
cal and policy advancements to others, often located in diverse

This article adopts the case study method and examines the role

field of public administration during the mid-­20th century. As
the former focal point of the American public administration or-
ganizational network, as well as the parent institution that nur-
tured many professional associations with extensive influence
in American public administration (including ASPA), PACH
has been almost forgotten by contemporary scholars. Only a
few pieces mention its name, and they predominantly confine
their discussions to PACH's domestic contributions within the
United States (Karl 1963; Stillman II 1998; Lee 2022). Based on
the relevant archival data, this article, for the first time, studies
in depth and presents the rich history of PACH's overseas ac-
tivities. During the quarter-­century from the early 1930s to the
mid-­1950s, the leaders of PACH ambitiously transplanted its so-­
called “clearing house” idea and institutional model, as well as
the networking practices it took pride in, to other regions of the
world such as Western Europe, Latin America, and even Asia.
Using the analytical framework of policy transfer, this study ex-
amines: (1) What drove the leaders of PACH to transfer its net-
working idea and experiences to other regions of the world in
different periods? (2) Who was involved in these transfers and
what roles did they play? (3) What strategies did the leaders of
PACH adopt to facilitate these transfers? And what factors have
enabled or hindered their implementation? The findings indi-
cate that the formation of a particular type of public adminis-
tration interorganizational network within a specific country or
region can be achieved through the transnational policy trans-
fer operated by professional organizations. And certain levels
of factors may have an impact on the transfer processes. These
findings can not only deepen our understanding of the role of
non-­state actors in policy transfer and the dynamic interaction
mechanism of multilevel factors in the policy transfer process,
but also help contemporary professional organizations consider
how to promote cross-­border and cross-­jurisdiction cooperative
governance more effectively.
2   |   Theoretical Background: Non-­State Actors and
Transnational Policy Transfer
The theoretical framework of this article is drawn from the liter-
ature on policy transfer, which stems from comparative politics
and public policy analysis in the 1990s and has been commonly
used by scholars to analyze broad phenomena such as global-
ization, Europeanization, and policy innovation (Benson and
Jordan  2011). Policy transfer refers to the “process in which
knowledge about policies, administrative arrangements, insti-
tutions etc. in one time and/or place is used in the development
of policies, administrative arrangements and institutions in an-
other time and/or place” (Dolowitz and Marsh 1996). In their
seminal works, Dolowitz and Marsh develop a framework to an-
alyze the process of policy transfer, which is organized around
several questions such as who are the key actors involved in the
policy transfer process, why do they engage policy transfer, what
is transferred, from where are lessons drawn, what is the degree
of transfer, what factors restrict or facilitate the transfer process,
and how transfer leads to policy success/failure (Dolowitz and
Marsh 1996, 2000). This model was further refined thereafter
and widely applied in multiple empirical researches (Benson
and Jordan 2011).
A noticeable trend that can be observed in policy transfer stud-
ies is that scholars are increasingly aware of the limitations of
the conventional state-­centered perspective and recognize that a
plethora of actors and multiple-­authority structures may emerge
through the cross-­border policy diffusion processes (Stone 2008;
de Oliveira 2021, 8). Recent research in the field has dedicated
more attention to transnational spaces and transfer agents (de
Oliveira and Pal 2018, 206; Pal and Spence 2020). For instance,
as a pioneer of this trend, Diane Stone reflects on the “meth-
odological nationalism” in early policy transfer literature and
calls for rescuing public policy from the “prison of the state”
(Stone  2008). She identifies transnational networks as an im-
portant vehicle for the spread of policies and “best practices,”
in which she stresses the role of international organizations and
non-­state actors (Stone 2004).
Along with the tendency to “methodological transnational-
ism,” the focus of researchers has also gradually shifted from
“hard transfers” (programs and implementation) to “soft
transfers” (ideas, concepts, attitudes) (Evans and Davies 1999,
382). Some scholars argue that the contents of the policy trans-
fer may well be more extensive than merely public policies.
Moreover, non-­state actors such as international foundations,
transnational think tanks, professional societies, and multi-
national consultants are considered to be better at the “soft
transfer” of broad policy ideas that influence public opinion

Recognition of non-­state actors and transnational networks
roles has inspired researchers to understand the process of pol-
icy transfer from a more holistic perspective. Questioning the
utility of intraorganizational policy transfer analysis, Evans
and Davies pose a multilevel model which integrates three di-
mensions—global, transnational and international levels; the
macrostate level and the interorganizational level (Evans and
Davies 1999). Likewise, Diane Stone argues that “policy devel-
opment is not the exclusive prerogative of governments or in-
ternational organizations (or NGOs acting on their behalf) but
incorporates independent societal and corporate interests into a
policy network” (Stone 2004, 558–559). Accordingly, a more dy-
namic and more comprehensive understanding of policy trans-
fer processes should recognize the joint interactions between
different types of international actors rather than be limited to
observing simple bilateral relationships.
Furthermore, recent literature in this field has also applied
multilevel and multidimensional analytical models to analyze
significant themes such as the “transferring power” and “pol-
icy transfer capabilities” (Evans 2010, 17). Some scholars high-
light the centrality of agency of individuals, organizations and
networks to understand the speed and direction of the circu-
lation of policy ideas. In their view, different types of knowl-
edge diffusion agents each have their own advantages, but also
face various constraints from the external environment (Stone
et al. 2019). Another latest research has drawn on the analysis
of policy capabilities in the literature on state studies and put
forward the concept of “policy transfer capabilities,” which is
defined as “the set of skills and resources-­or competences and
capabilities-­necessary to perform a movement (in time and
space) of policy instruments.” It also outlines the main frame-
work for analyzing and measuring policy transfer capabilities
from three levels—individual, organizational, and systemic,
and three dimensions—analytical, operational, and political (de
Oliveira and Koga 2023).
Despite the above-­mentioned progress, the scholarship on the
role of non-­state actors in transnational policy transfer is still
inadequate in some aspects. First, the number of relevant em-
pirical studies remains relatively small. A recent literature re-
view shows that, although at the conceptual level the field of
policy transfer research has deviated from its initial focus on
transfer between nation states, compared with the research on
state actors (including national governments or international
governmental organizations), only a small proportion of empir-
ical studies involves one or multiple non-­state actors (Minkman
et al. 2018, 228). Second, most of the existing empirical research
only focuses on non-­state actors themselves. There is not much
discussion on the impact of system-­level or environmental fac-
tors on the routes of policy transfer, as well as the complex in-
teractions between non-­state actors, states, and international
organizations during the transfer process. This study responds
to the scholars' call for recognizing that policy transfer is the
result of the joint participation of multilevel factors and advo-
cates that the motives and mechanisms of transnational transfer
should be examined dynamically and comprehensively. At the
same time, based on the analysis of the transnational history of
PACH, this article proposes that networks are not only a crucial
channel for policy transfer but also a possible result of policy
transfer. Through the construction of cross-­border professional
interorganizational networks, the experience of forming net-
works among public administrative organizations generated
within one nation can be transplanted to other countries or re-
gions. Factors at the individual, organizational, and systemic
levels will all have either a facilitating or hindering impact in
this process.
3   |   Data and Method
3.1   |   Data Sources and Characteristics
The empirical materials used mainly come from the archi-
val materials collected in the Hanna Holborn Gray Special
Collections Research Center, Regenstein Library, University of
Chicago.1 These include the personal papers of several found-
ers of PACH, such as Charles E. Merriam, Louis Brownlow, and
Beardsley Ruml, especially their correspondence, diaries, and
memoirs, as well as the annual reports and meeting minutes
of PACH (roughly from the early 1930s to mid-­1950s). These
archives systematically record the organizational activities of
PACH at home and abroad, especially its transnational policy
transfer strategies.
It should be acknowledged that the data used in this study have
a major limitation. The archives mainly reflect the perspective
of the PACH leadership. The lack of records from the recipients
(such as institutions in Japan and Latin America) may affect a
comprehensive assessment of the effect of some transfers.
3.2   |   Research Methods
This study employs the case study method. PACH was selected
as the case not only due to its pioneering role as a non-­state actor
which successfully promoted the transnational transfer of its
networking ideas and institutional model in the field of public
administration, but also because its complete archives provide a
unique opportunity for analyzing multilevel interactions during
the policy transfer process.
Although in recent years, it has been strongly advocated by some
leading scholars (Raadschelders  1994, 2000; Raadschelders
et  al.  2000; Durant  2014; Lee  2021), research that examines
public administration phenomena in historical context is still
in a relatively marginal position within the field of American
public administration (Raadschelders  2010; Raadschelders
and Lee  2011). However, for understanding the transnational
transfer of networking experience, the historical perspective
is evidently beneficial. In contrast to quantitative research
that relies on extensive large-­scale data, qualitative analysis of
cases, grounded in archival materials and integrated with the

The author scanned, read the relevant archives, and sorted out

transfer” (the “clearing house” idea) and “hard transfer” (such as
the institutional model of PACH); (4) environmental constraints
and their impact on the differences in PACH's transfer strategies
in different regions.
4   |   The Case of PACH
The networking experience that PACH transferred overseas
originated from the interorganizational experiment it conducted
approximately during the same period within the United States,
which aimed to gather several professional organizations inter-
ested in improving public administration practice in a common
location and facilitated their information interchange and co-
operation. This unique idea and institutional structure of public
administrative organization was referred to “(central) clearing
house” by PACH's founders. Before reviewing the overseas ac-
tivities of PACH, it is necessary to first trace the origins of the
clearing house idea, which later constituted the main content of
PACH's transnational transfer.
The emergence of the clearing house idea was the result of the
convergence of certain novel trends within the American public
administration profession and American society after the Great
war. These trends include the dissatisfaction among leaders in
the political science discipline and the public administration
profession with respect to the fragmentation of the American
public administration system, as well as the interest of the
Rockefeller Foundation in scientific philanthropy within the
public administration realm. Furthermore, the investigations of
the PACH founders into the situation of contemporary European
public administrative organizations also inspired the design of
the new agency.
4.1   |   The Emergence of the Clearing House Idea
in American Public Administration and the Birth
of PACH
The clearing house idea in Western public administration is,
to a large extent, an American-­born phenomenon. Originally a
term circulated within the banking system in the second half of
the 19th century, the concept of “clearing house” was first intro-
duced into the field of American public administration between
the two World Wars. It reflected the gradually forming con-
sensus among some leading scholars and practitioners on the
structural weaknesses and future directions of American public
administration at that time. Walter Lippmann, the renowned
public intellectual and editor of The New Republic, pointed this
out in his Public Opinion:
Each department of government is all the time
asking for information that may already have been
obtained by another department, though perhaps in
a somewhat different form … This establishment [of
the central clearing house] would pretty soon become
a focus of information of the most extraordinary kind.
And the men in it would be made aware of what the
problems of government really are … It is there that
the political scientist would find the real nuts to crack
and the real researches for his students to make … The
central agency would, thus, have in it the makings
of a national university … In its main outlines the
principle is equally applicable to state governments,
to cities, and to rural counties.
(Lippmann 1922, 391–392)
Lippmann's vision about a central clearing house in the realm
of government was echoed by political scientist Charles E.
Merriam at the University of Chicago, who also complained in
1922 that, despite the emergence of various types of institutions
dedicated to specialized municipal research in the United States
since the late 19th century, there was “no adequate central clear-
inghouse for interchange of information, and for mature analy-
sis and interpretation of all the various types of data collected”
(Merriam 1922, 279).
Merriam had a strong interest in the development of public ad-
ministration since the beginning of his career. During his term
as alderman of Chicago City Council in the early 1910s, he urged
the mayor to appoint a commission to investigate the city's fi-
nances, and a “bureau of efficiency” was also created under his
pressure (Karl 1974, 62). After World War I, Merriam returned
to academia and headed the Department of Political Science at
the University of Chicago, and was elected as the president of the
American Political Science Association in 1925. Under his lead-
ership, research in public administration rapidly flourished over
the next two decades, and the University of Chicago also became
a major center for political science research in the United States
(Heaney 2006).
In his early manifesto of a new political science, Merriam re-
affirmed Lippmann's view on the rise of “organized intelli-
gence” in modern society, noting “the general tendency toward
the closer integration and association of political prudence”
(Merriam 1931, 173). During the interwar years, he launched a
series of what historian Barry Karl called “super associations,”
such as the Social Science Research Council, the PACH, and the
National Resources Planning Board in the federal government
(Karl 1974, 262). These institutions all played the role of cen-
tral coordinating bodies, aiming to strengthen communication
and cooperation among different disciplines, administrative ju-
risdictions, or government departments. These institutional in-
novations were devised to address the structural deficiencies in
American political and knowledge production systems.

At the annual meeting of the City Managers' Association in
1925, Louis Brownlow, then chairman of the City Managers'
Association, and John G. Stutz, secretary of the Kansas
Municipal League, cordially invited Merriam to convene a
committee to promote the establishment of a national central
bureau of municipal research adjacent to a university. Merriam
responded positively to this proposal, describing it as “one of the
most promising developments in the whole field of government”
(Merriam 1925).
In 1929, Merriam and Brownlow's initiative received crucial
support from the Rockefeller Foundation, which had just de-
clared a significant reorganization the previous year. Worrying
about the sensitivity of the field of public administration, the
Rockefeller Foundation cautiously established the Spelman
Fund committing three million dollars to support programs re-
lated to public administration. This became the largest grant by
the Rockefeller Foundation in the general field of public admin-
istration (Fosdick 1989, 205). Under the guidance of Executive
Beardsley Ruml, Secretary Guy Moffett, and Merriam, who
joined the Board of Trustees of the Spelman Fund in 1935 and
later served as its chairman in May 1937 until it was dissolved
in 1949, “assist[ing] in the development of a comprehensive plan
for the clearance of technical information” became one of the
main interests of the newly created foundation. Several steps
were then approved by the Board of Trustees with respect to
organizational activities in the field of public administration,
which included “strengthening the secretariats of the major
public-­official organizations, assembling them in the same
building, and creating a small but extremely competent central
organization to clear between these secretariats and various or-
ganizations” (Merriam 1941).
In designing the new central coordinating agency, the found-
ers of PACH consciously drew lessons from their counterparts
across the Atlantic Ocean. Sponsored by the Spelman Fund,
Merriam, Brownlow and Ruml visited Belgium, the United
Kingdom, France, Switzerland and Germany in the spring of
1930 to obtain a broad view of the most important trends in pub-
lic administration in Western Europe. But as Merriam described
in a memorandum written after this trip, their ultimate goal was
to create an unprecedented type of agency:
Our problem is that of studying the ways and
means by which public officials interchange their
experience in governmental affairs, and further
the contacts of organized public officials with the
organizations of technicians or scientists, industrial
organizations and labour organizations on the
research side … The eventual goal is that bringing
together more intimately the best tendencies in
Government, Science, Industry and Labour, in
such a manner as to construct a higher and more
effective form of public control.
(Merriam 1930a)
Merriam was, particularly, impressed by the German situa-
tion, which he thought illustrated “most vividly the possi-
bilities of interchange of experience and information among
administrators,” and was “worthy of the most careful, contin-
uous study” (Merriam 1930b, p. 21). He especially mentioned
the Deutscher Städtetag, which represented the German asso-
ciation of jurisdictional units, a type of organization that did
not exist in the United States at that time. And Merriam also
noticed that, compared to the United States, most public ad-
ministration research in Germany was conducted by senior
civil servants rather than professional scholars. Additionally,
although in Germany, where the organization of the various
groups had proceeded to a high level, he was still surprised
to find no single source that seemed well informed on the
broad development of public administration. There was much
inter-­associational collaboration on specific problems, but no
continuing mechanism of cooperation. Merriam attributed
this phenomenon to the strength of individual associations in
Germany. Conversely, he thought that in the United States,
where associations were for the most part weak, the presence
of a central clearing organization would be welcomed as an
additional source of strength for the individual association
(Merriam 1930a).
Merriam's observations ultimately shaped the PACH, which
was incorporated as a nonprofit corporation in December
1930, with Brownlow elected as its first director, and former
Illinois Governor Frank O. Lowden was invited to serve as the
chairman of the Board of Trustees. By January 1945, PACH
had coordinated 16 national or international public admin-
istration organizations and provided services to them. This
figure further rose to 21 before PACH was dissolved in 1956.
PACH and its affiliates initially operated out of a building
at 850 East 58th Street. Then, in April 1938, they relocated
to a four-­story building at 1313 East 60th Street, built and
owned by the University of Chicago, with a nominal payment
of one dollar per year. Subsequently, they became known in
the American public administration community as “1313
Group,” “the Chicago Group,” or “the Chicago Center.” In an
unpublished official history completed in 1945, political scien-
tist C. Herman Pritchett described “1313” as “an experiment
in propinquity,” which he believed had demonstrated the pos-
sibilities of a chain reaction in advancing the art of public ad-
ministration, just like the terrifying power demonstrated by
the atomic bomb (Pritchett 1945, ii) (Table 1).
During the process of establishing PACH, the Spelman Fund
reiterated that its funding should be strictly limited to techni-
cal and nonpolitical objectives, “consistent with a cautious ap-
proach that would not involve embarrassment to the Spelman
Fund” (Roberts 1994; Merriam 1929). This statement, to a large
extent, shaped PACH's impartial and anonymous guiding poli-
cies that it later adhered to both within and outside of the United
States.
4.2   |   The Atlantic Crossings of PACH's Networking
Experiment in the 1930s
In a nutshell, the purposes and functions of PACH encompassed
three aspects: (1) to provide support for public administrative
organizations that lack funding, essential personnel, and facil-
ities; (2) to facilitate the interchange of information, ideas, and
experience among operating public officials, research units and

technical experts in public administration; (3) to encourage
a closer and more effective cooperation among these groups
(Public Administration Clearing House 1938, 2; Brownlow 1958,
251). From the outset, the scope of PACH's activities and inter-
ests was not confined to the boundaries of the United States.
Both Brownlow and Merriam adhered to the theory that “ad-
ministrative methods and practices were transferable despite
radical differences in political controls and that what had
proved successful and workable as an administrative device in
one country could be imported and adapted for use in another”
(Brownlow 1958, 302).
Taking World War II (WWII) as the watershed, PACH's over-
seas activities roughly fell into two stages. In the first phase,
through various means including holding international con-
ferences, fostering transnational professional organization
networks, as well as promoting the establishment of regional
coordinating mechanisms modeled after PACH, and along
with the financial support of the Spelman Fund, PACH man-
aged to export its clearing house idea and networking expe-
rience to Western Europe and Latin America. By 1940, this
unprecedented networking experiment in the history of public
administration had been hailed as “the finest example of ad-
ministrative service on a democratic basis to be found any-
where in the world” (Merriam 1940).
PACH's transfer of its clearing house idea overseas started
from its connection with two important international organiza-
tions of government officials in Europe, namely International
Union of Local Authorities (IULA) and International Institute
of Administrative Sciences (IIAS). IULA was jointly estab-
lished by national associations of local governments from sev-
eral European countries in Ghent in 1913 (Vinck 1936). Since
1922, this organization had established contact with American
Municipal Association (AMA) through John G. Stutz. After
AMA joined the 1313 Group, PACH decided to advance its co-
operation with IULA. At the request of Brownlow, the State
Department and the Department of Commerce agreed to send
a delegation to the fifth IULA Congress in 1932, and autho-
rized PACH to select its representatives (Pritchett  1945, 205).
IIAS, on the other hand, was established in 1931 and comprised
members from the administrative staffs of central governments
(Lesoir 1936). In the summer of 1934, PACH organized a general
conference in Paris, inviting 30 representatives from national
and local governments as well as universities and academic in-
stitutions of 16 countries. Brownlow hoped to demonstrate “the
utility of the exchange of information in the Western world of
experiments, experiences, and advances” through this meeting
(Brownlow 1958, 304).
To achieve this goal, the Paris conference unanimously estab-
lished a Brownlow-­led joint committee to create a Brussels-­
based center where IULA and IIAS could collaborate following
the pattern which had been instituted by PACH and its 1313
Group partners. During the conference, Donald C. Stone, one
of the directors of  Public Administration Service, a newly
founded agency engaged in consulting, research and pub-
lishing with crucial help from PAC, also shared his working
experience on behalf of the American delegation. The name
“International Clearing House” was promptly adopted for this
Western European version of PACH. And with a grant-­in-­aid
from the Spelman Fund, Brownlow selected Rowland Egger
of the University of Virginia to plan for further cooperation
between these two organizations, which involved establishing
a common library, maintaining a cooperative secretariat, and
holding joint conferences on topics of mutual interest to both
(Pritchett  1945, 207; Brownlow  1958, 307–308). Egger also
notably compiled the first directory of international organi-
zations in the field of public administration (Joint Committee
on Planning and Cooperation  1936). By the late 1930s, the
Brussels Center expanded membership to include major en-
tities like the International Housing and Town Planning
Federation (Wagner  2016, 307). The International Clearing

The second wave of PACH's transnational diffusion, encom-
passing its core idea, organizational models, and networking
practices, emerged in Latin America. This geographic shift
in policy transfer was principally driven by escalating na-
tionalist movements during the late 1930s. As some scholars
have indicated, PACH's expansion of transatlantic coopera-
tion rested on its leaders' belief in the politics-­administration
dichotomy. However, this belief was shaken after the 1936
conferences in Warsaw and Berlin. The German delegates
invited by PACH relentlessly attacked democratic regimes
and defended Nazism during these conferences, which led
to dissatisfaction among representatives of other countries
(Oren 2003, 75–76; Roberts 2018). As a result of these ideolog-
ical controversies, PACH's efforts in promoting international
cooperation in Europe were gradually brought to a halt until
the end of WWII. But PACH soon redirected its focus into the
inter-­American field.
PACH's Latin American engagement commenced with the 1938
First Pan-­American Municipal Congress, initially convened by
the president of Havana City Council Ruy de Lugo Viña (an
IULA participant) before his 1937 plane crash. PACH's critical in-
tervention salvaged the jeopardized conference (Brownlow 1958,
408–411). The most important result from this meeting was the
contacts established between persons and groups with common
professional and intellectual interests, who had never come to-
gether previously due to language and cultural barriers. Mirroring
European strategies, Brownlow secured Spelman Fund support
to establish the Havana-­based Pan-­American Commission for
Inter-­Municipal Cooperation. This commission functioned as a
transnational platform of exchange and cooperation among ad-
ministrative officials of the Americas and was also responsible
for liaison between American administrators and their Brussels
counterparts (Lepawsky 1941; Pritchett 1945, 211). Nevertheless,
the outbreak of war in late 1941 eventually suspended some
planned initiatives of the Commission, which included a com-
prehensive survey of municipal institutions in the Americas.
4.3   |   Spreading the US Democratic Public
Administration Model Through Reconstruction
Guidance and Development Assistance
During WWII, PACH and other 1313 Group members pivoted
to wartime mobilization, exemplified by Herbert Emmerich—
PACH's deputy director (and Brownlow's postwar successor)—
serving as the executive secretary of the War Production Board,
the principal US economic mobilization agency (Brownlow 1958,
458). This personnel change embodied the strategic wartime co-
operation between PACH and federal authorities, while presag-
ing their closer relationship in the postwar decade.
Amidst postwar US global hegemony and Cold War competition
with the Soviet Union, the third wave of PACH-­facilitated trans-
national transfers of networking experience unfolded through
multidimensional
transformations:
geographic
expansion
beyond the Western Hemisphere, redefined transfer motives,
diversified transfer agents, and restructured implementation
mechanisms. To propagate its clearing house model and net-
working practices, PACH strategically partnered with the US
State Department and nascent international institutions like
the United Nations, supplementing its traditional reliance on
professional networks. These shifts crystallized in PACH's 1950
organizational restructuring, coinciding with critical funding
realignments. In 1949, the Spelman Fund was dissolved and
replaced by a five-­million-­dollar Ford Foundation grant, which
explicitly targeted “expand[ing] its activities in the field of na-
tional and international administration so that it may enlarge
and accelerate its efforts to help improve (a) the ability of the
U.S. to administer and coordinate its international and domestic
policies and (b) the administrative effectiveness of international
organization including the United Nation and various special-
ized agencies” (Gaither 1950). Following this guideline, PACH
expanded its Washington office and established a new office in
New York to facilitate liaison with international organizations,
with political scientists Rowland Egger and Charles S. Ascher in
charge (Public Administration Clearing House 1951, 13).
In the postwar democratization of defeated nations' administra-
tive systems, PACH played a prominent role through transna-
tional knowledge transfers. Under a contract with the Panel on
Governmental Affairs of the Commission on Occupied Areas,
PACH served as the primary sponsor for 30 West German
city and county officials who were selected by the US High
Commissioner for Germany to make a three-­month trip to the
United States in 1950 for the purposes of observing democratic
governmental institutions at work. During the same year, PACH
also received nine delegations of Japanese officials sponsored by
the Institute of International Education. These delegations to-
taled 55 Japanese officials, and some of them were top-­ranking
national officials in the fields of personnel, finance, and election
administration. It was claimed that the programs were
Designed to help the Japanese to find solutions for
specific problems they are encountering in their
efforts to decentralize administration and build up
the autonomy of local governments, emphasized
American experience with the federal system and the
part that our voluntary associations of government
officials play in improving administrative practice
and strengthening local institutions.
(Public Administration Clearing House 1950, 9)
As the result of the subsequent institutional reforms, the au-
thority of Japanese local governments was fortified (Agata
et  al. 2024, 41). In 1951, with funding from the Rockefeller
Foundation, PACH hired George A. Warp, a political scientist
from the University of Minnesota, to travel to Japan to promote
the establishment of a public administration coordinating orga-
nization modeled after PACH (Public Administration Clearing
House 1951, 26).

an important platform for PACH to demonstrate its clearing house

try, agriculture, health, and education to other parts of the world.

abroad in most cases with no orientation concerning the attitudes,
customs, and institutions of the countries they were going to visit.
PACH keenly perceived this urgent need and retained Professor
Wallace S. Sayre from the City College of New York to design a re-

sistance of the Committee on Area Research of the Social Science
Research Council (Public Adiministration Clearing House 1951,

close contact with UNESCO to establish an international training
school in Geneva for missions sponsored by the UN and its special-
ized agencies (Public Administration Clearing House 1951, 17).
During the 1950s, the Americas remained PACH's primary
transfer site, where it leveraged its strategic partnership with
the US-­backed Organization of American States (OAS), a re-
gional intergovernmental organization established in 1948 as an
anti-­communist hemispheric alliance, to propagate its clearing
house model. In 1953, PACH secured an OAS mandate through
the Inter-­American Economic and Social Council to assess Latin
America's public administration needs. Deploying special envoy
John B. Blandford on a year-­long fact-­finding mission, PACH
conducted extensive interviews with government officials

progress while also highlighting that “the public administration
resources are unevenly distributed and the individuals and cen-

report was a proposal for “an Inter-­American Institute or Center
for Public Administration”, which would almost replicate what

PACH's transfer efforts in the 1950s highly aligned with US Cold
War strategy to combat the Soviet influence in the Third World,
as evidenced by its May 1954 New York conference. Convened in
response to Emil J. Sady's memorandum warning of Soviet in-
roads in Middle Africa, this meeting assembled major US tech-
nical assistance providers, including State Department officials,
foundation representatives, and colonial administration special-
ists, to make administrative preparations for self-­government or
independence (Public Administration Clearing House 1954, 20).
At the conference, most participants expected that the US could
make a greater contribution toward the solution of administra-
tive problems than other countries, and the development of com-
munications in administration was among the fields in which
American experience might be most useful in dependent territo-
ries. Furthermore, some participants suggested that, in order to
dispel the political sensitivities of dependent territories regarding
Western aid, it would be better to implement technical assistance
through private sectors than through the US government (Public
Administration Clearing House 1954, 4–5). Although Charles S.
Ascher, the Associate Director of PACH, stated that PACH had no
mandate to pursue further any special initiative, he still promised
that his institution “could serve as a channel for communication”
(Public Administration Clearing House 1954, 10).

subsided due to the emergence of ideological disputes within
the international public administration community and the ad-

leader of the “free world” and competed with the Soviet Union
globally, PACH's administrative internationalism was reacti-
vated, while being significantly more driven by ideological and
geopolitical struggles. In participating in the democratization of
occupied countries and the UN technical assistance programs,
PACH emphasized the importance of democratic values and
the democratized public administration model, reflecting its
important role in the United States efforts to build the postwar
world order and implement the Cold War strategy. This kind of
transnational transfer activity backed by power sometimes car-
ried a certain sense of coercion.
Next, as the driving forces shift, the actors involved in the trans-
fer process and the transfer strategies may also change. In the
1930s, PACH mainly collaborated with regional public admin-
istration professional organizations. With the financial support
from the Spelman Fund, PACH took several actions. It held
international conferences, built regional centers, maintained
a cooperative secretariat, and compiled a directory of public
administrative organizations. Through these efforts, PACH
successfully exported its clearing house idea and networking
experience to Western Europe and Latin America. Conversely,
with the coming of the Cold War, when choosing the targets and
content of transnational transfer, PACH turned to align with the
geopolitical needs of the United States. Moreover, it increasingly
relied on cooperation with governments within and outside of
the United States, as well as international organizations like the
UN and the OAS.
Then, certain conditions and multiple levels of factors can
have an impact on the success or failure of policy transfer
by professional organizations. Among the facilitating factors
were the personal reputation of PACH leaders in the transat-
lantic public administration communities, and their ability to
obtain support from the government and the private sector,
all of which contributed to the success of PACH's transfer ac-
tivities. The consensus within the professional community on
cross-­border information exchange, resource complementar-
ity, and cooperation also laid a crucial foundation for PACH's
smooth transplantation of its networking experience overseas
in the 1930s. Meanwhile, the sufficient financial support from
the Spelman Fund and the Ford Foundation provided the nec-
essary conditions for PACH to continuously expand its trans-
national transfer activities.
Conversely, some systemic factors such as geopolitical and ideo-
logical competition played more complex roles. At the height of
the Cold War, the strategic need of the United States to counter
the influence of the Soviet Union prompted it to proactively pro-
mote its own development model globally, which undoubtedly
injected strong impetus into PACH's export of its networking
experience and administrative techniques. However, in the
late 1930s, the volatile international political environment and
intense ideological conflicts became the major factors imped-
ing the continuous implementation of PACH's clearing house
model in Western Europe and Latin America. In addition, other
significant constraints that emerged during PACH's transna-
tional transfer process included institutional differences within
the recipient regions (such as uneven distribution of administra-
tive resources), as well as political misgivings in some countries
regarding external technical assistance from the United States.
6   |   Conclusion
By tracing the origins as well as the transferring history of
PACH's clearing house idea and networking experience, and
using the policy transfer framework to analyze it, this study pro-
vides a complementary transnational perspective and empirical
evidence for explaining the formation of interorganizational
networks in the field of public administration. The case of PACH
shows that transnational interorganizational networks are not
only important channels for policy transfer, but also can be dis-
seminated and replicated through policy transfer. The imitation
of PACH's administrative coordination mechanism and tech-
niques by the International Clearing House (Brussels Center),
the Pan-­American Commission for Inter-­municipal Cooperation
(Havana Center), and the public administration centers in Japan
and Brazil, to some extent, reveals the symbiotic relationship
between the network formation within professional commu-
nities and policy transfer, which is rooted in the transnational
tendency inherent in professional organizations themselves
(Rose 1993, 56).
This study also supplements the existing policy transfer litera-
ture from two dimensions. First, the case of PACH highlights
the agency and capabilities of professional organizations, as a
specific type of non-­state actor, in transnational policy diffusion.
This breaks through the state-­centered perspective of conven-
tional policy transfer research and provides further empirical
evidence for extant research on the role of non-­state actors in
transnational policy transfer. It shows that professional organi-
zations in one country have the ability to promote the transna-
tional transfer of their own experience through means such as
knowledge/information inter-­mediation, to advocate collabora-
tion, and to establish and maintain enduring personal contacts
with cooperating agencies.
Second, the story of PACH demonstrates that policy transfer is
the result of the dynamic interaction of multilevel factors such
as individuals (e.g., promotion by leaders), organizations (e.g.,
cooperation between PACH and foundations, national and su-
pranational organizations), and systems (e.g., the Cold War
international order). This echoes the multi-­dimensional ana-
lytical framework proposed by some other scholars (Evans and
Davies 1999; de Oliveira and Koga 2023).
It should be admitted that, due to the constraints of available
data, the present study, which centers on the perspective of
PACH as the exporting side, lacks the in-­depth exploration of
the reception and local adjustment process of the PACH model
in the recipient regions. Thus, it is likely that the complexity and
bidirectional nature of part of the transfer process have been un-
derestimated. Future research could incorporate archives from
multiplecountries to further explore the agency of the recipient
countries or regions. It is also feasible to compare the disparities
in the effectiveness of policy transfer between different regions

and to analyze the moderating impacts of cultural factors such
as values, norms, and political systems including democratic
and authoritarian regimes on the policy transfer process.
Despite the consistent adherence of PACH's leaders to the so-­
called “resolute anonymity policy” (Brownlow  1958, 466),
which allowed their institution to serve as a behind-­the-­scenes
coordinator in the field of public administration for a long time,
it still left a legacy that can be drawn upon by contemporary
public administration practitioners. PACH is not merely a dis-
tinctive centralized communication mechanism and organiza-
tional structure established by the forerunners of the American
public administration profession. More importantly, it embod-
ies a precious cooperative ideal and professional culture, both
committed to breaking through geographical barriers and the
divisions of professional specialization. In an era marked by
the coexistence of globalization and anti-­globalization trends,
PACH's story offers professional organization leaders a ref-
erence to contemplate how to better utilize latent resources,
precisely evaluate the international landscape they are in, and
boost cross-­border cooperation through noncoercive and nonhi-
erarchical administrative approaches. In this regard, this study
may help overcome the fragmentation dilemmas in global gov-
ernance and offer fresh perspectives for establishing a more re-
silient international cooperation framework.

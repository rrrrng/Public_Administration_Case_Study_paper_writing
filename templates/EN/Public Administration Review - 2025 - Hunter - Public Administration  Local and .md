ABSTRACT
This study examines how the nature of public administration and local and regional governance affects domestic terrorism in
73 mixed and democratic countries from multiple regions and levels of development. In conducting a cross-­national statistical
analysis from 1991 to 2019 with standard political, economic, and social controls, and controlling for endogeneity, we find that
domestic terrorism increases when public administration is more partial, biased, corrupt, and unreliable, and as local and re-
gional governments are controlled to a greater extent by unelected bodies. In addition, we find that the nature of public adminis-
tration and local and regional governance influences the types of institutions that are most likely to be targeted in domestic terror
attacks. These findings have important implications in considering how public administration and local and regional governance
affect political violence in mixed and democratic regimes.
1   |   Introduction
This study examines how the nature of public administration
and local and regional governance affects political environments
and domestic terrorism in 73 mixed and democratic regimes
from multiple regions and levels of development from 1991 to
2019. Previous research has considered how political, economic,
and social factors affect domestic terrorism. However, missing
from much of this scholarship is an examination of how the
nature of public administration and local and regional gover-
nance affect domestic terrorism cross-­nationally. We argue that
the type of public administration institutions and the degree to
which local and regional governments are controlled by non-
elected entities impact the extent to which grievances and po-
larization are present within countries. We contend that when
public administration is more biased, unreliable, and corrupt,
and when nonelected entities usurp local and regional govern-
ment authority to a greater degree, domestic terrorism increases
due to a rise in grievances and polarization. Thus, our study
posits that the nature of public administration and local and re-
gional governance affect political climates and resulting levels
of political violence within countries.
This study offers a unique contribution to the public administra-
tion and political violence literature by explicitly examining the
relationship between public administration institutions, Regional
and Local Government Autonomy (RLGA), and domestic terror-
ism cross-­nationally. It analyzes these important topics across
multiple nations and regions. As Bhuiyan and Perry (2024) argue,
public administration has accumulated knowledge that is narrow
in its primary focus being the US context. There is a need for more
comparative public administration studies (Jilke, Meuleman, and
Van de Walle  2015; Jreisat  2011), leading the editors of Public
Administration Review to call for research on building public ad-
ministration knowledge outside of the US context (Willoughby
et  al.  2023). In particular, as Jilke, Meuleman, and Van de
Walle (2015) argue and demonstrate, studies in the public adminis-
tration and policy literature need to develop standard comparison

1.1   |   The Nature of Public Administration
and Domestic Terrorism
After the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, scholars of public
administration argued for a new paradigm to explain the relation-
ship between public management and terrorism (Roberts 2002).
This paradigm argued that executing terrorist attacks might
be more difficult in nations with professional bureaucracies.
Administrative states promote building trust in government, ef-
fective planning and coordination, streamlined processes, and
engagement from an educated public (Gormley 2002). These con-
ditions should in theory lead to less political grievances and sub-
sequent domestic terrorism. However, the influence of a nation's
capacity has been shown to have mixed effects on occurrences of
domestic terrorism (Hendrix and Young 2014). The lack of clear
answers may be due to oversights in research conceptualization.
We hold that a focus on the quality of public administration in a
country is a capacity-­related variable that is often not examined in
cross-­national scholarship but has significant effects on political
environments and domestic terrorism.
Our approach in defining “quality of public administration” is
rooted in the academic literature focusing on good governance
(Bovaird and Löffler  2003; Fukuyama  2013; Lapuente and
Van de Walle  2020; Rothstein and Teorell  2008; Suzuki and
Demircioglu 2019). This literature is concerned with measuring
the quality of public services in a nation and also the effects of
policy outcomes. There is concern for health measures, engage-
ment in politics measures, and governance measures (Bovaird
and Löffler 2003). The challenge in the good governance literature
is clearly defining these variables. Fukuyama (2013) argues that
the data on good governance is limited because the literature has
not clearly defined key concepts comprising quality government.
He argues for a focus on process or bureaucratic variables (i.e.,
Weberian features of rational bureaucracy), capacity measures,
output measures, and bureaucratic autonomy (Fukuyama 2013).
In this study, we focus on the bureaucratic side of the good gover-
nance literature by examining the effect of public administration
quality on the significant public problem of domestic terrorism.
Public administration literature has struggled to define the effects
of key theoretical ideas, such as New Public Management, on the
quality of public services and policy outcomes (Lapuente and Van
de Walle 2020). It has been demonstrated, though, that the qual-
ity of public services is highly dependent on the “administrative,
political, and policy context” of public administration (Lapuente
and Van de Walle 2020, 461). Thus, this leads to the question, what
are the important features of administrative and political context?
Rothstein and Teorell  (2008) demonstrate the importance of
“impartiality” in measuring good governance. Suzuki and
Demircioglu (2019) extend this focus on impartiality in govern-
ment by including the importance of a professional bureaucracy,
demonstrating that this combination encourages innovation in
nations. Thus, we continue this focus on the quality of public ad-
ministration by considering impartiality, professionalism, and
competence in bureaucracy. Based on this view, the quality of
public administration measures we include in the study, which
we discuss from a methods standpoint later in the paper, include
the level of corruption, biasedness, professionalism, efficiency,
impartiality, and overall effectiveness in a nation's administra-
tive state (Coppedge et al. 2021).
It is important to acknowledge that the quality of public admin-
istration can affect democratic governance and vice versa. Many
cross-­national measures of democracy often focus on elections and
voter enfranchisement. A smaller but substantial number of cross-­
national democracy metrics examine factors related to democratic
governance concerning whether governments rule democrati-
cally. This study focuses on the quality of public administration.
While public administration is an important aspect of democratic
governance, it is often not examined to the same degree as other
factors pertinent to democracy (such as executive constraints, po-
litical rights, civil rights, and civil liberties). Thus, while this study
focuses on public administration and not democracy specifically,
we contend that the quality of public administration affects and is
affected by democratic norms and institutions.

that corruption affects how public resources are allocated, in-
cluding the composition of public expenditures at all levels of
government (Sevüktekin et al. 2010). Additionally, administra-
tive countries that do not ensure appropriate levels of autonomy

Building on this literature, we contend that public administra-
tion that is ineffective, biased, and corrupt exacerbates griev-
ances and polarization within countries and increases the
likelihood of domestic terrorism. Previous research finds that
government corruption can affect terrorism (Azevedo  2018;
Boussiga and Ghdamsi  2016; Meierrieks and Auer  2024;
Zumve, Ingyoroko, and Akuva 2013). Relatedly, prior research
also finds that discrimination and political exclusion can af-
fect terrorism (Choi and Piazza 2016; Ghatak 2016; Gleditsch
and Polo 2016; Piazza 2011). Corrupt government officials and
administrators often create conditions that are more favorable
for terrorism (Azevedo  2018; Boussiga and Ghdamsi  2016;
Meierrieks and Auer  2024) as researchers have discovered
that corruption can increase terrorism by exacerbating eco-
nomic inequality, economic grievances, and decreasing the
effectiveness of counter-­terrorism operations (Meierrieks and
Auer  2024). The rationale is that greater corruption often
benefits certain individuals and groups economically while
negatively affecting others. Terrorism becomes more likely
as economic inequality and economic grievances increase.
Within these environments, anger is often directed at the gov-
ernment institutions that are perceived to be responsible for
the corruption and unequal distribution of resources. Further,
terrorist groups often cite government corruption as a reason
for carrying out their activities (Meierrieks and Auer 2024).
Previous research also found that corruption decreases citi-
zens beliefs that their government institutions are legitimate
and can be trusted (Anderson and Tverdova  2003; Chang
and Chu 2006; Clausen, Kraay, and Nyiri 2011; Dimant and
Tosato  2018; Stockemer, LaMontagne, and Scruggs  2013).
Additionally, corruption decreases the ability of govern-
ments to provide accountability, transparency, and apply laws
equally and appropriately (Anderson and Tverdova  2003).
Grievances should increase in these environments as more in-
dividuals become dissatisfied with perceived corrupt, ineffec-
tive, and less transparent public administration institutions.
Thus, our first hypothesis is:
Hypothesis 1.  Public administration that is biased, inef-
fective, partial, and corrupt is associated with greater domestic
terrorism.
Another important feature of public administration is the na-
ture of power-­sharing between different levels of government.
Power sharing and the division of power between national,
subnational, and local levels of government are important as-
pects of nonauthoritarian political systems. In addition, while
there are frequent debates regarding the correct distribution of
power among different levels of governance, many research-
ers contend that a reasonable degree of local and regional
government autonomy is important for governing (Ladner,
Keuffer, and Baldersheim 2016). As Ladner et al. (2016, 321)
remark: “Local autonomy is a highly valued feature of good
governance.” One rationale for this position is an appropriate
degree of autonomy provides local and regional governments
the ability to respond to the ever-­evolving needs of their cit-
izens. When citizens do not believe their local and regional
governments have the ability or power to respond to their is-
sues or concerns, they are more likely to develop grievances
against the state (Botes 2018).
One issue that can emerge from the weakening of local and re-
gional government autonomy is when nonelected entities (either
internal or external to government) usurp the power and au-
thority of local and regional government units. In these circum-
stances, the capabilities of local and regional governments may
be diminished to the point that they cannot effectively respond
to the dynamic needs of citizens and stakeholders. In these envi-
ronments, grievances and polarization are likely to increase and
lead to conditions more favorable to political violence. This leads
to the second hypothesis:
Hypothesis 2.  Less local and regional government auton-
omy (due to the influence of nonelected entities) is associated with
greater domestic terrorism.
Previous research has considered how grievances affect terror-
ism (Ghatak 2016; Gleditsch and Polo 2016; Kurrild-­Klitgaard,
Justesen, and Klemmensen 2006; Ross 1993). Researchers have
found that expanding political grievances are associated with
increased terrorism (Abadie 2006; Gaibulloev and Sandler 2019;
Krieger and Meierrieks  2010; Piazza  2006). Grievances have
been linked with terrorism as they indicate a greater amount
of anger directed at the state or other groups. Thus, in the ag-
gregate, more intense grievances are associated with increased
terrorism over time (Ghatak  2016; Gleditsch and Polo  2016;
Kurrild-­Klitgaard, Justesen, and Klemmensen 2006; Ross 1993).
Grievances are likely heightened in countries where more in-
dividuals view the government as corrupt (Meierrieks and
Auer  2024) and experience discrimination by government
institutions (Choi and Piazza  2016; Ghatak  2016; Gleditsch
and Polo 2016; Piazza 2011). Thus, grievances should be more
pronounced in environments marked by corruption and less
effective and more biased public administration. Individuals
are more likely to develop anger, frustration, and deeply held
grievances against the state in these settings due to their nega-
tive experiences with perceived ineffective, corrupt, and biased
government institutions and actors. In addition, public admin-
istration that is biased, ineffective, and corrupt should increase
polarization within countries as these types of institutions often
benefit certain groups and actors while excluding others, lead-
ing to increased political tensions between groups.
Prior scholarship has found that greater polarization is as-
sociated with increased terrorism (Hunter, Griffith, and
Warren 2020; Hunter et al. 2023; Piazza 2022, 2023). Growing
polarization often reflects increased grievances, tensions, and
animosity between groups within societies making political vi-
olence more likely. More corrupt, ineffective, and partial public
administration should increase polarization within countries as
more individuals become discontented with the state and as cer-
tain groups benefit from public administration institutions and
others do not. Thus, there should be a rise in domestic terror

2   |   Research Design
This study examines how the nature of public administration
and local and regional governance affects domestic terrorism in
73 mixed and democratic regimes from multiple levels of develop-
ment from Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America, the Middle East,
and North America. We utilize the Liberal Democracy Index
from the Varieties of Democracy Dataset (VDEM) (Coppedge
et  al.  2021) to identify mixed regimes and democracies. The
standard cutoff of 0.5 is employed where countries that receive
scores of 0.5 or above for a given country-­year are regarded as
mixed regimes and democracies and are included in the sample.2
Country-­year is the unit of analysis. The countries included in
the analysis can be found in Table A1.3 The summary statistics
for the primary variables can be found in Table A14. A correla-
tion matrix for the primary variables can be found in Table A15.
Finally, a list of the primary variables, their operationalization,
and the data sources used can be found in Table A16.
We utilize the term mixed regimes, which many scholars also
define as hybrid regimes, semi-­democracies, virtual democra-
cies, and pseudo-­democracies. These countries are often not
regarded as authoritarian because they contain notable demo-
cratic elements (Levitsky and Way 2002). In addition, they can-
not be regarded as fully democratic because they do not meet the
requisite requirements to be considered democracies. Therefore,
they are often defined as mixed regimes or hybrid regimes be-
cause they lie in the gray area between authoritarianism and full
democracy.
We analyze democracies and mixed regimes because fully
authoritarian countries have fundamentally disparate in-
stitutions and political processes (Hunter, Griffith, and
Warren  2020; Svolik  2012) that make comparisons with
mixed regimes and democracies difficult. Thus, analyzing
variations in the nature of public administration and local
and regional governance in fully authoritarian regimes and
comparing them with mixed regimes and democracies is prob-
lematic based on the unique government institutions, political
processes, and government norms present in authoritarian
countries. In addition, mixed regimes and democracies entail
many similar institutional features, norms, and processes.
Finally, many political, cross-­national studies employ a sim-
ilar framework in examining mixed regimes and democracies
(Wong 2021).
2.1   |   Dependent Variable
We employ the number of domestic terror attacks (per mil-
lion of the population) for each country and year (Jetter and
Stadelmann 2019) as the main dependent variable in the study.
We utilize a method that cross-­checks the Global Terrorism
Dataset (GTD) with the International Terrorism: Attributes of
Terrorist Events (ITERATE) Dataset to assess if a given attack
is regarded as domestic in nature. This method was developed
by Enders, Sandler, and Gaibulloev (2011) and is considered a
viable and accurate technique to identify domestic terror in-
cidents and has been employed in numerous terrorist studies
(Gaibulloev and Sandler 2019; Hunter et al. 2023; Piazza 2011;
Wang and Young 2020). In addition, the GTD is considered one
of the leading and most exhaustive terrorism databases glob-
ally.4 In additional models, we also assess the type of target in
domestic attacks based on the nature of the entities targeted. The
target types we focus on are: business, government, police and
military, journalists and the media, and telecommunications.
The data for the target types were collected from the target cate-
gories in the GTD. We include the target type of domestic attacks
in additional models to assess how the nature of public admin-
istration and local and regional government autonomy can im-
pact the institutions that are targeted in attacks. The additional
target type variables are included as previous scholarship has
found that terrorists may select their targets based on specific
motivations or as part of their messaging strategy that is linked
to the attacks (Matusitz 2014).
2.2   |   Independent Variables
The data for the primary quality of public administration and
local and regional governance measures are collected from
the VDEM. The VDEM (Coppedge et  al.  2021) collects, re-
cords, and codes expert surveys from in-­country experts for
every country and year as part of an interconnected data con-
sortium. Employing a Bayesian item response theory estima-
tion technique, responses are aggregated across expert coders.
The results are converted to intervals through the measure-
ment methodology and then coded as continuous variables
(for specific variables) and each value is recalculated for each
country-­year based on the responses from the in-­country ex-
perts. We lag the measures for one period to ensure changes in
the public administration and local and regional governance
variables occur before changes in the domestic terrorism-­
dependent variable.
The quality of public administration and local and regional gov-
ernance measures that are collected from the VDEM (Coppedge
et al. 2021) are: Public Administration Bias (PA Bias), Public
Administration Partiality (PA Partiality), and RLGA. PA Bias
gauges how rigorous and impartial public officials are in per-
forming their duties to the extent they adhere to the law, treat
cases and individuals fairly, and the degree that public admin-
istration is characterized by bias, and arbitrariness such as
nepotism, cronyism, and discrimination. Higher values indi-
cate less effective and more biased public administration. PA
Partiality refers to what extent laws are rigorously enforced,
and public administration is impartial. The measure also cap-
tures the extent that the government and public administration

2.3   |   Control Variables
A number of measures are included to control the political,
economic, and social features of countries that could impact
domestic terrorist events based on prior scholarship (Freytag
et al. 2011; Gurr 1988; Krueger and Laitin 2008; Piazza 2006;
Ross  1993). Thus, in the main models, we include variables
for: GDP per capita, inflation, unemployment, and trade. We
also utilize a measure for education based on previous research
that has found education levels can influence political violence
(Korotayev, Vaskin, and Tsirel 2021). The data for these vari-
ables are collected from the World Bank  (2020). In addition,
although the study analyzes mixed regimes and democracies,
we include a measure for the level of democracy (Democracy)
as a robustness check in additional models as the degree of
democracy within countries can vary over time, and it is im-
portant to account for any potential effects changing levels of
democracy can have on domestic terrorism based on previous
scholarship (Chenoweth  2013; Eyerman  1998; Gaibulloev,
Piazza, and Sandler  2017; Kurrild-­Klitgaard, Justesen, and
Klemmensen 2006; Magen 2018; Ross 1993). We utilize the lib-
eral democracy measure from the VDEM (Coppedge et al. 2021)
that captures the extent to which the idea of liberal democracy is
achieved. Finally, some scholarship has discovered that ethnic
fractionalization can impact domestic terrorism. (Danzell, Yeh,
and Pfannenstiel  2019; Foster, Braithwaite, and Sobek  2013).
Thus, we employ a measure for ethnic and cultural fractional-
ization (Fractionalization) in additional models to account for
any potential effects ethnic and cultural fractionalization have
on domestic terrorism. The measure captures the ethnic and
cultural distances between groups within countries (Fearon
and Laitin 2003).
3   |   Estimation Method
The data included in the study are pooled, time series data
with a count-­dependent variable. A Poisson estimate technique
with robust standard errors is employed since the observations
are nonnegative values distributed over a fixed time interval
(Crost, Felter, and Johnston 2014). This technique is commonly
used in cross-­national, time-­series scholarship (Cameron and
Trivedi 2009; Enders and Hoover 2012).

In examining Table  1, we see the effect PA Bias, PA Partiality,
CORR, and RLGA have on domestic terrorism in mixed regimes
and democracies. In examining Models (1–4), we see that PA Bias,
PA Partiality, CORR, and RLGA have a positive and significant
effect on domestic terrorism. We find similar results in Table 2
(Models 5–8) when including the democracy and fractionalization
control variables. Thus, in all models, the results provide support
for Hypothesis 1 that stipulates more biased, partial, ineffective,
and corrupt publication administration should be associated with

less RLGA should be associated with increased domestic terrorism.
In Tables 3–6, we examine the effects of PA Bias, PA Partiality,
CORR, and RLGA on the types of institutions targeted in domestic
attacks. In Table 3, we find that PA Bias, PA Partiality, CORR, and
RLGA have a positive and significant effect on business attacks.
In Table 4, we find that PA Bias, PA Partiality, CORR, and RLGA
have a positive and significant effect on government attacks. In
Table 5, we see that PA Bias, PA Partiality, CORR, and RLGA have
a positive and significant effect on police and military attacks. In
Tables 6 and 7, we find that PA Bias, PA Partiality, CORR, and
RLGA have an insignificant effect on attacks on journalists and
the media, as well as an insignificant effect on attacks on telecom-
munications targets. The results indicate that more biased, ineffec-
tive, partial, and corrupt public administration is associated with
a greater number of attacks directed at businesses, government
targets, and military and police targets. Also, we find similar re-
sults for local and regional governments that have less autonomy
as these types of governments are associated with more attacks di-
rected against business, government, police, and military targets.
We discover an insignificant effect on attacks related to journal-
ists, the media, and telecommunications. The results suggest that
terrorists may be selecting targets that are associated with the gov-
ernment due to their negative perceptions of specific government
institutions. Additionally, business targets may be chosen due to
their perceived connections or associations with the government.
The insignificant findings regarding journalists, the media, and
telecommunications could be related to the notion that terrorists
may not perceive the press and journalists as actors who are re-
sponsible for their grievances. Furthermore, terrorism is often car-
ried out to garner media attention (Nussio, Böhmelt, and Vincenzo
Bove 2021) and some terrorist groups may not identify media or-
ganizations as targets due to the desire of terrorist groups to have
media organizations report news of terrorist activity. However,
future research needs to be conducted to more clearly identify the
linkage between the nature of public administration and local and
regional governance and the type of targets selected in attacks.
3.2   |   Mediation Analysis
To better understand the relationship between the nature of
public administration and local and regional governments and
political climates and domestic terrorism, we conduct a me-
diation analysis in which we examine how the variables: PA
Bias, PA Partiality, CORR, and RLGA affect domestic terror-
ism with polarization employed as a mediating variable while
including the control variables found in the main models.6

Polarization is conceptualized as the degree to which there
are significant differences of beliefs regarding major political
issues within countries. The data for the polarization measure
are taken from the Digital Society Project (DSP) (Mechkova
et al. 2021). Higher values are reflective of greater polarization
within countries. We employ a structural equation modeling
(SEM) technique, which is a type of linear equation technique
(Baron and Kenny 1986; Cheong and MacKinnon 2012; Little
et  al.  2007) as it can identify the mediated effect of several
variables (Baron and Kenny  1986). In addition, prior re-
search has employed the coefficient methods utilized in the
technique and posited that the method produces valid results
regarding the mediation effect (Hunter and Biglaiser  2022;
Jo 2008; Piazza 2022).

A major element or extension of the reform was the passage of
the 73rd and 74th amendments to the Indian Constitution in
1992 which formally recognized local government bodies as
the third tier of government and mandated that state govern-
ments transfer planning responsibilities to the third tier for ac-
tivities such as: construction of buildings, roads, and bridges;
water; public health; and sanitation (Ahluwalia 2019). The prob-
lem with this arrangement was that local bodies now had all
the accountability for these projects, but neither the finances
nor expertise for planning and management (Meloche and
Vaillancourt  2015). While the Constitutional changes clearly
established the three-­tier system of governance, they did not
provide guidance regarding how to divide powers, particularly
those of taxation or responsibilities for each layer of govern-
ment. Politics factored into financial allocations with each level
being reluctant to tax for fear of losing votes (Chaubey 2007).
This fear was not unfounded because citizens tended to cast
their votes in alliance with their community identities and not
on the competence of the candidates which increased mass-­
scale corruption, nepotism, and communalism, particularly at
the local level (Singh 2023). The Modi government, aware of the
need for more effective and transparent structures, enacted the
Goods and Services Tax (GST) in 2017 to subsume all indirect
taxes (Nayyar and Singh I 2018). However, without correspond-
ing institutional governmental reforms, it was not enough to
create effective local governments capable of addressing their
constituents' basic needs.
Overall, the nature of many public administration institutions
and local and regional government units in India consisted of
inefficiencies, biasedness, and cronyism (Panja 2021; Ray 1994)
which increased discontent among many citizens and fostered
greater polarization between groups (Kishwar 2005; Soni 2008).
From 1991 to 2019, India averaged 386.68 domestic terrorist
attacks per year (GTD 2020). This is much higher than the av-
erage number of annual domestic attacks for countries world-
wide (24.70) during the same period (GTD 2020). While many
factors contributed to the political violence in India during the
time frame, the nature of public administration and local and
regional governance gave rise to increased discontent and griev-
ances that created a political climate more hospitable to political
violence.
Turkey provides another example of how the type of public
administration institutions and local and regional government
units can affect political environments and resulting political vi-
olence. It is important to note that numerous concerns regarding
the quality of public administration in Turkey coincided with
declines in overall democracy. Thus, many of the issues facing
Turkey involved both a reduction in the quality of public admin-
istration and a weakening of democratic institutions and norms.
Following the Presidential election of Recep Tayyip Erdoğan in
2014, several government reforms that were instituted over sub-
sequent years exacerbated pre-­existing weaknesses with public
administration institutions and local and regional governance.

The combination of reforms and long-­standing issues led to
problems regarding the excessive centralization of government
power concentrated overwhelmingly in the executive branch,
the disorganization of public administration institutions, and a
significant lack of effectiveness and efficiency related to the de-
livery of public administration services (Freedom House 2023;
Özgürlük Araştırmaları Derneği 2023; Tsarouhas 2023).
The over-­centralization of executive branch power created local
government administrations that lacked the initiative and flex-
ibility to respond to citizens' needs. The overarching public ad-
ministration infrastructure entailed complex personnel systems
in which civil servants, contracted personnel, and other work-
ers had different levels of rights and there was frequent confu-
sion over who was responsible for specific duties. Additionally,
Turkish laws created public procurement processes that were
nontransparent and politically biased which favored certain sec-
tors of society over others. Due to the nature of the relationship
between the executive and legislative branches regarding public
spending oversight, the allocation and distribution of public ad-
ministration resources could not be effectively monitored and
was plagued with bias (Özgürlük Araştırmaları Derneği 2023;
Tsarouhas 2023).
Another set of government policies that significantly affected
the nature of public administration in Turkey was the Erdoğan
regime's response to the failed coup attempt in 2016. It is es-
timated that the Erdoğan administration removed 100,000
public sector employees, including judges, teachers, doctors,
academics, police officers, and members of the armed forces fol-
lowing the failed coup. The purges included employees across
both the central and local government sectors (Yilmaz and
Bashirov 2018; Yilmaz, Caman, and Bashirov 2020). The purges
led to increased concerns over fair and impartial public admin-
istration practices in Turkey.
One important and overarching issue in Turkey has been the
politicization of public administration institutions and local
and regional governments in which political loyalty has been
prioritized over the efficient and nonbiased delivery of public
administration services. This environment has led to cumber-
some, inefficient, and slow-­responding public administration
institutions. It has also increased the arbitrary delivery of pub-
lic administration services, the disregarding of rules and regu-
lations pertaining to public administration, greater partiality
and bias, and the lack of competency and transparency within
the public administration bureaucracy (Freedom House 2023;
Özgürlük Araştırmaları Derneği  2023; Tsarouhas  2023).
As Somer  (2019, 56) remarks: “This constituency [the more
ideologically driven wing of the AKP] became increasingly
willing to support, overlook, and, at times, demand not only
the reformation but also the capturing and revolutionary dis-
mantling of existing democratic institutions and divisions of
powers. Captive audiences became increasingly ready to con-
done growing corruption, violations of the rule of law, and
opportunistic grabbing of power and wealth by the party and
its clients.” Greater discontent and more intense polarization
emerged in this environment (Somer 2019). Additionally, from
2014 to 2019, Turkey averaged 210 domestic terrorist attacks
per year (GTD 2020) compared with the average number of
annual domestic attacks (51.48) for countries worldwide
during the same period (GTD 2020). Thus, the nature of pub-
lic administration along with the deterioration of democratic
institutions, norms, and practices in Turkey have contributed
to a rise in domestic terrorism in the state.
In examining the brief cases of Colombia, India, and Turkey,
while many political, economic, and social factors affected do-
mestic terrorism within each country, it appears the nature
of public administration and local and regional governance
affected political climates. It is likely that greater discontent,
grievances, and polarization stemming from more biased,
ineffective, and corrupt public administration institutions,
combined with the lack of ability of local and regional govern-
ments to respond to the needs of their citizens, contributed to
political environments that were more conducive to domestic
terrorism.
4   |   Conclusion
This study finds that public administration characterized by
corruption, bias, and ineffectiveness is associated with an in-
crease in domestic terrorist incidents. Additionally, countries
with reduced local and regional government autonomy ex-
perience more domestic terrorist attacks. We attribute these
findings to the effects corrupt, biased, and ineffective public
administration institutions and decreased local and regional
government autonomy have on grievances and political po-
larization. In these environments, citizens have less confi-
dence in the ability of public administration institutions and
local and regional governments to respond to their needs in
a timely, efficient, transparent, and nonbiased manner. The
result is more intense grievances and polarization, which
produce political environments more hospitable to political
violence.
As noted earlier, the study's findings contribute to the good
governance literature in public administration (Bovaird and
Löffler 2003; Fukuyama 2013; Lapuente and Van de Walle 2020;
Rothstein and Teorell 2008; Suzuki and Demircioglu 2019). The
findings advance the literature's overall understanding of how
the quality of a nation's governing institutions, in this case, the
public bureaucracy, affects the likelihood of political violence
and domestic terrorism. With respect to public administration
strategies countries can pursue to attempt to reduce polariza-
tion, grievances, and domestic terrorism, government leaders
should work to ensure that public administration institutions
are as impartial, unbiased, transparent, and reliable as possible.
Also, government leaders need to ensure that local and regional
governments have the power and ability to respond to the dy-
namic needs of their citizens as effectively and promptly as pos-
sible. Accomplishing these tasks can be difficult at times due
to resource constraints, embedded bureaucratic hurdles, and
various forms of bias that can affect government institutions.
However, if countries can provide for impartial, reliable, and ef-
fective public administration and local and regional governance,
citizens should have higher levels of satisfaction with their gov-
ernments, leading to reduced grievances, less polarization, and
fewer incidents of political violence.

Public administration theory and practice have long debated
how nations implement these strategies to achieve the goals of
quality administration and good governance. The commonality
in this debate is a need for nations to have a professional ad-
ministrative state. In the context of this study and what can be
done to ensure a professional administrative state, our findings
point to the need for building capacity in national and subna-
tional governments, protecting merit-­based civil systems in pub-
lic bureaucracies, and empowering subnational governments to
address the needs of their citizens. Additionally, a professional
administrative state limits favoritism in its decisions and these
bureaucracies have robust regulatory apparatuses to ensure fair-
ness. In democracies, such a professional administrative state
serves as a nonviolent political outlet, in addition to elections, for
citizens to take their concerns with the government and have a
chance to address those issues. When citizens see the adminis-
trative state as not being professional and fair, they question the
utility of their interactions with the government, leading them
to possibly turn to actions that may be outside of the political
and civil process.
This study has examined the relationship between the nature
of public administration institutions, local and regional gover-
nance, and domestic terrorism from a cross-­national perspec-
tive. Future cross-­national research could expand upon this
research and consider how the precise structure of public ad-
ministration institutions and local and regional governments
can impact political climates and political violence. This re-
search could focus on the specific bureaucratic composition of
public administration institutions and local and regional gov-
ernments to analyze how they impact grievances, polarization,
and domestic terrorism.
Overall, we contend this research makes meaningful contribu-
tions to both international relations and public administration
scholarship. In particular, it advances needed research in public
administration by comparing the impact of bureaucracy qual-
ity across nations, instead of examining a single country. Given
that public administration is a critical aspect of governance, fu-
ture scholarship could extend this work and consider how addi-
tional aspects of public administration affect political violence
cross-­nationally.

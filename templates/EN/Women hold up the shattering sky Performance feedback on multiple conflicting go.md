Abstract
Previous research on the glass cliff phenomenon has primarily explored the impact of performance decline on women in leadership based on
single objectives, overlooking the complexity of managing performance in the public sector. This study adopts a multigoal perspective to explore
how performance feedback in a dual-goal scenario affects gender composition in top management teams (TMTs) in Chinese local governments.
Drawing on behavioral theory, it differentiates between unambiguous and ambiguous feedback as two distinct types of organizational perfor­
mance feedback, to test their influence on women’s proportion in TMTs. A unique data set covering 276 Chinese municipal governments from
2010 to 2018 was used to investigate how performance feedback on two conflicting objectives, economic growth and environmental protection
pursued by Chinese local governments, affects women’s proportion in mayoral teams. Results from fractional logit regression suggest that
unambiguous failure is positively associated with the proportion of women in TMTs. However, ambiguous performance feedback is negatively
related to the proportion of women in TMTs. Implications for research on gender composition in the upper echelons and performance feedback
on multiple objectives are discussed.

Introduction
Women’s career advancement to leadership has long been
a central focus of public administration (PA) research (e.g.,
Bishu, Sabharwal, and Reyes 2023; Kelly et al. 1991; Meier,
Mastracci, and Wilson 2006; Newman 1993; Sabharwal
2015; Stivers 2002). Women as chief executives in public or­
ganizations are receiving increasing recognition, but there is
still limited research on women in top management teams
(TMTs) (Huang and Villadsen 2023; Jackson, Neshkova, and
Newman 2024). In practice, women remain underrepresented
in the upper echelons of public organizations despite the
improvements in recent decades (Wang, Ma, and Christensen
2024; Yang, Connolly, and Connolly 2022). Prior research
has explored whether gender diversity in TMTs can affect
different organizational outcomes, such as financial perfor­
mance (Opstrup and Villadsen 2015), public service provision
(Wang, Ma, and Christensen 2024), and innovation (Arena
et al. 2021). However, relatively few studies investigate when
and why women are present in the upper echelons of public
organizations (Groeneveld, Bakker, and Schmidt 2020; Huang
and Villadsen 2023; Yang, Connolly, and Connolly 2022).
The appointments of women to top management positions
are indeed decisions made by decision-makers, such as
politicians or boards of directors. A core theory that explains
public organizations’ decision-making is the behavioral theory
(Cyert and March 1992; van der Voet 2023), which entails
how organizations, instead of individuals, learn from expe­
rience through collecting performance information, setting
aspiration levels based on past performance or that of peer
organizations, and adapting organizational practices if per­
formance falls short of aspirations (Hong 2019). Drawing on
this theory, the first strand of research examines the impact of
performance feedback (i.e., whether organizations respond to
performance below aspirations) on a variety of organizational
actions, such as upcoming performance improvements (Hong
2019), managerial goal prioritization (Nielsen 2014), and
problem focus and search behavior of top managers (Hansen
and Nielsen 2022; van der Voet 2023). A second strand links

An exception is an embryonic line of research that
investigates the glass cliff phenomenon (Ryan et al. 2016).
The glass cliff refers to the phenomenon where women are
more likely to be appointed to leadership positions in times of
poor organizational performance (Ryan and Haslam 2005).
While research has begun to investigate this new phenom­
enon, evidence remains limited and mixed within and beyond
PA (Groeneveld, Bakker, and Schmidt 2020; Reinwald, Zaia,
and Kunze 2022; Yang, Connolly, and Connolly 2022). These
studies exclusively adopt a sole-goal perspective and neglect
how performance feedback shapes gender composition at the
top when faced with multiple and conflicting goals. This is a
critical omission, as public-sector organizations often serve a
multiplicity of interdependent objectives to satisfy the needs
of various stakeholders (Chen and Jia 2023). For instance,
local governments in China often pursue two main objectives,
including economic and environmental targets, which have a
tricky trade-off relationship (Du and Yi 2022; Zhang 2021).
A multidimensional approach to assessing the impact of or­
ganizational performance on gender composition in TMTs
can help address this gap.
This current research adopts a multigoal perspective to
examine whether performance feedback on multiple con­
flicting goals affects gender representation in TMTs in the
public sector. It classifies unambiguous feedback (i.e., per­
formance of multiple conflicting goals being jointly below
or above aspirations) and ambiguous feedback (i.e., per­
formance on one goal exceeding aspirations while being
below aspirations on its competing goals) as two distinctive
types of performance feedback. I propose that unambiguous
failure (i.e., performance of multiple conflicting goals being
jointly below aspirations) can pose a dual threat to an or­
ganization, increasing the need for women leaders to com­
municate strategic shifts and bear the responsibility for any
failure. However, ambiguous feedback creates complexity in
interpreting performance information. I develop competing
hypotheses to link ambiguous feedback to TMT gender repre­
sentation. Decision-makers may allocate attention to negative
information over positive information, resulting in women
stepping into precarious leadership positions. Alternatively,
unambiguous feedback may allow positive interpretation
and trigger an inference process, thus negatively affecting
women’s presence in TMTs.
The context of Chinese local governments was em­
ployed to answer the research question. Gender segregation
is prominent in the top management of Chinese municipal
governments. Understanding the relevant causes not limited
to its patriarchal culture may have practical implications
for China as well as other contexts where gender segrega­
tion at the top is high. Furthermore, economic and environ­
mental targets have a trade-off relationship in China due to
the leading party’s (Chinese Communist Party, hereinafter
referred to as CCP) ambition to pursue both short-term eco­
nomic gains and long-term sustainability. This involved direct
conflict, as performance on environmental goals constrained
that of economic growth goals, as well as indirect competi­
tion for attention and resources (Chen and Jia 2023; Zhang
2021). Performance on the two targets has been the key to the
evaluation of leaders in local governments (Tang, Jiang, and
Mi 2021). It, therefore, sets an ideal ground for the purpose
of this study.
A unique data set covering mayoral teams in 276 Chinese
municipalities was applied to test the impact of performance
feedback on TMT gender composition in a dual-goal sce­
nario. The results suggest that unambiguous failure (eco­
nomic and environmental performance jointly falls below
social aspirations) is positively related to the proportion of
women in TMTs. However, ambiguous performance feedback
(a positive economic performance and a negative environ­
mental performance) is negatively associated with women’s
proportion in TMTs.
This study aims to make three contributions. First, it is one
of the first to examine the relationship between organizational
performance feedback and gender composition in TMTs from
a multigoal perspective. The current study distinguishes be­
tween unambiguous and ambiguous feedback by jointly con­
sidering performance on two conflicting objectives. Results
suggest how performance feedback of multiple conflicting
goals creates complexity in the decisions of TMT gender
composition.
Second, it seeks to contribute to existing gender and top
management research by taking a step back to explore the
antecedents of women’s representation in TMTs. Prior studies
have centrally investigated the consequences of women’s rep­
resentation in the top tiers (Huang and Villadsen 2023; Wang,
Ma, and Christensen 2024). This study contributes to this gap
by exploring how organizational performance feedback can
be an important influencing factor for the gender composi­
tion of TMTs.
Third, this study tests the glass cliff phenomenon in the
Chinese context, which has been underexplored compared to
the prior focus on the US and Western contexts (Morgenroth
et al. 2020). The results indicate how theories developed in
democratic contexts, such as the glass cliff phenomenon, can
be applied to an authoritarian political system but need to
consider its institutional characteristics (e.g., salient goal con­
flict in performance management, peer competition as an im­
portant source of aspiration).
The following section describes gender representation at
the top and specifically in China, followed by theory and
hypotheses. Next, the empirical context, methods and data
are described, and the results are reported. It ends with a dis­
cussion and conclusion of the paper.
Gender representation at the top
Gender representation in top management is considered cru­
cial, as it can fundamentally affect organizational and social
outcomes (Huang and Villadsen 2023). Drawing on represen­
tative bureaucracy theory, a stream of research has explored
the impact of gender representation in executive positions.
For example, Jackson, Neshkova, and Newman (2024)
found that counties with female top law enforcement officials
had fewer youth arrests and prosecutions than those led
by men. Another stream of research based on the diversity-
management literature found that gender diversity in TMTs is

Schein’s (1973) classic work asked participants to rate suc­

managers view managerial work as masculine and a better fit
for men. Research on social role theory suggests that men and
women possess different leadership styles, with men being
agentic and women being communal (Sabharwal 2015). The
role incongruity theory further indicates that incongruity
occurs when there is a discrepancy between the expectations
of women’s roles in society and the requirements of being a
leader (Sabharwal 2015). As such, women are less favored
in leadership positions. These theories describe how men and
women are perceived differently as leaders of well-performing
organizations. State differently, individuals tend to think-
manager-think-male in times of success (Ryan et al. 2011).
One burgeoning line of research focuses on the existence of
the glass cliff in the public sector by testing whether women
are disproportionally placed in the upper echelons in times of
crisis. Women’s communal characteristics, such as empathy
and understanding, are believed to be beneficial when organ­
izations encounter performance decline (Ryan et al. 2016).
Furthermore, women entering leadership teams can signal
strategic changes in response to failure and help take the
blame (Reinwald, Zaia, and Kunze 2022). However, empir­
ical research yields inconsistent results. For example, Smith
(2015) found that as the complexity of work (as a proxy for
the risk of failure) increases, women’s proportion in leader­
ship positions expands in US educational institutions. While
this positive relationship is supported by Yang, Connolly, and
Connolly (2022) by considering fiscal deficit as a risk to US
municipal governments, Groeneveld, Bakker, and Schmidt
(2020) did not find an association between organizational de­
cline in the workforce and women’s representation in Dutch
senior civil servant posts.
The equivocal findings indicate two critical gaps in under­
standing the role of performance in women’s presence in top
management positions. First, top managers attend to a multi­
plicity of objectives in the public sector, and goal conflicts occur
routinely in public organizations (Chen and Jia 2023). The as­
sessment of organizational performance is often not based on
the performance of any single target but on that of multiple
and interdependent objectives. The interactive relationships
between different targets may increase the complexity of sub­
sequent organizational actions (Zhang 2021). It is, therefore,
essential to gain a complete picture by exploring the role of
performance feedback on multiple conflicting goals.
Second, prior research on women in top management has
been primarily conducted in the US or Western contexts (see
Wang, Ma, and Christensen (2024) as an exception). While
evidence shows that performance plays an essential role in
overall leadership promotion in emerging contexts such as
China (Li and Zhou 2005; Tang, Jiang, and Mi 2021), we
know little about how organizational performance may in­
fluence women’s careers as top managers in non-Western
contexts.
This study advances existing research by exploring how
performance feedback on multiple conflicting goals affects
gender composition in TMTs in a dual-goal scenario. As the
hypotheses are tested in the Chinese context, I describe gender
representation in top management in China below.
Gender representation in top management in the
Chinese government
“Women hold up half of the sky” is a famous quote from
Mao Zedong, which indicates that women’s capabilities have
historically drawn attention in China. The central govern­
ment has worked to enhance women’s presence in the upper
echelons. Women’s political participation was initiated in 1995
when the State Council issued a national policy, the Outline
for Women’s Development in China, which recommends
that local governments include women in their leadership
teams. Later, the amended policy in 2001 introduced gender
quotas into the leading cadres of the government. It explicitly
regulates that there should be at least one female cadre in gov­
ernment leadership teams at all levels, and that the proportion
of women should be increased incrementally. However, actual
sanctions are absent when local governments do not follow
the policy.
In practice, gender disparity remains pronounced in China
due to its patriarchal social system and culture. Dominant
Confucian values place women in supporting roles rather than
leading or managing (Xu and Meier 2022). Women are espe­
cially underrepresented in the government, with an average of
13 percent occupying administrative leadership positions in
municipalities (see table 1 in the results section). This number
is even lower in chief executive positions (i.e., mayors, ac­
counting for 6.7 percent) or above (only 4.6 percent of women
work as governors or party secretaries at the higher provin­
cial level). The numbers indicate the challenges for women’s
career advancement to top management or above in such a
male-dominant political system. The need to understand the
causes of gender representation is pronounced. I note that this
study may have implications for contexts with high gender
segregation in public sector top management roles.
Performance feedback and gender
representation in TMTs: a single-goal
perspective
Next, drawing on behavioral theory and literature on gender
and representation, I develop the baseline hypothesis about
the relationship between performance feedback on a single
objective and gender representation in TMTs.
A central idea in the behavioral theory of the firm is that
organizations learn from their past performance, which mod­
ifies their subsequent actions (Cyert and March 1992). Greve
(2003) noted that there are two types of learning, including
individual and organizational learning. While both involve
receiving feedback and adjusting actions, organizational
learning concerns the collective learning process within the
entire organization. It describes how decision-makers learn
from performance feedback on behalf of their organizations.
The theory has been introduced to PA, and research found that
performance feedback can affect a variety of organizational
outcomes in the public sector (e.g., Hong 2019; Nielsen 2014;
van der Voet 2023; Wei et al. 2023). Consistent with research
on behavioral theory, this study focuses on performance feed­
back at the organizational level and takes a departure from
the decision-makers’ point of view. By decision-makers, I

mean groups of people who have the power to affect the ap­

In behavioral theory, decision-makers classify performance
as satisfactory or unsatisfactory by comparing organizational
performance with their aspirations, such as social aspirations
(i.e., comparing own performance with that of peers) or his­
torical aspirations (i.e., comparing current performance with
previous performance) (Holm 2017). Aspirations, therefore,
act as thresholds that partition organizations into two dis­
tinct areas: success and failure (Hong 2019). Research reports
that organizational responses differ when their performance
is classified as success or failure (Hong 2019; Nielsen 2014;
van der Voet 2023). Organizations tend to pursue substan­
tial changes when below aspirations and enhance the present
situation otherwise. This is because performances below and
above aspirations send different signals to decision-makers
and are associated with different responses (Levinthal and
Rerup 2021; Nielsen 2014). When an organization’s per­
formance exceeds aspirations, it sends positive signals to
decision-makers about the organization and the competencies
of its TMT. Therefore, it is likely that decision-makers are sat­
isfied with the status quo.
However, when performance is below aspirations, decision-
makers will likely question the status quo and undertake a
problem-driven search (van der Voet 2023). Small adjustments
in organizational operations may be initiated when organi­
zations perform slightly below aspirations. Nevertheless, an
organization performing far below its aspirations tends to
experience a crisis and is less likely to achieve an acceptable
performance through small improvements (Baum et al. 2005).
The crisis may disrupt organizational routines and create a
turning point for strategic changes. Performance far below
aspirations, therefore, becomes the momentum for a learning
process, leading to exploratory and strategic changes, such as
leadership changes (e.g., Greve 2003; Jacquart and Antonakis
2015; Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty, and Fernandez
2017).
To understand how performance feedback is related spe­
cifically to gender representation in the upper echelons, it
is worth revealing the theoretical mechanisms of the glass
cliff. An optimistic view is that women’s perceived feminine
traits are beneficial during a crisis (Ryan et al. 2011). Men
and women are stereotypically believed to possess different
leadership styles, with men being agentic and women being
communal. Communal traits attributed to women—for ex­
ample, being empathetic, understanding, and helpful—are
crucial when organizations experience performance shortfalls
(Sabharwal 2015). However, others see women’s role more
passively and claim that women are appointed as scapegoats
in times of crisis. Underperforming organizations are likely
to experience intense scrutiny and pressure from their
stakeholders and public media (Ryan et al. 2016). They might
hire women to take on a more symbolic role of weathering the
crisis and enduring uncertainty and failure (Yang, Connolly,
and Connolly 2022). Besides, research has shown that unlike
their male counterparts, who have wider networks to spot
other opportunities, women managers are less likely to con­
nect with decision-makers. As such, they have limited access
to critical resources or opportunities through networking ac­
tivities (Rho and Lee 2018). While women are generally more
risk-averse than men, they are likely to perceive precarious
positions as promotion opportunities when they have low ca­
reer self-efficacy (Darouei and Pluut 2018).
Taken together, the first hypothesis is based on two lines
of argument. First, organizational decision-makers may seek
strategic changes when performance is (far) below aspirations.
Second, feminine traits and leadership types are in demand
when an organization finds itself in a crisis.
Hypothesis 1: The further a municipality’s performance
falls below its aspirations, the higher the proportion of
women in its top management team.
Performance feedback on multiple conflicting
goals and gender representation in TMTs
While a single-goal perspective described above is valuable in
understanding the influence of respective targets, it neglects
the complexity of public sector performance management
(van der Voet and Lerusse 2024). Goal conflict is common
and salient in public organizations such as Chinese local
governments and beyond (Chen and Jia 2023). As Chun and
Rainey (2005) illustrated, there are two types of goal-related
conflicts in public organizations. One is direct goal conflict,
in which achieving one goal directly impedes the attain­
ment of another. Another is indirect goal conflict, indicating
that public organizations have limited resources and atten­
tion spans for addressing multiple objectives. I argue that a
multigoal approach is pivotal as it provides more comprehen­
sive information on organizational actual outcomes.
Figure 1 illustrates the possible combinations of perfor­
mance feedback in a dual-goal scenario. The dashed line on
both axes indicates respective aspirations for two conflicting
goals (i.e., economic and environmental targets in Chinese
local governments in this study). The upper right and bottom
left zones represent unambiguous success or failure when
performance on both objectives is simultaneously above or
below aspirations. However, the other two zones present
more complex situations: performance on one of the targets
exceeds aspirations, whereas performance on the other is
disappointing (named ambiguous feedback in this study).
Ambiguity arises in these two zones as performance feedback
is no longer conclusive.
Next, I zoom in on different types of performance feed­
back on multiple conflicting goals and outline the theoretical
arguments for the hypotheses.
Unambiguous failure
For organizations in position A in figure 1, performance on
both targets is below aspirations. It signals unambiguous
failure in which organizations underperform on any targets
expected by stakeholders. The combination of performance
feedback on multiple conflicting targets creates dual threats
to an organization. As a result, it may lead to a more exten­
sive problem-driven search and more fundamental strategic
changes than any single performance feedback would (Baum
et al. 2005).
According to attribution theory, organizational performance
is attributed to the leader’s competencies when performance
is clearly good (or bad) (Jacquart and Antonakis 2015). For
municipalities with performance on conflicting targets that
jointly exceed aspirations, it is likely that the current leadership

Furthermore, underperforming on two major objectives may

employees from working hard to contribute to future perfor­

to be enhanced, as such actions can send a signal of active stra­
tegic changes in response to prior failure. Besides, as mentioned
above, female leaders are considered suitable scapegoats in crises
(Ryan et al. 2016). Thus, the second hypothesis is:
Hypothesis 2: The further a municipality’s performance on
multiple conflicting goals falls below aspirations, the higher
the proportion of women in its top management team.
Ambiguous feedback
It is often the case that performance on multiple targets does
not arrive with clear labels of success and failure (positions
B and C in figure 1), and it is inconclusive whether actions
should be followed or not. Therefore, ambiguity is generated.
An interpretation process is likely to be launched before en­
gaging in any actual problem-solving activities (Baekgaard
and Serritzlew 2016). Research from general management
suggests that organizations may dynamically interpret ambig­
uous performance information on multiple goals by taking
either a negative or positive view (Pino and Greve 2021).
Therefore, I develop competing hypotheses: ambiguous feed­
back may either (1) lead to negative interpretation and is pos­
itively related to women’s proportion in TMTs or (2) allow
positive interpretation and inferences in leadership appoint­
ment, potentially reducing women’s representation in TMTs.
Public organizations operate in a political environment
where politicians and citizens have a tendency toward nega­
tivity bias (Holm 2018; Hong 2019). Negativity bias means
that individuals tend to respond more strongly to negative
events, experiences, or information than to positive or neu­
tral ones, all other things being equal (Rozin and Royzman
2001). As a key aspect of negativity bias, negativity domi­
nance suggests that evaluations tend to be more negative
when assessing a combination of negative and positive in­
formation (Rozin and Royzman 2001). In other words, neg­
ative information can attract more attention than positive
information when there is mixed performance information.
Following this line of research, I argue that ambiguous per­
formance feedback is likely to lead to more attention on goals
with performance below aspirations. As a result, poor perfor­
mance on any of the goals is a sign of things moving closer

Interestingly, prior studies found that stakeholders tend to

Leaders get credit for successes and are blamed for failures,

Furthermore, the contestation of goals indicates that
reallocating resources to one of the goals may inevitably lead
to a fall in resources on its conflicting goal (Chen and Jia 2023).
Ambiguous feedback constitutes a dilemma for organizations
in deciding how to maintain the current outperforming goal
while addressing the underperforming one. A new challenge,
therefore, arises in organizations facing ambiguous feedback,
which may enhance decision-maker’s perceived threats.
Overall, ambiguous feedback is likely to direct more at­
tention to the negative performance gap even though organ­
izations outperform on the competing goal, which creates a
new type of crisis for an organization. As described earlier
in the paper, women are preferred in times of crisis due to
their perceived leadership advantages, good signalers of
strategic changes, and suitable scapegoats for failure. These
perceptions may apply to situations when performance feed­

Hypothesis 3a: The further a municipality’s performance
falls below the aspirations on one of the goals and exceeds
aspirations on the other, the higher the proportion of

However, ambiguous feedback may also create space for pos­
itive interpretation, limiting problem-driven search. There
is no doubt that ambiguity is reduced when performance is
poor on more than one target. Hence, it is challenging to de­
velop a favorable interpretation that is reliable. Nevertheless,
when performance exceeds aspirations on one of the main
goals and falls below aspirations on the other, opportunities
occur for building favorable interpretations by giving more
weight to the outperforming goal and downplaying the
underperforming goal (Pino and Greve 2021).
Empirical research on private firms found that ambig­
uous feedback dampens responsiveness in introducing new
products (Joseph and Gaba 2015). This effect may be transfer­
able to public organizations with lower goal clarity and often
coping with more conflicting demands than private organi­
zations (Nielsen 2014). Recent PA research found that local
governments in China tend to adopt a differentiation strategy
by prioritizing a goal when observing performance decline in
its competing objective (Chen and Jia 2023). Furthermore, per­
formance of a single target below aspirations leads to upward
attribution of responsibility in a multilevel governance system
(van der Voet, Rimkutė van der Voet, and Rimkutė 2023). This
involves upward blaming of national political actors who set
objectives, assign resources, and create the circumstances that
influence local-level results. Ambiguous feedback containing
less clarity of performance outcomes may offer more
opportunities to make external attributions to, for example,
goal contestation. I argue that performance on any target that
exceeds aspirations generates opportunities to guide attention
discretionally and discourages strategic changes.
Since attributions to leadership competencies are hard to
make when receiving ambiguous feedback, decision-makers
will likely turn to prior heuristics in personnel management.
From a psychological perspective, when individual managers
experience ambiguity in various organizational processes,
they engage in sense-making to search for widely shared cul­
tural frames to reduce ambiguity (Correll et al. 2020). This
generates occasions on which widely shared gender beliefs
step into the gaps in knowledge and subtly frame their
interpretations and evaluations, which depresses women’s
ratings in evaluations (Correll 2017). Ambiguity-induced
cognitive bias has been extended to the organizational level.
Jacquart and Antonakis (2015) found that decision-makers
rely on attributional mechanisms when performance signals
are explicit but turn to inferential processes when signals are
unclear (i.e., the absence of a clear positive or negative organ­
izational performance signal), which leads to the selection of
charismatic leaders with prototypical leadership characteris­
tics. This is because the greater the inference required to draw
implications from performance information, the more likely
stereotypes will be amplified (Heilman 2001). Following this
line of thought, I argue that decision-makers may make heu­
ristic decisions when performance signals are ambiguous,
which enhances gender bias in leadership selections. As such,
they are likely to “think-manager-think-male”.
Taken together, ambiguous feedback creates opportunities
for positive interpretation and the attribution of external re­
sponsibility, which may block unconventional changes in or­
ganizations. Furthermore, it allows decision-makers to rely
on heuristics or inferences, which may impede women’s pres­
ence in TMTs. The competing hypothesis is presented below:
Hypothesis 3b: The further a municipality’s performance
falls below aspirations on one of the goals and exceeds
aspirations on the other, the lower the proportion of
women in its top management team.
While research on performance feedback and the glass cliff
phenomenon has been primarily based on the US or Western
contexts, the above theoretical mechanisms can be applied to
the Chinese context for several reasons. First, recent studies
report that Chinese local governments respond to perfor­
mance declines in order to improve future performance (see
Chen (2024); Wei et al. (2023)). The connection between per­
formance and leaders’ promotion or mobility has long been
established (Li and Zhou 2005; Tang, Jiang, and Mi 2021). It
implies that performance feedback is likely to influence per­
sonnel decisions regarding TMT composition.
Second, prior research suggests that women leaders tend to
be placed in less important functions in China, such as edu­
cation, culture, and healthcare (Lee Cooke 2003). Therefore,
women’s appointments to top management have symbolic
implications. A recent study found that top female leaders play
a positive role in performance within public services (Wang,
Ma, and Christensen 2024). It indicates that women leaders
hold values beyond symbolic roles in Chinese local govern­
ment. Additionally, economic and environmental objectives
are both dominant targets pursued by local governments (as
described in the next section) (Chen and Jia 2023; Zhang
2021). Organizations are, therefore, likely to experience un­
ambiguous and ambiguous performance feedback in this
context.
Overall, China presents an excellent context to examine
whether performance feedback on multiple conflicting
objectives affects gender compositions in TMTs. In the

Research Setting
China has four tiers of local government below the central
government level, including the provincial, municipal, county,
and township levels. Municipal government is the interest of
this study. The country is known as fragmented authoritar­
ianism, characterized by political power centralization and
administrative responsibility decentralization. Administrative
top managers in Chinese municipalities (e.g., mayors and
vice mayors) are often set and assessed by upper-level leaders
within the CCP. Specifically, the provincial Party Committee,
led by a party secretary, the governor-cum-vice party secretary,
decides the appointment, promotion, transfer, and removal of
the mayoral team in a municipality. Therefore, leaders in the
upper-level government, rather than voters, control admin­
istrative leaders’ personnel management. At the same time,
public services and policies are delivered by local-level actors.
Local cadres lead the government to pursue tasks set top-
down, which affects their subsequent career development.
Performance has been an essential criterion in personnel
management in the contemporary political system in China.
Under Mao’s leadership, the assessment of local leaders relies
on the subjective evaluation of political loyalty to the CCP.
Since the post-Mao reform and opening-up policy in 1978, a
target responsibility system was implemented to assign targets
and achieve top-down controls. Together, a cadre evaluation
system has been introduced, within which the performance
of a municipality in achieving top-down targets has been
tied to the promotion of administrative leaders. It indicates
a transformation of performance evaluations from subjec­
tive political loyalty to objectively quantifiable indicators of
work achievements (Zuo 2015). Research has shown that
underperforming governments experience increased visibility
and oversight from higher-level governments and citizens
(Wei et al. 2023). Leaders in such governments often have
a lower probability of promotion and a higher likelihood of
termination (Li and Zhou 2005; Tang, Jiang, and Mi 2021).
Among multiple goals, economic development has been
regarded as a dominant target of local governments since
the national economic transition enacted in 1978. Local
governments often set specific economic objectives inde­
pendently based on their own ambitions and peer pressure
(Zhang 2021). There is intense competition in economic
growth among local governments—a phenomenon known
as the “Promotion Tournament,” as economic performance is
vital for the career advancement of local leaders (Li and Zhou
2005). In this context, relative performance among peers is
an important reference in deciding the appointment and pro­
motion of administrative leaders. However, China’s economic
progress was not achieved without cost. Intense economic ac­
tivities have led to severe environmental pollution, which has
raised concerns at the central government level and beyond
China.
To prevent further environmental degradation, the cen­
tral government started to depart from the growth-at-all-
cost incentive structure to pursue more inclusive targets by
incorporating environmental and other social goals (Chen and
Jia 2023). Obligatory targets on environmental protection
were formally introduced as part of the 11th Five-Year
Plan in 2006. Specifically, the central government allocates
sub-targets of environmental pollution reduction to local
governments and assesses the latter’s performance. Failing to
meet the obligatory targets can even negate the high perfor­
mance on economic objectives (Tang, Jiang, and Mi 2021).
Therefore, local leaders must work to reduce environmental
pollution to be eligible for promotion. Prior research shows
that economic and environmental performance have been key
factors in the appointment and mobility of local cadres since
the early 2000s (Wu and Cao 2021; Zuo 2015).
While economic and environmental goals have become im­
portant criteria in local leaders’ appointments, there are tricky
trade-off relationships between the two objectives for several
reasons. First, as a developing country, China is still on the left
side of the environmental Kuznets curve, denoting a negative
relationship between its economic growth and environmental
conservation. Zhang (2021) found that provinces assigned
higher pollution-reduction objectives by the central govern­
ment tend to reduce their expectations of economic growth.
In past decades, as economic growth was the only priority, it
did not lead to conflict between different objectives. However,
goal contestation becomes substantial when performance on
environmental objectives has been set as “core” criteria in the
cadre evaluation system.
Second, China’s economic and environmental objectives
compete indirectly regarding attention and resources. While
the environmental objectives are not set in rank order for
peer competition, environmental performance possesses a
one-veto veto power, which signals that the central govern­
ment has made environmental protection an unprecedented
priority. Local cadres are motivated to exceed the coercive
target and the performance of their peers in order to im­
press their higher-level evaluators (Chen and Jia 2023). A
promotion tournament has been found to extend to environ­
mental targets due to the top-down green transformation (Yin
and Wu 2022). As a result, environmental objectives possess
rank-order characteristics. Chen and Jia (2023) suggest that
an increase in the priority of environmental goals inevitably
leads to a decrease in the attention and resources of other
objectives. Indirect conflict between economic and environ­
mental goals, therefore, arises.
The role of economic and environmental performance in
the personnel management of local administrative leaders and
the trade-off relationship between the two objectives make
China an appealing context for this study.
Methods and data
Data
To empirically examine the impact of performance feedback
on women’s representation in TMTs, a unique dataset cov­
ering 276 Chinese municipal governments from 2010 to 2018
was analyzed (see the map in figure 3). The data come from
multiple sources. The lists of mayoral teams (including the
mayor and vice mayors) were primarily collected by hand
from the Yearbooks of China’s City. Several missing values
(97 municipalities) were gathered from provincial or mu­
nicipal Yearbooks. Data on the sex of the mayoral teams
were mainly collected from the China Yearbooks at different
levels, complemented by searching the Baidu Encyclopedia
and People’s Daily Online. The individual-level data were

Measurement
Dependent variable.
The dependent variable used in this study is the proportion
of women in administrative mayoral teams. A mayoral team
consists of a mayor and several vice mayors in charge of local
economic development, environmental protection, and service
provision. The mayor is the chief administrative officer who
oversees the overall work of the government. Vice mayors
(one level lower than the mayor) often work as the heads of
different functional departments. The team size varies from 5
to 18, with an average of 8.8.
The proportion of women in each TMT was obtained
by dividing the number of women by the total team size. I
note that most observations experienced positive or negative
changes in the gender composition of TMT, though 21 per­
cent had no changes (see more details in table S1). Table S2
lists example municipalities with their year-by-year propor­
tion of women in TMTs. Overall, the gender composition in
TMTs undergoes notable changes during the study period.
Independent variables.
The independent variables of this study are performance rel­
ative to aspirations. This study focuses on the performance of
economic growth and environmental pollution reduction in
Chinese municipalities. Economic performance is measured
by GDP growth rate. Environmental performance is meas­
ured by emissions of Sulfur Dioxide (SO2), which has been
widely used in existing research to indicate air pollution (e.g.,
Tang, Jiang, and Mi 2021; Wu and Cao 2021). SO2 emission
is reverse coded to allow a higher value representing better
environmental performance.
The relative importance of different aspirations within
public organizations depends on the type of performance
management system implemented (Chen 2024; Hong 2019).
In China, a promotion tournament system has been estab­
lished, where local leaders compete with peers in the same
province to get a higher rank in performance assessment
and thus gain limited promotion opportunities (Li and Zhou
2005). Extensive research documents how organizational per­
formance relative to peers acts as an important reference in the
promotion and actions of local leaders (e.g., Tang, Jiang, and
Mi 2021; Wei et al. 2023). Social aspirations, therefore, are
considered much more influential than historical aspirations
in Chinese local governments (Chen 2024). Following pre­
vious research, this study focuses on social aspirations.
The social aspiration level is based on the average per­
formance of municipalities within the same provinces.
Performance relative to social aspirations is constructed as
(Pm,t − Pa,t) /Pa,t, where Pa,t is the average performance in a
province at time t. A spline function was implemented to com­
pare groups exceeding and falling below social aspirations
(Baum et al. 2005). Each relative performance was split into
two variables. Performance above social aspiration equals zero
for all negative values and equals relative performance oth­
erwise. Symmetrically, performance below social aspiration
equals zero for all positive values and equals relative perfor­
mance otherwise. Following previous research (Baum et al.
2005), I reverse coded the variable of performance below so­
cial aspiration, making its values positive to ease the inter­
pretation. A 1-year time lag was applied to all independent
variables to reflect causality better. The data suggests that the
performance of approximately 21 percent of observations
fall below social aspirations for economic and environmental
targets, 35 percent exceed aspirations on both targets, and
44 percent receive ambiguous performance feedback (18 per­
cent with positive economic performance and negative envi­
ronmental performance; 26 percent with negative economic
performance and positive environmental performance).
Control variables.
Women’s representation in TMTs is likely to be shaped by
a series of factors. Several control variables were included
in the analysis. Prior studies suggest that gender representa­
tion among decision-makers may reduce gender segregation
and inequality in subordinates (Arvate, Galilea, and Todescat
2018). A dummy variable indicating the gender of provincial
top leaders (including party and government chiefs) was in­
cluded in the analysis (female = 1, male = 0). It is likely that
municipalities with well-educated citizens may have more
women at different levels of government. To take this into
account, the number of students enrolled in higher edu­
cation was added as a control variable. To account for the
differences in larger and smaller governments, population size
representing the number of households within each registered
population was included. In addition, the economic structure
of a municipality—that is, the proportion of the secondary
industry—was also controlled. All models include municipal
and year dummies to account for fixed effects.
Estimation
To account for the bounded nature of the dependent variable,
fractional logit regression was used in estimation (Villadsen
and Wulff 2019).1 The variance inflation factors (VIFs) were
calculated to detect multicollinearity. The mean VIF is 1.48,
with the highest values at 1.81. Therefore, multicollinearity is
not a serious concern.

Discussion and Conclusion
Women face a variety of barriers in their career advance­
ment. This paper explores whether performance feedback
on multiple conflicting goals affects gender representation
in TMTs in the public sector. Results from 276 Chinese
municipalities from 2010 to 2018 suggest that a unidimen­
sional approach to assessing the role of performance feed­
back provides mixed findings. While economic performance
below social aspiration is positively associated with women’s
proportion in TMTs, environmental performance shortfall

Looking at performance feedback in a dual-goal scenario,
this study distinguishes between unambiguous and ambig­
uous feedback depending on whether performance results
can clearly signal success or failure. Findings show that
municipalities experiencing greater economic and environ­
mental performance declines than peers are likely to have a
higher proportion of women in TMTs. It implies that women
tend to be placed in top management positions in times of
unambiguous failure. The current study responds to a recent
call to adopt a multigoal view in PA research that considers
the interplay of multiple goals beyond a sole-goal perspective
(Chen and Jia 2023).
One might argue that women are afforded opportunities to
enter TMTs in circumstances of unambiguous failure, which
they might otherwise struggle to attain. It allows women
to secure a position in male-dominated TMTs, thereby
enhancing the passive gender representation. However, from
a longer-term perspective, such opportunities can harm
women’s careers. For one thing, these positions tend to be
highly visible and subject to heightened scrutiny. It allows
stakeholders to associate performance declines with women’s
leadership, perpetuating gender stereotypes (Ryan et al.
2016). For the other, recent research suggests that the risk of
failure in such positions is likely high for women but not men
(Yang, Connolly, and Connolly 2022). Consequently, a track
record of underperformance may hinder future promotional
prospects for women, exacerbating existing gender disparities
in leadership. In short, the glass cliff created by unambiguous
failure presents challenges for women leaders’ career devel­
opment. This study adds to existing literature by highlighting
the additional barriers women encounter in advancing to
top management (Bishu, Sabharwal, and Reyes 2023; Guy,
Newman, and Mastracci 2008; Meier, Mastracci, and Wilson
2006; Newman 1993; Stivers 2002).
A second finding is that women’s presence in TMTs
seems to be impeded in another complex situation: ambig­
uous performance feedback. Results suggest that the fur­
ther a municipality outperforms its economic target while
underperforming on its environmental target, the smaller the
proportion of women in its TMT. It implies that ambiguous
feedback limits unconventional change in the gender compo­
sition of TMTs, which lends support to research in general
management, claiming that ambiguous performance feedback
impedes problem-driven searches (Joseph and Gaba 2015)
and counters previous research on general negativity bias in
the public sector (Hong 2019).
There may be several reasons to explain the insignificant
results for the interaction between economic performance
below social aspirations and environmental performance ex­
ceeding social aspirations. First, environmental performance
possesses veto power in China, which has a threshold effect on
the role of economic performance in local leaders’ promotion
(Tang, Jiang, and Mi 2021). Environmental performance that
exceeds social aspirations may create a slightly safer situa­
tion for a municipality, though behind peers on the economic
target. Therefore, a current TMT may have the opportunity
to remedy its economic performance. This is reflected in table
2, where the impact of environmental performance above the
average is independent of economic performance declines.
Second, there are rather limited observations with economic
performance falling far behind social aspirations (based on
additional analysis). The interaction effect is significant when
economic and environmental performance are near social
aspirations, but the confidence interval becomes substantial
when moving far away from their aspirations.
Third, looking more broadly, Western literature suggests
that economic performance has been widely regarded as a
masculine policy area, while environmental performance
is considered more feminine (Smith and Monaghan 2013).
Municipalities with negative economic performance and
positive environmental performance may favor male leaders
perceived as more suited for masculine tasks over females.
Where women are expected to receive more seats in the op­
posite situation (i.e., economic performance higher than peer
average and environmental performance lower than peer av­
erage), the actual proportion of women decreases. It further
indicates the inequality women experience under ambiguous
performance feedback, and that gendered policy areas may
play a role in times of ambiguity. I call for future research to
develop a nuanced understanding of performance feedback
that differentiates different policy areas and different degrees
of success and failure.
Taking the previous results together, women experience
massive challenges in leadership positions in times of un­
ambiguous failure and encounter barriers in times of per­
formance ambiguity. It substantiates the existence of gender
discrimination in a variety of organizational situations (Ryan
et al. 2011). There are opportunities to understand further
whether performance feedback of multiple conflicting goals
is related to other types of diversity, such as ethnicity, age,
and tenure.
This research makes four notable contributions. First, it
contributes to the glass cliff research by investigating what
constitutes the glass cliff in the presence of multiple conflicting
goals. Research on the glass cliff in the public sector is sparse
and has generated mixed findings (Groeneveld, Bakker, and
Schmidt 2020; Yang, Connolly, and Connolly 2022). Public
organizations often work on a multiplicity of objectives.
Therefore, the assessment of organizational status may be
much more complex than the prior solo-goal approach. By
developing novel theoretical insights on the role of perfor­
mance feedback on multiple conflicting objectives, this study
explains past mixed findings. It shows that underperforming
among peers on multiple conflicting goals is positively related
to the proportion of women in the upper echelons. However,
performance falling short of one objective while succeeding
on its conflicting target attenuates this tendency.
Second, this study contributes to gender diversity and rep­
resentation research by taking performance relative to peer
organizations as an antecedent. Extensive studies have pre­
dominantly examined the impact of gender representation in
TMTs but rarely take gender representation or gender diver­
sity as a dependent variable (Huang and Villadsen 2023). A
small amount of research has examined the role of perfor­
mance decline (Ryan and Haslam 2005), fiscal stress (Yang,
Connolly, and Connolly 2022), and organizational growth
(Krøtel, Ashworth, and Villadsen 2019) by comparing an
organization’s current situation with its own historical per­
formance. This research advances this embryonic stream
of research by indicating how performance relative to so­
cial aspirations can affect gender representation in TMTs

and generate stronger effects than historical aspirations in
a “tournament” political system (Li and Zhou 2005; Tang,
Jiang, and Mi 2021).
Third, this research extends the role of performance feed­
back to the gender composition of TMTs. Prior studies have
primarily examined the impact of performance feedback on
various organizational and managerial outcomes, including
strategic changes (Greve 2003), managerial risk-taking
(Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty, and Fernandez 2017),
prioritization of managerial goals (Holm 2018; Nielsen 2014)
and the selection of charisma leaders (Jacquart and Antonakis
2015). The current study suggests that performance feedback
on multiple conflicting goals creates ambiguity and com­
plexity in organizational responses, and it can shape gender
composition above the glass ceiling.
Fourth, while a general theory was outlined, it was tested
in the Chinese context, which is not often employed for repre­
sentation research. Using a context with salient goal conflicts,
this study provides a more nuanced understanding of what
constitutes a crisis for public organizations that pursue mul­
tiple conflicting goals (Groeneveld, Bakker, and Schmidt 2020;
Yang, Connolly, and Connolly 2022). These results show that
the glass cliff appears when performance on multiple con­
flicting objectives jointly falls short of social aspirations. It
adds evidence to the glass cliff literature from the Chinese con­
text (Ryan and Haslam 2005; Yang, Connolly, and Connolly
2022). Furthermore, this study extends existing research by
taking social aspiration as a reference in the specific context.
The additional tests suggest that social aspirations are more
salient than historical aspirations in China’s “tournament”
political system, indicating how existing theories need adap­
tation by taking the local context into consideration. Since
there is a common tendency for economic advancement and
environmental pollution reductions in China, performance
relative to peer organizations may offer more comparable in­
formation on how well a municipality is operating.
There are several practical implications. First, decision-
makers, whether those in higher-level government in China
or elected politicians in Western societies, must recognize
the intricate nature of public sector performance feedback,
particularly in light of multiple conflicting objectives. This
complexity is particularly pronounced within public organ­
izations, where conflicting objectives are frequently estab­
lished amid resource constraints and competing priorities
(Chen and Jia 2023). Compared to performance feedback
based on single objectives, learning from performance on
multiple conflicting objectives introduces ambiguity and com­
plexity in the appointment decisions of top managers, which
are likely to impact various public sector outcomes (Huang
and Villadsen 2023).
Second, decision-makers must discern the fragile passive
gender representation when women are included in TMTs
due to unambiguous failure. Women are more likely than men
to leave such positions if organizational performance fails to
improve (Yang, Connolly, and Connolly 2022). It is particu­
larly relevant to the Chinese government, as gender disparity
is prominent in leadership roles. To reap the policy benefits of
women’s presence in governmental organizations (Wang, Ma,
and Christensen 2024), higher-level decision-makers could
try to avoid sending disproportionately more women to such
risky positions or provide additional support for women who
are competent in handling such risks.
Third, research shows that individuals tend to think-
manager-think-male when organizations experience success
(Ryan et al. 2011; Schein et al. 1996). This study further
suggests that such cognitive bias may be present when re­
ceiving ambiguous performance feedback. These findings un­
derscore the additional challenges public sector organizations
encounter in ensuring equitable selection of top managers.
This study has several limitations that deserve further ex­
ploration. First, I only tested how performance feedback of
multiple-goal conflicts creates ambiguity and did not consider
ambiguity from other sources. A recent study suggests that
there are two types of ambiguity, including objective ambi­
guity (e.g., goal clarity, priority goal ambiguity) and subjective
ambiguity (e.g., role ambiguity, mission comprehension am­
biguity) (Oppi, Campanale, and Cinquini 2022). Ambiguity
may also arise when different aspirations are referenced
(Baum et al. 2005). There is potential to examine whether
ambiguous feedback from other sources affects gender com­
position in TMTs.
Second, while municipal-fixed effects were included to
account for time-invariant factors, there may be omitted
variables that have not been included. For example, a crisis can
arise due to environmental turbulence that is dynamic, risky,
and stressful for an organization (Jacquart and Antonakis
2015). Future research could examine how broad environ­
mental and institutional factors affect gender composition in
TMTs. Furthermore, while performance at the organizational
level was considered in the current study, the mechanisms
could be advanced by considering additional individual- and
team-level factors. Examples of factors could be the past per­
formance of newly designated TMT members, external or
internal sources of succession, and leadership style configu­
ration of TMT members. It could be inspired by advances in
psychology in applying natural language processing to trans­
late work history or large amounts of text data into measur­
able outcomes (e.g., Sajjadiani et al. 2019). Relatedly, future
research could explore nuances within TMTs regarding the
glass cliff phenomenon. For example, are top executives, who
are in more conspicuous roles, more likely than other TMT
members to experience the glass cliff?
Third, the present study develops its theoretical arguments
mainly from the decision-makers’ point of view. Future studies
could consider the applicants’ perspective by exploring, for
instance, why female applicants accept precarious leadership
positions, to what extent women thrive in these positions,
how performance feedback at the micro-individual level may
either increase or reduce inequality in top management (see
Bear et al. (2017) as an example), and whether men are more
likely to emphasize their achievements while minimizing
their setbacks than women in times of ambiguity. Another
promising direction is to examine post-appointment influ­
ence when women have been placed in top management
positions. It is not limited to testing whether TMT gender
diversity can have a notable impact on the improvement of
performance on multiple conflicting objectives, and how
contextual factors may play a role in such a process (Wang,
Ma, and Christensen 2024).
Besides, this study simplifies the theoretical mechanisms
of performance feedback in a dual-goal scenario in China.
Future research could test whether the findings apply when
there is a multiplicity of conflicting tasks in public organiza­
tions or contexts beyond China.

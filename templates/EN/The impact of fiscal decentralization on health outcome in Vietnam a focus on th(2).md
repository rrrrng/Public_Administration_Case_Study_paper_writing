ABSTRACT
This study examines the applicability of fiscal decentralization the­
ory, originally developed in Western countries, to Vietnam at the
provincial level. The aim is to explore the indirect impact of public
service delivery on the relationship between fiscal decentralization
and health outcomes, with corruption as a moderator. The study
employs the conditional process analysis. The findings reveal that
the multi-dimensional nature of fiscal decentralization results in
different statistically significant effects on health outcomes. While
fiscal autonomy does not guarantee an impact on health outcomes,
revenue-fiscal decentralization positively improves health out­
comes. These effects are intensified by more significant control
over corruption, suggesting that limiting corruption is crucial to
ensuring the benefits of fiscal decentralization. Moreover, our study
shows that public service delivery does not mediate the effect of
fiscal decentralization on health outcomes. Overall, our findings
suggest that applying Western theories of fiscal decentralization
to the context of Vietnam is challenging. The research recommends
reforming the fiscal transfer mechanism and strengthening control
over corruption in public services.

1. Introduction
In the 2000s, globalization efforts led by Western countries brought about a widespread wave
of decentralization globally (Shah & Thompson, 2004). Scholars from the West introduced the
concept of decentralization as a means to balance power between central and local govern­
ments, enhance local governance, and promote development. Among the different dimen­
sions of decentralization, fiscal decentralization focuses on collecting and distributing revenue
and expenditure between different levels of government (Asfaw et al., 2004; Fritzen, 2006).
Vietnam actively embraced decentralization during the 2000s as a means to achieve
economic growth and global integration. While political decentralization remained a lower
priority, the country made gradual progress in implementing fiscal and administrative decen­
tralization. Furthermore, the traditional practice of formulating policies based on five-year
plans, aligned with the five-year terms of the government, could potentially impact the

2. Literature reviews
2.1. Decentralized theory and the mediating role of public service delivery
Theories on decentralization have proposed both positive and negative effects on the
delivery of healthcare services and ultimately affect health outcomes. Decentralization
supporters argued that the primary rationale behind implementing a fiscal decentralization
system is to diminish central regulations while enhancing local accessibility to improved
conditions (Oates, 1999; Stiglitz, 1995). Second, decentralization transfers responsibility and
accountability to local authorities, compelling them to utilize all accessible resources to
achieve their objectives, consequently enhancing their efficiency, adaptability, and innova­
tion (Keen & Marchand, 1997). Third, the participation of citizens in the decision-making
process is another consequence of decentralization, which collects community knowledge
and improves the quality of public service provision (Weingast, 2009).
However, decentralization sceptics pointed out several drawbacks of fiscal decentrali­
zation on public service delivery. Morgan and England (1984) and Oates (1999) discussed
that decentralization fragments government responsibility, scatters the resources, and
creates more constraints between different layers of government, resulting in lower
public sector efficiency. The second major argument against decentralization is diseco­
nomies of scale, which means the benefit from decentralization may be reversed when
the scale of sub-national governments becomes smaller and reduces efficiency in public
service delivery (Huther & Shah, 1998; Oates, 1999). Finally, compared to the central
government, sub-national governments often face difficulties in the administrative capa­
city and compromise the efficiency of service delivery (Prud’homme, 1995).
In summary, while decentralization theorists continue to debate the direction of the
impact of decentralization activities, there is a consensus on the underlying impact
mechanism. Essentially, decentralization is poised to influence health outcomes through
two distinct pathways: the first being a direct route and the second operating indirectly
through the delivery of public services.

2.2. The moderating role of corruption
Previous literature shows that the complex nature of fiscal decentralization involving
various interactions requires analysing moderating effects (Huynh & Tran, 2021; Thanh &
Canh, 2020). Among the multiple factors, control of corruption was considered
a significant moderator, which is a significant challenge for developing countries
(Mabeba, 2021; Thanh & Canh, 2020). In particular, the healthcare sector is at a high risk
of corruption (Nguyen et al., 2017). While decentralization can have both positive and
negative consequences for corruption, it is generally perceived as a catalyst for corruption
within the public sector (Martínez‐Vázquez et al., 2017).
Thus, several studies highlighted that corruption moderates the relationship between
fiscal decentralization and health outcomes. Sow and Razafimahefa (2015) noted that
there needs to be better control of corruption and strong accountability to improve
public service delivery and health outcomes. Likewise, Nakatani et al. (2022) argued that
countries need to control corruption and improve government effectiveness to create the
precondition for decentralization benefits health outcomes.
Previous studies have shown that the primary mechanism for fiscal decentralization to
influence health outcomes comes from direct and indirect effects through the provision of
public services. Contextual and institutional factors, such as development, local socio­
economic, and local governance, also influence this mechanism. However, how things
actually work in Vietnam is still in the black box with little empirical research. Furthermore,
testing the applicability of the Western theory of fiscal decentralization is essential
considering the context of Vietnam. Thus, this study further attempts to decipher the
black box of the relationship between fiscal decentralization and health outcomes con­
sidering the role of public service delivery and control of corruption.
2.3. Empirical evidence
Scholars attempted to investigate the relationship between fiscal decentralization and health
outcomes in different contexts with varied findings (Appendix II), but mainly support the
positive effects of fiscal decentralization. Research conducted in developed nations consis­
tently revealed a typical pattern when fiscal decentralization positively impacts health out­
comes (Cantarero & Pascual, 2008; Cavalieri & Ferrante, 2016; Rubio, 2011). Studies of
developing countries, including Argentina, India, Nigeria, and Colombia, suggest a positive
association between fiscal decentralization and health outcomes but emphasize the critical
role of context in strengthening or weakening the relationship (Akpan, 2011; Asfaw et al.,
2007; Habibi et al., 2003; Soto et al., 2012). Moreover, the significance of context is under­
scored in cross-nation studies, where fiscal decentralization’s positive impact on health out­
comes is contingent upon certain governance conditions (Nakatani et al., 2022), levels of
financial autonomy (Jiménez-Rubio, 2011), or strong political autonomy (Robalino et al., 2001).
China was a unique case of fiscal decentralization within a high political centralization
structure. China’s unique state model led to contrasting results with other studies,
whereby the major study flow is that fiscal decentralization in China negatively affects
health outcomes (Hao et al., 2021; Jin & Sun, 2011). Even when a positive impact is
observed, the prerequisites for the success of a fiscal decentralized system in China
include the establishment of intergovernmental transfers and the enhancement of county

beyond a country’s healthcare system. Furthermore, it did not concern mediating and
moderating effects. Despite Bui et al. (2023), there is still insufficient empirical evidence
regarding the impact of fiscal decentralization in Vietnam.
3. Research method
3.1. Data and research model
This study examines the direct and indirect impact of fiscal decentralization on health
outcomes through public service delivery in Vietnam and tests whether control of
corruption has a moderating effect. The provincial-level data was collected from 2017
to 2022. The research model is presented in Figure 1. The dependent variable is the infant
mortality rate (t, t + 1) to consider that policy effects need time to become apparent.
A conditional direct and indirect effect model was constructed to quantify the effect at
a different level of control of corruption. The empirical analysis used the process macro for
R and model 59, as suggested by Hayes (2018). The indirect effect was estimated using
a bootstrapping method and the conditional effect was estimated at different values
(mean −1 SD, mean, mean + 1 SD). Considering the characteristics of panel data, we
employed a one-way fixed effect model by including year dummy variables as covariates
and the HC3 variance-covariance matrix to estimate robust standard errors.1

3.2. Variables
Considering the complexity of health factors, the study used three different indicators to
measure health outcomes, including infant mortality rate (IMR), under-five mortality rate
(UMR), and life expectancy (LIFE). Despite debates about the statistical accuracy of IMR in
developing or middle-income countries or the possibility of data manipulation, IMR is still
a valuable indicator because it reflects both child and pregnant women’s health and is
sensitive to policy changes (e.g. decentralization) (Jimenez-Asfaw et al., 2007; Rubio,
2011). Besides, LIFE and UMR are used as comparison indicators with IMR in the robust­
ness check.
The choice of the measure of fiscal decentralization is likely to affect the study findings
critically. Many studies only focus on one dimension, however, it is essential to consider
both the revenue dimension and the expenditure dimension of fiscal decentralization
(Asfaw et al., 2007; Bui et al., 2023; Cavalieri & Ferrante, 2016). For this reason, the study
adopted two separate indicators based on the fiscal data from the fiscal transparency
portal of Vietnam (Appendix III). The first indicator, revenue decentralization FDre, is
calculated by the ratio of local own-source revenue to total local revenue (Cavalieri &
Ferrante, 2016). The selection of FDre is driven by the recognition that the structure and
composition of local revenue play a critical role when evaluating the potential expansion
or reduction of local public services.
The second indicator, fiscal autonomy (FDau), is the ratio of locally generated revenue
to the total local expenditure (Cavalieri & Ferrante, 2016). FDau serves as a measure of the
extent to which local governments possess autonomy in managing their expenditures.
When fiscal autonomy is higher, it signifies that local governments have more self-
determination in budget allocation, resulting in increased accountability to local voters
and an overall enhancement in the quality of public services. Conversely, a low level of
financial autonomy, characterized by heavy reliance on central government budget
transfers, can lead to reduced accountability to local citizens and a higher susceptibility
to engaging in rent-seeking behaviours. Utilizing FDau enables an assessment of the local
government’s capacity to be proactive, adaptable, and responsible in budget utilization.
The study uses the dimension ‘Public service delivery’ (PSD) of PAPI as a mediator to
represent public service delivery in the research model. Unlike objective indicators such as
the number of hospital beds or doctors per capita, the PSD indicator captures subjective
perspectives based on a survey of service users. It could reflect unobserved factors that
may influence outcomes. Besides, the sub-dimension ‘Limits on corruption in service
delivery’ (COR) is used as a moderator to represent corruption. As it contains perspectives
about ‘no bribes at public hospitals’ and ‘no bribes for teachers’ favouritism, it reflects the
grey corruption of healthcare in Vietnam.
Both medical and non-medical characteristics of each province were included as
control variables. The number of doctors per 1,000 people (DOC) and the number of
patient beds per 1,000 people (BED) were used as a proxy for the level of the current
health system. Income level is measured by the monthly average income per capita (INC),
and the provincial population density (POP) is employed to reflect the demographic
factor. The variables and data sources in this research are described in Table 1.

The findings from Table 6 display that the coefficients of the variables in each model are
statistically significant and show the same meaning, even though the signs of coefficients
differ. In addition, the results are consistent regardless of whether dependent variables at
time t or time t + 1 are employed. In this regard, the explanation is centred around IMR(t + 1).
First of all, it reveals only the revenue dimension of fiscal decentralization (FDre) in Model
I exhibited a significant impact on health outcomes. This suggests that an increase in the
ratio of local own-source revenue to total revenue could improve health outcomes.
However, the mediating variable, public service delivery (PSD), was not statistically signifi­
cant in all models. On the contrary, limits on corruption in service delivery (COR) significantly
impacted health outcomes statistically. These results indicate that measures taken to curb
corruption in service delivery can contribute to reducing IMR.
To estimate the conditional direct effect and indirect effect, the analysis incorporated
interaction terms. Although only interaction terms between PSD and COR showed statistical
significance across all models, the statistical significance of the independent variable cannot
be determined solely based on the coefficient itself. The effects of moderation are condi­
tional estimates, necessitating the consideration of moderating variables when interpreting
the coefficients (Wooldridge, 2013). Thus, it is crucial to test the statistical significance of
fiscal decentralization in relation to the moderating variables.
Regarding control variables, the number of patient beds per 1,000 people (BED) demon­
strated a negative impact on the health outcomes. Conversely, the monthly average income
per capita (INC) and population density (POP) were found to have a reducing effect on the IMR.
Table 7 provides insights into the conditional direct effect and conditional indirect effect at
different levels of the moderating variable, specifically at the mean and + 1/-1 standard
deviation (SD) from the mean.2 In Model I, FDre exhibits statistically significant conditional
direct effects on the IMR when the moderating variable is at the mean and + 1SD from the
mean. This result indicates that a higher level of limits on corruption in service delivery
intensifies the positive impact of fiscal decentralization in reducing the IMR. However, Model
II indicates no statistically significant conditional direct effects depending on the value of the
moderating variable. Thus, the evidence only partially supports the hypothesis because the
revenue side of decentralization impacts health outcomes while fiscal autonomy does not.
Furthermore, the enhanced limits on corruption in service delivery also magnify the beneficial
impact of fiscal decentralization on improving life expectancy and under-five mortality rate.

does not consistently lead to improved health outcomes, but its impact depends on the
dimension of fiscal decentralization. Specifically, revenue decentralization showed an inverse
relationship with the IMR, supporting the theory that a higher percentage of local-owned
revenue corresponds to better health outcomes for the community. However, fiscal autonomy
did not demonstrate a significant impact. Consequently, the impact of fiscal decentralization in
Vietnam seems to primarily hinge on the revenue dimension. During this process, local
governments find themselves incentivized to augment local revenue for the purpose of
funding local initiatives. Conversely, local authorities prefer receiving financial transfers from
the central government, thereby lacking motivation to embrace financial autonomy. This
divergence in the impact of fiscal decentralization is consistent with studies conducted in
China, suggesting a different pattern in decentralization theory in countries with high levels of
political concentration.
Secondly, the absence of an indirect pathway linking fiscal decentralization to health
outcomes through the mediation of public services raises significant questions. This finding
challenges the assumption of the Western model, which suggests that fiscal decentraliza­
tion would enhance the quality of public service delivery, subsequently leading to an
improvement in health outcomes. One plausible explanation can be traced to the source
of local revenue generation in Vietnam and how the local government allocates funds for
healthcare services. Because taxes from real estate account for the majority of local-own
revenue, local governments tend to reinvest these resources into construction or real estate
projects, leading to a shortage of funds for public services. This issue is exacerbated in
smaller localities that lack corporate tax revenues to sponsor public service delivery.
Furthermore, along with the fiscal decentralization system, the central government evalu­
ates the local government’s performance through socioeconomic targets, such as GRDP
growth, budgetary expenditures and revenues, and poverty reduction (Thanh & Canh, 2020),
which is the cause of low priority given to local health issues. This mechanism effectively
utilizes local revenue for economic growth but does not motivate local leaders to invest in
improving public services such as health or education. Lastly, studies in China provide insights
into the absence of mediating impact from public service delivery in Vietnam, including local
government prioritizing economic growth, the medical cost burden of local government, and
low incentive for considering residents’ needs (Jin & Sun, 2011, Ko & Zhi, 2013; Uchimura &
Jütting, 2009).
Thirdly, the level of control over corruption exerts an influence on health outcomes,
specifically within the case of revenue dimension, but its impact is absent in the context of
fiscal autonomy. Moreover, the degree of corruption control plays a determining role in how
fiscal decentralization affects health outcomes. The results demonstrate that the impact of
revenue decentralization on IMR is more pronounced in provinces with higher levels of
corruption control (coeff=−.061) than provinces with the mean level of corruption control
(coeff=−.042). However, this observation disappears in the case of the provinces with low
levels of corruption control. Similar results were obtained where the proxy for health out­
comes is life expectancy or under-five mortality rate. The finding suggests that a certain
threshold of corruption control must be achieved before fiscal decentralization’s benefits on
health outcomes can be realized. These findings align with prior research conducted in
developing countries, emphasizing the necessity of meeting certain institutional conditions,
particularly addressing corruption, to magnify the positive impacts of decentralization on
health outcomes.

5.2. Practical implications
Regarding policy implications, findings outline that fiscal decentralization policy led to
ambiguous outcomes in Vietnam. The impact of fiscal decentralization on health out­
comes is limited and contingent upon specific conditions. In addition, the impacts of fiscal
decentralization on public service delivery and public health in Vietnam are inconsistent
and do not achieve the goal of improving national health.
Revenue decentralization positively impacts health outcomes, advocating for a policy
shift that encourages local governments to expand their scope of locally generated
revenue. The intergovernmental sharing arrangement could be the starting point of
a change. Allowing large provinces to have a higher revenue retention rate will incentivize
these localities to invest in public services. At the same time, increasing the responsibility
of small localities when requiring them to maintain a minimum percentage of contribu­
tions to the national budget will force them to use local budgets more efficiently.
The absence of fiscal autonomy in contributing to public health underscores the role of
fiscal transfers from the central government. This budget transfer mechanism diminishes the
fiscal autonomy of localities, leading to a heavy reliance on central support to provide public
services, which contradicts the benefits of fiscal decentralization. Therefore, a restructuring of
fiscal policies is needed to align the impacts of revenue decentralization and fiscal autonomy.
At the same time, a high level of corruption control is a precondition for a favourable
institutional environment in which the positive effects of fiscal decentralization can spill
over into public health.
5.3. Limitations and future research
This study is significant as it explores the applicability of fiscal decentralization theory to
Vietnam. However, there are some limitations. Firstly, the study period from 2017 to 2022
contains the COVID-19 pandemic. The overall trend of improving health outcomes continued,
and this study incorporated a year dummy variable to control for the COVID-19 shock.
However, an alternative model, such as a random coefficient model, could be considered to
address a challenge. Secondly, the analysis in this study focused on the IMR, the life expec­
tancy, and the mortality rate under five years despite being aware of the risk of under­
estimated data (Anthopolos & Becker, 2010). The use of other indicators, such as the outcomes
of secondary, tertiary, and quaternary care as health outcomes, would be meaningful, but the
limited availability of this data constrained its inclusion in the study. If data availability
increases, the generalizability of the results will not only be enhanced but it is also expected
that the feasibility of employing analytical methods such as the simultaneous equation
models will increase.
This study’s findings, similar to the China cases, suggest the necessity for additional
research that emphasizes the shared contextual factors between Vietnam and China. Such
research would serve to enrich our understanding of decentralization within the frame­
work of socialist nations characterized by a high degree of political centralization. Finally,
the uncertainty in the causal relationship between fiscal decentralization and health
outcomes suggests further research on how these two dimensions impact economic
and social factors.

6. Conclusion
This paper explores whether the theory of fiscal decentralization, initially developed in the
context of Western countries, can be applied to Vietnam. The findings indicate that
appropriate limits on corruption are essential for the positive impact of fiscal decentrali­
zation on health outcomes, but there is no connection between fiscal decentralization,
public service delivery, and health outcomes. Our research highlights the difficulty in
applying Western theories of fiscal decentralization to the context of Vietnam and the
need to reconstruct fiscal decentralization policies.

Appendix I. Fiscal decentralization in Vietnam: design and implementation
In Vietnam, fiscal decentralization began in 1989 with the Council of Ministers Resolution No. 186.
This effort was upgraded with the introduction of the first State Budget Law in 1996, which
regulated the rights and responsibilities of sub-national authorities regarding revenue budget
and expenditure budget. The State Budget Law was amended in 2002 and 2015 to strengthen
the expenditure side of decentralization.
In terms of revenue decentralization, the local government budget comes from three
sources: (i) 100% retained revenues assigned by the central government, (ii) shared tax
between local-central, and (iii) local borrowing. Among these three types, retained tax
revenues, which derive mainly from taxes and fees from land users, local fees, and other
charges, are the most important to local governments because they have complete discre­
tion. Besides, the tax-sharing arrangement was implemented for certain taxes, such as
corporate income tax (Table A1). Moreover, to finance the central budget and redistribute
resources nationally, the central government collects a certain proportion of the local-owned
revenue of the 16 major provinces. These intergovernmental-sharing arrangements will be
revised after 3-5 years, while the remaining 47 provinces with budget deficits are not
obligated to implement this agreement (Table A2).

Appendix III. Data collection and Calculation of financial indicators
1. Reason for the data collection period
The 5-year study period was chosen due to the socialist context in Vietnam. Firstly, the State Budget
Law amendment in 2017 led to a reform of the budget reporting system. This new system requires
extensive input of budget data and incorporates innovative rules for data calculation. Consequently,
utilizing data from both pre-2017 and post-2017 periods can lead to inconsistencies. Additionally,
selecting the 2017–2021 period aligns with the government’s policies and plans for the 2016–2021
term in Vietnam. The country has a tradition of implementing five-year plans corresponding to
a typical government term. It is essential to highlight that studying longer time spans could
complicate policy impact analysis across different government terms. Therefore, limiting the
study period to five years ensures a more manageable and meaningful analysis of the policy’s
impact.
2. Sources of data
2.1 Financial data
Financial data for this study is sourced from the Ministry of Finance via the Fiscal Transparency
Portal of Vietnam, accessible at https://ckns.mof.gov.vn/eng/SitePages/Home.aspx. In instances
turn to an alternative source, which is the local fiscal transparency report. In accordance with
the 2017 budget law, localities are mandated to conduct annual fiscal transparency reports
and make them publicly accessible on the homepage of the provincial finance department. It
is worth noting that the data from these local fiscal transparency reports also contributes to
the national fiscal transparency report, which is compiled and made available through the
fiscal transparency portal of Vietnam. In other words, while our primary data collection
method relies on the national fiscal transparency portal for its convenience, we adopt
a contingency plan in case of missing or inaccessible data. In such situations, we turn to
the primary sources provided by local fiscal transparency reports to ensure comprehensive
and accurate data collection.
The paper utilizes data derived from budget final accounts reports rather than budget plan
reports. This approach enables us to work with actual local budgets year after year, enhancing the
reliability of our findings. The key fiscal indicators used in the study include:
(1) Total local revenue (collected from form B62-TT343): This figure represents the comprehensive
sum of income generated at the local level (Including three sources of revenue: (i) 100% of
retained revenues assigned by the central government, (ii) shared tax revenue between the local
and central governments, and (iii) local borrowing).
(2) Total local-own revenue (collected from form B62-TT343): This metric reflects revenue sources
that belong solely to the local government, excluding shared tax revenues with the central
government and any budget transfers from the centre.
(3) Total local expenditure (collected from form B63-TT343): Actual annual local government
budget expenditure.
2.2. Public service and corruption data
Public service and corruption data were collected from The Viet Nam Provincial Governance and
Public Administration Performance Index (PAPI). PAPI, launched by the United Nations
Development Program in Vietnam in 2009, is a prominent governance initiative. Its primary
objective is to assess and set standards for how citizens perceive and experience the effectiveness

and quality of policy execution and service delivery by all 63 provincial administrations in Vietnam.
PAPI combines eight dimensions, 29 sub‐dimensions, and more than 120 indicators.
In this research, the dimension known as ‘Public service delivery’ (abbreviated as PSD) within the
PAPI framework is employed as a moderator to represent the variable of public service delivery
within the model. PSD encompasses various dimensions, such as education, infrastructure, law and
order, and health. Unlike objective indicators such as the number of hospital beds or doctors per
capita, the PSD indicator captures subjective perspectives based on a survey of service users. It
could reflect unobserved factors that may influence outcomes. Specifically, recognizing the sig­
nificant role that all aspects of public service delivery play in reducing IMR, we specifically employ
the PSD rather than solely focusing on the health sub-dimensions.
‘Limits on corruption in service delivery’ (COR), one sub-dimension of controlling corruption in
the public sector in the PAPI index, is used as a moderator to proxy corruption. As it contains
perspectives about ‘no bribes at public hospitals’ and ‘no bribes for teachers’ favouritism, it reflects
the grey corruption of healthcare in Vietnam.
2.3. Health and demographic data
The annual National Statistical Yearbook, released by the General Statistics Office of Vietnam, is
a data source for health and demographic factors. Using only one data source ensures consistency
and reliability of the data source. The definitions of the specific elements are as follows:
- Infant mortality rate (IMR): The number of children under one year of age per 1,000 live births in
the reference period on average
- Life expectancy (LIFE): the prospect that a newborn could live for how many years
- Under-five mortality rate (UMR): The number of deaths of children under age 5 per 1,000 live
births in the reference period on average.
- Number of Doctors (DOC): Number of doctors, masters, doctorates, professors, associate
professors with professional qualifications in medicine and holding a doctor’s degree or higher,
currently working in the health sector
- Number of patient beds (BED): Number of beds used for patients at the healthcare
establishments
− Annual average income per capita is calculated by dividing the total income of households in
a reference year by their headcounts in one year.
- Population density (POP): The average population per square kilometre of the territory
3. Fiscal indicator calculating
- Total local revenue: collected from form B62-TT343
- Total local-own revenue: collected from form B62-TT343
- Total local expenditure: collected from form B63-TT343
- The local budget deficit/surplus
= Total local-own revenue – Total local expenditure
- Revenue decentralization (FDre): The ratio of local -own revenue to total local revenue (Cavalieri
& Ferrante, 2016)
Formula: FDre = (Total local-own revenue/Total local revenue) × 100
- Fiscal autonomy (FDau): The ratio of local-own revenue to total local expenditure. Fiscal
autonomy is the ability to finance local expenditures without using budget transfers from the
central government. The formula below shows that FDau has a similar meaning as the vertical
fiscal imbalance (VFI) used by Cavalieri and Ferrante (2016), but is expressed in an opposite
form, focusing on local autonomy.
Formula: FDau = (Local-own revenue/Total local expenditure) × 100
= (Total local expenditure – Budget transfer)/Total local expenditure × 100
= (1 - Budget transfer/Total local expenditure) × 100
= (1- VFI) × 100

ABSTRACT
Allan Rosenbaum was a dedicated public administration professor and administrator who made significant contributions to the
field over his six-­decade career. He held various academic and administrative positions and played leadership roles in profes-
sional associations, becoming a global champion of public administration. Allan was known for his passion, humanity, decency,
and friendship, which were evident in his professional contributions and the way he nurtured those who worked with him. In
this remembrance piece, we highlight Allan's impact on the field and many public administrators and scholars across the globe.
Professionally, Allan Rosenbaum was a public administra-
tion professor and itinerant administrator, with stints at the
University of Chicago, University of Connecticut, the US
Department of Education, the Maryland General Assembly, the
University of Maryland, and ultimately at Florida International
University. Over the course of a six-­decade-­long career, he also
served in leadership roles for professional associations, nota-
bly the American Society for Public Administration (ASPA),
the International Association of Schools and Institutes of
Administration (IASIA), and the International Commission on
Accreditation of Public Administration Education and Training
Programs. This brief biography is accurate but woefully in-
complete. Allan Rosenbaum was a global champion of pub-
lic administration, a field-­builder, and a teacher. Mentored by
public administration intellectual giants Theodore Lowi, Aaron
Wildavsky, and Robert Dahl, Allan became a lion in the field,
roaring against injustices and fighting for the honor and merit of
bureaucracy, public service, and democratic values. But he was
a gentle lion, friendly to all, a friend to many, and nurturing to
those who were blessed to have worked with him.
It is difficult to say goodbye to Allan at this moment in history.
Confidence in public institutions and public service is on the
decline around the globe (OECD  2024). Democracy and free-
dom are in retreat in many countries that have made tremen-
dous gains (Freedom House 2024). Public discourse has become
spiteful, divisive, and mean (Pew Research Center 2023). Allan
sought to build confidence in public administration, expand de-
mocracy, and promote civil discourse and debate. Charting his
life's work could be read as Don Quixote tilting at windmills
in vain.
Taking a step back, though, and reflecting on his efforts reveals
a deeper, more enduring impact. Allan built institutions, many
of which still stand. Allan connected and broadened networks,
many of which continue to expand. And Allan recruited, wel-
comed, and taught scores of academics and practitioners in the
field of public administration, many of whom have carried on
his spirit and teachings.
The five of us who contributed to this remembrance all knew
Allan in different capacities and at different phases of his pro-
fessional career. When invited to craft this essay, each of us was
eager to share our thoughts and the stories of others about Allan
and his influence. We benefited from talking to many of his for-
mer colleagues and students, some of whom are quoted in this

1   |   Global Champion of Public Administration,
Democratic Governance, and Academic Freedom
Allan was voluble and garrulous. He was an attentive listener,
but his energy sprung forth when given a stage, whether it be
in front of a professional audience, in a classroom with stu-
dents, or at a bar or social gathering with friends. He was not
afraid to speak his mind and his heart, both of which were
firmly aligned in support of the field of public administration,
the institutions and values of democracy and democratic gov-
ernance, and the principle and practice of academic freedom.
For many of us, as academics, our professional careers have
been sparked by ideas that wound us down a narrow path of
research and publication. For Allan, these sparks drove his cu-
riosity, but ultimately stirred a passionate fire that he wanted
to share with others in his native United States and around the
world. Allan was a globalist, aware of cultural differences, but
committed to spreading what in his mind were universal ideas
and principles. As Dr. Marcelo Giugale, former Director at the
World Bank, remembers,
Allan Rosenbaum was a true global academic. It
would be no exaggeration to say that he did not believe
in geographic, intellectual, or cultural frontiers. To
him, the world, in its many conceptions, was just one.
He traveled everywhere, all the time. How else can
one learn about others? How else can we know what
we do not know? And how else can one spread the
word about the beauty of democratic governance?
In a 2014 column he wrote as president of ASPA, Allan posed
a question that was a constant drumbeat in his scholarship, his
presentations, and his teaching: “Have Public Administrators
(and the discipline of Public Administration) failed our nation?”
(Rosenbaum 2014).
Allan's swift and resounding answer: “No!” Allan repeatedly
reminded the field and anyone who would listen that public
administrators serve and advance the interests of citizens in
democratic systems. From protecting the food and water we eat
and drink to ensuring our safety while traveling on roads, rails,
and airways to advancing the interests of our most vulnerable
citizens, the work of public administrators impacts each of us
every day.
However, Allan pointed out that many of us—practitioners and
academics alike—have sometimes failed our nations and dem-
ocratic governance. He admonished our collective hesitancy to
speak of the central importance of the field we serve. Allan went
beyond merely modeling what it meant to publicly champion the
virtues of the field by openly challenging us to speak more, to
write more, and to teach more about the power and centrality
of public administration, public service, and democracy. Many
of his writings are a call to action—to sustain democratic insti-
tutions, to push against the threats to democratic values, to dis-
pel the myth of public sector failure and incompetence, and to
counter public sector disillusionment. He recognized the threats
that each was facing and the vulnerability of democratic princi-
ples around the globe.
Allan's essay entitled, “Democratic Decline and Public
Administration: What is to be Done?” (2022) highlighted the
twin critical issues in democratic nations of the significant de-
cline over the past several decades in government resources and
capacity and, consequently, their ability to respond to the most
critical societal needs. He highlighted “the profoundly destabi-
lizing, worldwide growth in inequality [“a growing poisonous
reality”] which frequently underlies declining public confidence
in democratic governments.” Allan answers his own question
with a rhetorical question: “Is it really in the best long-­term
interests of any society that a profession whose members are
experts in matters concerning effective governance in far too
many instances have withdrawn from the policy making and
implementation process?” This is yet another call to action for
our field—to reengage in the policy fray by shaping public policy
and advocating for core principles.
An earlier example is Allan's article (Kolisnichenko and
Rosenbaum  2009) entitled, “Building a New Democracy in
Ukraine: The Unacknowledged Issue of Ethnic and Linguistic
Diversity in Public Administration Education and Training.”
Allan was keenly interested in the transition to democracy
around the world, especially in central and eastern Europe. In
this piece, he highlights the divisive, and politically fraught
“roles of the Ukrainian and Russian languages in the task of
nation-­state building.” He concludes with a call to develop cur-
ricula “designed to sensitize current and future civil servants to
the complexity of engaging in public management in a multieth-
nic and linguistically diverse community,” and for academics to
conduct research and write about issues of ethnic and linguistic
diversity. Similar themes are found in Allan's scholarship and
public commentary on democratization and government decen-
tralization in Latin America, Africa, and Asia.
Allan's passion for the field and for strengthening democracy
was matched by his advocacy for academic freedom. Parallel to
his concern for the erosion of democratic principles around the

saw a direct link between the strength of democratic institutions

In his final years, Allan railed against policies that circum-
scribed what could be taught in schools and universities and
cut away at the sanctity of academic freedom in his home state
of Florida. In public speeches and written commentary, he
objected to a variety of laws passed by the legislature and the
governor that limited the teaching of certain topics, eliminated
public funding for diversity, equity, and inclusion measures, and
restricted tenure for faculty members. In these bills and others,
Allan feared that the country was following the path of nations
that have “limited academic autonomy, weakened freedom of ex-
pression, and harmed democracy itself” (West 2022). Combined
with other changes in academic practice, notably the growing
reliance on nontenure track faculty and online education, Allan
called out the erosion of pillars of the academy. He expressed
his views respectfully and forcefully (as a faculty member, as a
faculty senator, and as chair of the faculty assembly). It was hard
to silence a man committed to speaking truth to power.
2   |   Association Leader, Network-­Expander, and
Field-­Builder
Allan talked and wrote a lot. In an age of information overload
and disposable news, some might dismiss Allan as a blowhard
spouting words that were lost as soon as he uttered them. Why
follow his urging to speak out more if no one listens? Allan's
retort was to speak more and then act. For as much time as he
spent spreading his ideas and thoughts through speeches and
writings, he matched it by embedding those ideas into organi-
zations, associations, and networks in the United States and
around the globe. Through multiple leadership roles and mem-
berships, he bolstered existing organizations and helped launch
others. Notably for Public Administration Review readers, he
twice served as president of ASPA, the second time shortly
before he passed. Convinced that public administration was a
force for positive change, he worked to strengthen democratic
institutions worldwide, while also helping individual public
administration schools and institutes responsible for preparing
a civil service capable of delivering more equitable, inclusive,
and fair public services. Andrew Podger, President of ASPA's
International Chapter and former President of the Institute of
Public Administration Australia, a collaborator and friend,
observed:
Allan's concern about good public administration
internationally and in the US was widely appreciated.
We in Australia benefited from this commitment,
as did so many others across the globe, particularly
in Latin America and Eastern Europe. He led the
resistance to the illiberalism and disregard for
democratic institutions that have emerged over the
last decade and more, as well as the encouragement
of positive reforms in other countries and regions.
After spending the first half of his career working as a faculty
member and in state and federal government bodies and agen-
cies in the United States, Allan made his most significant in-
vestments in building the field of public administration through
efforts outside the United States. Having grown up in Florida
and spending the longest period of his professional career there,
his initial forays into building networks of pro-­democracy and
good government practitioners was across the Gulf of Mexico
in Latin and South America. For more than 25 years, he orga-
nized the annual Inter-­American Conference of Mayors and
Local Authorities (cosponsored by Miami-­Dade County, the
World Bank, Organization of American States, Inter-­American
Development Bank, and USAID), a forum for local, regional, and
national governments and others “interested in the strength-
ening of local governments … to discuss common goals for the
promotion of democratic local governance …” (https://​ipmcs.​
fiu.​edu/​mayor​s-­​confe​rence/​) throughout Latin America and the
Caribbean. The conference became the oldest and largest annual
gathering of Latin American local municipal officials.
Allan also supported democratic institutions through a US
Agency for International Development (USAID)-­funded pro-
gram for strengthening local governments in South America,
particularly in Paraguay and Chile, which, in the mid-­1990s,
were transitioning to democracy after decades of authoritarian
rule. The Paraguayan political scientist, author, and journalist
Dr. Victor J. Flecha remembers,
I cannot think of a better way to define Dr. Allan
Rosenbaum, having known him and worked with him
for nearly 40 years, than as a true apostle of freedom
and democracy. He displayed zeal in his work in areas
of governance reform, decentralization, legislative
development, strengthening local governments, and
the creation of democratic institutions in all those
places that needed support, such as Latin American
states that had just emerged from long, barbaric, and
bloody dictatorships.
Working against such negative emerging global trends was not
easy, but “he stood his ground and firmly believed in the im-
portance of having a democratic context as a prerequisite for
the effective, efficient and responsive public administration,”
said Professor Laila El Baradei from The American University
in Cairo.

tional level, particularly in highly centralized countries, Allan
frequently worked directly with local officials. The first dem-
ocratically elected mayor of Asunción, Paraguay, Dr. Carlos

Allan parlayed his efforts with individuals into networks that
spanned geographic boundaries. He was instrumental, for
example, in creating the Inter-­American Network of Public
Administration Education (INPAE), working closely with col-
leagues from Argentina, Colombia, Chile, Brazil, and the United
States.
Spurred by his work in Latin and South America, Allan
then invested in schools and institutes and built networks in
Ukraine, Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, and other countries in
Central and Eastern Europe, another part of the world mov-
ing toward democracy throughout the 1990s and 2000s. Of
particular relevance was his long-­term commitment to the
Network of Institutes and Schools of Public Administration
in Central and Eastern Europe (NISPAcee), a professional
organization he supported from 1998 until his death. As Ms.
Ludmilla Gajdošová, NISPAcee's Executive Director and Dr.
Juraj Nemec, professor at Masaryk University in the Czech
Republic and Former NISPAcee President and Steering
Committee member, noted, “To the best of our knowledge, no
other American academic or public administrator has given as
much of their time and energy to support the development of
public administration education and training and government
reforms in Central and Eastern Europe as has Professor Allan
Rosenbaum.”

European region, Prof. Gyorgy Hajnal from Corvinus University
in Hungary said,
Rosenbaum was deeply engaged with the issues
and challenges facing the region, be they related to
democratic consolidation and related administrative
reforms or problems of autocratization. For many
years, he was a constant presence at the region's main
academic event, the NISPAcee annual conferences,
where his insights and passion for the region inspired
countless scholars. His work and attention focused
not only on established academic topics but on
such critical ones as academic freedom and human
rights in increasingly authoritarian contexts—a
topic of profound relevance for the region. Professor
Rosenbaum's dedication to fostering scholarly
growth and his ability to address traditional and
contemporary challenges will continue motivating
future

Allan's impact extended beyond individual countries and regions
to global forums and networks, notably his involvement with the
United Nations Committee of Experts in Public Administration
(CEPA), where he worked tirelessly with many colleagues from
around the world on strengthening the public sector to deliver
equitable, effective, and inclusive public services. One of his en-
during contributions is the central role he played in the develop-
ment of the Standards of Excellence for Public Administration
Education and Training, a collaboration between the UN
Department of Economic and Social Affairs and IASIA, where
he chaired its accreditation body, the International Commission
on Public Administration Education and Training. He led the
creation of the original Standards in 2008 and spearheaded a re-
view and upgrade still underway after his passing. To date, those
Standards have been translated into French, Spanish, Arabic,
and Chinese.
Allan was a citizen of the world with a “rolodex” of cabinet min-
isters, leaders of national legislative bodies, high-­ranking US
government and UN officials, and academics. He built relation-
ships and then connected his colleagues to create overlapping
networks driven to support the ideas that motivated his work.
A student of public administration, he succeeded in embedding
these ideas in the policies and practices of schools and institutes,
governments, and international associations.
3   |   Teacher, Mentor, and Friend
The markers of legacy can be found in speeches and writings,
and the evidence of impact can be found in policies and prac-
tices. And yet, our contributions can be unwound unless there
are people motivated to continue to champion the ideas and
broaden the networks that spread the ideas. Perhaps because he
had been shaped by public intellectuals like Lowi, Wildavsky,
and Dahl, Allan cultivated followers through teaching, mento-
ring, and friendship. Fundamentally, Allan was an academic, a
practitioner scholar who believed in the transformative power
of education and teaching. Allan's contributions to education,
public service, and global governance are remarkable, but his
teaching and mentorship will leave the most enduring legacy.
He shaped the careers of numerous students, many of whom
now hold prominent positions around the world. His influence
will continue to resonate in the halls of academia, in govern-
ment institutions, and in the hearts and minds of those he
mentored.
At Florida International University (FIU), Allan was deeply
involved in all aspects of the master's and doctoral programs
in Public Administration.1 Under his leadership, the doctoral
program grew from a small cohort of a dozen students to an in-
ternationally recognized program with over 40 students. Allan
was instrumental in helping the program achieve its interna-
tional stature, playing a critical role in collaborating with in-
stitutions around the globe. He brought his vision of a globally
connected public administration program to foster collabora-
tion and engagement across borders. He chaired over a dozen

The foundation of Allan's mentorship was his relentless pur-
suit of knowledge, fairness, and justice. His influence extended
far beyond the traditional roles of a teacher and mentor, as de-
scribed by his former student, Heidi Jane Smith, Profesora de
Economía at Universidad Iberoamericana. Her reflections cap-
ture the essence of his mentorship philosophy:
Central to Rosenbaum's approach was his unwavering
belief in the transformative power of education and
knowledge dissemination. As a beloved teacher and
mentor, he moved beyond conventional teaching
methods in favor of a more inclusive and interactive
approach. He valued intellectual curiosity above all
else, pushing his students to question conventional
wisdom, challenge empirical assumptions, and seek
out innovative solutions to complex problems. When
recruiting new students, he was less interested in
having them provide the ‘right’ answers and more
focused on their pursuit of knowledge. This often
made his committee meetings challenging to pass,
as he encouraged deeper exploration rather than
simple conclusions. His passion for tackling societal
issues—whether related to democracy, sustainability,
equity, or justice—drove him to recruit students
eager to seek out these answers. His commitment to
this approach often led to long, thought-­provoking
discussions and numerous paper revisions, which
frequently posed more questions than answers for his
doctoral students.
His mentorship left a lasting impression on George Atisa,
Associate Professor of Public Affairs and Security Studies at the
University of Texas Rio Grande Valley, who shared:
Dr. Rosenbaum admitted me to a PhD program in
Public Affairs at Florida International University
(FIU). He was also my mentor during the first year of
my PhD program at FIU. Professor Allan Rosenbaum
was not just a kind person, he put himself in my shoes
and helped me to navigate my first year in the program
with a lot of success. The first year in the program was
very challenging, but as PhD program coordinator, he
was so inspirational and created conditions in which
all of us, the PhD students, came together as a team,
or can I say, as family. I remember the evening get-­
togethers we had at his house, we all felt at home with
his family. All this made me successful.
Allan's impact as a mentor extended well beyond his students'
time at FIU. For many, he continued to be a source of guidance
long after they graduated. Gina Scutelnicu Todoran, Professor at
Pace University, recounted:
Allan Rosenbaum has had a profound impact on my
career and life … He shared valuable guidance about
navigating the job market at different career stages …
Allan and I kept in touch after my graduation when he
continued helping me navigate the “unwritten rules”
of the academic workplace and kept introducing me
to the key individuals in the field. I owe a significant
part of my success to Allan and his impactful work.
Allan's resolve to promote good governance through better pub-
lic administration also led him to support the careers of young
professionals through mentoring and international exchanges.
He supported the careers of young academics around the world
by mentoring them and giving them his time and advice. Dr.
Daniel Cravacuore, Professor at the University of Quilmes in
Argentina, says,
I would like to pay tribute to Dr. Allan Rosenbaum,
whose generosity and support have been instrumental
in my professional development. Researchers from
the Global South do not always have the opportunity
for a high-­level American colleague to take an interest
in our work and offer us opportunities. Throughout
my career, Allan did just that. I remember, with
gratitude, the day he offered me the position of
Distinguished Researcher at his Institute, which was
one of the most significant moments of my career.
He also promoted colleagues from Bulgaria, Spain,
Romania, and Ukraine, among others, offering them
similar opportunities.
Many can attest to Allan's heightened cultural understanding
and generosity toward students, making him a revered and iconic
scholarly mentor. Dr. Pan Suk Kim, ASPA's International Director
and Professor Emeritus at Yonsei University, South Korea, said,
Korean public administration professors, graduate
students majoring in public administration, and
civil servants in the central and local governments
remember him as a cooperative and open American
public administration scholar. He was a seasoned
scholar, but he was never authoritarian. He
enjoyed talking to them and socializing with
scholars, graduate students, and civil servants
without formality. Therefore, I think he is a truly
international scholar who interacted widely with
public administration experts from all over the world
regardless of their social backgrounds, such as region
of origin, nationality, race, gender, and school of
origin.
The key that unlocks Allan's impact as a teacher and mentor is
his humanity. Allan, simply put, was a nice person who cared

and he wanted to help them succeed and grow. He was quick
to make friends and committed to building a relationship. Each
of us writing this essay remembers time spent with Allan away

Allan balanced his dedication to improving global governance
with his zest for life, playful spirit, and deep appreciation for the
people and cultures he had the fortune to learn about. Professor
Andrew Messy of the International School for Government at
King's College London recalls:
We met through our mutual work with the
International Institute for Administrative Sciences
and its constituent groups, and Allan led various
parts of the IIAS at different times, often alongside
his leadership role for ASPA. He integrated all these
activities alongside being a consummate teacher,
writer, wit, and raconteur. A dinner with Allan after
a day's work usually resulted in a string of anecdotes,
all of which drew on his vast international experience
and were instructive to even the most experienced
members of his audience.
This was typical of Allan's way of living and working. After a
hard day of work, Dr. Najat Zarrouk, who served with Allan as
a member of CEPA, remembers “We would finish our work and
sometimes meet up for coffee, lunch, or dinner somewhere in
the avenues and streets of Manhattan, with Judy, his wife, as
well as other colleagues and friends, to share jokes, to laugh and
to spend friendly and unforgettable moments.”
Allan was more than a professor and an administrator. He was
a transformative figure whose passion for public service, de-
mocracy, and academic freedom helped shape the practice and
teaching of public administration through advocacy, policy de-
velopment and implementation, and education. We will miss
Allan, but we will feel his impact through our own memories of
his friendship and through the continued work of his friends all
over the world.

ABSTRACT
This paper examines the moderating effects of socio-­political factors on the influence of issue salience and prosocial values on
citizen activism in different governance contexts. While the literature on citizen activism has examined the emergence, motiva-
tions, and channels of activism, we still lack a broader understanding of how different social (membership of social groups and
sense of attachment to local communities) and political (ease of citizen involvement and trust in government) contexts affect
people's ultimate decisions about activism in different governance systems. This paper fills this gap, drawing on a comparative
research methodology and using data from the World Values Survey Wave 7.
1   |   Introduction
Public management scholarship acknowledges the important
role of citizens in local development processes and for good
reason. In the past decade alone, citizen activism has grown
exponentially across the globe (Green 2016; Hope et  al.  2016;
Fagerholm et  al.  2023; Trott  2024). From the Arab Spring to
more recent events like George Floyd and the 2024 tax revolts in
Kenya, citizens continue to push for change through mass pro-
tests, boycotts, strikes, and other civil disobedience. Although
not a new phenomenon, as citizens have participated in activism
throughout modern political history, the current form and in-
fluence of citizen activism evince a new age of collective action.
Green (2016) has attributed these impetuses to global transfor-
mations such as an increase in literacy, openness to democratic
ideals, and the introduction of social media technology.
Scholars have addressed different aspects within the vast and
evolving literature on activism. Some have focused on how ac-
tivism emerges (Martin et al. 2007; Pianta et al. 2012; Biekart
and Fowler  2013), “who” participates in civic activism (e.g.,
Norris  2004; Uzochukwu and Thomas  2018; Bovaird  2007),
what psychological (including the perceptual, attentional,
cognitive, affective, and motivational factors) drive activism
(see Barrett 2014), the channels through which activists express
themselves (e.g., Norris 2004; Alkhaldi et al. 2024; McMellon
et al. 2024), and the effectiveness (impacts) of citizen activism as
a tool for social change (Green 2016).
Notwithstanding this broad ranging interest in the topic, our
understanding about the moderating effects of citizens' social
and political contexts on individual activism decisions remains
less nuanced. At best, many existing works focus on single or
multiple country case studies, with very limited interest in in-
ternational comparative research focused on contrasting socio-­
political contexts.1 The absence of a larger contextual pool limits
generalization of findings and linearity of progress (Alford 2014;
Brandsen and Honingh 2016).
The central objective of this paper is to address this gap from
an international comparative perspective. The paper argues that
the social and political contexts within which people find them-
selves in many ways influence how they make everyday deci-
sions about participation in civic activities. It posits that people's
attitudes and beliefs about civic activism are often influenced
by different social capital (general social trust, networks, and

2   |   Unpacking Citizen Activism
Citizen activism has been defined variously by different authors.
Taylor (2022, 7) has described it as “representative participation in
the public sphere.” Green (2016, 181) has taken this description a
step further by activism as “any individual action with social conse-
quences, and much of it involves collective activity, including par-
ticipation in faith groups or neighborhood associations, producer
organizations and trade unions, village savings and loan groups,
and funeral societies, among others.” Green's (2016) definition
highlights two levels of civic activism—individual and collective
levels—meaning activism can occur when a citizen acts alone or as
part of a collective. Contacting public officials and providing vital
feedback to state decision-­makers on service issues are examples of
individual activism (Green 2016; Uzochukwu and Thomas 2018;
Norris 2004; Barrett and Brunton-­Smith 2014), while joining oth-
ers in protests and boycotts are collective activism.
On his part, Norris (2004) has identified two channels of activism:
(1) citizen-­oriented activities—those that generate individual ben-
efits with little need for cooperation with other citizens, and (2)
cause-­oriented activities—those that generate collective benefits
and requires collective efforts. While citizen-­oriented actions relate
mainly to elections and politics generally, cause-­oriented actions
are geared toward policy changes and are therefore mostly di-
rected at government institutions. Despite these conceptual differ-
ences, Norris (2004) notes that the distinction between citizen and
cause-­oriented activities is not always water-­tight, as, for example,
community groups can contact elected officials while individual
citizens can engage in one-­man protests.
Similarly, Barrett and Brunton-­Smith (2014) have drawn con-
ceptual distinctions between two types of citizen activism—
political participation and civic participation. Drawing on the
works of Verba et al. (1995), Barrett and Brunton-­Smith (2014,
6) have defined political activism as any “activity that has the
intent or effect of influencing either regional, national, or su-
pranational governance, either directly by affecting the making
or implementation of public policy or indirectly by influencing
the selection of individuals who make that policy.” They further
distinguish between two forms of political participation—con-
ventional and non-­conventional political participation—where
conventional political participation involves electoral processes
(e.g., voting, vote canvassing, etc.) and non-­conventional political
participation involves political activities outside electoral pro-
cesses (e.g., signing petitions, participating in political demon-
strations, etc.). Moreover, drawing from Zukin et  al. (2006),
Barrett and Brunton-­Smith (2014, 6) define “civic participation”
as the “voluntary activity focused on helping others, achieving
a public good or solving a community problem, including work
undertaken either alone or in cooperation with others in order
to effect change.” These include being a member of a social
group, community service, and undertaking organized volun-
tary work. For the purposes of this study, activism refers to the
conventional and non-­conventional types of political activism.
3   |   Theory and Hypotheses: Why Citizens Engage
in Civic Activities
The motivating factors influencing citizen activism can be suc-
cinctly explained by Lowndes et al.'s (2006) CLEAR framework.
Drawing on citizen participation theory and Verba et al.'s (1995)
Civic Voluntarism Model (CVM), in particular, Lowndes
et al. (2006) surmised that citizens participate when they Can,
Like to, are Asked, are Enabled, and are Responded to.3 People
“can do” when they have the resources and knowledge (capabil-
ities) to participate. They “like to” participate when they have a
sense of attachment that reinforces participation. They are “en-
abled to” participate if they are provided with the opportunity
for participation. They are “asked to” participate if they are mo-
bilized by official bodies or voluntary groups. Finally, citizens
are “responded to” when they see evidence that after they partic-
ipated in a civic activity, their views were considered.
Beyond Lowndes et al. (2006), a considerable body of works
(e.g., Huang  2017; Barrett  2014; Barrett and Brunton-­
Smith 2014; Brunton-­Smith 2011)4 have linked participation
and activism in particular to four main factors—personal
psychological factors, macro-­politico-­institutional contextual
factors, proximal social contextual factors, and demographic
factors. Personal psychological factors relate to people's

3.1   |   Personal Psychological Factors
Several theories point to the rational and evaluative nature of
people. Weberian social action theory observes people's social
interventions as actions that are evaluative and oriented in their
course (Weber 1978). Public choice and transaction cost theo-
ries also suggest that people are benefit maximizers and that for
efficiency gains, rational actors consider their self-­interest (e.g.,
utility, monetary gains) in excess of the associated transaction
costs before undertaking any action (Verschuere et  al.  2012).
Before engaging in any form of civic activity, a rational citizen
may first establish salience, that is, if the undertaking is import-
ant to them, their family or loved-­ones (Pestoff et al. 2013). If
people feel a need exists, they are more likely to get involved
(Verschuere et al. 2012). Based on these views, I expect:
H1a.  Citizens who perceive the salience of a social issue to have
strong willingness to participate in activism.
Beside issue salience, scholars have also noted the influence of
innate convictions and personal social (prosocial) values that
drive individuals to participate (e.g., Wise et  al.  2012). These
innate convictions are often the result of “individual beliefs,
risk attitudes, and choices” developed through personal expe-
riences (Malmendier 2021, 2857). They are driven by “personal
psychological orientations,” (Schlozman 2002, 439) that makes
them hold the view that individuals have responsibility to con-
tribute to the common good of society (Pestoff 2009; Eijk and
Steen 2016). I hypothesize that:
H1b.  Citizens who have feelings that they have responsibility to
contribute to the common good (prosocial values) will have strong
willingness to participate in activism.
3.2   |   Moderating Factors
3.2.1   |   Macro-­Politico-­Institutional Context
The macro-­politico-­institutional context refers to the overarch-
ing political and institutional factors that influence how people
are involved in deliberative processes as well as people's trust in
government institutions. It includes factors such as the ease of
participation and trust in institutions.
3.2.1.1   |   Ease of Participation.  The nature of a society's
political environment influences the extent to which peo-
ple are “asked” or “enabled” to participate (Pestoff et al. 2013;
Alford 2009). People may participate in civic activities as far as
the governance system allows them. When the political envi-
ronment is deliberative and civil society organizations provide
opportunities for citizen engagement, they enable participation
(Bovaird  2007; Nabatchi et  al.  2017). In the same vein, when
societal structures permit government and voluntary bodies to
organize civic activities and regularly invite citizens to partic-
ipate, they are “asking” citizens to participate. Indeed, studies
have shown that contacting and asking people to participate in
a civic or political process via door-­to-­door campaigns or phone
calls significantly increase the chances of them participating
later in civic and political activities (Green and Gerber  2019;
Zukin et al. 2006). Based on these arguments, I expect individu-
als who feel their society creates opportunities for participation
to be more driven to engage in activism, especially when they
view the issue at hand to be salient or they have the desire to do
good for society. On the contrary, I expect individuals who feel
there are limited opportunities for public participation or who
feel their inputs have limited impact to be less driven to engage
in public activities even when they view the issue at hand to
be salient or they have the desire to do something for the good
of society.
I hypothesize therefore that:
H2a.  The ease of participation will positively moderate the im-
pact of issue salience on the willingness to participate in activism.
H2b.  The ease of participation will positively moderate the
impact of prosocial values on the willingness to participate in
activism.

known to have institutional structures that promote deliberative

3.2.1.2   |   Institutional Trust.  Several studies (e.g., van
Eijk and Steen 2014, 2016; Fledderus and Honingh 2016; Fled-
derus et  al.  2015; Kang and Van Ryzin  2019; Liu et  al.  2023;
Torney-­Purta et  al.  2004; Barrett and Brunton-­Smith  2014)
have examined the relation between trustworthiness of pub-
lic institutions and various forms of citizen activism includ-
ing voting, volunteering, or contacting public officials. Barrett
and Brunton-­Smith (2014) note that institutional trust is built on
the public believing that societal and political institutions have
their best interest at heart and that they are operating in ways
that will benefit citizens rather than harm them. Because insti-
tutional trust is inherently related to the performance of pub-
lic institutions (Catterberg and Moreno 2006), institutions that
are trustworthy have legitimacy and compliance from the pub-
lic (Tyler and Jackson 2014) while those that lack public trust
attract pushback and opposition (Norris 2004). Based on these
arguments, I hypothesize that:
H3a.  Trust in government institutions negatively moderates
the impact of issue salience on the willingness to participate in
activism.
H3b.  Trust in government institutions negatively moderates
the impact of prosocial values on the willingness to participate in
activism.
Since more democratic societies provide forums for accountabil-
ity, openness, and citizen responsiveness, I expect institutional
trust to be significantly influential in less democratic societies. I
therefore hypothesize that:
H3c.  The moderating effect of institutional trust on the impact
of issue salience on the willingness to participate in citizen activ-
ism will be more significant in democratic countries.
H3d.  The moderating effect of institutional trust on the impact
of prosocial values on the willingness to participate in citizen ac-
tivism will be more significant in democratic countries.
3.2.2   |   Proximal Social Context—Horizontal Ties
Proximal social factors refer to relations and social trust devel-
oped through family, community, and peer groups. These fac-
tors influence people's motivations for activism.
3.2.2.1   |   Social Trust.  Social trust is built on the belief that
other people will generally take into consideration one's best
interest when making personal decisions. From the seminal
work of Putnam (i.e., “Bowling Alone”), we understand that
social trust enhances people's capacity to join together (see Put-
nam 1993, 2000) to achieve a collective action. Thus, individuals
who trust their social circles will be more strongly to act in ways
that will improve the general good of others (Pestoff 2009; Eijk
and Steen 2016), especially when the issue at stake is important.
I hypothesize therefore that:
H4a.  Social trust will positively moderate the impact of issue
salience on the willingness to participate in activism.
H4b.  Social trust will positively moderate the impact of proso-
cial values on the willingness to participate in activism.
Prior studies have highlighted the repression on citizen activ-
ities in less democratic regions over the past decade (Rutzen
2015; Yabanci 2019; Lorch 2017). Such acts of repression can
sow seeds of doubts and distrust among neighbors and even
family members who may harbor fear of being reported as anti-­
government agents. Since levels of trust also influence social
capital (Catterberg and Moreno 2006), I expect the compound-
ing effects of increased associational life to be more pronounced
in democratic societies. I hypothesize therefore that:
H4c.  The moderating effect of social trust on the impact of
issue salience on the willingness to engage in activism is more
significant in democratic societies.
H4d.  The moderating effect of social trust on the impact of
prosocial values on the willingness to engage in activism is more
significant in democratic societies.
3.2.2.2   |   Membership of Associations.  Citizens regularly
interact in horizontal layers of associations and civil society orga-
nizations. Such principled engagement in horizontal relations
promotes shared motivation (Emerson et al. 2012). When people
have a sense of solidarity and pride toward peer group organiza-
tions such as a former school, religious groups, and sports groups,
especially if such individuals are active members, they are more
likely to commit to civic and political goals (Zukin et al. 2006).
I expect individuals who are members of social organizations to
view activism as a salient issue and be more strongly driven to do
something for the good of society. I hypothesize therefore that:
H5a.  Membership of a social organization will positively mod-
erate the impact of issue salience on the willingness to participate
in activism.
H5b.  Membership of a social organization will positively mod-
erate the impact of prosocial values on the willingness to partici-
pate in activism.

when the issue at hand is salient or they have the desire to do

H5c.  The moderating effect of the membership of a social or-

3.2.2.3   |   Community Attachment.  Community attach-
ment reflects the social identification and a sense of belonging
individuals have with their immediate environment (Rollero
and De Piccoli 2010). It affects the values and interests one holds
and the resulting behavior one demonstrates toward their imme-
diate environment. Community attachment reflects a deeper
sense of connectedness, shared goals, and values in society. It
facilitates cooperation because of the importance one attaches
to it (Talò et al. 2014; Voorberg et al. 2015). Local residents that
identify strongly with a community are more likely to contribute
out of concern and consideration for other community members
than those with a weak sense of community attachment (Pen-
nington and Rydin 2000; Cicognani and Zani 2009). As such,
community attachment (operationalized as a sense of affinity
with one's local community) is one of the key concepts applied
to explain local engagement (Nowell and Boyd  2010, 2014;
Talò et al. 2014; van Eijk and Steen 2014, 2016). I expect that
individuals who feel attached to their local community will be
more strongly driven by desire to do something good for society
(Pestoff 2009; Eijk and Steen 2016). But this effect will be absent
when community attachment is weak. I hypothesize therefore
that:
H6a.  Attachment to community positively moderates the im-
pact of issue salience on the willingness to participate in activism.
H6b.  Attachment to community positively moderates the
impact of prosocial values on the willingness to participate in
activism.
4   |   Methods
The hypotheses above were tested using data from World
Values Survey (2017–2022) Wave 7 (Haerpfer et al. 2022). The
WVS is an international representative comparative social
survey that measures the social, political, economic, religious,
and cultural values of people across the globe. Beginning in
1981, conducted every 5 years and currently in its 7th wave,
the WVS has become one of the most authoritative and widely
used cross-­national surveys in the social sciences. Specifically,
it measures social factors including democracy, trust in
neighbors, trust in government institutions, support for gen-
der equality, religiosity, attitudes toward the environment,
work, family, politics, national identity, culture, diversity,
insecurity, and subjective well-­being. Data for the WVS are
collected through interviews of representative national sam-
ples. For each wave, suggestions for questions are solicited
by social scientists from all over the world and a final master
questionnaire is developed in English and then translated into
various national languages. To achieve the objective of this
paper, the data were disaggregated by four government types
(democratic, flawed democracy, anocracy, and authoritarian).
A description of the variables used in the study is displayed in
Tables 1 and 2.
4.1   |   Dependent Variable
The dependent variable—willingness to participate in activ-
ism—is measured as two separate variables: (1) conventional
political activism and (2) non-­conventional political activism.
This separation is similar to what is highlighted in prior liter-
ature (Barrett and Brunton-­Smith 2014; Brunton-­Smith 2011).
Civic activism, although important, is not captured in this study.
This limitation is explained in detail in the concluding section
of this study.
Conventional political activism is operationalized as a compos-
ite of four questions (willingness to encourage others to take ac-
tion about political issues; vote; donate to a group or campaign;
and contact a government official). The final composite variable
(sum of individual responses) produced more than five catego-
ries of responses, and as a result, it is analyzed as a continuous
variable.5 Similarly, non-­conventional activism is operational-
ized as a composite variable of four WVS questions (signed a
petition, attended demonstrations, joined boycotts, joined unof-
ficial strikes). The final composite variable is also measured as a
continuous variable.
4.2   |   Predictors and Moderating Variables
Two predictors and four moderating variables (see Table 1) are
selected for this study—issue salience, prosocial values, social
trust, community attachment, membership of organizations,
ease of participation, and trust in government. Similar to
Barrett and Brunton-­Smith (2014), issue salience is operation-
alized as the composite of 12 variables—importance attached
to politics; importance attached to democracy; interest in pol-
itics; how often discusses political matters with friends and
frequency of accessing news via daily newspaper, TV, radio,
mobile phone, Email, Internet, social media, talk with friends
or colleagues.
Prosocial values are operationalized as a composite of five ethics
variables that measure respondents' inclinations to do good for
the benefit of society: claims of government benefits to which
one is not entitled; avoiding a fare on public transport; stealing
property; cheating on taxes; accepting a bribe in the course of
one's duties.
Community attachment is operationalized as a composite mea-
sure of five variables—trust in people from respondent's neigh-
borhood; attachment to respondent's village, town, or district;
voting in local elections and feeling of security in respondent's

4.3   |   Control Variables: Social Economic Status
(SES)
Prior literature has indicated the importance of controlling for
socio-­demographic and geographic characteristics (Alford and
Yates 2016; Parrado et al. 2013; Verba et al. 1995; Pattie et al.
2004). Socio-­demographic arguments dominate explanations for
variations in local participation rates (Verba et al. 1995; Pattie
et al. 2004). Verba et al. have noted that citizens' voices are es-
pecially unequal when participation depends on contributions
of money rather than contributions of time. Again, traditionally,
citizens of high socioeconomic status (SES) tend to participate
more than low-­SES citizens (Ostrom 1996). Disadvantaged cit-
izens may be constrained in their participation due to lack of
knowledge (about how to participate and the importance of their
input) and by a lack of materials that facilitate their participation.

4.4   |   Model
To test the hypotheses, I employed ordinary least-­squares es-
timations using SPSS package 28. The inclusion of interacting
variables in the model resulted in high variance inflation fac-
tors (VIFs), depicting high multicollinearity. To address this, all
variables in the models were mean-­centered.
One of the limitations of the study methodology is that it relies on
cross-­sectional data instead of panel data. As prior studies (e.g.,
Savitz and Wellenius 2023; Maier et  al. 2023) have indicated,
cross-­sectional data do not allow scholars to make causal infer-
ence because observations of the units of analysis are made at a
specified point in time, giving room for selection and informa-
tional bias (Maier et al. 2023). Owing to this limitation, results
from this study should be made with some caution.
5   |   Results
The results of the OLS estimations are displayed in Table 3 (con-
ventional activism) and Table  4 (non-­conventional activism),
respectively. Based on the combined models, non-­conventional
activism has the highest number (7) of statistically significant
predictors compared to political activism (6). Nonetheless, the
predictors' explanatory power is higher (18.5%) in the conven-
tional activism model than that in the non-­conventional activ-
ism (14.4%).
5.1   |   Test of Hypotheses
The first hypothesis (H1a), that “issue salience has a positive
influence on activism,” was supported under both conven-
tional and non-­conventional political activisms. This is evident
from the combined models, which show that issue salience im-
proves the willingness to participate in conventional and non-­
conventional activisms by 7.9 and 5.7 units, respectively. On the
contrary, the findings did not provide conclusive support for the
second hypothesis (H1b) that “prosocial values have a positive
influence on activism.” While the combined models showed
positive relations between prosocial values and conventional
activism, they indicated a negative relation between prosocial
values and non-­conventional activism.
5.1.1   |   Moderating Effects
Regarding the moderating factors, I hypothesized (H2a, H4a,
H5a, H6a) that community attachment, social trust, member-
ship of associations, and ease of participation positively moder-
ate the effects of issue salience on the willingness to participate
in activism. The combined models (with moderating variables)
of conventional and non-­conventional activisms supported all
the above hypotheses except membership. Under conventional
activism, each one-­unit shift toward community attachment,
social trust, and ease of participation improved the influence of
issue salience by 0.3%, 0.3%, and 0.8%, respectively. Similarly,
under non-­conventional activism, each one-­unit increase in
community attachment, social trust, and ease of participation
improved the effect of issue salience by 0.4%, 0.3%, and 0.8%,
respectively. The findings further supported hypotheses (H3a)
that institutional trust negatively moderates the effect of issue
salience on the willingness to participate in both conventional
and non-­conventional activisms. Each one-­unit increase in in-
stitutional trust reduced the influence of issue salience on con-
ventional and non-­conventional activisms by 0.2%, and 0.3%,
respectively.

6   |   Discussion
The study provides important insights for both theory and
practice of citizen activism. First, the study underscores the
significance of issue salience when it comes to personal deci-
sions to participate in conventional and non-­conventional po-
litical activisms. As the study demonstrated, regardless of the
government type or mode of activism, the participation of cit-
izens in any form of political activism hinges on how salient
the issue at hand is to them. In the context of this study, what
was important to respondents in their activism decisions was
politics and the general perception of democracy. This finding
provides theoretical and practical support for prior studies that
examine the importance of issue salience in citizen participation
activities (Verschuere et al. 2012). For scholars of activism, this
study underscores the need to model citizen activism not only
through the lens of interests and disaffection but also through
the lens of the different channels through which citizens receive
social and political news (social media, Email, and the Internet),
as these influence their construction of what is salient. Beyond
theoretical insights, the significance of issue salience has prac-
tical implications as well. It demonstrates to practitioners about
the need to understand what citizens value the most when they
develop plans to pressure decision-­makers for change. If practi-
tioners and civic actors want maximum involvement from cit-
izens, then understanding citizens’ priorities and interests are
necessary for effective involvement.

Another significant finding of the study is that having the de-
sire to do good for society (prosocial values) has very little to
do with willingness to participate in activism. This essentially
means that generally, citizens may have the desire to do good
for society (operationalized in this study with questions fo-
cused on one's ethical values), but as long as that desire is not
aligned with the subject matter in question, they will not be
willing to participate activism. In the case of this study having
the desire to do good, improved people's willingness to pro-
test and engage in demonstrations but the same values pushed
them to be unwilling to vote or donate toward campaigns.
Perhaps, respondents have become disenchanted about the ef-
fectiveness of voting and external efficacy of decision-­makers.
Indeed, the WVS wave 7 was conducted between the years
2017 and 2022, a period that coincided with the George Floyd
and Black lives matter protests movement that rocked many
parts of the globe. People generally were dissatisfied with
the state of inequities within this period and perhaps saw
engagement with decision-­makers as activities that were not
moving the needle enough in a way that demonstrations and
protests could.
The study also highlighted the moderating effects of commu-
nity attachment, social trust, institutional trust, and ease of
participation when examining the influence of issue salience
on activism. We can glean from the findings that people are
more than likely to translate their interest in politics and de-
mocracy into real actions (voting, giving to campaigns, and
demonstrating) when they have a strong sense of attachment
to where they live or they trust in their social circles (neigh-
bors, people they meet for the first time, or family). This sup-
ports Putnam's  (1993, 2000) views about the importance of
social capital. For activists, this finding suggests that while
promoting interests in activism is important, they can gener-
ate better results if they focus attention on people embedded
in their communities or support actions that promote social
trust. Concerning ease of participation, the findings suggest
the need for activists to push for more avenues for improve-
ment in public participation if they expect conventional and
policy-­oriented demands to be effective.
When it comes to institutional trust, the study provides an im-
portant lesson for activists and public institutions alike. For
activists, the study demonstrates that much of the change they
seek in society could be addressed if activists focus some atten-
tion on working to push for mechanisms that will improve insti-
tutional trust. As the study showed, the more accountable public
institutions are, the more trust they gain from the public and
the less interest people have in engaging in political activism.
Similarly, the study signifies the practical need for state institu-
tions to position themselves in a positive light as people's desire
to pressure the state for change is reduced where trust in insti-
tutions is high. This finding supports prior studies (e.g., van Eijk
and Steen 2014, 2016; Fledderus and Honingh 2016; Fledderus
et al. 2015) that highlight the importance people place on trust-
worthiness of governments when it comes to their participation
in general.
Furthermore, the study reinforced prior studies that compara-
tively democratic societies (particularly in flawed democratic so-
cieties) provide better contexts for social trust and membership
of associations to improve the impact of issue salience on con-
ventional and non-­conventional activisms.
Despite the findings, it is worth noting that the study's use of
cross-­sectional data instead of panel data limits the ability to
make causal inference. Generalizations from the results dis-
cussed so far must be made with some understanding of this
limitation.
7   |   Conclusions
For activists and public managers to optimize society's partic-
ipatory inputs, our knowledge regarding the moderating ef-
fects of social and political contexts on individual decisions
to participate in matters that concern them must improve be-
yond the existing focus on single country studies (Brandsen
and Honingh 2016). This study has sought to contribute to this
endeavor through a comparative lens. Developing testable hy-
potheses from the political participation literature and Lowndes
et al.'s (2006) CLEAR framework, the paper has found that, in-
deed, the social and political contexts within which people find
themselves in many ways influence their decision to participate
in conventional and non-­conventional activisms. The impli-
cations of these findings to the existing activism literature are
manifold.
First, the study has expanded on prior works that emphasized
the importance of the social and political contexts (Bryson et al.
2013; Fledderus 2014; Letki and Steen 2021) on individual incen-
tives to participate. Taking a step further, the study has explored
broader contextual factors such as the ease of participation, trust
in government institutions, community attachment, and mem-
bership of associations to demonstrate that some contexts are
more challenging for citizen activism than others.
The second contribution of this study is its comparative ap-
proach. Despite the preponderance of activism across the globe,
most of the existing works have tended to focus on a few com-
parative case studies creating a gap in international comparative
literature. The application of a regression model on a large data-
set has offered a methodological improvement over previous
studies, as it has allowed us to examine variations in citizen ac-
tivism processes in order to generate generalizable conclusions.
Most importantly, the results of this study have demonstrated
to practitioners and local government leaders that in develop-
ing citizen participation, supportive contexts that promote trust,
inclusion, and freedom of association are very vital. Equally
important in society is people's perception of issue salience
and their willingness to do good for society. Full and flawed
democracies are known to maintain traditions of collabora-
tion and consultation between state and civil society (Letki and
Steen  2021), while anocracies and authoritarian regimes are
known to “develop policy in an exclusive manner …[in order to]
retain as much control as possible” (Voorberg et al. 2018, 366,
emphasis mine). Thus, in the context of democratic societies,
practitioners focused on political activities may rely on social
trust, membership of associations, institutional trust, and ease
of participation to foster citizen activism. In anocracies and au-
thoritarian societies, practitioners may gain more cooperation

Despite its valuable contributions, this study has some limita-
tions. Notably, its use of cross-­sectional data limits generaliza-
tions from the findings. Also noteworthy is that the focus on just
conventional and non-­conventional is too narrow. While these
modes of participation are relevant, care must be taken when
generalizing the findings to other types of civic activism such as
being a member of a non-­political organization, participating in
school-­based community services, and undertaking organized
voluntary work (Barrett and Brunton-­Smith  2014). Because
of this limitation, any generalizations of the findings must be
made with care. Future research can address this limitation by
combining different databases as Brunton-­Smith (2011), and
later Barrett and Brunton-­Smith  (2014) demonstrated in their
study of “Processes Influencing Democratic Ownership and
Participation” in Europe. In these studies, the authors combined
databases from the European Social Survey, Eurobarometer,
International Social Survey Program, Comparative Study of
Electoral Systems, and the World Value Survey. Doing in future
studies so may provide more critical nuances of citizen activism.

ABSTRACT
Limited awareness impedes take-­up in low-­income utility assistance programs, which often suffer from low uptake. This study
uses a field experiment to evaluate direct mail as a means of reducing learning burdens and thereby increasing participation
in financial assistance programs offered by a large American sewer utility. Employing a conjoint design, we sent customers
mailings that varied by medium (postcard vs. letter), frequency, color, sender (government vs. nonprofit), message framing (per-
centage discount vs. dollar savings), and language (English vs. English-­Spanish). Compared to the control group receiving no
mail, direct mail significantly increased program inquiries. Notably, postcards were as effective as letters, and sending even one
mailing yielded a substantial response increase; additional mailings provided minimal benefit. Sender and message framing had
no significant effect. Surprisingly, bilingual mailings resulted in slightly lower response rates. Our findings suggest that direct
mailings, particularly postcards, can help improve take-­up of income-­qualified public assistance programs.
1   |   Practically Speaking Executive Summary
This study examines the effectiveness of direct mail in promot-
ing low-­income assistance programs. Learning burdens—the
costs of learning about public programs—are among the admin-
istrative burdens that citizens experience when interacting with
government. Researchers to date have arrived at virtual con-
sensus that learning burdens depress participation in assistance
programs, as studies in a variety of contexts link low take-­up of
government benefits to a lack of awareness and/or poor under-
standing of benefits and eligibility. Past studies provide strong
reason to expect that burdens contribute to program participa-
tion in general and that governments can facilitate greater par-
ticipation by reducing learning costs. Early studies on learning
burdens mainly employed ethnography, focus groups, and/or
case study interviews. A wave of recent research applies exper-
imental methods to yield stronger causal evidence on how to
reduce learning burdens and drive increased program uptake,
which remains rare.
Direct mail is one means by which governments can provide in-
formation to the public and so reduce learning burdens. Despite
the rise of digital media and online communications, postal
mail remains a popular medium for commercial advertising.
In recent years, public administration researchers have begun
using experiments to test the effectiveness of direct mail.

1.2   |   A Field Experiment
Seeking to reduce information burdens pursuant to greater
take-­up, in 2023 NEORSD deployed a field experiment to evalu-
ate the effectiveness of direct mail in promoting its low-­income
bill assistance programs. The experiment featured a conjoint de-
sign that simultaneously varied media (postcards and letters),
frequency of mailing (one or two), and mailing characteristics
(color, message, and language). A thousand NEORSD residen-
tial customers were assigned to one of 64 separate treatment
groups (N = 64,000), with all other customers serving as a con-
trol group (N = 220,233).
Mailings were deployed over a 3-­week period. NEORSD cus-
tomer service staff logged inquiries about bill assistance in the
6 weeks following the initial mailing. We matched customer
service logs to experimental assignments to determine whether
customers who inquired about assistance received mailings and,
if so, which mailings they received.
Overall response to direct mail was strong, with an average of
1.5% of customers who received at least one mailing contacted
NEORSD to inquire about assistance—a result higher than
the 1.0% that is typical for commercial direct mail campaigns.
Moreover, customers who received one or more mailings were
significantly more likely to inquire about assistance compard to
the control group that received no mail.
The experiment's conjoint design allowed for statistical analysis
of mailing characteristics, yielding more useful findings:
•	 Simple postcards are sufficient: Both postcards and letters
generated responses, but postcards yielded the highest av-
erage response rate. This result indicates that simple, inex-
pensive postcards can be just as effective as letters.
•	 Multiple mailings may have a limited effect: While a second
mailing yielded slightly more responses, the additional cost
may not be justified by the small increase.
•	 Color and message do not significantly impact response:
Black-­and-­white postcards performed as well as color ones,
and messages framing the program's benefits in terms of
percentages or dollar savings had similar results.
•	 Bilingual mailings may have unintended consequences:
Surprisingly, English-­Spanish bilingual mailings yielded
marginally lower response rates than English-­only mail-
ings. This result requires further investigation, as it contra-
dicts expectations.
This experiment was conducted in a single geographic area and
involved one specific program; replication in other contexts can
provide greater confidence about our findings. Limited informa-
tion about participants also prohibits us from knowing which
customers responded to mailings; future studies could explore
how mailing characteristics impact responses from different de-
mographics. Our surprising findings on the effects of English-­
Spanish bilingual communication are especially worthy of
further investigation.
1.3   |   Implications for Public Managers
Public managers can leverage these findings to improve the
implementation of programs aimed at low-­income populations.
Direct mail can reduce learning burdens associated with assis-
tance programs, increase awareness of those programs, and lead
to greater public engagement with public agencies. In particular,
simple black-­and-­white postcards can be a powerful and inex-
pensive tool for outreach. Public managers can put our findings
into practice quickly and so help deliver services and support to
households in need.

programs, utility assistance involves learning costs, one kind
of administrative burden that stymies take-­up (Herd and
Moynihan 2018; Moynihan, Herd, and Harvey 2015). Direct
mail marketing is one means of reducing learning costs, and

This study presents the results of a large-­scale, direct mail
conjoint field experiment designed to promote utility bill as-
sistance programs in a major U.S. metropolitan area, featuring
a large sample of sewer utility customers (treated N = 64,000;
control N = 220,233). This study joins a surge of randomized,
controlled trials of outreach methods for public assistance
programs published in recent years; to our knowledge, this
is the only such experiment involving utility assistance. The
experiment featured a conjoint design, randomly assigning
utility customers to receive various combinations of postcards
and letters with varying color, messages, and language. The
conjoint design allows us to infer the effects of multiple fac-
tors on program inquiry rates simultaneously and with a high
degree of confidence. In taking this approach, our aim is not
to advance cognitive or behavioral theory or to explain low
take-­up of utility assistance. Rather, we seek to test several
direct common mail techniques simultaneously in a way that
offers practical insights for public managers who seek to re-
duce learning burdens.
To summarize our main results, we found that direct mail sig-
nificantly increased inquiries about assistance, as utility cus-
tomers who were sent mailings were significantly more likely
to contact the government seeking assistance than customers
who were not. Postcards and letters generated similar responses,
but multiple mailings did not markedly improve response rates.
Color postcards did not yield higher responses than black-­and-­
white postcards, and variations in messaging had no significant
effect on response rate. Surprisingly, English-­Spanish bilin-
gual messages generated lower response rates than English-­
only mailings, with effects approaching statistical significance.
Although inquiries do not necessarily result in applications or
ultimate delivery of assistance, these findings carry useful im-
plications for the design and implementation of utility assistance
specifically and social programs generally. The present results
join a growing body of research demonstrating that direct mail
is an effective way to communicate with potential assistance
program participants, and that simple black-­and-­white post-
cards can be a potent, cost-­effective medium for communicating
with low-­income populations.
We begin with an overview of learning costs and how they con-
tribute to the administrative burdens that depress take-­up of
government programs. We then review recent field experiments
that test the effects direct mail and other methods on program
take-­up. We introduce the empirical setting and describe the
experimental design and administration. After presenting re-
sults, we discuss their significance, their limitations, and their
implications for future research on and implementation of low-­
income utility assistance programs.
3   |   Learning Burdens and Direct Mail Messaging
Administrative burdens are the costs that citizens experience
when interacting with government (Moynihan, Herd, and
Harvey 2015). Although administrative burden is a subject of
longstanding interest in public management research, interest
in the topic has surged in recent years, especially as it relates
to the implementation of social welfare policies (Heinrich 2016;
Herd and Moynihan 2018). Moynihan, Herd, and Harvey (2015)
identify three types of costs that contribute to administrative
burdens: learning, psychological, and compliance costs. Applied
to social assistance, learning costs are the costs of learning about
government programs that are necessary to benefit from them.
Psychological costs include distress from loss of autonomy and
social stigma that accompany dependence on government assis-
tance. Compliance costs reflect the time and effort required to
receive government assistance, such as completing applications,
documenting eligibility, and periodic re-­enrollment. A line of
research on understanding low take-­up and interventions to re-
duce administrative burdens has grown alongside the literature
on administrative burdens (e.g., Benish et  al.  2024; Bertrand,
Mullainathan, and Shafir 2006; Cantarelli et al. 2024; Janssens
and Van Mechelen 2022).
An exhaustive review of research on administrative burdens
and interventions to reduce them is beyond our present scope,
for the literature on these topics is voluminous—particularly on
psychological and compliance costs (for a recent thorough re-
view, see Halling and Baekgaard 2023). Rather, we focus on the
more modest literature most relevant to this study: recent empir-
ical research linking learning costs to public program take-­up.
Researchers to date have arrived at virtual consensus that learn-
ing costs depress participation in assistance programs, as studies
in a variety of contexts link low uptake of government benefits
to a lack of awareness and/or poor understanding of benefits
and eligibility (Barnes 2021; Barnes and Riel 2022; Daigneault
and Macé  2020; Duffy et  al.  2022; Madsen, Lindgren, and
Melin 2022; Nisar 2018). This line of research provides strong
reason to expect that burdens contribute to program participa-
tion in general and that governments can facilitate greater par-
ticipation by reducing learning burdens.
Until recently, most published studies on learning burdens and
program participation are qualitative, employing ethnographic,
focus groups, and/or case study interviews (Halling and
Baekgaard 2023). Over the past decade, however, a burgeoning
body of research has sought more solid causal evidence about
the relationship between learning burdens, information, and
program take-­up, with particular attention to a longstanding
and widely used communication medium: mail.
3.1   |   Direct Mail Outreach
Direct mail—that is, unsolicited advertising via postal deliv-
ery—is one means by which governments can provide informa-
tion about public programs. Direct mail is a perennially popular

widely used despite the rise of electronic media (Simon and
Andrews 2015; Stafford, Lippold, and Todd Sherron 2003). A ro-
bust line of marketing research has emerged over decades eval-
uating various aspects characteristics of commercial direct mail
campaigns (Feld et al. 2013).
Governments also employ direct mail for outreach and adver-
tising. Importantly for present purposes, a burst of recent stud-
ies have used controlled experiments to test the effectiveness
of direct mail as a means of boosting take-­up of various gov-

ple, in the United States, Bhargava and Manoli (2015) showed
that mailing information to eligible federal taxpayers led to
increased claims of tax benefits for low-­income households (al-
though Linos et al. (2022) found no such effect). Finkelstein and
Notowidigdo (2019) found that letters and postcards boosted ap-
plications for food assistance in Pennsylvania; Chen et al. (2023)
found that postcards were effective in promoting vaccines to
senior citizens in Louisiana. Similar experiments found that
letters led to increased take-­up of a health care reimbursement
program in Belgium (van Gestel et al. 2023) and a dental care
program in Greece (Linos et al. 2022).
Building on these findings about the effects of direct mail,
subsequent studies have begun to experiment with media and
messages delivered by mail. Linos et al. (2023) found that for-
mal style in writing and graphic presentation of letters elic-
ited greater response than informal communications. Bhanot
et al. (2019) mailed postcards to thousands of government em-
ployees in a large city, randomly assigning one of four varying
messages to test the effects of different framing on participation
in a wellness program. Hock et al. (2021) tested the effects of dif-
ferent postcard designs on participation in an employment pro-
gram for disabled individuals, finding that folded-­over postcards
prompted higher responses than single-­faced postcards. Lasky-­
Fink and Linos  (2024) found that postcards substantially in-
creased inquiries about a low-­income rental assistance program
and that destigmatizing messages further raised response rates.
Taken together, this emerging body of research offers encour-
aging indications that direct mail can reduce learning costs in
ways that can drive greater participation in social benefit pro-
grams. Which direct mail characteristics are most and least
effective at inducing participation remain open questions and
an area of lively inquiry. This study contributes to this line of
research, with attention to practical application and a novel em-
pirical subject: utility assistance.
4   |   A Direct Mail Field Experiment to Promote
Utility Assistance
In 2023, we deployed a field experiment in the Cleveland, Ohio
metropolitan area to expand and refine our understanding of di-
rect mail as a means of reducing learning costs and thereby driv-
ing increased take-­up of a utility bill assistance program. The
study was undertaken by the Northeast Ohio Regional Sewer
District (NEORSD, or “the District”), a regional government
that provides wastewater collection and treatment services to
the U.S. City of Cleveland and most of it surrounding suburban
area. NEORSD served a population of approximately 1 million
in 2023, including 284,233 single-­family residential customers.
The District's service area features considerable racial and socio-
economic diversity according to the 2022 American Community
Survey, with a 16.4% poverty rate and a population that is 29.1%
Black/African American and 6.9% Hispanic/Latino.
At the time of this writing, NEORSD offers two bill assistance
programs for low-­income households: Crisis Assistance and
Affordability. The primary focus of the present study is the
Affordability program, which provides ongoing 40% bill dis-
counts for customers with household incomes below 250% of the
Federal Poverty Level. Although the exact value of the discount
varies depending on individual household water consumption,
the discount's value was roughly $29 per month for a typi-
cal family. As with many American public utilities' assistance
programs, participation in NEORSD's Affordability program
is very low, despite rising utility prices and moderate-­to-­high
poverty levels in the District's service area.2 At the time of this
study, about 1700 customers were enrolled in the program, out
of roughly 98,000 eligible customers according to District esti-
mates—a participation rate of less than 2%.
4.1   |   Experimental Design
Seeking efficient means of increasing participation, in early
2023 the District fielded a large-­scale direct mail experiment
employing a conjoint design, following the method advanced by
Vriens et al. (1998). Any direct mail campaign involves a series
of decisions about media (e.g., letters or postcards?), design (e.g.,
color or black and white?), messaging (e.g., emotional or rational
appeal?), and more. Conjoint design involves randomly varying
multiple characteristics within a single mail campaign. Analysis
of response rates from a conjoint experiment allows researchers
to determine the degree to which various characteristics affect
the likelihood that recipients respond, as well as an optimal
design to maximize response. In the present study, NEORSD
transmitted information about its low-­income bill assistance
programs through direct mail. Each mailing invited customers
to the District by telephone or email to inquire about assistance,
but several characteristics of the mailings varied simultane-
ously. Conjoint design maximizes the practical inferences for
future District communications.
4.1.1   |   Treatments
Following a review of existing research on direct mail cam-
paigns, six mailing characteristics were selected in consulta-
tion with NEORSD's administrative and customer service to
be tested in the experiment. Each characteristic involved one
of two treatments, selected to evaluate commonly used direct
mail techniques. Table  1 reports the selected characteristics
and treatments, as well as the expected effects of the various
treatments on response rates. Since our principal interest is in
generating actionable findings for public managers, we forego
detailed discussions of cognitive and behavioral theoretical the-
ories underlying these treatments; we refer readers who are in-
terested in the theoretical underpinnings of these treatments to
the sources cited with each of them.

The first characteristic of interest was mailing medium, with cus-
tomers receiving letters and/or postcards. Letters and postcards
both have proven effective in prompting response, although ev-
idence about which is more effective is mixed (Crane et al. 2016;
Feld et al. 2013; Grubert 2019; Ley, Duan, and Parsonnet 2023;
Linos et al. 2022; Paquin et al. 2021). We also varied the number
of mailings: some customers were sent a single mailing; others
were sent an initial postcard, and then a second postcard or let-
ter 2 weeks later. Past research suggests that multiple, repeated
mailings can yield greater program recruitment response rates
than single-­wave mailings (Grubert 2019; Steffen et al. 2008).
The third characteristic tested was color, with postcards printed
in either color or black-­and-­white. Although colorful paper and/
or images are commonplace and widely used in direct mail, past
research consistently finds that color has a negligible or even a
negative effect on response rates compared to black-­and-­white
(Bekkers and Crutzen 2007; Bender 1957; Dunlap 1950; Etter,
Cucherat, and Perneger 2002; Feld et al. 2013).
Fourth, we randomly varied the sender: some customers re-
ceived a mailing from NEORSD with the District's name and
logo prominently displayed, while others received mailings from
Community Housing Solutions, a local nonprofit community or-
ganization. We included this characteristic in this study to eval-
uate whether generalized distrust of government exacerbates
learning burdens. The low-­income populations that might ben-
efit from bill assistance programs also tend to distrust govern-
ment generally and utilities specifically (Teodoro, Zuhlke, and
Switzer  2022). If distrust of government exacerbates learning
and/or psychological burdens, then receiving information from
a local nonprofit organization rather tha a government might in-
crease the likelihood of response (Nisar 2018). At the direction
of District staff, the Community Housing Solutions letters were
sent only to customers in the City of Cleveland, so effectively
this treatment applied only to customers within the city.
We also varied two message characteristics. Since NEORSD's af-
fordability assistance program aims to provide participants with
lower bills, we tested two discount frames that communicate
the program's value in different ways. Some customers received
messages that described the discount as a percentage reduction
(“Save 40% on your sewer and stormwater bill”); other custom-
ers received a message describing an estimated dollar value of
the discount (“Save $29 a month on your sewer and stormwa-
ter bill”). Commercial marketing research finds that framing
discounts in percentage terms usually elicits stronger consumer
response for low-­priced items, but that framing discounts in
monetary terms drives greater response for higher-­priced items
(Liang et al. 2024; McKechnie et al. 2012). We had no specific
expectation about whether customers perceive NEORSD's ser-
vice prices as high or low, and so we had no specific expectation
about the effects of framing discounts in terms of percentages or
dollar values.
Finally, we varied the language of the mailings. Some custom-
ers received letters or postcards in English, while others re-
ceived bilingual mailings with matching messages printed in
both English and Spanish. Although most advertisers believe
that Spanish and/or bilingual mailings are most effective in
reaching Spanish-­speakers, evidence is mixed (Hernandez and
Newman  1992; Noriega and Blair  2008). The 2022 American
Community Survey indicates that 4.4% of Cuyahoga County's
total population and about 7.6% of the population in poverty
speaks Spanish at home. Trust in water utilities is relatively low
among Hispanic/Latino residents in the United States (Teodoro,
Zuhlke, and Switzer  2022), and case study research indicates
that English-­Spanish bilingual communication can improve
communication on water issues (Marsh, Gold, and Strong 2023).
Because Spanish-­speakers compose a nontrivial share of the
target population, testing English-­Spanish bilingual messaging
was a high priority for NEORSD.
Various combinations of treatments resulted in a total of 64
treatment groups. NEORSD staff developed the mailing ma-
terials, including specific wording for letters and postcards;
Figure  1A,B offer examples of the resulting treatments.
Customers in groups 1–16 received either a single postcard or
a single letter. Customers in groups 17–64 received a postcard
followed by a second mailing (either a letter or a second post-
card) 2 weeks later. Customers within the City of Cleveland were
included in all 64 groups; customers outside of Cleveland were
excluded from the groups receiving a community group letter
and so only 44 treatment groups included these suburban cus-
tomers. Table A1 in the Appendix provides a detailed summary
of the 64 treatment groups.

nication cannot prevent information “spilling over” between
customers. A customer who receives a letter or postcard might
not read it carefully, or might share it with friends or neighbors
who received different mailings or who received no mailings

In more formal terms, this experiment involves intent-­to-­treat

some customers may not have actually read the postcards they

tomers. This method is particularly useful in field experiments
with imperfect compliance, as it provides a more realistic esti-
mate of the treatment effect in a real-­world setting.
4.1.2   |   Sample and Randomization
The experiment's sampling frame included all 284,233
NEORSD single-­family residential customers who were not
enrolled in any of the District's assistance programs at the time
of the study; this total included 101,812 customers within the
City of Cleveland and another 182,421 customers in NEORSD's
suburban service area.3 We randomly assigned 1000 custom-
ers to each treatment group. We assigned 300 Cleveland cus-
tomers and 700 non-­Cleveland customers each to the groups
that included both Cleveland and non-­Cleveland customers.
We assigned 1000 Cleveland customers each to of the 20 treat-
ment groups that were only for customers within the City of
Cleveland. For purposes of privacy and pragmatism, the re-
searchers had very limited information about customers. That
is, we had mailing addresses associated with utility bills but no
information about the people who lived in the homes receiving
NEORSD services. Although a lack of household-­specific in-
formation limits our ability to conduct randomization checks,
formal analysis of group assignment by zip code indicates that
the randomization procedure was effective. The remaining
220,233 residential customers in the sampling frame received
no direct mail as part of this campaign and so serve as a con-
trol group in analysis.
4.2   |   Administration
NEORSD contracted with a commercial mailing service to de-
ploy the experiment in early 2023. All customers assigned to
treatment conditions were mailed the first wave of letters and
postcards on January 10–11. Postcards and letters for customers
assigned to treatment conditions involving a second mailing
(groups 17–64) were sent 2 weeks later, January 24–25. An
error by the mailing service during the second wave caused a
misapplication to 16 treatment groups. As a result of this error,
8000 customers who were supposed to receive a bilingual let-
ter from the nonprofit community group in the second wave did
not receive a second mailing.4 Instead, another 8000 customers
received a third bilingual letter from NEORSD.5 This mistake
unbalanced the experimental design, but also created new in-
ferential opportunities. In the analysis that follows, we report
results that both include and exclude these mistaken treatments.
As we will see, the error does not change the main results sub-
stantially, but does offer additional insight.
4.2.1   |   Measuring Response
NEORSD's customer service staff routinely logs records of
contacts from customers in its customer management soft-
ware, including inquiries about assistance programs. Calls
or emails in which the customer inquired about assistance
programs were coded as positive responses. Since we are in-
terested in the effects of mail in reducing learning costs, this
protocol measures inquiry in response to information treat-
ments, not program take-­up. All customer service inquiries
logged from January 10 through March 1 were included in the
present analysis, after which the experiment was considered
concluded. We matched these inquiries to treatment and con-
trol group records using customer identification numbers to
facilitate analysis.

5   |   Results
NEORSD received 4658 customer service contacts over the
period of analysis, 2818 of which involved inquiries about as-
sistance. A total of 955 of these assistance inquiries were from
customers who were sent at least one mailing, for a response
rate of 1.5%. This overall response rate is higher than the roughly
1.0% that is typical for direct mail campaigns (Imai 2005; Linos
et  al.  2022). More importantly, the average treated response
rate was far higher than the control group. During the period
of analysis, 1863 customers who were not sent a mailing con-
tacted NEORSD to inquire about assistance, or 0.8%. Table  2
reports these results with several formal tests of statistical sig-
nificance, each of which shows that, customers who NEORSD
sent mail were significantly more likely to inquire about assis-
tance following the mailing than customers who did not receive
a mailing.
5.1   |   Mailing Characteristics
To evaluate the effectiveness of various mailing characteristics,
we fitted a series of logistic regression models that estimate the
likelihood that experimental subjects inquired about assistance
as a function of each treatment. To gauge the effects of different
types of mailings (postcards vs. letters, single vs. multiple mail-
ings), we separately estimated one model that includes all cus-
tomers (Model A) and one model that excludes the 16 treatment
groups that were affected by the mailing service error (Model
B). Table 3 reports odds ratios and p-­values generated by these
models. To interpretation, Figure 2 displays the effects of each
treatment relative to the control group generated by Models A
and B, respectively. To evaluate the effects of mailing design, we
separately modeled responses as a function of medium, number
of mailings, color, sender, message, and language for customers
in the experimental treatment groups. Again, we separately fit-
ted one model that included all customers (Model C) and one
that excluded the treatment groups that were affected by the
mailing error (Model D).
5.1.1   |   Media
We find that postcards and letters both increased the likeli-
hood of response relative to the control (no mailing) condi-
tion. The effects of all postcards and treatments were positive
and statistically significant by conventional standards when
modeled with the full sample (Model A) and with the mail-
ing service error cases omitted (Model B). Despite their higher
cost, letters were no more effective than postcards in statis-
tical terms. Indeed, Figure 2 shows that consistent with past
research, simple black-­and-­white postcards generated the
highest average response—a point to which we return in the
discussion.

5.1.3   |   Messages
The results reported in Table  4 show little effect of differ-
ences in mailing design. Consistent with past research, color
postcards were no more effective than black-­and-­white post-
cards. Letters from NEORSD and the nonprofit community
group yielded very similar response rates, suggesting that nei-
ther the government nor the nonprofit organization enjoyed
greater trust or legitimacy with utility customers. We find
no significant difference in response between mailings that
communicated the value of the assistance program in terms
of a percentage discount and mailings that expressed the pro-
gram's value in terms of average dollar savings. Surprisingly,
we found that English-­Spanish bilingual mailings reduced the
likelihood that customers contacted NEORSD about bill assis-
tance compared with the control group. Although this effect
falls short of conventional statistical significance (one-­tailed
p = 0.09 in Model C; p = 0.084 in Model D), it is notable because
it contravenes expectations.6
6   |   Discussion
In an age of ever-­increasing online communications, direct mail
remains a staple of commercial advertising. This study is part
of an emerging wave of research on direct mail as a means of
reducing the learning burdens that inhibit participation in gov-
ernment programs aimed at helping low-­income populations.
Seeking to optimize a regional government's efforts to promote
utility assistance, we deployed a direct mail communications
campaign featuring a large-­scale field experiment. The exper-
iment's conjoint design simultaneously tested the effects of six
common mailing characteristics that might affect response
rates; Table 5 summarizes our key results in substantive terms.
We found that direct mail was effective in prompting customers
to inquire about assistance from the utility, as customers who
were sent mailings were significantly more likely to contact the
utility than those who were not. Although postcards and letters
were similarly effective, postcards yielded the highest average
response rate. A second mailing increased customer contacts
slightly. Letters from the local government agency and a local
nonprofit organization yielded similar responses. Color post-
cards and black-­and-­white postcards yielded similar responses,
and messages framing the benefits of the assistance program
in terms of dollars or percentage savings also yielded similar
responses.
Taken together, these results indicate that direct mail can reduce
learning burdens associated with public assistance programs
and that the optimal direct mail medium is a simple, inexpen-
sive, black-­and-­white postcard. Multiple waves of mailings may
yield greater responses, but the limited additional responses
may not justify the additional expense. Our findings also align
with what Linos et al. (2023) have called a “formality effect”:
austere black-­and-­white postcards and official letters embla-
zoned with government logos prompted the highest average
response rates in this study. They argue that formality in gov-
ernment communication serves as a heuristic that signals the
sender's credibility in ways that prompt responses. The average
1.5% response rate observed in this study is markedly higher
than the 1.0% typical in direct mail campaigns (Imai 2005; Linos
et al. 2022). Although this response rate is modest, direct mail's
low cost makes it a highly efficient means of reducing learning
burdens. In this study, the total cost of production, postage, and
mailing service for black-­and-­white postcards resulted in an av-
erage unit cost of $31.25 per respondent, which compares quite
favorably with typical customer acquisition costs in commercial
marketing research (Farooq 2024). Whether postcards are cost-­
effective means of outreach for a given program or agency de-
pends on the available alternatives.
A final notable result of our study was the effect of English-­
Spanish bilingual mailings. Contrary to expectations, custom-
ers who were sent bilingual mailings were less likely to inquire
about low-­income bill assistance than were customers who re-
ceived no mailings at all. This counterintuitive negative effect
falls just short of statistical significance by conventional stan-
dards in the present experiment, but it merits discussion be-
cause utilities communications experts have promoted bilingual
messaging in recent years (e.g., Payne 2020; Smith, Campbell,

this vein should be a high priority in future public management
research as societies grow more ethnically diverse.
6.1   |   Limitations and Directions for Future
Research
Like any empirical investigation, our study has several limita-
tions. Most obviously, the present experiment involves a single
geographic area, a single, specialized government, and a specific
type of low-­income assistance program. Fortunately, similar di-
rect mail field experiments are feasible anywhere that govern-
ments offer voluntary assistance programs and postal services
are provided to the population. Experimental research is most
effective when results accumulate over time and in a variety of
contexts.
Importantly, interventions in the present study are aimed only
at reducing learning burdens by providing low-­cost information
to potential program participants; there are many other burdens
and barriers to program participation that can affect take-­up.
Relatedly, the outcome or dependent variable in the present
experiment is the likelihood of inquiry about an assistance
program, not application to or ultimate take-­up of the program.
Although inquiry is the first step in take-­up for NEORSD's
program, it is not the last step. Future studies can and should
evaluate means of improving applications and take-­up of utility
assistance.
A dearth of information about our experimental subjects also
limits our analysis. Although we know that each of our research
subjects is a single-­family residential customer of NEORSD, we
know little else about them. With the present data we cannot, for
example, test hypotheses about how various mailing character-
istics affect people of different ages, genders, races, ethnicities,
and/or incomes. Sampling frames from educational, health, or
other contexts where such data are available allow researchers
to explore these possibilities. Future direct mail experiments
might use predictive analytics—now commonplace in commer-
cial marketing (Siegel 2013)—to craft communications tailored
to various groups and individuals.
6.2   |   Conclusion
We conclude with some observations about the substantive
focus of our study: utility bill assistance for low-­income cus-
tomers in the United States. So long as water and sewer systems
operate on a fee-­for-­service model, some low-­income customers
will struggle to pay for these essential services. Rising utility
prices, consumer inflation, and growing income inequality have
combined in recent years to drive increased affordability chal-
lenges for low-­income households. In response, local and state
governments have introduced income-­qualified assistance pro-
grams for water and sewer services.7 Despite widespread needs
and efforts to boost enrollment, take-­up of utility assistance pro-
grams remains stubbornly low. High administrative burdens,
including learning burdens, contribute to the poor performance
of these utility assistance programs (Breslin 2023).
This study demonstrates that direct mail is a potent way to in-
crease customer awareness of utility assistance. Happily, our
results demonstrate that a simple, inexpensive technology—
black-­and-­white postcards—can generate a marked increase
in inquiries about bill assistance. Public managers can put our
findings into practice quickly, and so help deliver financial
support to households in need. This study also invites further
research on the administrative burdens that frustrate take-­up

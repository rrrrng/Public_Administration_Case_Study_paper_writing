Abstract
Publishing is a source of capital and power in academia, and coauthoring is a com-
mon way to publish. However, studies in public administration have not yet exam-
ined the structure of coauthorship patterns, how these patterns have evolved over
time, or the extent to which these patterns are gendered. We use bibliometric data
to examine coauthorship in public administration scholarship over four decades
with a particular focus on gendered patterns. Descriptive statistics, regression, and
social network analysis suggest that when women are first authors, the research
team is more likely to contain other women and while women are increasingly
represented in coauthorship structures, men-only groups of coauthors continue to
persist. These findings have implications for the coauthoring practices of individual
scholars, perceptions of coauthorship in hiring, tenure, and promotion decisions,
and efforts in the field to promote diversity, equity, and inclusion.
Evidence for practice
• Coauthorship patterns and the extent to which these patterns are gendered
should be of interest to public administration scholars because collaborations
are important in the production of scientific research, affect individual

• Given the high value placed on social equity in public administration, the inclu-

• Individual scholars should be mindful of how coauthorship provides access to
research networks. In this context, considering gender diversity in research
teams is important.
• In tracking bibliometric measures, journals should consider not only gender
diversity in authorship of published articles, but also of those submitting to the
journal.

Most, if not all, academic disciplines espouse equity in
the profession to be important—equity with respect to
pay, access to jobs, tenure, promotions, awards, etc. Pub-
lic administration is no different in this respect. However,
public administration, as a discipline, trains those doing
the work of government that social equity, along with
economy and efficiency, should be the guiding forces in
decisions
and
actions
of
public
servants
(Frederickson, 1990). From a normative stance, public
administration asserts that public servants are responsible
for ensuring that all societal groups have access to oppor-
tunities, that government is responsive to the varying cir-
cumstances of those that it serves, and that government
benefits all. This is encapsulated in Frederickson’s (1990)
assertion that social equity is the “third pillar” in the prac-
tice of public administration next to economy and effi-
ciency. In public administration, a discipline ardently
espousing social equity as a core value in what we study,
teach,
and
practice
(Frederickson,
1990;
Frederickson, 1971), there are a surprisingly limited num-
ber of studies examining how its own social equity value
is manifested in its operational structures, in the case
under consideration here—gender and coauthorship
(Corley & Sabharwal, 2010; Su et al., 2013).
This study relies on descriptive statistics, regression
analysis, and social network methods to analyze biblio-
metric data retrieved from the Social Science Citation
Index (SSCI) accessed through the Web of Science (WOS)
platform. We examine coauthorship in public administra-
tion scholarship over four decades with a particular focus
on gendered patterns.2 This study examines factors asso-
ciated with the gender composition of research teams,
the evolution of the structure of coauthorship patterns
over time, and the extent to which there are gender dif-
ferences in coauthorship in public administration scholar-
ship. This study has implications for the coauthoring
practices of individual scholars, perceptions of coauthor-
ship in hiring, tenure, and promotion decisions, and
efforts in the field to promote diversity, equity, and inclu-
sion. The descriptive and exploratory nature of our study
builds knowledge for the field of public administration.
This knowledge is important for enacting, within our pro-
fession, the social equity we eagerly espouse.
BACKGROUND LITERATURE
Coauthorship is on the rise in the social sciences
(Henriksen, 2016), with growth in coauthorship in disci-
plines as varied as economics (Kuld & O’Hagan, 2018),
political
science
(Teele
&
Thelen,
2017),
sociology
(Akbaritabar
&
Squazzoni,
2021;
Leahey
&
Reikowsky,
2008;
Pontille,
2003),
nursing
(Benton
et al., 2017), lodging (Köseoglu et al., 2018), and talent
management
(Gallardo-Gallardo
et
al.,
2017).
Public
administration scholarship more recently has contributed
to this trend. Corley and Sabharwal (2010) document a
variety of publication trends in the field, including the
increase in coauthorship from 40% of publications in
1973 to 84% of publications in 2007. Focusing on Public
Administration Review, Ni et al. (2017) find an increase in
coauthorship as well, from 10% in 1940s to 54% in the
decade beginning 2010. From a topical perspective, Hat-
maker et al. (2017) found significant growth in the size of
the coauthorship networks in public service motivation
research.
Some scholarship in the social sciences points to the
factors that have contributed to the growth in research
collaboration (see Acedo et al., 2006; Henriksen, 2018).
For example, it may be that technology has created a
“global village” of colleagues making coauthoring much
more feasible than even 10 years ago (Kuld & O’Hagan,
2018; Rosenblat & Mobius, 2004). And coauthorship cre-
ates social capital that can translate to even more coau-
thorship opportunities (Li et al., 2013). Promotion and
tenure standards appear ever-increasing, making coau-
thorship desirable because combining efforts leads to
greater productivity (Barnett et al., 1988; Li et al., 2013).
As research becomes increasingly specialized, coauthor-
ship provides the means for a clear division of labor
(Barnett et al., 1988; Jones, 2009) and the ability to lever-
age expertise in increasingly complex technical skills
(Sugimoto & Larivière, 2018). Along with boosts to pro-
ductivity, coauthorship provides learning opportunities
and intellectual stimulation (Aldrich & Al-Turk, 2018).
Compounding the need and desire for coauthorship is
also a greater awareness of giving credit to all who have
participated in the labor of a project (Sugimoto &
Larivière, 2018). These benefits paint a clear picture of
coauthoring as a career resource and perhaps a necessity
for career advancement.
In addition to coauthoring in and of itself, author
order on a coauthored paper is also important (Biagioli &
Galison, 2014; Birnholtz, 2006; Frances et al., 2020; Smith
et al., 2020). Interest in author order is typically moti-
vated by who gets credit, visibility, reputation, and
reward for the work, with the lead author being in the
most prominent position for such recognition. Thus,
research on author order tends to focus on factors that
are associated with the ordering of author names and
considers the role of gender in these associations
(Ni et al., 2021; West et al., 2013). While there is some
variation across disciplines, first authors are typically
considered the lead with the most responsibility for pro-
ducing the paper (Larivière et al., 2016).3 Thus, first
authors may be responsible for or heavily influence the
composition of the research team. Consistent with theo-
ries of homophily as discussed shortly (McPherson
et al., 2001), when the first author on a research team is
a woman, this may be an access point for other women
to gain coauthorship connections.

The effects of coauthorship
Research shows that those who have garnered greater
prestige and power from publishing, continue to publish
more, further burnishing their stature (Bourdieu, 1988; de
Solla
Price,
1976;
Siler
et
al.,
2022;
Sugimoto
&
Larivière, 2018). In science, this has been referred to as
the “Matthew effect” where recognition for discoveries is
most likely accorded to those who already have promi-
nent reputations (Merton, 1968). More recently, the
“Matilda effect” has been advanced to highlight the
lower
recognition
afforded
to
women
in
particular
(Rossiter, 1993). With respect to coauthoring, a conse-
quence of the Matthew effect is “preferential attachment”
where prominent scholars are desirable coauthors and
those who are eminent tend to coauthor with each other
(Eckl et al., 2019; Vlegels & Huisman, 2021). This can cre-
ate a cluster of prominent scholars dominating research
in a particular area mirroring the social concept, “the rich
get richer and the poor get poorer.”4
Coauthoring can also have sponsorship effects, where
senior scholars can use their social capital and reputation
to promote junior scholars (Burt, 1998; Kanter, 1977). A
few studies suggest that junior scholars who coauthor
with prominent scholars early in their career enjoy a “per-
sistent competitive advantage” when compared to junior
scholars who do not coauthor with top scholars in the
early stages of their careers (Li et al., 2019). However,
the impact of sponsorship may depend on the gender of
the sponsor. For example, job candidates recommended
by male sponsors are more likely to be hired than those
recommended by female sponsors (Campbell et al., 2023).
In short, coauthoring affords scholars greater power or
prestige that benefits them throughout their careers
(Bourdieu,
1988;
de
Solla
Price,
1976;
Sugimoto
&
Larivière, 2018).
Coauthorship and gender
With increasing attention to gender disparities in acade-
mia, studies have begun to explore gendered patterns in
coauthorship. Some research shows that mixed-gender
teams are well-represented in certain disciplines (e.g., for
Library
and
Information
Sciences
see,
Cobo-Serrano
et al., 2024). In other disciplines mixed-gender teams con-
tinue to be underrepresented (e.g., for Medical Sciences
see, Yang et al., 2022). Fisher et al. (1998) find that while
single authorships have decreased in political science, all-
male coauthorships have increased. Similarly, in political
science, all-male coauthor teams make up 24% of articles
while all women teams make up only 2.4% and teams of
more than one gender only 15.4% (Teele & Thelen, 2017).
Some research finds the impact of coauthoring on career
outcomes varies by gender in economics, with women
taking a penalty for coauthoring while men do not
(Sarsons et al., 2021). Similarly, according to Hussey et al.
(2022), women in economics who coauthor with men or
with their PhD advisor are less likely to get tenure, but
the same is not true for men.
Theories of homophily or the preference to interact
with others who are similar to ourselves (McPherson
et al., 2001) offer insight into the persistence of same-
gender groups in coauthoring. Kwiek and Roszka (2021,
101171), for example, suggest that “gender-based homo-
phily… is reported to simplify communication, enhance
the predictability of behavior, entail reciprocity in collabo-
ration, and increase trust between collaborating parties”
(also see Bunker Whittington et al., 2024; McPherson
et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2023). Kegen (2013) points out
that men prefer to coauthor with other men in that they
perceive male collaborators to be more predictable and
the collaboration to be less costly. Interestingly, Dakin
and Ryder (2019) find that gender homophily in coauthor-
ing increases as the field of biological sciences moves
toward gender parity. Gertsberg (2023) finds that efforts
to raise awareness of sexual harassment (i.e., #MeToo) has
resulted in a sharpened perception of risk among men in
the economics discipline, lack of clarity as to what is con-
sidered appropriate behavior, and fear of public scrutiny
which has led to reductions in collaborations with
women.
Still other research points to the perception that coau-
thoring with women will diminish reputation and reduce
the likelihood of a manuscript being accepted for publica-
tion. Some find that coauthoring with men will burnish
an author’s reputation or competitiveness, as compared
to coauthoring with women (see Schmal et al., 2023).
Others find that all-women teams have the lowest accep-
tance
rates
for
journal
submissions
(Tudor
&
Yashar, 2018).
Despite the substantial attention to gender and coau-
thorship in other disciplines, only a handful of studies
have addressed gender and coauthorship in public
administration. One, noted earlier by Corley and Sabhar-
wal (2010), showed no statistically significant difference in
the number of coauthors by women and men publishing
in public administration journals between the years of
1973 and 2007. In another study, Su et al. (2013) found
that in 722 articles of five Taiwanese public administration
journals between the years of 1996 to 2007, male and
female scholars tended to author solo pieces most often,
followed in frequency by publishing with male-only coau-
thors. Public administration scholarship has given little
attention to gender and coauthorship, revealed inconsis-
tent findings with respect to coauthoring, and has not yet
examined how first author gender may impact the gen-
der composition of the research team.

We conduct analyses on the articles as well as on the
authors to address the following two research questions:
1. How is first author gender associated with the gender
composition of research teams?
2. How has the structure of coauthorship patterns
evolved over time?
To answer these questions we rely on the two data-
sets, with distinct samples and measures.
First author gender and gender composition
of research team
Sample and measures
To address our first research question, we rely on the
pooled cross-sectional dataset of articles to examine how
first author gender is associated with gender composition
of the research team. Because this research question
focuses on the gender composition of research teams, we
include only the 6511 articles that are coauthored.
We use two dichotomous dependent variables to
measure gender composition of the research team. First,
we examine whether or not the article includes mixed
gender authors (0 = not mixed gender, 1 = mixed gen-
der). Second, we examine whether or not there are
women coauthors (other than the first author) (0 = no
women coauthors, 1 = women coauthors). In our data,
author gender includes only women and men. However,
we acknowledge that gender is socially constructed and
not limited only to these two categories. To determine
author gender, we apply an algorithm developed by
Larivière et al. (2013). The algorithm compares the author
first names in our data with a library of birthnames by
country and associated sex assigned at birth to ascribe a
“probable gender.” In instances where the algorithm
could not determine a probable gender, we used Internet
searches and our own knowledge of the author to assign
a probable gender. A detailed description of this process
can be found in the Appendix.
Our primary predictor of interest is gender of the first
author (1 = first author is a woman, 0 = first author is not
a woman). In addition to gender of first author, we control
for a number of characteristics of the first author that may
impact coauthoring practices and thus, the gender compo-
sition of the research team. We include first author’s experi-
ence in the field (academic age = year of publication—
year of first publication in dataset) and their general
research productivity (total no of publications in the
decade). To account for variation across cultures in the
extent to which coauthoring includes mixed gender teams
or women coauthors, we have included a categorical vari-
able for the geographical region of the first author’s institu-
tional affiliation. This variable is comprised of the seven
analytical groups employed by World Bank (n.d.).
We also account for article characteristics including
the total number of authors (Peters & van Raan, 1994),
age of the publication (Aksnes, 2003), funding status
(1 = funded, 0 = not funded; see Quinlan et al., 2008),
journal tier (top, important, or other; see Tahamtan
et
al.,
2016),
and
scope
of
content
(1 = global,
0 = national; see Peng & Zhu, 2012).6
Because our dependent variables are dichotomous, we
use logistic regression to examine how the gender of the
first author is associated with the likelihood that an article
includes authors who have different genders and the likeli-
hood that an article has any women coauthors. Because our
dataset is a pooled cross section, we include year dummy
variables to account for unmeasured factors that may vary
across years, but not across individual articles within a year
(Wooldridge, 2013). Such factors could include, for example,
the number of women in the field and the quantity of publi-
cations in the discipline, among others.
As the descriptive statistics in Table 1 show, just under
half (45%) of the coauthored articles include a mix of gen-
ders among authors, 38% of articles have coauthors who
are women, and 30% of articles have a woman as the first
author. First authors produce, on average in a decade,
4.23 articles. First authors are also predominately from
North America (54%) and Europe and Central Asia (31%).
On average articles include 2.5 total authors, are almost
11.5 years old, and are published in journals with a global
scope (88%).

Coauthorship patterns over time
Sample and measures
Because our second research question focuses on how
coauthorship patterns have evolved over time, our
descriptive
analysis
examines
patterns
across
four
decades: 1980s, 1990s, 2000s, and 2010s. We select these
decades for our analysis over time because the onset of
each decade approximately coincides with an increase in
the proportion of public administration dissertations com-
pleted by women (Yun et al., 2020).7
Using the set of authors in our data, we performed
the same analysis in each decade to examine how coau-
thorship patterns have changed over time. In each
decade, we selected those authors who had published
three or more articles in the time period. We made this
selection because this level of productivity indicates that
the scholar is a regular contributor to mainstream public
administration scholarship. There are many authors who
may publish only one article in public administration jour-
nals over the course of a decade because their primary
scholarly home is in another discipline (e.g., sociology,
political science, etc.) and the public administration article
is a one-time occurrence or they are simply not research
active. Because such scholars would not be active partici-
pants in the network of public administration researchers
for the time period, we exclude them from the network
analysis.8
This results in 151 authors in the 1980s, 230 in the
1990s, 419 in the 2000s, and 991 in the 2010s. The num-
ber of authors publishing at least three articles in general-
ist public administration journals in the 2010s is almost
seven times larger than that number in the 1980s with
the largest increases occurring in the 2010s—a 137%
increase from the 2000s. Similarly, the proportion of these
authors that are women increased from only 11% in the
1980s to 34% in the 2010s, with the largest increase—11
percentage points—in the 2010s.
We use descriptive statistics and social network
methods to identify characteristics of coauthorship over
time and generate maps of coauthorship to visualize how
patterns have evolved over the four decades of our analy-
sis. As Sugimoto and Larivière (2018) note, one limitation
of existing research on collaboration is it typically
accounts for the extent to which an author collaborates
(e.g., how many coauthorships, how many coauthors), but
does not consider the relational nature of collaboration—
where an author sits in the “larger scholarly ecosystem”
(p. 61). We build on this work by visualizing coauthorship
over four decades of public administration scholarship.
We generate network maps linking authors who have
published together and examine the characteristics of the
network as well as the centrality of the authors within
the network overtime (Wasserman & Faust, 1994).
We begin our analysis of coauthorship structures in
public administration by examining characteristics of the
network as a whole (c.f. Provan et al., 2007). We focus on
network size, connectedness, and subgroups.
Network size
Size refers simply to the number of authors in the net-
work. Network size is important because networks with
many members offer multiple possible connections for
authors. Alternatively, in networks with fewer authors it
may be easier for authors to connect to one another.
Network density
To understand the extent to which authors are con-
nected, we examine network density. Density is measured
by calculating the proportion of possible connections
among authors that exist in the network (Wasserman &
Faust, 1994). Because it is a proportion, density can take
on values between 0 and 1 with values closer to 1 indicat-
ing higher density and greater connectedness. Networks
with higher densities imply more connections among the
actors.
Network subgroups
Finally, to examine the existence of subgroups within the
network, we identify components and the gender compo-
sition of the authors in the components. A component in

Author productivity
Table 3 presents descriptive statistics on author produc-

have become increasingly productive in terms of the
number of publications they generate. In each decade,
men were, on average, more productive than women,
with the greatest differences in these averages in the lat-
ter two decades (2000s and 2010s). In addition, on aver-
age, men tend to coauthor more than women. The only
decade in which women coauthored more than men is
the 1990s.
Author collaboration
Table 4 presents descriptive statistics for coauthorship

decades with the average number of unique coauthors

with more women with the exception of the 1980s.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
In sum, our findings suggest gendered patterns in coau-
thorship of publications in public administration journals.
We find that when women are first authors, as compared
to men, the research team is more likely to include other
women. Similarly, we also find that when women are first
authors, as compared to men, the research team is more
likely to be mixed-gender. One possible explanation for
these gendered patterns may be found in the strategies
scholars use to invite collaborators. Future research could
use qualitative data to explore how scholars form
research teams and whether there are gendered patterns
in invitation strategies. In addition, because scholars often
learn their research practices from mentors as they are
being
socialized
into
the
profession
(Smith
&
Hatmaker, 2014; Van Maanen & Schein, 1979), exploring
how and what doctoral students learn about coauthoring
and collaboration may also provide insight into the gen-
dered patterns of coauthorship in public administration.

Our findings also point to a considerable increase in
mixed-gender coauthorship relations over time in public
administration scholarship. Yet, there are still many coau-
thor subgroups comprised of men-only and the largest
subgroups are predominately men, even when the field
of public administration is at least half women (Yun
et al., 2020). However, our data does not offer an explana-
tion as to why men continue to dominate coauthorship
subgroups. Research on gender homophily—preference
to collaborate or interact with individuals of the same
gender—may shed light on these patterns (Kegen, 2013;
Kwiek & Roszka, 2021; McPherson et al., 2001; Wang
et al., 2023). Alternatively, the persistence of men-only
subgroups and the largest cluster of coauthors being pre-
dominately men may also reflect forces of preferential
attachment (Eckl et al., 2019; Vlegels & Huisman, 2021).
Future research could test theories of homophily and
preferential attachment using, for example, surveys of
scholars publishing in public administration journals.

in public administration continue to be dominated by
men, we do not know how membership in these net-

Our findings also reveal that men continue to have
the most coauthorship connections and the largest
number of publications within the coauthorship net-
works. Connections and publications are sources of
power and influence. Those with power and influence
can generate or restrict coauthorship opportunities, and

gendered lines in the field. Underscoring the impact of

networks—especially informal networks—is crucial both
for doing research and for achieving a career.” Future
research should examine the extent to which those who
are prominent in coauthorship networks provide access
to these networks to new members and if these patterns
are gendered.
More broadly, our analysis also points to the role of
coauthorship in the growth and maturity of the field
of public administration. Similar to our findings, Corley
and Sabharwal (2010, 644) found that the field of public
administration emerged largely as a “non-collaborative
discipline” but has matured to become a more collabora-
tive one. For scholars, particularly those in the early stages
of their careers, access to coauthorship networks is then
critical for status in the discipline and career advance-
ment. Scholars in prominent positions in coauthorship
networks (e.g., those with many coauthorship links) can
shape the future of collaboration in terms of who gains
access. In addition, our findings indicate coauthorship is
now the norm for publication and should be considered
in this context by those reviewing tenure and promotion
decisions. As Corley and Sabharwal (2010, 644–645)
emphasize, given this “new collaborative model, …uni-
versity administrators need to be attentive to potential
changes in the collaboration patterns of the field over
time and make sure that younger scholars are not pun-
ished for following recent disciplinary trends in their
research.”
Before concluding, we note some limitations to our
study
and
suggest
additional
directions
for
future
research. Our dataset includes only generalist public
administration journals. Future research should examine
specialist journals, and in particular if there are gendered
patterns in terms of who publishes and coauthors in such
journals. Our analysis does not take into account research
topic or methods. Future research could also consider
examining coauthorship networks within particular topic
areas or methods and making comparisons of such net-
works across areas. Similarly, our data does not include
field of expertise for each author or the extent to which
an author’s institutional affiliation emphasizes research
and how that might impact coauthoring strategies. An
important next step for future research is to move
beyond exploration and description to identify causal
mechanisms for gendered patterns in coauthorship in
public administration. Research should also consider the
extent to which women’s and men’s coauthorship net-
works are stable over time and how these networks
change or not as scholars gain prestige in the field.
Understanding the power dynamics and system of pres-
tige in academia is an important factor for understanding
change over time around gender dynamics in academic
scholarship.
Finally, we note while bibliometric studies contribute
to knowledge in a field, there is also criticism of the use
of quantitative assessments (e.g., bibliometric indicators)
as potentially exacerbating inequalities in science when
used to measure quality and contributions to science
(Biagioli
&
Lippman,
2020;
Dahler-Larsen,
2014;
Daraio, 2021). With this in mind, a priority for future
research should be gathering in-depth data on the
nuances of coauthorship such as how coauthorship rela-
tionships are formed and sustained. Such an endeavor
could include qualitative methods such as one-on-one
interviews to understand how coauthoring relationships
are initiated and the dynamics of such relationships in
public administration.

APPENDIX
SELECTION, CLEANING, AND PREPARATION OF WEB
OF SCIENCE DATA
We use the Web of Science (WOS) electronic database to
search the Social Science Citation Index (SSCI) for all avail-
able articles in 13 generalist public administration jour-
nals (journals listed in Figure A1). Our initial search
retrieved all English language articles available in WOS for
the 13 journals included in our analysis. This search
resulted in 14,598 articles beginning from 1978 through
May 2020, the date of our search. We used the results of
this search to generate two datasets for analysis. The first
dataset is comprised of articles and their characteristics
and the second dataset is comprised of authors and their
characteristics. Both datasets required significant cleaning
and preparation before they were suitable for analysis.
The process for cleaning our data and establishing the
datasets for analysis was a two-year endeavor involving
the five authors and six graduate student research assis-
tants. Here, we describe the process for generating both
datasets.
ARTICLES
Our cleaning process eliminated articles based on year
boundaries,
anomalous
and
anonymous
authors,
retracted articles, editorial acknowledgments, and dupli-
cates. This process reduced the initial 14,598 set of arti-
cles to 13,626, which forms the dataset for the larger
project. Figure A1 displays the distribution of articles by
journal for the 13,626 articles retained after our cleaning
process.
First, we ensured that all articles had a publication
year. The initial WOS data included 201 early access arti-
cles. These articles are those that have been published
electronically ahead of print by a journal. Thus, early
access articles do not include a publication year. For con-
sistency, we coded the publication year for early access
articles to be the early access year—either 2019 or 2020.
Because decades are a practical unit for discussing phe-
nomena over time, we bound our dataset by the four
decades for which we have complete data—1980s, 1990s,
2000s, and 2010s. Thus, we eliminated 950 articles from
1978, 1979, and 2020 leaving articles for the 1980s, 1990s,
2000s, and 2010s comprising our dataset. Notably, these
particular decades also approximately coincide with an
increase in the proportion of public administration disser-
tations completed by women (Yun et al., 2020).
Next, we eliminated articles containing anomalous
and anonymous authors. We eliminated articles by one
author that is actually two authors, one man and one
woman, who publish together under one name. Because
this is an anomaly in our data, we removed the two arti-
cles associated with this author. We also eliminated one
retracted article, two articles acknowledging editorial
board members or reviewers, 14 articles with anonymous
authors, and three duplicate articles.
In addition to the bibliographic information retrieved
from WOS, we generated additional characteristics for
each article. We included a dichotomous variable to iden-
tify an article as either “sole-authored” or “coauthored”
and calculated the total number of authors on each arti-
cle. Once we determined the probable gender for authors
in our dataset (see discussion of authors below), we also
categorized the gender mix of authors for coauthored
articles as either “all men,” “all women,” or “mixed.” As
one indicator of importance of an article, we generated a
dichotomous variable coding articles as “funded” if the
article declared any funding source.

AUTHORS
WOS data include a short name and long name for each
author on an article. The short name is an abbreviation
and the long name is the full author name. For example,
an author may have a long name of Jones, Ann E. and a
short name of JONES AE. These names are critical as these
are the fields used to uniquely identify authors and allow
us to use an algorithm to assign a probable gender for
the author—a central component of our analyses. How-
ever, WOS data generally includes a number of errors
with respect to author names. Our author dataset
required substantial cleaning of author names prior to
using the algorithm to assign a probable gender. The
13,626 articles in our dataset originally included 13,709
authors. Our cleaning process reduced the number of
authors to 12,049 and focused on several general sources
of errors.
Homonyms and synonyms
A common issue with the bibliometric data in the WOS
includes homonyms and synonyms for author names (Ni
et al., 2017). A homonym occurs when the same name
refers to more than one person. For example, the short
name, JONES A, may reference Ann Jones, Alex Jones,
and Andrew Jones. Similarly, the long name Alex Jones
may actually reference two different authors who happen
to have the same name. Synonyms occur when multiple
names are used to refer to a single person. For example,
the short names, SMITH T and SMITH TR, may both be ref-
erences to the same Timothy R. Smith. Similarly, the long
names Timothy Smith and Timothy R. Smith may refer-
ence the same person.
Homonyms were addressed by adding a sequential
number to the end of an author short name to ensure
that each author had a unique short name. For example,
if JONES A referred to both Ann Jones and Alex Jones, we
assigned one author a short name of JONES A1 and the
other a short name of JONES A2. In most cases, a hom-
onym referred to two or three people at a time. However,
for very common last names, such as Lee or Kim, a hom-
onym could refer to as many as eight different authors.
Synonyms were addressed by standardizing all varia-
tions of a name that referred to a single person. For
example, if the three long names, Smith, Timothy; Smith,
Tim; and Smith, Timothy R., all refer to the same person,
we standardized all instances of this author’s long name
to be Smith, Timothy R.
Missing full names or initials only
While the inclusion of full author names is one of the fea-
tures of WOS, WOS data did not regularly include com-
plete first names until 2008 and even after 2008,
complete first names is not an assurance (Sugimoto &
Larivière, 2018). Thus, we needed to identify any incom-
plete full names in our data and find the author’s
full name.
For each incomplete author name in our list of
authors, we searched the article dataset to find the title
of the article(s) for which the author was associated. Then
we used Google Scholar, university library databases, and
interlibrary loan when necessary to access a copy of the
article in order to identify the full name of the author. In
many cases, particularly for older articles written by prac-
titioners in Canada or Australia, the article did not contain
a full author name, only first and/or middle initials. In this
instance, we used author information in the article
(e.g., affiliation), and general Internet searches to identify
the author’s first name.
Misspellings, special characters, typos, and
miscellaneous issues
WOS data includes misspellings, misinterpretations of
special characters, typos, and other miscellaneous errors.
For example, the first name “Paul” may be misspelled as
“Pual” even if the name is spelled correctly on the article.
In addition, some alphabets do not translate accurately to
the text-based data in the WOS. For example, certain Dan-
ish letters (e.g., ø or Æ) end up being replaced with mean-
ingless symbols such as “?” in WOS data. To address
these errors, we replaced the meaningless symbols with

We used a pdf of the article itself to verify the correct
name and spelling and removed the text that was not
part of the author’s name.
Cleaning process
The focus of our initial data cleaning process was to
detect and correct homonyms and synonyms, missing full
names, misspellings, special characters, typos, and any
other miscellaneous issues. The goal of this process is to
have a complete list of authors with full names and
unique short names.
To organize our cleaning process, we generated an
alphabetical list (by last name) of all 13,709 authors of the
articles retrieved in our search of WOS. We divided this list
by alphabetic letter among the five authors and six grad-
uate students. After several training sessions to ensure
understanding of all the issues in the bibliometric data
and a consistent approach to detecting and correcting
them, each individual cleaned their assigned portion of
the author names. One author then double checked each
cleaned list and generated the R code to input the
changes into our dataset. Ultimately, we cleaned issues
for 6408 authors which resulted in the final 12,049
authors in our authors dataset.
Probable gender
With a complete list of authors, including their names, we
used an algorithm developed by Larivière et al. (2013) to
identify a probable gender for each author. The algorithm
compares the author first names in our data with a library
of birthnames by country and associated sex at birth to
assign a “probable gender.” The algorithm assigns a
probable gender value as male, female, unisex, initials,
unknown, or null based on first name. So, for example,
the algorithm records the first name “Asif” as being
“male”, the name “Taylor” as “unisex,” and the name
“Jingzhong” as “male.” If a first name was assigned more
than one probable gender in the algorithm library, we
gave authors with those names an “unknown” classifica-
tion for probable gender. The algorithm was able to
assign 87.1% of authors a probable gender of male or
female. For the 1561 authors where the algorithm
assigned a gender as unisex, initials, unknown, or null, we
manually searched for pronouns in author biographies
contained on the articles themselves, in other publicly
available bios, organization websites, and general Internet
searches. As a last resort, and in a small number of cases,
we estimated gender based on the combination of a pub-
licly available photo, full name, the gender of other
authors with the same name, and our own knowledge of
the author. We were able to assign an additional 1464
of authors a probable gender for a total of 99.2% of
authors with a probable gender assigned. Ultimately, the
authors are 67.04% men, 32.15% women, and 0.81%
unknown. These gender categories represent those that
appear in our data. However, we acknowledge that gen-
der is socially constructed and not limited only to the cat-
egories here. We also acknowledge that the terms “male”
and “female” are associated with biological sex not gen-
der. We use the terms male and female here as these are
terms used in the algorithm.
Additional characteristics
For each of the authors in our data, for the full time
period and for each decade, we calculated their total pub-
lications, total number of sole-authored publications, total
number of coauthored publications, and total number of
first authorships on coauthored publications.

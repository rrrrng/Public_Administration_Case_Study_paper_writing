Abstract
This paper examines how tax salience—or how aware individuals are of their property tax payments that finance local public services—correlates
with citizens’ evaluation of their local government. Drawing on theories of expectation formation for public services and fiscal illusion, we hy-
pothesize that reduced tax salience is associated with lower normative expectations, which will correlate with increased reported satisfaction
with their local government and a higher willingness to support additional taxation. Using data from a nationwide survey of homeowners
(n = 10,066), we provide evidence of robust correlations between lower tax salience and more positive citizen evaluation of government. This
paper contributes to a growing literature in public administration that finds that the design of tax systems, tax complexity, and other factors out-
side of the control of street-level bureaucrats that provide public services can influence how individuals evaluate a government or public service.
Key words: citizen evaluation; salience; survey methods; federalism.
Introduction
As elected officials and public managers increasingly view
their citizens as customers of public services and use satisfac-
tion surveys to guide decision-making and resource alloca-
tion, public administration scholars have examined how these
satisfaction surveys fail to fully measure service quality and
government performance. The existing literature has focused
explicitly on how implicit cognitive biases, the presentation
of performance information, or a respondent’s political align-
ment with a government can influence an individual’s satisfac-
tion of governmental services (Andersen and Hjortskov 2016;
Baekgaard and Serritzlew 2016; Hjortskov 2017; James and
Van Ryzin 2017; Jilke and Baekgaard 2020; Marvel 2016;
Olsen 2015).
Implicit within many of these studies are questions about
how aware citizens are of which government provides a cer-
tain public service and the quality of these services. Variations
in citizen awareness of which government provides services,
how these services are provided, and how these services are
financed may influence how citizens evaluate a particular gov-
ernment. For example, a citizen who is more aware of how
much they pay to finance a particular service may be more
critical of the quality of that service than individuals who
are less aware of how much they pay. Similarly, citizens who
are more aware of their aggregate tax burden to finance a
particular public service may expect more from the govern-
ment providing that service. If certain citizens have lower (or
higher) expectations from a service or a government, then, all
else equal, they are more (or less) likely to be satisfied with
that service (or the government that provides the service) than
individuals with higher (lower) expectations who receive the
same public service.
This paper explores the relationship between citizen aware-
ness of their aggregate tax burden to finance a local public
service (or the tax salience) and whether they feel they are
“getting their money’s worth” for their spending (a proxy for
citizen satisfaction), as well as the extent to which they would
support increased spending on a public service (i.e., public el-
ementary and secondary education). We focus on the role of
citizen awareness of their property tax burden, which reflects
the price of financing local public services. We specifically ex-
amine if citizens who are less aware of their property tax (and
property tax payment amount) are more (or less) satisfied
with their local government than other citizens in the same
school district or same county that are likely more aware of
the size of their property tax payment.
In this study, we argue that there may be two (interrelated)
channels through which tax salience might influence citizen
satisfaction and support for higher public spending—changes
in expectations and fiscal illusion. Relying on insights from the
theoretical work on the formation and role of citizen expec-
tations in evaluating public services, we first hypothesize that
reduced awareness (i.e., lower tax salience) of one’s annual
tax burden to finance local public services will be correlated
with lower expectations of the taxing government. When
individuals have lower expectations, they are more likely to be
satisfied with the public services they receive (i.e., ED). Second,
lower tax salience due to indirect taxation may also lead cit-
izens to underestimate their aggregate tax liability (fiscal il-
lusion). Therefore, due to such fiscal illusion, citizens can
become more favorable to increasing government spending on
public services and taxes that finance these services.

awareness of their tax burden) and citizens evaluations of
government, we exploit variation in how individuals pay their

These taxes are highly salient to these individuals, and they
are very aware of their annual property tax burden. This sali-
ence is one reason why the property tax is considered to be the

support for local school districts for two reasons. First, virtu-
ally all residential homeowners in the US live within a school

local property tax. Thus, if the perception of a government is
influenced by the salience of the taxes these governments rely
on, changes in property tax salience are more likely to influ-
ence perceptions of public schools in the US than other types
of governments.

with higher satisfaction with their local school districts. That
is, homeowners who use a tax escrow account report higher
satisfaction with their school district than homeowners who
pay the same amount of property tax but pay their tax bill
directly, even after we control for a wide range of individual,
zip code, school district, and county-level characteristics.
Moreover, taxpayers who pay their property taxes via an es-
crow and are thus less aware of their aggregate tax burden
are more supportive of increased spending on public educa-
tion than homeowners who pay their property taxes directly.
To caveat these conclusions, however, we are only able to
identify conditional correlations and thus these correlations
should not be interpreted as implying a causal link between
tax escrow account utilization and increased citizen satisfac-
tion with school districts.
This paper makes several contributions to the public ad-
ministration and public management literature. First, we
contribute to the growing literature that finds (1) cognitive
and behavioral biases in how citizens evaluate government
(notably in citizen satisfaction surveys) and (2) many of
the determinants of citizen satisfaction of local public serv-
ices are outside of the control of local governments and the
street-level bureaucrats that provide these services (Hjortskov
2017; James and Van Ryzin 2017; Jilke and Baekgaard 2020).
Specifically, we find a negative correlation between salience
and satisfaction, which is robust to controlling for the size
of the tax and the quality of public services. Thus, we show
how simply changing the way a government administers a tax
could prime citizens to be either satisfied or dissatisfied with a
particular level of government and its services.
We also provide novel evidence on how different modes of
taxation and payment methods can influence citizen expecta-
tions of government. These findings directly add to the large
body of literature in public administration on citizen satisfac-
tion, which largely relies on the expectancy-disconfirmation
(ED) model. Specifically, we show how policies can be
correlated with citizens’ expectations (a key component of
the ED model). Thus, researchers should not automatically
assume that (1) expectations within a jurisdiction are ho-
mogenous even after controlling for observable characteris-
tics and (2) expectations are exogenous (Van Ryzin 2004).
Second, we demonstrate that, controlling for a wide range of
individual and community-level characteristics, tax salience
and the related correlations with expectations not only matter
for citizen satisfaction but also matter for citizen support for
additional government spending.
Lastly, in this paper, we hope to highlight the importance
of tax salience for public administrators and public adminis-
tration scholars. We demonstrate that reduced tax salience is
correlated with increased support for governments that rely
on the property tax, as well as reduced aversion to the prop-
erty tax. While reducing the tax salience of relatively stable
taxes, such as the property tax, can improve the financial
management of local governments by increasing cash flow
(via more frequent payments), lower tax salience may be
correlated with lower propensity for citizens to monitor the
actions (and efficiency) of local governments and more im-
plicitly tolerate tax increases. Therefore, understanding tax
salience is crucial for balancing effective fiscal policy with
core public administration values such as democratic engage-
ment, transparency, and governmental accountability.
Background and theory
The stability of expectations in evaluations of
government
In the public administration literature, studies that ex-
amine the determinants and role of expectations in citizen
evaluations of government, particularly studies of citizen sat-
isfaction, largely rely on the ED model (hereafter referred to
as the ED model) (James 2009; Morgeson 2012; Petrovsky,
Mok, and León-Cázares 2017; Poister and Thomas 2011;
Roch and Poister 2006; Van Ryzin 2004, 2006, 2007;
Zhang et al. 2021). Originating from psychology and mar-
keting studies of consumer behavior, the ED model holds
that consumers develop different expectations of the quality
of a public good or service based on the information avail-
able to them, how they evaluate and rank information, their
historical experience with a government or receiving a par-
ticular service, the performance of peer governments, among
other factors (Cardozo 1965; Favero and Kim 2020 ; Oliver
1997). As summarized by Van Ryzin (2004), individuals form
judgements about service quality and compare this to their
expectations using information available to them. Any differ-
ence between an individual’s expectations of a service and the
perceived quality of that service is “disconfirmation.”2 Any

Despite the prominent role that citizen expectations of gov-
ernment play in the ED model, many studies in the public ad-
ministration literature find that net effect of expectation on
satisfaction is often weak, potentially overstated in models that
include subtractive measures of disconfirmation, and the direc-
tion of the relationship between expectations and satisfaction is
inconsistent across studies (Favero and Kim 2020; Filtenborg,
Gaardboe, and Sigsgaard-Rasmussen 2017; Hjortskov 2019;
James 2009; Van Ryzin 2004, 2006, 2013; Van Ryzin et al.
2004). As argued by Favero and Kim (2020), the inconsistency
across studies may stem from weaknesses in how expectations
are defined and measured. Favero and Kim (2020) differentiate
between two types of expectations: normative expectations, or
what should happen, and predictive expectations, or what will
happen. Consistent with their theoretical argument, they find
that normative expectations are strongly and negatively related
to satisfaction in an experimental vignette study.
Beyond questions of measurement, the existing literature
may have had limited success identifying any consistent rela-
tionship between expectations and satisfaction (or evaluations
of government more broadly) because there may be limited
variation in the expectations of comparable respondents.
While some survey experiments and vignette studies induce
variation in expectations by priming subjects or introducing
a social or historical reference point (see Favero and Kim
2020), individual expectations may not vary significantly
within governments over time or across governments once a
comparable group of respondents is identified. If there is lim-
ited variation in expectations among comparable respondents
(e.g., respondents within the same government), then there
is limited variation with which to identify any meaningful,
statistically significant relationship between expectations and
individual evaluations of governments or public services.
Why, then, might individuals have stable expectations of
governments and particularly of their local government?
Specifically, we center our discussion on the potential for nor-
mative and predictive expectations to remain stable with re-
spect to school districts. We argue that there are two primary
reasons for expectations, as articulated by the ED model, to
be stable among citizens within school districts.
First, expectations may be stable because individuals
choose a community (i.e., “sort” into a community) based
on the bundle of public services and taxes (Nechyba 2020;
Tiebout 1956). Beginning with Oates (1969), studies have
consistently shown that properties in high-performing school
districts are more expensive than those in lower performing
school districts (i.e., school quality is capitalized into home
values), which implies that there is more demand for homes
in these school districts than in lower performing school
districts (Nguyen-Hoang and Yinger 2011). Since the pas-
sage of the No Child Left Behind Act (in 2001), states are
required to report school quality measures, most significantly
performance on standardized state exams. Not only do high-
performing schools often tout their performance, but private
real estate companies communicate this information to cur-
rent and future residents. Zillow, a popular residential and
rental property search tool in the US, for example, rates all
local schools out of ten with scores reflecting standardized
test performance, racial/ethnic equity, disciplinary and at-
tendance performance (e.g., percent of students chronically
absent), and allows public reviews of the local schools and
neighborhoods. There is significant evidence that these school
quality metrics influence where individuals, especially those
with school-age children, choose to live (Bayer, Ferreira, and
McMillian 2004; Figlio and Lucas 2004; Francisco 2010 ).
Second, once individuals have chosen a community (or a
school district), there may be no significant variation in the
average performance of a school over relatively short time
periods (at least with respect to public school districts and
individual schools within school districts). While there may
be significant variations in expectations across different
schools and school districts, there might be less or no sig-
nificant variation within a school over short periods (Gray
et al. 1995; Heinrich and Lynn 2001; Lloyd and Schachner
2021; Manderville and Anderson 1987).3 While the changes
in performances of nearby competitive school districts may
influence satisfaction (Favero and Kim 2020), unless there is
a significant change—either positive or negative—in the per-
formance of a given school, especially for the years in which
a household’s children may be enrolled in that school, citizens
may have continually high (or low) expectations of a school
based on how they perceived the quality of that school when
they located to that particular district.
As we explain more below, in our initial empirical models,
we focus on how individuals living within the same school
district in the US differentially evaluate their school districts.4
In subsequent empirical models, we relax this assumption
that preferences are homogenous within school districts and
the performance of (i.e., quality of) school districts does not
change in relatively short periods of time by expanding our
counterfactual to individuals within the same county in the
same US state and then controlling for a variety of school
district-level socioeconomic characteristics and performance
measures, including changes in school district academic per-
formance over time and other local factors that may reflect
changing satisfaction with local public schools, for example,
changes in private school enrollment.
In summary, due to the sorting behavior of citizens to school
districts of their choice and the stability of school performance
within school districts, expectations may not significantly
vary over time. What might induce variation in expectations,
particularly normative expectations about what individuals
should get from a school district, is variation in how aware
individuals are of their tax liability that reflects the price of
providing public education. In other words, variation in the
tax salience of local taxes used to finance education services.

in their circumstances. Tax salience has been shown to af-
fect individuals’ perceptions of government, as well as their
responses to taxation and public spending. Early polit-
ical economists such as McCulloch (1845) and John Stuart
Mill (1848) categorized taxes as either “direct,” meaning
those paid explicitly by individuals, or “indirect,” meaning
those embedded in transactions or distributed across mul-
tiple payments (Cabral and Hoxby 2012). Direct taxes are
often paid in lump sums, allowing individuals to observe the
total amount paid to a government. Indirect taxes, in con-
trast, may be included in the price of goods or paid in small
amounts across different contexts, which may make it more
difficult for individuals to observe or aggregate their total tax
payments. Mill (1848) suggested that this structure could lead
individuals to estimate their total tax burden inaccurately, a
concept later termed “fiscal illusion” (Mills 1848; Sausgruber
and Tyran 2005). The concept of tax salience, therefore, refers
to the extent to which individuals are aware of a tax and can
identify its magnitude.
In this paper, we examine whether variations in tax salience
are associated with citizens’ satisfaction with public services
funded primarily through local taxes. We propose that this
relationship may reflect how tax salience is correlated with
citizens’ expectations of public services, which are, in turn,
associated with their satisfaction and preferences regarding
government taxation and spending. Taxes that are more sa-
lient may be more frequently noticed by citizens, potentially
influencing how they evaluate government performance,
while less salient taxes may be associated with lower levels of
citizen awareness regarding public revenue and expenditures.
Among many dimensions to tax salience, the central
features include the frequency, size, method of the payment,
and ability for the taxpayer to aggregate their annual tax li-
ability. To elaborate on this distinction, Table 1 lists three of
the major taxes in the US—the local property tax, the federal
income tax, and the sales tax.
For homeowners, property taxes when paid directly, are
highly salient because they are paid infrequently (typically
once a year, thus individuals remember when they pay the
tax), in large amounts, and it is easy to determine their annual
tax burden at the time of payment. Property taxes are also
taxes raised by local governments, which are geographically
closest to most homeowners, and these taxes finance locally
provided public services (e.g., education, police, and garbage
collection).
The salience of the federal and state income tax will vary
across individuals and households, but it is arguably less
salient than the property tax. For most non-self-employed
individuals, federal and state income taxes are withheld by
their employer each pay period. However, each taxpayer
still needs to complete and file a federal and state tax return
each year, and thus they become aware of their aggregate tax
burden when filing their return. This act of filing increases the
salience of an, otherwise, less salient tax due to withholding.
The sales tax is a tax frequently paid on many commer-
cial transactions in all but four US states; however, most
individuals never need to (or even can) easily aggregate the
amount of sales tax they pay over the course of a year. Unlike
with state and federal income taxes, there is no governmental
entity tracking the aggregate sales tax paid by an individual
in all jurisdictions where the individual purchases taxable
goods, pays the sales tax, and provides this information to
them (e.g., unlike with a W2 form). Thus, given its frequent
and fragmented nature, and the fact that the sales tax does
not apply to all types of transactions (e.g., many states ex-
empt food purchases from the sales tax), it is hard for an in-
dividual to aggregate their (annual) total sales tax liability. As
a result, the sales tax is relatively less salient than the income
tax or the property tax.
Previous studies have found that decreased tax salience
increases tolerance for higher tax rates and government
spending. Finklestein (2009) uses the implementation of elec-
tronic toll collection (ETC) by state and local governments in
the US, which reduces the salience of tolls on US highways,
to examine how changes in tax salience impact tax-rate set-
ting behavior, as well as the timing of when tax rates change.
She finds that after the implementation of ETC, toll rates
increased by between 20 and 40 percent compared to when
tolls were collected manually (i.e., when tolls were more sa-
lient). She also finds that following the adoption of ETC, local
officials are more likely to increase toll rates during election
years, and thus decreased tax salience reduces the political
costs of raising tax rates (and thus revenues). Similarly, Cabral
and Hoxby (2012) find that when the local property tax is
less salient, politicians are more likely to increase the prop-
erty tax levy. Moreover, they further find that, in communities
where the property tax is less salient to homeowners, they are
less likely to enact property tax limits, which statutorily cap
increases in the property tax rate or levy. Findings from both
studies imply fiscal illusion at the aggregate level, suggesting
that low tax salience makes public services appear cheaper
than they actually are, and this fiscal illusion may contribute
to suboptimal and excessive growth of the public sector (Mills
1848). Several experimental studies also provide evidence of
fiscal illusion at the individual level, suggesting how taxpayers
may underestimate the true cost of services depending on the
structure and design of the tax system (Chetty, Looney, and
Kroft 2009; Numa 2024).

Importantly, an individual can pay two taxes of the same
liability (i.e., the same monetary amount); however, if one tax
is highly salient and another tax is less salient, we expect the
highly salient tax to have a greater effect on an individual’s
expectations. Tax liability, the amount an individual pays,
may arguably increase predictive expectations. That is, one
might expect, holding all else constant, that the higher the tax
liability, the more or better-quality public services a house-
hold will receive. The salience of the tax, however, draws the
taxpayer’s attention to the presence of the tax, the size of the
tax liability, and the performance of the government, whereas
less salient taxes (or policies that reduce the salience of taxes)
reduce the likelihood that taxpayers pay attention to their tax
bill or any increases in public spending (Bordalo, Gennaioli,
and Shleifer 2022; Chetty, Looney, and Kroft 2009; Taubinsky
and Rees-Jones 2018). Moreover, less salient taxes, compared
to more salient taxes, reduces the propensity for the payee to
ask if “they are getting their money’s worth” when paying
their tax bill, and lower salient taxes will lower citizens’ ex-
pectations of what exactly they should be getting for their
taxes. Thus, consistent with the underlying cognitive model
implicit in the ED model, the theoretical argument of Favero
and Kim (2020), and the theory of fiscal illusions induced by
indirect taxes, we argue that high tax salience draws taxpayer
attention to a government’s performance and the services it
provides, whereas low tax salience reduces such attention
and lowers expectations by obscuring the cost of financing
local services. As a result, we expect less salient taxes to be
correlated with higher levels of support for and satisfaction
with the taxing government.
Tax salience and tax escrow
To examine the extent to which tax salience influences re-
ported citizen satisfaction with school districts, we exploit
variation in the way a majority of first-time homeowners with
mortgages in the US pay their property tax bills: via a prop-
erty tax escrow.5 If a homeowner uses a tax escrow service, the
tax bill is sent to the lender (e.g., the bank) that services the
mortgage, and this servicer will bill the homeowner monthly
for their combined mortgage, taxes, and any related insurance
(Cabral and Hoxby 2012). Essentially, if a homeowner has
a tax escrow, their tax bill is spread over 12 months, rather
than paid in one or two large payments. The tax bill is also
combined with their mortgage and insurance payments, and
thus it becomes difficult to easily determine total tax burden
over the course of the year. Thus, utilizing a tax escrow service
reduces the salience of the property tax.
Importantly, a tax escrow does not change the amount of
a household’s property tax. A homeowner who pays their
property tax via tax escrow is still paying the same amount
of money to their local school district each year as a home-
owner (of a home with the same value) that directly pays their
local school district. The only difference is that the home-
owner paying their property taxes via an escrow is paying
their property taxes in smaller amounts in conjunction with
their mortgage each month while the other homeowner is
paying their property tax bill directly and in one or two large
payments.
Exploring the link between tax salience (induced by
paying the property tax via an escrow) and citizen satisfac-
tion with a particular government or government service is
complicated by the institutional features of the local gov-
ernment system in the US: there are many levels of local
governments in the US that can levy different types of taxes
(e.g., sales taxes and property taxes) to finance a range of
various public services.
To better identify any correlation between tax salience
and citizen satisfaction, we focus on school districts in the
US.6 While about half of school funding in the US comes
from intergovernmental aid provided by state governments,
the remaining half of funding comes from local revenue
sources and over 80 percent of these local revenues come
from the local property tax (National Center for Education
Statistics 2023). Moreover, not only is there a clear link be-
tween local property taxes and local school districts in the
US, but school districts also primarily provide one type of
public service—public education from kindergarten to grade
12. Thus, citizen satisfaction with their local school govern-
ment should closely correlate with citizen satisfaction with
the quality of their local primary and secondary educational
services.
Drawing on variation in the use of a tax escrow and the
proposed theory about the relationship between salience and
satisfaction, our first hypothesis is:
Hypothesis 1: Homeowners who use a tax escrow will be
more satisfied with their school districts than those who do
not use a tax escrow.
Specifically, we hypothesize that tax escrows will decrease tax
salience. Citizens less aware of their tax liability will expect
less from their tax dollars (citizen expectations will be lower),
and this will increase citizen satisfaction (holding school
district performance constant). Conversely, homeowners
who do not use a tax escrow (and thus pay taxes directly)
will be more aware of the cost of funding local education,
have higher expectations, and thus be less satisfied with their
school districts.
While citizen satisfaction is an important outcome that
is carefully monitored by local public servants, these local
officials also are sensitive to a host of complementary
outcomes that could be related to citizen satisfaction. If cit-
izens are satisfied with a local government or the quality of
some local public service, then one might expect that this sat-
isfaction should be positively correlated with support for ad-
ditional government spending.
To explore this possibility, we follow Van Ryzin et al.
(2004) and examine whether citizens support higher taxes
and government spending on a particular service. Specifically,
we measure citizens’ willingness to support further govern-
ment spending on public schools through tax increases. Thus,
our second hypothesis is:

Data and methods
Survey data
Our survey data are from a proprietary survey conducted
by Dynata, a national private survey company, from
September 20, 2022, through December 10, 2022. Dynata
recruited participants into the survey balanced by region of
the US, sex, age, race, and education. We utilize the responses
from individuals who report being homeowners rather than
renters, and thus they directly receive a property tax bill from
their local governments rather than indirectly paying prop-
erty taxes via their rent. These individuals are located in 39
US States and the District of Columbia.7
For this sample of homeowners, we have detailed individual-
level demographic, socioeconomic, and political characteris-
tics, which are summarized in Table 2. Column 1 of Table
2 reports the average of these characteristics for the 3,989
individuals (44 percent of the sample) who report using a tax
escrow while Column 2 reports the average of these charac-
teristics for the 5,094 individuals (56 percent) who do not use
a tax escrow. In Column 3, we report the absolute value of
the difference of each characteristic between the escrow and
non-escrow groups, and we indicate whether this difference is
statistically significantly different from zero.
We find many differences between those who use tax escrow
compared to those that do not, namely: the escrow sample is
younger, more likely to be Hispanic or African American, more
likely to be married, less likely to be widowed or divorced,
and more likely to be in the higher income brackets. Given
this imbalance between the two groups of respondents, we
will control for these characteristics in our empirical models,
as described in the next section. To further address any
differences between the escrow-using and non-escrow-using
groups, we also test the robustness of our results to the in-
clusion of different zip code, school district, and county-level
covariates, as well as to specifications in which we vary our
fixed effect strategy. However, given the nonrandom nature of
escrow utilization, we only present conditional correlations.
All results should not be interpreted as causal estimates.
Our outcomes of interest also come from these survey data.
Table 3 lists our outcomes of interest, the key concept it meas-
ures (e.g., citizen satisfaction of public service and support for
additional taxes and spending), the full text of the survey ques-
tion, the response options available to respondents, and how
we transformed these response options into binary outcomes.
For example, in our “Support Increase Spending” question,
which is one measure of support for government (proxied for
by a respondent’s willingness to support additional taxes and
spending), we construct a binary measure of support where
our outcome of interest will adopt the value one if the re-
spondent answered they “strongly support” or “somewhat
support” additional spending, and zero if they “somewhat
oppose” or “strongly oppose” additional spending.
Previous studies have emphasized how the order in which
questions are asked can influence reported satisfaction or
other outcomes by priming respondents to answer in a spe-
cific manner (Hjortskov 2017; Van de Walle and Van Ryzin
2011). In our study, the question about property tax escrow
use was asked in the demographic section at the end of the
survey, and thus these questions could not influence responses
to the government evaluation questions.8
In addition to these data, we use data from the US 1990,
2000, 2010, and 2020 decennial census data, the 2021 and
2022 1-Year American Community Survey Data, 2020 5-year
American Community Survey Data, data from the National
Center for Education Statistics, and data from the Stanford
Education Data Archives. We describe specific variables used
in the Results section.

Heterogeneity across age groups and community
characteristics
As we note above, escrow use is likely endogenous: older
individuals are less likely to have a mortgage and may have
more financial resources, making them less likely to use escrow
accounts. Prior research has shown that older individuals are
generally less supportive of K-12 spending (Figlio and Fletcher
2012; Fletcher and Kenny 2008; Ladd and Murray 2001;
Poterba 1997). To assess potential heterogeneity, we interact
escrow use with age, zip code-level median household income,
and both self-reported and zip code-level property values, and
we estimate marginal effects across the distribution of each var-
iable. Supplementary Appendix Figures A1 through A8 visu-
alize these marginal effects with 95 percent confidence intervals
for both the satisfaction and support outcomes. Collectively, in
these figures, the results suggest that while individual charac-
teristics like self-reported home value show minimal heteroge-
neity, the relationship between tax salience and satisfaction and
support for school spending appears to vary modestly by age
and are more pronounced across broader contextual factors
like neighborhood income and property values. Please see the
Supplementary Appendix for a more detailed discussion.
Relationship between satisfaction and support
Up to this point, we have examined support and satisfaction
separately. However, there may be a joint relationship be-
tween these outcomes. For example, individuals who are sat-
isfied with a service may not necessarily demand more of that
service. Conversely, dissatisfaction with a service could drive
support for increased spending, particularly if current services
are seen as insufficient, or even lower support for the service.
To examine joint patterns of satisfaction and support, we
construct four categorical outcomes representing all pair-
wise combinations of responses: satisfied and supportive of
increased spending (40.47 percent of the sample), satisfied
but opposed to increased spending (16.08 percent), dissat-
isfied but supportive (13.32 percent), and dissatisfied and
opposed (30.10 percent). These categories capture different
attitudes toward school quality and funding—ranging from
endorsement of current services and further investment to
skepticism about the effectiveness or adequacy of spending.
Supplementary Appendix A Table A2 analyzes the relation-
ship between escrow account use and each outcome. We find
that escrow use is positively associated with being both satis-
fied and supportive (Panel A) and negatively associated with
being dissatisfied and opposed (Panel D). However, there is
no significant relationship between escrow use and the two
intermediate categories (Panels B and C), suggesting that tax
salience is jointly correlated with both satisfaction and sup-
port for increased spending.
Tax salience and tax preferences
Next, we turn our attention to other implications of decreased
tax salience at one level of government (e.g., a local school
district or municipality) on perceptions of revenue sources for
other levels of government (e.g., the federal government). The
underlying idea is to test whether changes or differences in the
salience of one particularly salient tax significantly shapes the
taxpayer’s overall aversion to other taxes. We speculate that
decreased property tax salience may shift citizens’ focus (and
expectations) toward the level of government with the next
most salient taxes. In the US context, the federal income tax is
arguably one of the most hated taxes, following the property
tax (Jimenez and Rafool 2023). As noted in Table 1 above,
different taxes have different levels of salience.
To examine this question, we use the responses to the fol-
lowing survey question: “What do you think is your least fa-
vorite tax—federal income tax, federal social security tax, state
income tax, state sales tax, or local property tax.” In the survey,
we ask respondents to choose one of these tax options that is
their least favorite. As shown in Table 5, we find that, regardless
of the specification, paying one’s property tax via tax escrow
is associated with less aversion to the local property tax (Panel
A) and increased aversion to the federal income tax (Panel E).
These results suggest that when the salience of the property
tax is reduced, respondents become more averse to paying the
federal income tax, a relatively more salient tax compared to
other options available (social security tax, state sales tax, etc.).
Meanwhile, tax aversion against social security tax levied by
the federal government as well as state income and sales tax
are not statistically different between escrow users and non-
users. This may be related to the fact that the average tax aver-
sion against these three taxes is smaller, and the variations are
meager, compared to federal income tax and local property tax.

in Supplementary Appendix Table A3, we use a conditional
logit specification (a McFadden choice model), which explicitly

The results in Table 5 are telling. For homeowners facing
a less salient property tax, they become more adverse toward
arguably the next most salient tax, which is the federal income
tax. Stated directly, for homeowners that use a tax escrow ac-
count and are less aware of their tax liability, their aversion to
the property tax decreases, and this aversion shifts to more sa-
lient taxes. If a respondent’s aversion toward a tax proxies for
their satisfaction with public services and support for the level
of government that levies this tax (i.e., increased aversion to the
federal income tax is associated with less satisfaction with the
federal government), then these results suggest that increased
tax salience associated with one level of government (e.g., a
local school district or municipal government) can reduce satis-
faction with this government while increasing satisfaction with
another level of government with the next most salient tax.
These results further suggest that merely changing the
way one level of government taxes its residents can influence
not only how these residents evaluate this level of govern-
ment, but also how they evaluate other levels of government.
While comparing the performance and service quality of
one’s government to similar “peer” governments is generally
considered to be an important component of how individuals
form expectations and determine their overall satisfaction
with a government or service (Favero and Kim 2020), it has
been implicitly assumed in the literature that this comparison
happens between peer governments that provide the same type
of public service or bundle of services. When evaluating the
quality of their local police departments, for example, a house-
hold might compare crime levels in their community to crime
levels in other communities. However, these results suggest that
crime may not only influence how they evaluate a particular
government, but how other overlapping governments in the
same area impose and administer taxes may also influence how
they perceive the quality of and determine their level of satis-
faction with their police department. If their school district, for
example, imposes highly salient property taxes paid directly,
whereas the police department primarily receives its tax rev-
enue from less salient sales taxes, individuals may evaluate the
police department more favorably independent of high-quality
services and/or low crime rates.

and the expectation disconfirmation model, we argue that cit-
izens with lower (higher) expectations for a public service or
government tend to have higher (lower) satisfaction with that
public service or government and be more (less) willing to
support increases in government spending. Using variations
in tax salience induced by how different households pay the
property tax in the US, we find that homeowners who pay
their property tax indirectly via an escrow, and thus are less
aware of the property tax, are more satisfied with their school
district and more willing to support tax increases to finance
additional spending.
These results are robust across a number of different model
specifications, including varying our fixed effect structure (and
thus our comparison group), as well as the covariates we in-
clude in the model. Collectively, these results provide support
for our proposed hypotheses that tax salience can draw the
taxpayers’ attention to a specific government, and this increased
attention is positively correlated with the expectations of these
taxpayers and correspondingly negatively associated with sat-
isfaction and overall evaluation of this government.
This paper contributes to the public administration litera-
ture by showing that reduced tax salience—how noticeable
taxes are—is positively correlated with citizen satisfaction
with government services, even when service quality is held
constant. It highlights that taxation methods and payment
frequency can shape citizen expectations and support for gov-
ernment spending, challenging assumptions of uniform and
exogenous expectations in the ED model. Finally, the paper
underscores the dual implications of tax salience: while re-
ducing tax salience can improve fiscal management and re-
duce political pressure to reduce local government reliance
on the property tax (which is a very stable revenue source), it
may also weaken citizen oversight and accountability.
Moreover, these results contribute to a growing literature
on how psychological biases (e.g., the presentation of per-
formance information) and a range of other factors (e.g., the
political party in power)—which are largely outside of the
control of government and public servants—can influence cit-
izens reported satisfaction with public services (Andersen and
Hjortskov 2016; Baekgaard and Serritzlew 2016; Hjortskov
2017; James and Van Ryzin 2017; Jilke and Baekgaard 2020;
Marvel 2016; Olsen 2015). While the existing literature has
focused mostly on citizens’ perceptions about government
performance or service quality, we introduce another missing
component that may enter into an individual’s calculus that
shapes attitudes toward government: citizens’ relative aware-
ness of their tax liability which is the price they pay for local
public services in the form of local property tax may also
be an important determinant of citizens’ attitude toward
governments and their services.
We note that this study has several limitations in addition
to our inability to firmly establish a causal link between sali-
ence and expectations. Our findings may have limited gener-
alizability and mostly help explain the differences in citizens’
evaluation of local governments that are geographically prox-
imate to the residents in a decentralized system, as well as
governments that provide public services primarily funded by
local tax revenue. Also, the relationship between tax salience
and citizen satisfaction and attitude toward the government
may only apply to public services where there is a close link
between taxes paid to a government and benefits and serv-
ices received from that government. Moreover, homeowners
are usually aware of (and highly sensitive to) the quality
of services provided by their local school districts (due to
Tiebout-type sorting). While we have demonstrated that our
results are robust to controlling for various community and
neighborhood characteristics, the true relationship between
tax salience and citizen perception of the government may
vastly depend on various contextual factors related to local
governance, such as the transparency of the quality of a local
public service or total tax liability.

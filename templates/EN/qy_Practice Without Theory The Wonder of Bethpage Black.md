ABSTRACT
Bethpage State Park's Black Course offers a rare historical case of administrative capacity emerging under conditions of uncer-
tainty, fiscal constraint, and urgent public purpose during the Great Depression. Built with relief labor and without the benefit
of fully developed public administration or budgeting doctrines, the project required administrators to invent workable practices
as construction unfolded. Drawing on extensive archival records, this article reconstructs Bethpage as a historical case of ad-
ministrative capacity emerging through practice rather than formal design. The analysis identifies four interrelated mechanisms
through which capacity developed: alignment of ambition and necessity, coordination through routine budgeting and reporting,
discipline embedded in oversight, and endurance through institutional memory. Together, these mechanisms show how effective
governance can arise from improvisation, constraint, and learning under pressure. The case demonstrates that administrative
discipline and creativity need not be opposites and offers practical insights for contemporary public managers facing complex,
uncertain, and time-­sensitive challenges.
1   |   Introduction
In the spring of 1936, the fences came down around a newly built
public golf course on Long Island. Where forests and fields once
lay, there now stretched a landscape of deliberate form: Bethpage
State Park's Black Course. Conceived in the depths of the Great
Depression, the course was financed through relief programs
and built by thousands of untrained workers under the supervi-
sion of superintendent Joseph Burbeck (Blakelock 1949; Federal
Works Agency 1943). It bore the creative mark of architect A.
W. Tillinghast and the administrative imprint of Robert Moses,
the ambitious public official who would soon define New York's
transformation (Caro 1974; Ballon and Jackson 2007).
The result of Moses' vision, Tillinghast's artistry, and Burbeck's
endurance was more than a golf course. It was a work of public art
produced within an administrative environment that standard
accounts of public administration would predict to be highly
unfavorable to success. The project unfolded before the vocab-
ularies of public administration, budgeting, and performance
had fully matured. Core theories of hierarchy, accountability,
and control were still taking shape (Goodnow 1900; White 1926;
Gulick and Urwick  1937). In their place stood improvisation,
contingency, and governance by necessity rather than by de-
sign (Smith  2006; Federal Works Agency 1943; Macmahon
et al. 1941).
In this sense, the creation of Bethpage Black can be understood
as a wonder of practice without theory. It was a wonder in the
institutional sense: a product of contingent alignment among ad-
ministrative structure, improvisation, and individual judgment
that was unlikely to be replicated. The project took shape at a
moment when emerging administrative forms met urgent public
purpose, and when Depression-­era labor policy collided with de-
manding design objectives. During that moment, Robert Moses
used the flexibility of new institutional arrangements to pro-
duce a result that contemporaries and later observers regarded
as exceptional in both quality and ambition. Working with
Burbeck and Tillinghast, he brought into being a public golf
course that has been widely recognized for its exceptional de-
sign quality and public significance. That reputation is reflected
both in contemporaneous press accounts and in its consistent

By reconstructing the creation of Bethpage Black as a histori-
cal case of administrative practice preceding theory, the article
contributes to the study of public administration in three ways.
First, it places the limits of institutional design in historical con-
text by examining a moment when modern administrative the-
ory had not yet been codified or systematically applied. The case
shows how coordination, discipline, and endurance emerged
through action under constraint rather than through formal
prescription.
Second, it extends contemporary debates on institutional design
and adaptive governance (Meier and O'Toole Jr.  2011; Ansell
and Torfing  2021). The case shows that administrative excel-
lence cannot be reduced to prior administrative design or for-
mal planning but is produced through temporary misalignment,
improvisation, and disciplined friction among competing forms
of authority.
Finally, it demonstrates how administrative capacity can
emerge under conditions that are normatively risky as well as
productive. The Bethpage case shows how judgment, discre-
tion, and informal coordination operated within constraints
that translated urgency into durable practice. At the same time,
the institutional flexibility that enabled Bethpage underscores a
classical insight rooted in Adam Smith's political economy and
later popularized by James Madison. That insight holds that in-
stitutions must be designed with human fallibility in mind, for
men, not angels (Smith 1776; Madison 1788). Bethpage thus be-
comes an empirical puzzle of governance: how, and under what
fleeting conditions, flawed actors operating within imperfect
institutional arrangements can nevertheless produce something
enduring.
Drawing on extensive archival materials, this article traces
how administrative capacity emerged through four interrelated
mechanisms rather than through formal design. The analysis
does not advance hypotheses for testing but instead identifies
recurring patterns in how administrative capacity took shape at
Bethpage: the alignment of ambition and urgency, coordination
through routine practice, discipline embedded in oversight, and
endurance through institutional memory. Taken together, these
mechanisms illustrate how administrative capacity was gener-
ated through practice, with theory, and formal doctrine follow-
ing over time.
2   |   Historical Context: Building Without Theory
The creation of Bethpage State Park's Black Course unfolded
during a period of profound transformation in American gov-
ernance. Constructed between 1934 and 1936, in the depths of
the Great Depression, the project coincided with federal experi-
mentation in new forms of authority, finance, and coordination.
These experiments would ultimately help define the modern ad-
ministrative state.
Prior to the economic crisis, Progressive reformers had treated
budgeting and administration as moral instruments for impos-
ing order and restraint (Willoughby 1918; Mosher 1954). These
ideals proved inadequate under conditions of mass unemploy-
ment and fiscal collapse, requiring a reinvention of public ad-
ministration in real time. The New Deal transformed emergency
measures into standard operating procedure, compelling offi-
cials at all levels to act first and rationalize later. Improvisation
became routine as federal, state, and local arrangements were
layered together in practice (Smith 2006).
Relief agencies such as the Temporary Emergency Relief
Administration (TERA), the Public Works Administration
(PWA), and the Works Progress Administration (WPA) were
forced to develop systems capable of operating at unprece-
dented scale and speed. Planning often followed action rather
than preceding it, as administrative practices such as reporting,
budgeting, and oversight evolved alongside ongoing projects,
producing what contemporaries later described as planning by
doing (Smith 2006).
Federal administrators later acknowledged that early relief ef-
forts struggled not because of flawed intent, but because state
and local governments lacked advance planning capacity and
instead learned through experience in the course of operation
(Federal Works Agency 1943, iv). Contemporary observers like-
wise noted that stable administrative routines often emerged
at state and local levels before federal headquarters had fully
clarified organizational roles. This pattern reinforced a se-
quence in which implementation preceded formal coordination
(Macmahon et al. 1941; Fesler et al. 1946).
New York State's prominence in federal relief spending further
shaped this administrative environment. When New Deal pro-
grams were launched, New York could absorb WPA labor imme-
diately because land, financing mechanisms, and administrative
arrangements were already in place. Moses actively leveraged
this readiness to move projects from authorization to execution
with minimal delay (Caro 1974; Doig 1966).
State authorities emerged as laboratories of improvisation in
this environment. One such authority was the Bethpage Park
Authority (BPA), created in 1933 specifically to acquire and

Organizationally, the Bethpage project was launched by the
Long Island State Park Commission (LISPC), which functioned
as the operational arm of the BPA. Although legally distinct, the
two entities operated as a unified administrative team under
common leadership. As chairman, Moses adapted Progressive-­
era budgeting practices to the conditions of crisis. Through the
BPA, he leveraged park revenues to secure land, meet bond ob-
ligations, and coordinate with federal relief programs, allowing
construction to proceed without waiting for large legislative
appropriations (Caro  1974; Ballon and Jackson  2007). Under
this model, public works became both self-­financing and self-­
legitimatizing. Fiscal constraint and administrative invention
met on common ground, reflecting a broader shift from rigid
control toward coordination under pressure (Schick  1966;
Peters 2019).
Bethpage exemplified this improvisational environment in
both scale and ambition. While initially leased, the state for-
mally acquired the 1368 acres of parkland in May 1934 for
$1,000,000 (Blakelock  1949, 291, 293). The acquisition was
intended both to democratize access to an exceptional recre-
ational facility previously available only to private club mem-
bers, and to demonstrate the productive capacity of relief labor.
In this spirit, Moses explicitly framed Bethpage as a “people's
country club,” emphasizing both public access and civic pur-
pose (Brooklyn Daily Eagle 1935, May 27). At peak periods, ap-
proximately 1800 relief workers were employed at the site, all
drawn from unemployment rolls (Blakelock 1949, 294). Once
WPA labor was deployed, construction proceeded rapidly,
with the Black Course completed in roughly 2 years despite
its exceptional length and extensive earthmoving and design
requirements. At the time, no public golf complex had been
built at comparable scale or speed, with the explicit ambition
of equaling any championship course in the nation (New York
Times, April 19, 1935). Nor had such a project been undertaken
under relief rules that prohibited the architect from using his
own construction crews. Complicating matters further, the
Black Course was built alongside multiple other Bethpage
courses and park facilities subject to strict efficiency require-
ments, without being administratively privileged.
Under these conditions, it depended on a contingent alignment
of ambition, constraint, administrative craft, and endurance.
Conceived within the logic of emergency relief but executed
with artistic ambition, the project reflected the tensions of the
New Deal state between discipline and improvisation. These
conditions created a setting in which administrative capacity
emerged through practice rather than prior design.
3   |   Method and Analytical Approach
This study employs a historical-­institutional and interpretive
design to examine how Bethpage Black was conceived and built
at a moment when administrative practice preceded the codi-
fication and systematic application of modern administrative
theory. Rather than treating the project as the implementation
of established models, the analysis reconstructs Bethpage as a
lived experiment in governance, shaped by administrative prac-
tice and institutional routines under conditions of urgency, con-
straint, and evolving authority.
The interpretive approach builds on a long tradition in public
administration that emphasizes learning from experience rather
than deduction from formal theory. This tradition includes
Mosher's (1954) call to ground administrative theory in practice,
Waldo's  (1948) insistence on the moral and historical context
of administration, and Goodsell's (1981) conception of admin-
istration as craft. More recent scholarship extends this lineage
through narrative inquiry, historical contextualism, and inter-
pretive approaches that emphasize meaning, historical context,
and the reconstruction of administrative beliefs and practices
(Ospina and Dodge 2005; Moynihan 2009; Raadschelders 2000;
Bevir and Rhodes 2022). Recent scholarship further underscores
how qualitative research strengthens theoretical development
by fleshing out context, enhancing transparency about method-
ological decisions, and enabling inquiry into how processes un-
fold and how explanatory mechanisms operate in institutional
settings (Hendren et al. 2023). The archival reconstruction of
the Bethpage project follows this logic by tracing how budget-
ing, oversight, and improvisation generated patterned adminis-
trative capacity in the absence of formal doctrine.
Consistent with this tradition, the analysis develops analytic
mechanisms inductively from practice rather than testing for-
mal hypotheses or models. The focus is on reconstructing deci-
sion sequences, coordination routines, and oversight practices as
they unfolded within the institutional environment of the New
Deal. This interpretive orientation mirrors how contemporane-
ous administrators themselves framed large-­scale public works,
emphasizing adaptive coordination, learning through practice,
and administrative improvisation under conditions of incom-
plete plans and evolving rules (Federal Works Agency 1943, iv).
3.1   |   Evidence Base
The analysis draws on archival records, contemporaneous ac-
counts, and secondary historical sources to reconstruct admin-
istrative practice during the creation of Bethpage State Park's
Black Course. These materials are used to triangulate evidence
and trace administrative processes rather than to recover indi-
vidual intent or retrospective justification.
Primary archival evidence consists of administrative, correspon-
dence, engineering, and project records generated by the LISPC
and the BPA between 1932 and 1936. These include commission
minutes, superintendent-­level reports, labor allocation summa-
ries, engineering and inspection materials, and correspondence
related to financing and oversight. These materials are held by
the New York State Office of Parks, Recreation and Historic

3.2   |   Analytical Strategy
Drawing on interpretive approaches that emphasize meaning
in administrative practice (Bevir and Rhodes 2003), the anal-
ysis treats administrative action as historically situated rather
than as the mechanical application of fixed rules. Historical-­
institutional perspectives further inform the analysis by fore-
grounding temporal sequencing and institutional development
(Orren and Skowronek 2004). Together, these perspectives guide
the tracing of causal process observations (Collier 2011) to ex-
amine how institutional design, administrative behavior, and
individual judgment interact over time to produce outcomes that
are later formalized in administrative theory. Such observations
are identified when archival decisions, contemporaneous re-
ports, and material outcomes converge across multiple sources.
Decision-­making and coordination are reconstructed through
systematic triangulation of commission minutes, correspon-
dence, superintendent-­level reports, and secondary accounts.
The analysis focuses on the observable texture of administra-
tive practice, including sequencing, coordination, conflict and
endurance, as reflected in the archival record, rather than on
inferred subjective experience. This approach allows the study
to recover dimensions of governance that are often flattened in
formal documentation, while remaining grounded in the avail-
able evidence.
3.3   |   Case Selection and Inference
Bethpage Black functions as a critical, or “least-­likely,” case of
administrative excellence (Flyvbjerg  2006). The project was
executed under conditions that standard theories of public
management and public budgeting would predict to be highly
unfavorable to success, including ambitions of unprecedented
scale, severe time constraints, fragmented authority, reliance
on relief labor, and the absence of a formalized administrative
doctrine. Its successful completion therefore permits analytic
inference about how administrative practice can, under certain
conditions, transcend institutional design limitations.
Bethpage is substantively distinctive among New Deal public
works projects. As described in the historical context above, it
combined extraordinary scale, accelerated construction sched-
ules, and reliance on large numbers of relief workers with re-
quirements to maintain high architectural and landscape
standards throughout execution. At the same time, the project
required tight coordination across state and federal adminis-
trative rules and pursued the explicit ambition of equaling any
championship course in the country, all while operating without
administrative privilege or exemption from relief-­era controls.
Moreover, unlike many public works of the period, Bethpage was
conceived as a strictly self-­liquidating enterprise (Herring 1937,
29). This structure reduced reliance on state legislative appropri-
ations and the oversight that accompanied them, but it did not
remove constraint. Instead, it shifted discipline toward immedi-
ate revenue performance, making administrative improvisation
accountable not only to bureaucratic procedure but to the proj-
ect's ability to sustain itself financially, thereby increasing both
administrative complexity and exposure to failure.
The goal of this article is not statistical generalization but ana-
lytic illumination. The case is used to clarify how administrative
improvisation, rather than formal design, structured effective
action prior to the codification of settled administrative theory.

project files, rather than on metaphor or retrospective inference.

4.1   |   Mechanism 1: The Alignment of Ambition,
Necessity, and Constraint
The construction of Bethpage Black occurred during a period
of rapid institutional change triggered by the Great Depression.
During this time, federal and state officials undertook large pub-
lic projects in the absence of the standardized budgeting, per-
sonnel, and oversight systems that later became routine. Relief
programs such as TERA and the WPA supplied labor and basic
administrative rules, but policy development frequently lagged
behind the pace of implementation, as administrators learned
through experience rather than formal design (Smith  2006;
Macmahon et  al.  1941; Federal Works Agency 1943). At
Bethpage, this dynamic is visible in the formal record of gover-
nance itself. BPA meeting minutes document early delegation of
operational authority and asset control prior to full institutional
consolidation, while LISPC minutes later acknowledged and ac-
commodated work already underway (Administrative Meeting
Minutes, Boxes 1 and 3).
Within this unsettled environment, the LISPC operated as a site
of administrative experimentation, while operational authority
for Bethpage was exercised through the BPA and its predecessor
corporation. Rather than resisting external reporting require-
ments, administrators incorporated standardized documenta-
tion and compliance procedures into routine project execution.
These practices positioned Bethpage as a legitimate and govern-
able undertaking within overlapping state and federal systems.
That administrative accommodation is evident in repeated
administrative routines documented across project correspon-
dence and records generated within the LISPC and the BPA
between 1933 and 1935, as well as in standardized accounting
schedules, engineering exhibits and audit-­oriented documen-
tation prepared for external review (see, e.g., memoranda in
the Robert Moses correspondence, 1934–1935; LISPC adminis-
trative correspondence, 1933–1935; LISPC engineering project
files).
The Bethpage case illustrates how administrative success can
emerge without a fully specified plan. Rather than being de-
signed in advance, the project depended on a balance among
three forces often treated as incompatible in governance: am-
bition, necessity, and constraint. In much public administration
theory, ambition is framed as a threat to control, necessity as a
justification for suspending rules, and constraint as an impedi-
ment to creativity (Simon 1947; Waldo 1948). At Bethpage, these
forces were not resolved through hierarchy. They were held in
productive tension, allowing vision, artistry, and administrative
discipline to reinforce one another in practice. Moses supplied
ambition through his drive to think and act on a monumental
scale. Tillinghast contributed artistry and design integrity, en-
suring that esthetic judgment did not give way to haste. Burbeck
provided the discipline of daily management, translating large
ideas into steady, organized work on the ground. The Depression
supplied necessity, making labor abundant, funds scarce, and
urgency unavoidable, thereby encouraging administrators to ac-
cept risk in the service of relief and public purpose (Smith 2006;
Federal Works Agency 1943).

constraint but its institutionalization. The New Deal imposed a
variety of constraints on the project through the oversight sys-
tems associated with the WPA and state bond authorities. These

At the operational level, this equilibrium was sustained through
concrete administrative constraints embedded in the daily rou-
tines of relief administration. These constraints took tangible
form in federal relief programs that required workers to be enu-
merated and tasks documented as a condition of continued sup-
port. While Moses traditionally relied more on authority than
persuasion, the Depression forced him to operate within an ad-
ministrative environment defined by shared responsibility and
external oversight (Doig 1966; Caro 1974). Rather than resisting
these constraints, he incorporated standardized documentation
and compliance procedures into the administration of relief-­era
projects, using them to discipline implementation and stabilize
authority. In this setting, regulatory oversight functioned less as
a limitation on discretion than as a mechanism for legitimizing
ongoing work and insulating projects from political interference.
Contemporaneous memoranda, financial schedules and prog-
ress reports produced within the LISPC and the BPA show that
reporting requirements were treated as routine administrative
tools embedded in project execution (Engineering Project Files,
Box 17, 1934–1936), with oversight and compliance documented
through Administrative General Correspondence (Boxes 2–4).
These practices reinforced continuity and control rather than
impeding action.
Necessity served as a counterweight. The economic crisis
made labor abundant and funds scarce, turning mass unem-
ployment into an urgent political problem for both federal and
state officials. That urgency opened space for administrative
experimentation. With several public works projects and plans
already underway, Moses could promise immediate jobs and
visible results, while administrators could justify taking risks
in the name of relief. Necessity, thus, became the moral force
behind the project, giving ambition a sense of public purpose
(TERA 1935; Hopkins 1936).
Constraints, in turn, served to establish discipline. State and
federal auditors tracked spending, engineers reviewed plans
and supervisors maintained detailed records of daily work.
These controls did not necessarily restrict creativity. They
helped shape it. The rules of the WPA required administrators
to translate broad ideas into measurable actions, forcing vision
to be rendered legible through documentation and reporting
(Federal Works Agency 1943; Smith 2006). Burbeck's field-­level
working records, including annotated engineering documents,
material and cost notes, and records of construction sequenc-
ing prepared under his supervision, provide direct evidence of
the craft of administration that held the effort together (LISPC
Engineering Project Files, Box 17, 1934–1936). Taken together,
these materials show how administrative control was produced
in practice through careful tracking, adjustment, and on-­site
problem-­solving, rather than imposed through formal rules or
centralized design.
From the balance of these forces arose a form of administrative
improvisation that functioned effectively even in the absence of
a clear manual. Ambition supplied momentum, necessity gave
purpose, and constraint supplied order. None of these forces
would have been sufficient on its own, but together they created
a moment of balance where productive action became possible.
This suggests that Bethpage Black's success did not emerge from
flawless design but from the contingent alignment of adminis-
trative structure and historical circumstance.
4.2   |   Mechanism 2: Coordination Through
the Budget as Dynamic Instrument
If alignment made Bethpage possible, coordination allowed an
ambitious idea to be transformed into sustained work. The day-­
to-­day organization, pacing, and management of the project did
not follow formal procedures or instruction manuals. Instead,
coordination emerged through the continuous interaction of
three practical forces: money (the flow of funds and the require-
ment to account for them), labor (the availability, scheduling,
and deployment of workers), and purpose (the shared public ob-
jective guiding the work, including the preservation of design
integrity). In this sense, the project's management structure
evolved from practice itself, rather than from top–down design.
Routine recordkeeping became a form of active coordination,
and budgeting emerged as the medium through which vision
was translated into sequence. This coordination is documented
most directly in the Engineering Project Files, where sequenc-
ing, materials, labor deployment, and expenditure adjustments
appear as ongoing, iterative traces of execution (Engineering
Project Files, Box 17, 1933–1936).
Operational records generated on-­site, together with engineer-
ing and construction correspondence prepared under Joseph
Burbeck's supervision, show how this translation occurred in
practice. WPA project summaries and expenditure tabulations
document how labor deployment and work sequencing were re-
peatedly adjusted to meet reporting requirements and funding
schedules as site conditions evolved (WPA Project Summaries,
1935–1936). At the same time, public statements during peak
construction translated these coordinated efforts into visible
narratives of progress, reinforcing the legitimacy of contin-
ued spending and work (Brooklyn Daily Eagle 1935, May 27).
Administrative control thus emerged through practice itself, as
coordination was achieved through tracking, adjustment, and
problem-­solving rather than through formal rules or centralized
design.
Robert Moses treated the budget not as a set of fixed limits but as a
flexible guide. Contemporaneous memoranda and reporting prac-
tices suggest that administrative procedure functioned as a practi-
cal means of securing approval and sustaining momentum. Forms
and reports were shaped to support that purpose. Accounting re-
cords operated as more than numerical summaries. They served
as structured representations of progress addressed to legislators,
auditors and the public, with each column of figures signaling ne-
cessity and forward motion. WPA project summaries for Nassau

completed and percentage-­of-­completion metrics. These figures
were repeatedly updated as conditions changed, turning account-

When federal administrators demanded evidence of efficiency,
Moses responded with demonstrable outputs. In this way, spend-

At the operational level, Burbeck's field records translated this
broader narrative into daily coordination. He managed the
constant exchange between activity on-­site and reports trans-

offices. He understood that progress in the field shaped the
flow of funds and that those funds, in turn, sustained contin-
ued work. Quantities of materials such as sand, pipe, and seed
were recorded to align expenditures, labor deployment, and
construction pace. Selected figures were reviewed during site in-
spections to support contract valuation and financing decisions
(Administrative General Correspondence, Box 3). Weekly sum-
maries prepared under Burbeck's supervision entered state and
WPA reporting systems, where they became part of the project's
administrative justification. In this way, recordkeeping func-
tioned as an active mechanism of coordination rather than a
passive record of activity. Budget awareness and operational ad-
justment were integrated in practice, making reporting central
to both accountability and management (LISPC Engineering
Project Files, 1934–1936).
This logic extended beyond Bethpage through the broader relief
framework. Reports issued by the TERA consistently framed
work relief as a conversion of emergency assistance into produc-
tive public activity. Spending was justified simultaneously as
economic necessity and civic investment. Projects like Bethpage
carried a dual mandate: to employ the unemployed and to pro-
duce durable public improvements. Each dollar was expected to
demonstrate not only efficiency but public value (TERA reports,
1934–1935).
At the time, no fully developed or standardized framework
existed to budget for and manage large-­scale relief projects.
Progressive-­era budgeting principles emphasized control, re-
straint and ex ante planning, but they were ill suited to projects
that required rapid mobilization, continuous adjustment and
the integration of relief labor as both means and end. Rather
than slowing progress, this absence forced administrators to
invent workable methods of coordination as projects unfolded.
Labor and materials were adjusted in response to observed
conditions rather than fixed plans. As grading and drainage
conditions varied across sections of the property, work was re-
directed to maintain momentum. Engineering and construction
records document repeated revisions in sequencing and deploy-
ment as conditions changed (LISPC Engineering Project Files,
1934–1936). In this setting, coordination became the essence of
budgeting, and budgeting became the medium through which
coordination occurred.
As a result, the boundary between budgeting and management
dissolved in practice. WPA procedures required that every task
be tied to a funding line, and every laborer to a payroll number,
but supervisors learned to treat allocations as signals of priority
rather than rigid constraints. Budgets circulated as instruments
of coordination among engineers, clerks, and foremen, aligning
effort rather than prescribing behavior. In this respect, Bethpage
anticipated what V. O. Key would later identify as the central
problem of public budgeting: deciding what deserves funding
when no objective rule can determine what is right (Key 1940).
Long before this problem was formalized in theory, Burbeck
and his foremen confronted it in daily practice, responding op-
erationally by keeping workers employed, maintaining credible
records, and sustaining forward momentum.
By the project's completion in 1936, Bethpage's budget no lon-
ger functioned as a fixed plan but as a set of ongoing adjust-
ments among labor deployment, funding schedules, and site
conditions. Decisions about sequencing and expenditures were
revised in response to changing circumstances rather than dic-
tated in advance. Budgeting thus operated as a mechanism of
continuous coordination and learning, resembling what later
scholars would describe as elements of adaptive governance
(Folke et al. 2005; Ansell and Torfing 2021). Moses' financial as-
surances, Burbeck's operational records, and WPA performance
summaries formed a single feedback system linking vision,
labor, and administrative legitimacy (LISPC and BPA financial
correspondence and administrative reports, 1934–1936; LISPC
Engineering Project Files, 1934–1936; WPA project summaries,
1935–1936). Accountability flowed upward through reports,
while coordination and decision-­making spread laterally in re-
sponse to immediate conditions. In this way, the budget func-
tioned both as a record of accomplishment and as an instrument
that sustained forward motion through the discipline of report-
ing and adjustment.
4.3   |   Mechanism 3: Discipline Through Oversight
and Limits of Design
If coordination allowed Bethpage to take form as a working
public project, discipline gave that form order and administra-
tive stability. That stabilizing force emerged less from any sin-
gle regulatory authority than from the convergence of multiple
oversight regimes, including federal relief administration, state
accounting and engineering review, bond counsel and auditors,
and ongoing scrutiny of revenue practices and public conduct
(Administrative General Correspondence, Boxes 2–4). Federal
programs, such as the WPA, established funding conditions, re-
porting requirements, and inspection regimes, while state agen-
cies, including the LISPC and relief authorities, implemented
these rules and coordinated them with state policy. Although
inspections, audits and reporting requirements might have ap-
peared to slow progress, they established the conditions under
which progress could occur. Rather than suppressing creativity,
these constraints structured the timing and form of decision-­
making, requiring administrators to translate ambition into
actions that could be documented, defended, and revised under
continuous scrutiny.
In practice, this discipline took concrete administrative
form. Relief administration operated at a high level of detail,
with expenditures itemized, payrolls certified, and material

was subject to inspection by federal officials and review by state
engineers and auditors, embedding oversight directly into daily
project execution (Federal Works Agency 1943). At Bethpage,
these requirements were met through ledgers, progress reports,
and administrative correspondence prepared under Joseph
Burbeck's supervision. Rather than functioning as a separate
layer of control, these records embedded oversight directly
into day-­to-­day coordination. Reporting did not merely docu-
ment work after the fact. It shaped how labor, materials, and
funds were sequenced on the ground, allowing administrative
control to emerge through tracking, adjustment, and on-­site
problem-­solving rather than through centralized design (LISPC
Engineering Project Files, 1934–1936; Administrative General

periodic legal and financial review, including revision of bond
instruments, deadline-­driven clearance of documentation, and
controlled custody of executed releases, further embedding
discipline into administrative routine (Administrative General
Correspondence, Box 3).
For Burbeck, discipline was a matter of habit rather than
politics. Regular inspection routines and systematic report-
ing created a rhythm that gave the project its internal order.
Engineering logs and operational notes served both supervisory
and reflective purposes, guiding subsequent decisions on labor
deployment and work sequencing. Administration at Bethpage
thus functioned as a craft, requiring judgment, adaptation, and
technical skill rather than adherence to formalized rules (LISPC
Engineering Project Files, 1934–1936; Blakelock 1949).
Moses approached oversight strategically. Although he fre-
quently expressed frustration with administrative delay, he
understood that paperwork and procedure provided political in-
sulation and institutional legitimacy. As broader assessments of
his administrative style note, compliance was often treated less
as an obstacle than as a protective shield that allowed projects
to proceed with reduced interference (Caro 1974; Doig 1966). At
Bethpage, reporting requirements were incorporated directly
into execution, with schedules, ledgers, and progress summa-
ries treated as routine instruments of control rather than exter-
nal impositions. Oversight thus became a governing strategy,
stabilizing authority by translating action into documented
compliance.
Tillinghast experienced discipline differently. Where Moses and
Burbeck operated within bureaucratic oversight, his restraint
derived from internal standards of craft, judgment, and moral
seriousness about course design. Across his published writings,
he emphasized proportion, strategic balance, and restraint, re-
jecting spectacle in favor of courses that demanded thought,
adaptability, and decision-­making. A round of golf, in his view,
should function as a learning experience, cultivating judgment
through interaction with the land (Tillinghast 1995). This phi-
losophy carried a public dimension. Long before Bethpage,
Tillinghast promoted broad access to the game, making the am-
bition to create a publicly accessible championship-­level course
a natural extension rather than a contradiction of his ideals.
Institutionally, however, Tillinghast operated under constraint.
Engaged as a consultant under a limited WPA-­era contract, he
was prohibited from using his own construction crews and de-
pended on state-­supervised labor and reporting systems man-
aged by Burbeck (Tillinghast 1934). This separation of artistic
authority from administrative control created friction but also
structured execution. Other courses on the property (i.e., the
Red, Green, and Blue courses) were designed with efficiency and
standardization in mind. The Black Course, however, was de-
liberately insulated from these economies to preserve strategic
difficulty and design integrity. Under Burbeck's coordination,
efficiencies realized across the broader project absorbed the
Black Course's extraordinary resource demands without under-
mining fiscal discipline (Brooklyn Daily Eagle 1935, May 27).
Although Tillinghast's formal association with Bethpage ended
in 1935, he continued to write positively about the project and
Burbeck's role (Tillinghast  1998). Artistic discipline and bu-
reaucratic discipline thus coexisted without one subordinating
the other.
At higher levels, WPA and LISPC accounting requirements re-
quired daily documentation of labor, materials, and progress,
including counts of workers employed and quantities of work
completed (Federal Works Agency 1943). These measurements
functioned as early, improvised performance indicators, allow-
ing administrators to demonstrate that park construction could
be accounted for as efficient public work rather than discretion-
ary political favor. Oversight therefore served not only to track
activity but to legitimize the project as both humane in purpose
and disciplined in execution.
The case also illustrates that discipline carried a moral di-
mension. For laborers drawn from unemployment lines, the
routines of timekeeping, inspection, and certification restored
a sense of order and predictability. Each pay slip confirmed
not only wages earned but dignity regained. In this sense,
discipline functioned as both a social and an administrative
practice. It connected individual effort to public purpose
and rendered the abstract machinery of government tangi-
ble through the daily rhythm of work (Hopkins 1936; Fesler
et al. 1946).
At the level of execution, discipline at Bethpage was not im-
posed from above alone. It emerged through the interaction
of formal authority and administrative self-­restraint, and
through the continual negotiation between regulation and
judgment in practice. Oversight functioned not as an obstacle
but as a shared administrative practice. The daily circulation
of reports, receipts, and progress notes created a continuous
dialog between action and review. Through this steady pat-
tern of reporting and adjustment, administrators such as
Moses and Burbeck came to understand accountability not as
a formal requirement but as a lived practice embedded in the
work itself.
Taken together, this mechanism shows how oversight and
constraint added order and stability to an otherwise fluid and
improvised environment. At Bethpage, rules and reporting
structured communication without extinguishing creativity.
Discipline did not suppress ambition. It anchored ambition to
shared public purpose during execution, turning paperwork
into an ongoing process of justification, adjustment, and public
accountability.

4.4   |   Mechanism 4: Endurance as a Bridge From
Stalwart Practice to Institutional Memory
If alignment brought the project into being, and coordination
and discipline sustained its execution, endurance ensured that
its effects extended beyond the moment that produced them. At
Bethpage, endurance first took human form. Long before formal
acquisition or the consolidation of authority, Joseph Burbeck
provided continuity across shifting institutional arrangements.
He served as president of the Bethpage Corporation in 1932,
overseeing construction and site management during a 2-­year
lease period, and remained in place as superintendent after the
property was acquired and incorporated into the BPA. His role
did not reset as institutions changed; it evolved. Endurance, in
this sense, consisted of sustained presence, accumulated site
knowledge, and the capacity to carry practices forward as gov-
ernance structures shifted (Administrative Meeting Minutes,
Boxes 1 and 3).
That continuity was repeatedly tested. Burbeck managed
construction before ownership was secured, through the
transition to state control, and during the intense expansion
of relief-­era work. Thousands of workers passed through the
site and the project became subject to overlapping federal and
state reporting, payroll and inspection regimes. Burbeck re-
mained the constant administrative figure on the ground. His
endurance was more than persistence. It lay in his ability to
maintain operational coherence, as legal authority, funding
mechanisms, and organizational forms shifted around the
project.
Over time, practices initially developed to manage daily con-
struction stabilized into routine forms of maintenance and
administration. Improvised solutions hardened into stan-
dard practice. Administrative arrangements developed at
Bethpage, including authority-­based financing, cross-­agency
contracting, and delegated supervision, stabilized and were
retained across subsequent park projects (Blakelock 1949). As
Doig (1966) shows, such Moses-­era arrangements then func-
tioned as reference points for later authorities across New
York State. The WPA's insistence on documentation, once
viewed as burdensome, thus became a foundation for institu-
tional memory.
If Burbeck's endurance stabilized practice on the ground,
Moses' endurance stabilized the project in law and politics.
Bethpage was conceived years before funding was available,
and its realization required persistence across failed appropri-
ations, renewed purchase options, and repeated institutional
redesign. When legislative support stalled, Moses secured in-
terim financing, renewed land options, and ultimately drafted
legislation creating an authority capable of issuing bonds re-
paid through golf-­related revenues. This persistence kept the
project viable until formal acquisition became possible in 1934.
Together, Burbeck's operational endurance and Moses' insti-
tutional endurance ensured continuity across uncertainty and
administrative resistance (Administrative Meeting Minutes,
Boxes 1 and 3).
Moses also understood endurance as precedent. As he later
did with Jones Beach, he cited Bethpage's completion as
evidence that administrative autonomy could coexist with
accountability. Its demonstrated efficiency and integrity be-
came rhetorical resources in subsequent appeals to boards
and commissions, transforming individual accomplishment
into institutional identity (Caro 1974; Doig 1966; Ballon and
Jackson 2007).
That rhetorical use of precedent translated directly into insti-
tutional durability. Although many Moses-­era authorities were
formally designed to dissolve once bonds were retired, in prac-
tice, they were sustained through successive issuances tied to
new projects. Perpetuity thus emerged not as an explicit statu-
tory condition, but as a cumulative outcome of repeated renewal
grounded in demonstrated performance.
For Tillinghast, endurance took an architectural form. His de-
sign philosophy emphasized permanence rather than short-­term
effect, arguing that great courses should be discovered within
the landscape itself rather than imposed upon it. This philoso-
phy shaped Bethpage Black. Its routing, bunkering, and use of
natural landforms responded directly to the site's sandy soils
and relied on local materials rather than artificial construction.
These features were functional as well as esthetic, stabilizing
playing surfaces and allowing the course to mature through use,
weather, and time. Although no working drawings survived,
administrative records confirm Tillinghast's consulting role,
and the finished course reflects the principles he articulated
throughout his career (Tillinghast 1995, 1998). In this way, art-
istry became a medium through which administration achieved
permanence.
Endurance was also social. Projects such as Bethpage functioned
as sites where administrative habits were learned and normal-
ized under conditions of urgency and constraint. Relief workers,
foremen, and clerks became accustomed to routines of daily re-
porting, shared problem-­solving, and attention to precision that
characterized WPA-­supported construction. As relief programs
fed labor and experience into maintenance crews, engineering
offices, and municipal departments across New York, these
practices diffused quietly through public service. Bethpage thus
shaped administrative culture as much as it produced physical
infrastructure, reflecting broader patterns through which New
Deal relief administration transformed emergency practice into
durable routines (Federal Works Agency 1943; Smith 2006).
A final dimension of endurance appears in the paradox of ne-
glect. Despite long periods of limited maintenance, Bethpage
Black retained its basic routing and structural logic, enabling
later restoration without fundamental redesign, and confirming
the durability of both Tillinghast's design philosophy and the
project's original construction practices (Young 2002, 101–104;
Ballon and Jackson 2007).
Endurance, then, was more than persistence. It involved turn-
ing experience into understanding. By retaining, reusing, and
adapting what they had learned, Bethpage's administrators
transformed daily work into shared knowledge with lasting ef-
fects on practice. What began as improvisation became routine,
and routine became institutional memory. Bethpage endures not
because it was flawless, but because it learned how to carry ex-
perience forward as conditions changed.

5   |   Discussion: The Wonder of Practice Without
Theory
The four mechanisms identified in this study show that admin-
istrative capacity is not a product of static design but of adaptive
interaction. Bethpage illustrates how institutions learn through
use and evolve through strain rather than prescription. This
section situates those findings within broader debates about in-
stitutional design, performance, and learning. The analysis is
necessarily bounded by the archival record and does not claim
that similar mechanisms operated uniformly across New Deal
public works. Rather, Bethpage illustrates a historically specific
configuration of administrative practice whose durability de-
pended on contingent alignment among actors, institutions, and
constraints.
The case offers five principal insights. First, the way Bethpage
and the Black Course were built challenges linear models of re-
form that treat design as the precursor to performance. Instead,
Bethpage demonstrates how practice can precede formal the-
ory, and how institutional coherence can emerge from experi-
mentation rather than prior architectural design. The interplay
of constraint and creativity at Bethpage parallels what Ansell
and Torfing (2021) describe as co-­creation in governance. The
project foreshadowed ideas that scholars would later develop
about collaborative, multilevel problem-­solving in modern gov-
ernance (Ostrom 1990; Agranoff and McGuire 2001). Bethpage's
improvisation was not participatory idealism, but pragmatic ad-
aptation under duress. This pattern also resonates with Moses'
later reflection that his administrative philosophy was “based
on practice rather than theory,” emphasizing the gap between
formal plans and realizable objectives in public administration
(Moses  1956, ix). The case provides empirical grounding for
that retrospective assessment, showing how coordination, disci-
pline, and learning emerged through action rather than design.
Second, the analysis reframes budgeting and oversight as gen-
erative rather than restrictive. Scholarship often positions fi-
nancial controls as instruments of compliance (Schick 1966). At
Bethpage, these tools became mechanisms of coordination and
legitimacy. Accountability procedures translated ambition into
action by requiring administrators to articulate, document, and
justify decisions (Federal Works Agency 1943). This inversion
suggests that control systems can enable creativity when used as
frameworks of learning rather than barriers to autonomy. This
view aligns with reformers in the Bureau of Municipal Research,
who treated disciplined procedure as a precondition for demo-
cratic experimentation (Fesler et al. 1946; Stillman II 1987).
Third, Bethpage broadens the concept of performance.
Administrators did not understand performance solely through
quantified outputs, but through the visible alignment of pub-
lic purpose and administrative practice. Reports and ledgers
functioned as narratives of progress, combining empirical
and symbolic dimensions of accountability, consistent with
recent work treating narrative as a communicative resource
through which managers sustain coordinated action over time
(Nielsen et  al.  2024). This resonates with Goodsell's  (1981)
and Moore's  (1995) understanding of administration as a
moral art, grounded in judgment, stewardship, and credibility.
Bethpage's administrators achieved legitimacy not by following
rules perfectly, but by performing their purpose visibly and
convincingly.
Fourth, the endurance of Bethpage's methods demonstrates how
improvisation becomes institutionalized. Temporary routines
hardened into durable templates that informed subsequent state
park development. This pattern aligns with Selznick's  (1957)
insight that institutions are cultivated through habit, memory
and interpretation. Bethpage illustrates how administrative
knowledge accumulates through repetition, documentation and
adaptation. Institutional learning, in this sense, required reflec-
tion embedded in action rather than explicit theory. The project
thus exemplifies what Peters  (2019) describes as institutional
layering, in which incremental adaptation produces structural
change. In retrospect, this dynamic also resonates with Elinor
Ostrom's concept of “rules-­in-­use,” which emphasizes that gov-
ernance capacity becomes embedded through repeated practice
rather than formal design alone (Ostrom 1990, 93; Raadschelders
and Fry 2023, 333).
Finally, the Bethpage case complicates normative assump-
tions about good governance. It suggests that administrative
excellence depends less on the perfection of systems than on
the alignment of human character with institutional oppor-
tunity. Moses, Tillinghast, and Burbeck exemplified distinct
administrative virtues of ambition, artistry, and stewardship
that balanced each other only temporarily. Their collaboration
reinforces March and Olsen's (1989) argument that institutions
are arenas of interpretation as much as instruments of control.
Greatness in governance, as Bethpage shows, is achieved not
through harmony, but through disciplined friction.
This dynamic is echoed in research on adaptive governance
and resilience (Duit et al. 2010; Ansell and Boin 2019), which
highlights how public organizations thrive when they combine
stability with flexibility and cultivate the capacity to learn under
uncertainty. Bethpage offers an early historical analog of these
insights, demonstrating how constraint and improvisation can
be balanced to turn crisis into capability. The lessons of Bethpage
thus remain relevant for contemporary public managers con-
fronting complex, uncertain, and interdependent challenges.
At the same time, these insights and their practical implications
intersect with recent disciplinary reflection on theorizing in pub-
lic administration. A recent systematic review of theories and
theorizing in public administration highlights both the vitality
of contemporary theory-­building and concerns about uneven
societal relevance (Hattke and Vogel 2023). Elliott et al. (2026)
further argue for renewed connection between public admin-
istration scholarship and practice. The Bethpage case extends
these debates historically by demonstrating how administrative
coherence can emerge in practice before it is formally theorized,
making practice visible as an upstream source of administrative
knowledge.
In summary, the Bethpage case serves as both artifact and
argument. It reminds scholars that institutional theory must
account for practice as a living process, and practitioners
that procedural discipline need not extinguish imagination.
Rather than illustrating theory applied, the case reveals
practice as generative: budgeting, oversight, sequencing, and

6   |   Conclusion
This article set out to explain how an administrative outcome
that appeared improbable came into being. The creation of
Bethpage State Park and the Black Course was a wonder of
practice without theory, understood not as accident or mystery,
but as a contingent achievement. It emerged at a moment when
institutional forms were incomplete, administrative vocabular-
ies were still forming, and public purpose pressed harder than
formal design could accommodate. What made the outcome
remarkable was not the absence of constraint, but the way con-
straint was interpreted and worked through in practice.
The four mechanisms identified here show how adminis-
trative capacity developed through action rather than blue-
print. Alignment occurred before institutional consolidation.
Coordination emerged through budgeting and reporting prac-
tices that translated ambition into sequence. Discipline stabilized
behavior through oversight without extinguishing judgment or
creativity. Endurance carried these practices forward through
continuity of personnel and routines that hardened into institu-
tional memory. Together, these mechanisms show how practice
generated coherence before theory existed to explain it.
Revisiting the idea of wonder clarifies its meaning. Bethpage
did not succeed because it escaped bureaucracy or conformed
to an ideal administrative model. It succeeded because admin-
istrators learned how to work within uncertainty, strain, and
imperfect structure. Moses, Burbeck, and Tillinghast contrib-
uted distinct forms of ambition, stewardship, and craft that
were held in productive tension rather than resolved through
hierarchy.
In this sense, the wonder of Bethpage lies not only in what was
built, but in how it was built. It demonstrates that administrative
excellence can arise before formal doctrine, that learning can be
embedded in routine practice, and that bureaucracy can enable
creativity when inhabited with judgment and public purpose.
Bethpage's deeper legacy lies in showing how public adminis-
tration can convert necessity into capability, and momentary
alignment into lasting capacity.

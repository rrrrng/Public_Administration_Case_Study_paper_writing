Abstract
Robustness has recently taken center stage as an emerging paradigm to cope with turbulence and “build back better” toward new normalcy.
Existing literature has shown how robust governance, with its mix of flexible adaptation and proactive innovation, is well-suited to addressing
turbulence. However, there remains a gap in understanding the empirical variations within robust governance arrangements. In this article, we
address three questions: how (1) structures, (2) coordination mechanisms, and (3) leadership are designed and unfold in robust governance.
Through a qualitative approach grounded in case studies, interviews, and archival data, we provide evidence from six Italian regions, examining
how they addressed the challenges of the COVID-19 vaccination campaign. Results enable the formulation of propositions about organizational
arrangements in robust governance, in addition to suggesting competing pathways for flexible adaptation and proactive innovation.
Key words: robustness; organization; multiple case study; crisis; leadership.
Introduction
Robustness has recently gained attention as the way to “build
back better” from turbulence and move toward new normalcy
(Sørensen and Torfing 2007; Ansell and Trondal 2018; Ansell,
Sørensen, and Torfing 2023). We live in an era in which tur­
bulence, defined as “situations where events, demands, and
support interact and change in a highly variable, inconsistent,
unexpected or unpredictable way” (Ansell and Trondal 2018,
p. 43), can be considered a chronic and endemic condition
of contemporary life (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2021),
requiring public organizations to find a new way to “react,
manage and adapt to it” (Ansell and Trodal 2018, p. 45;
Howlett, Capano, and Ramesh 2018).
Scholars have started shedding light on the need to respond
to turbulence with robust governance (Ferraro, Etzion, and
Gehman 2015; Capano and Woo 2018; Howlett, Capano,
and Ramesh 2018; Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2021;
Sørensen and Ansell 2021; Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing
2023). Robust governance is defined as the ability to “uphold
or realize a public agenda, function, or value in the face of
the challenge and stress from turbulent events through flex­
ible adaptation, agile modification, and pragmatic redirec­
tion” (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2021, p. 952) of policies
and practices. In this sense, robustness is similar to the con­
cept of resilience but places greater emphasis on the ability
of organizations to flexibly adapt to changes, rather than
survive or bounce back from them (Ansell, Sørensen, and
Torfing 2023). Thanks to its two constitutive dimensions,
namely, flexible adaptation and proactive innovation, robust
governance enables organizations to “continue providing
public value in the face of variable, inconsistent, unexpected,
or unpredictable events and demands” (Ansell, Sørensen, and
Torfing 2023, p. 4).
Despite the growing academic interest, robust governance
is still a nascent field in a semi-formative stage, dominated by
a macro-level perspective focused on policy design and the
capacities the public sector must develop to be robust (Ansell,
Sørensen, and Torfing 2023). With a few exceptions (Boswell,
Dean, and Smith 2023; Trondal 2023), the exploration of
robustness from an organizational standpoint is notably ab­
sent. There is still much to uncover regarding how robustness
operates within the organizational boundaries, considering
the organizations’ structures and operational mechanisms
(Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023).
From this perspective, our study aims to explore the em­
pirical variation of organizational arrangements in robust
governance, by “stepping inside” robust organizations and
investigating how they function. We address three questions:
how (1) structures, (2) coordination mechanisms, and (3) lead­
ership are designed and unfold in robust governance. We aim
to explore the possible configurations of these arrangements
in robust governance cases. Moreover, we investigate the
varying levels of flexible adaptation and proactive innova­
tion in robust governance and how they relate to different
configurations of organizational arrangements.

For this purpose, we conducted a comparative case anal­
ysis grounded in case studies (Denzin and Lincoln 2011; Yin
2013), interviews, and archival data from six Italian Regions
to explore how they effectively navigated the challenges of
the COVID-19 vaccination campaign. As shown by Cristofoli
et al. (2023) and Capano and Toth (2023), the experiences of
the Italian Regions facing the COVID-19 pandemic offer a
rich informational context for exploring robust governance
arrangements and responses in turbulent times. As one of the
first countries to experience the pandemic, Italy’s experience
serves as a paradigmatic example of how to react to the un­
expected. After identifying which structures, coordination
mechanisms, and leadership characterize robust governance
cases, we analyzed how they perform to ensure flexible ad­
aptation and proactive innovation. We also tried to explain
the empirical variation (and discrepancies) in the relationship
between these organizational arrangements and flexible adap­
tation and proactive innovation in robust governance. Then
we formulated propositions to guide future research.
Our article contributes to the emerging literature on ro­
bust governance in four main ways. First, it sheds light on
organizational arrangements in robust governance, showing
how structure, coordination mechanisms, and leadership,
singly or in combinations, can be designed and unfold to
build flexibility and innovation within institutional infra­
structure (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023). Second, our
article provides empirical evidence to the theoretical asser­
tion made by Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing (2023), who claim
that robustness stands out as a key property for redefining
the interplay between stability and change. Robust govern­
ance, as portrayed in our empirical evidence, is marked by
a diverse array of structures, coordination mechanisms, and
leadership approaches that harmonize flexible adaptation and
proactive innovation in different ways, thus configuring a dif­
ferent mix of order and disorder, stability and change. This
observation opens up alternative pathways in robust gov­
ernance, warranting further exploration through future re­
search. Third, our article provides additional insights into the
characteristics of leadership for robustness (Ansell, Sørensen,
and Torfing 2023). Fourth, it contributes to making sense
of the “puzzle” proposed by our previous work on robust­
ness (Cristofoli et al. 2023), exploring its results through a
qualitative research design. On the one hand, it supports our
argument about the existence of multiple models of robust­
ness. On the other, it provides possible explanations about
how even a rational, centralized, and conventional model can
incorporate some degree of flexibility and innovation, thus
resulting in “robust.”
The emerging paradigm of robust governance:
a journey toward flexible adaptation and
proactive innovation
There was a time when environments were relatively stable
and predictable, and public bureaucracies adapted to them
through a hierarchical, rule-based, and command-and-control
system (Downs 1967). Changes in the environment were
considered exceptional, and bureaucracies sought to manage
them through careful planning and adhocracies (Mintzberg
and McHugh 1985). Then the emergence of wicked problems
ushered in a shift toward relatively unstable and challenging
environments. In this situation, networks arose as endeavors
to establish “islands of provisional stability” (Ansell, Sørensen,
and Torfing 2023, p. 7; Sørensen and Torfing 2007) through
horizontal collaboration, self-organization, and informality.
Changes in the environment were considered situational,
and networks tried to respond to them through flexible and
adaptive arrangements (Provan and Kenis 2008).
Today, new and disruptive challenges have emerged and
taken center stage: turbulence represents our “new normalcy”
(Ansell and Trondal 2018; Ansell, Trondal, and Øgård 2017;
Boin, McConnell, and ’t Hart 2021) and change is increasingly
difficult to foresee, understand, and track (Ansell, Sørensen,
and Torfing 2023). Traditional paradigms such as bureauc­
racy and network governance appear ill-suited to address
this dynamic landscape (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023).
On the one hand, bureaucratic organizations exhibit limita­
tions in adapting to the continuous changes in their environ­
ment due to their overemphasis on stability and control. On
the other hand, networks often prove sluggish in the face of
change because of their accent on flexibility and informality.
In this situation, scholars in public policy and public ad­
ministration have started highlighting the need for a new par­
adigm to address turbulence (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing
2021, 2023; Capano and Woo 2018; Howlett, Capano, and
Ramesh 2018). In their introduction to the symposium on
“Robust politics and governance in turbulent times,” Ansell,
Sørensen, and Torfing (2023) shed light on robustness as an
emerging and more fitting approach to address turbulence
(Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2021; Capano and Woo 2018;
Howlett, Capano, and Ramesh 2018); moving from public
bureaucracy and network governance, robustness enables
compensating for the limitations of the previous approaches,
thus presenting a new way to balance stability and change.
Originally coined in the natural sciences, the concept of ro­
bustness was later transposed to policy, public governance,
and public administration studies (Ansell, Sørensen, and
Torfing 2021; Howlett 2019; Trondal, Haslerud, and Kühn
2021) where it preserves its main initial meaning. Indeed, it
describes the ability of public organizations to continue pro­
viding public value “across a range of different circumstances
and temporal flux, implying same basic stability in the face of
change” (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023, p. 8). From this
stance, robustness is a dynamic property of public organiza­
tions, combining stability and change in a novel way through
the blending of its two main constitutive dimensions, namely,
flexible adaptation and proactive innovation.
To begin with, robustness in public organizations serves to
uphold their basic functions by continuously adapting their
processes and instilling flexibility in their organizational
arrangements (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2021; Capano
and Woo 2018; Howlett 2019; Trondal, Haslerud, and Kühn
2021). Public organizations must assimilate flexibility and
variety, essentially cultivating “multiple repertoires” that can
be flexibly deployed when circumstances demand change
(Ansell and Trondal 2018). From this perspective, adaptation
transcends a mere search for optimal fit with the environment
or marginal adjustments to existing structures. Rather, it
embodies flexibility, maintaining an array of possible courses
of action to swiftly shift from one to the other in response to
rapid changes (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023).
Moving on, robustness ensures the preservation of public
organizations’ essential functions through proactive innova­
tion. The ability to innovate continuously is paramount for

Theoretical framework: structures,
coordination mechanisms, and leadership in
robust governance
Drawing from the literature on public bureaucracy and net­
work governance, in the following, we will shed light on the
main characteristics that structure, coordination mechanisms,
and leadership need to develop in robust governance. In tur­
bulent times, robust organizations need to go beyond the
requirements of the bureaucracy and network governance
paradigms, combining them toward a new way to balance
stability and change.
Designing under turbulence: incorporating change
in structural arrangements
Scholars have extensively explored the organizational
structures that best navigate crises, emergencies, and turbu­
lence (Boin, Comfort, and Demchak 2010; Comfort, Waugh,
and Cigler 2012; Nowell et al. 2018). This is because it is
proved that organizational structures have an explanatory
role (Egeberg and Trondal 2018) and matter for behaviors and
decision-making processes (Egeberg 2012; Fligstein 2001).
As March and Olsen (1976, p. 13) highlight, organizational
structures provide actors with “a systematic and predictable
selection of problems, solutions, and choice opportunities,”
thus making some choices more likely than others (Egeberg
and Trondal 2018).
On the one side, top-down, centralized, and vertical
structures tend to stifle and sterilize change (Moynian 2008,
2009), and, when this proves impractical, “adhocracies” are
created. These are temporary organizations that draw their
members from different parts of the organization and are
flexible and adaptable enough to address change (Mintzberg
1989). Typically, once the change is sterilized, adhocracies
usually dissolve. On the other side, flat and decentralized net­
work structures adapt flexibly to change (Moynian 2009).
They can engage new members, incorporate, or dismiss re­
sources, and enlarge or reduce boundaries as evolving events
demand.
For years, scholars have oscillated between two contrasting
approaches to organizational structure design: the traditional
top-down, bureaucratic approach inherent in a command-
and-control system, opposing a more collaborative, dynamic,
and flexible network approach. Each approach comes with
its set of pros and cons. The former enables order and ra­
tionality, facilitating coordination, but falls short in adapting
flexibly to the rapid changes typical of crises and emergencies
(Moynihan 2008, 2009). The latter enables rapid mobiliza­
tion of resources, engagement of new actors, and flexible re­
sponse to change (Boin and Bynander 2015; Kenis et al. 2019;
Nohrstedt et al. 2018). However, it often lacks coordination,
order, and authority, thus introducing the undesirable possi­
bility of paralysis in action and decision-making.
After much debate, scholars have reached a consensus that
the advantages of establishing network structures to address
crises outweigh their drawbacks. Recognizing that “crisis re­
sponse in modern society is best characterized in terms of a
network” (Boin, Hart, and Kuipers 2018, p. 32; Nowell et
al. 2018), there is ongoing debate about the network struc­
ture most suitable for these circumstances. Some scholars
stress the importance of establishing a centralized, vertical
network structure, as centralization facilitates coordination
among the actors involved (Christensen, Lægreid, and Rykkja
2016; Moynihan 2009). Others advocate a decentralized,
flat network structure, as horizontal ties contribute to flex­
ibility, enhance communication, foster trust, and build social
capital among network partners (Hu, Knox, and Kapucu
2014; Nowell et al. 2018). Yet others support the establish­
ment of hybrid models that combine the benefits of vertical
coordination with the flexibility essential for crisis adapta­
tion (Boin and t’Hart 2003; Kenis et al. 2019; Nowell and
Steelman, 2019; Nowell et al. 2018). Kenis et al. (2019), for
instance, argue that the more complex the crisis, the more
hybrid the network structure should be. They propose two
innovative governance modes capable of addressing partic­
ularly complex crises: a combination of lead and Network
Administrative Organization (NAO) governance modes, and
a core-periphery network structure, where a core of network
partners lead and manage subgroups of actors with different
degrees of autonomy.
Today, scholars emphasize that turbulence, with its con­
tinuously changing events, needs structures that incorporate
change. In other words, to navigate turbulence, it is necessary
to establish network structures embodying the requisite flex­
ibility for change (Trondal 2023). Some authors advocate for
concepts such as “overdesigning,” building “redundancies,”
and adopting a versatile design that can respond to a variety
of conditions over time (Capano and Woo 2018; Christensen
and Laegreid 2020; Ferraro, Etzion, and Gehman 2015;
Howlett and Ramesh 2023). Summing up, network structures
for robust governance “need to incorporate procedural
mechanisms that allow for ongoing review and revision”
of policies and governance practices (Ansell, Sørensen, and
Torfing 2023, p. 12; Trondal 2023).
Coordinating under turbulence: mixing formality
and informality
Major crises, Black Swans, and turbulence pose multifaceted
challenges for public organizations, amidst which coordina­
tion stands out as particularly crucial (Christensen, Lægreid,
and Rykkja 2016). The importance of coordination lies in its

very definition, as it envisages the process of aligning decisions
and actions among interdependent actors/organizations to
achieve specific goals (Koop and Lodge 2014). As highlighted
by scholars, coordination represents a critical area for the suc­
cess of organizations grappling with problems that transcend
organizational boundaries (Ansell, Boin, and Keller 2010).
Coordination mechanisms facilitate collaborative endeavors
(Hocevar, Jansen, and Thomas 2004; Moynihan 2005),
which, in turn, enable the pooling of resources and exchange
of information to adapt to new circumstances (Agranoff and
McGuire 2003; Simo and Bies 2007).
In dealing with the coordination “trick or treat” formula,
two dominant modes of coordination emerged (Bouckaert,
Peters, and Verhoest 2010; Hu et al. 2020). On the one
side, hierarchical coordination, anchored in a top-down ap­
proach, relies on well-defined lines of authority and formal
mechanisms, such as plans, procedures, and routines (Hunt
et al. 2014). On the other side, horizontal coordination,
grounded in a bottom-up perspective, emphasizes flexibility
and informal mechanisms to adapt actors and actions in the
response network (Bardach 2001; Waugh and Streib 2006).
These two seemingly contradictory coordination archetypes
exhibit distinct strengths and weaknesses, largely tied to their
different levels of formality/informality. Hierarchical coordi­
nation, with its formal mechanisms, provides strong central
direction and clear-cut responsibilities (Rykkja and Lægreid
2014). However, as uncertainty levels escalate, they may
falter, necessitating more emergent forms of coordination that
facilitate rapid feedback and adaptation to ongoing changes
(Faraj and Xiao 2006). In contrast, the informal mechanisms
of horizontal coordination prove valuable for the timely ex­
change of information and resources, sidestepping the need
for authority, bureaucratic rules, or standardization (Lai
2018). Nevertheless, since they emerge beyond formal lines of
authority, they lead to dispersed performance among various
actors/organizations, diminishing accountability (Morris,
Morris, and Jones 2007).
Given such a mixed picture where there is no silver bullet,
coordination may evolve toward more hybrid arrangements,
incorporating a more nuanced blend of formal and in­
formal procedures (Christensen, Lægreid, and Rykkja 2015;
Nowell and Steelman, 2019). Put differently, coordination
should strive for a combination of formality and infor­
mality, embracing a mix of mandated and “self-organizing”
approaches (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023). This implies
that the rule-based structuring of work practices should in­
corporate more emergent solutions that function akin to “col­
laborative platforms.” These platforms serve as purpose-built
arenas where actors from different hierarchical positions can
interact and cooperate in decision-making (Ansell and Gash
2018; Torfing and Bentzen 2020; Van der Voet, Groeneveld,
and Kuipers 2014). This ad hoc “participatory architecture”
(Ferraro, Etzion, and Gehman 2015), where a variety of ac­
tors pool their experiences, knowledge, and ideas, facilitates
the development of a common operating picture of the
unfolding crisis, by exploiting real-time communications to
collect and verify information distributed among a large and
dynamic number of actors (Demchak 2010; Moynihan 2008).
Following the principles of robustness, coordination should
evolve into a more self-regulating and dynamic process that
sustains decision-making, placed at the crossroads of formal
central control and informal flexible self-organization (Ansell,
Boin, and Keller 2010).
Leading under turbulence: the role played by
energizing leadership
Leadership becomes especially crucial during crises, when un­
certainty looms large and there is a scarcity of past experiences
to draw upon and to take crucial decisions while successfully
managing change (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2021; Kjeldsen
et al. 2023). It stands at the core of preparing for, making sense
of, implementing, and motivating change in public organiza­
tions (Vogel and Werkmeister 2021) facing escalating com­
plexity and the inherent ambiguity arising from competing
values and accountable forums (Karp and Helgø 2008).
Public leadership literature has long debated the most
effective leadership approaches for public organizations
navigating uncertain and complex environments (Higgs,
Kuipers, and Steijn 2023; Kuipers et al. 2014). Recognizing
that not all changes are equal—stemming either from a pre­
dictable or an emergent process (Van der Voet, Kuipers, and
Groeneveld 2016)—the choice of leadership approach cannot
be one-size-fits-all. One perspective within such literature
distinguishes the rational from the pragmatic leader. Rational
leaders act swiftly in the face of uncertainty, assuming the
role of sole “commander” and decision-maker (Ansell and
Bartenberger 2016). They engage in a comprehensive anal­
ysis of all available information, build alternative scenarios,
and evaluate the pros and cons of each possible decision
(Ansell and Boin 2019; Boin and Lodge 2021). They appear
strong and reassuring (Boin and Lodge 2021), embodying a
leader-centric approach, as they face relatively low levels of
uncertainty (Kjeldsen and Van der Voet 2021). Conversely,
pragmatic leaders recognize that the information and knowl­
edge they acquire will never be complete. As they encounter
rapid change, they adopt incremental strategies and exper­
iment (Ansell and Boin 2019). Instead of being guided by
a predefined set of preferences, they formulate hypotheses
about the relationships between means and ends, choose a
course of action, and reorient their action based on the results
(Ferraro, Etzion, and Gehman 2015).
Addressing turbulence calls for a revision of leadership and
the emergence of a new “specimen.” Leaders facing turbulent
situations will find it difficult to precisely identify the goals
and strategies for achieving them: they will need to adapt
to emergent problems, which will be facilitated by existing
patterns of collective leadership (Ospina 2017), and engage­
ment with stakeholders to prompt their inputs and feedback,
and to encourage them to test new approaches in practice
(Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2021). Notions of collective,
distributed, and shared leadership focus on the relational na­
ture of such concepts1 (Crosby and Bryson 2018) and view
leadership as a process whereby interacting actors lead one
another toward common goals (Ospina 2017), which may
be decoupled from formal positions of authority (Pearce and
Conger 2003; Clarke 1999), and which may emerge in dif­
ferent locations of a system with high interdependence and
connectedness (Bolden 2011). Leaders will need to adjust their
habitual behavior to create results together with and through
others (Lund and Andersen 2023) as a remedy to complex
contexts dipped into an emergent process of change (Kjeldsen

not only to choose and communicate a course of action but
also to change direction if needed. Such adaptability requires,
at the micro level, the ability to motivate, energize, and en­
gage collaborators and at the macro level, the capacity to
effectively mobilize a broad range of stakeholders from dif­
ferent sectors (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023; Bolden
2011; Crosby and Bryson 2010).
Methods
Given our aim, we employed a research design incorporating
multiple data collection and analysis techniques. The integra­
tion of diverse methods was deemed most suitable to explore
a phenomenon with complex implications against a backdrop
of existing theory being scarce (Mele and Belardinelli 2019).
We employed case studies, interviews, and archival data
providing evidence from six Italian Regions. More specif­
ically, we took Italian Regions as the unit of analysis and
explored how they organized (in terms of structure, coordina­
tion mechanisms, and leadership) to provide robust responses
to the challenges of the COVID-19 vaccination campaign.
Comparative case study provides a more solid base for theory
building (Denzin and Lincoln 2011; Yin 2013) than single case
studies, which may describe a phenomenon well but often lack
support from diverse empirical evidence (Siggelkow 2007).
Case selection combined opportunity, feasibility, and the­
oretical sampling to provide conceptual insights rather than
general data. Theoretical sampling, essential for explaining
robust governance, allowed the identification of consistently
observed organizational arrangements and their impact on
variations in robust governance (Strauss and Corbin 1998).
Regions were chosen based on our prior research (Cristofoli et
al. 2023) showing their success in navigating the turbulence of
the vaccination campaign through robust governance models.
A multimethod research strategy combined qualitative compar­
ative analysis (QCA) for cross-case examination and in-depth
case studies for theory-building (Goertz 2017; Schneider and
Rohlfing 2013). Success in the vaccination campaign was
measured by the daily average ratio of doses administered to
doses supplied, with selected regions meeting or outperforming
the national average of 0.83%, achieving ratios from 0.83%
to 0.86%. The higher the ratio, the better the regional gov­
ernment was at reaching its goal of vaccinating the maximum
number of citizens in the shortest time, considering the doses
available. Six cases, all located in the north-central part of Italy,
were included. These regions are populous, feature developed
healthcare systems, and have diverse geographical contexts,
encompassing urban and rural areas. This deliberate selec­
tion balanced between-group variation and contextual simi­
larity, mitigating the influence of external factors (Ragin 1987;
Rihoux and Ragin 2009). Although the study’s geographical
scope limits generalizability, its focus on developed health sys­
tems and varied settings provided meaningful insights into ro­
bust governance. Further information on governance structures
and responses to vaccination campaign challenges is detailed
in the Supplementary Appendix (Supplementary Figure A and
Supplementary Table A1).
Data for this research were collected from diverse sources,
including newspaper articles, archival documents, and semi-
structured interviews, to offer a comprehensive understanding
of the vaccination campaign. Source triangulation enhanced
the analysis by revealing complementary perspectives and
addressing potential blind spots in decision-making and the
groundwork for vaccinations (Denzin and Lincoln 2011;
Flick 2022). Newspaper articles provided insights into the
campaign’s progression and the organizational arrangements
for its management. Policy documents, such as the mass vac­
cination and vaccination center plans, helped illuminate the
organizational foundations of mass vaccination. Interviews
with key informants of the vaccination campaigns offered
situational narratives and process-related perspectives on the
campaign. This multifaceted approach highlighted how re­
gions adapted to the challenges of mass vaccination, blending
flexible innovation with traditional solutions. Additional
information about the number of references coded by data
sources and analytical categories is provided in Supplementary
Tables A2 and A3 in the Supplementary Appendix.
We analyzed 968 newspaper articles from prominent
Italian local and national dailies to study the vaccination
campaign across six regions. Covering December 27, 2020 to
June 2, 2021, this period preceded a shift from government-
led strategies to outcomes influenced by vaccine hesitancy and
no-vax movements. During this time frame, the campaign
faced significant turbulence, including vaccine shortages,
AstraZeneca suspensions, emerging virus variants, and system
disruptions. These challenges forced public organizations to
reassess their initial strategies, offering insights into regional
responses to the complexities of mass vaccination during this
critical phase. The articles provided an objective overview of
organizational and strategic adaptations.
Besides newspaper articles, we delved into archival
documents, including the mass vaccination plan, the vacci­
nation centers’ plan, and Regional Decrees establishing the
Crisis Unit. Subsequently, we conducted in-depth interviews
with key informants from each of the six cases, including ac­
tors from regional governments, local healthcare agencies,
and inoculation centers, spanning political, technical, admin­
istrative, and operational levels (Table 1).

The interview process commenced with an introductory

interviewees’ perceptions of flexible adaptation and proac­

conducting the interview and the other taking field notes.
Field notes were de-identified and participant rights were
protected (Tsai et al. 2016).
Data analysis
Data analysis followed a sequential iterative approach,
encompassing both deductive, a priori coding, and induc­
tive emergent themes (Blaikie and Priest 2019). The rela­
tional approach embedded in iterative coding enabled us
“to move from a current understanding of the relation of
names, ideas, and observations in a project to a next under­
standing” (Locke, Feldman, and Golden-Biddle 2022, pp.
277–278).
The study employed iterative coding cycles to bridge
empirical data with theoretical insights (Locke, Feldman,
and Golden-Biddle 2022), uncovering new themes such as
adhocracies, hybrid coordination, and energizing leader­
ship. First-order codes were grouped into theoretically in­
formed second-order codes (Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton
2013), highlighting structures, coordination mechanisms,
and responses to turbulence. NVIVO 12 facilitated coding,
supported by intercoder reliability protocols (Campbell et al.
2013), with workshops ensuring consistency and refinement
(Patton 1990). An overview of the coding scheme is provided
in Supplementary Table A5 in the Supplementary Appendix.
The data analysis process was conducted in three phases. The
first phase focused on identifying different structures, coor­
dination mechanisms, and leadership approaches based on
the empirical data. The second phase examined the varying
levels of flexible adaptation and proactive innovation, as
perceived by those managing vaccinations. Cases were clas­
sified into three categories: those emphasizing flexibility and
innovation, those based on standardization and planning, and
a combination of both. Standardization and planning were
seen as opposite to flexible adaptation and proactive inno­
vation; the former represents efficiency through predefined
solutions (du Gay 2000), while the latter involves tools that
stabilize actions through predictability (Clarke 1999). In the
third phase, the study linked these perceptions to the organ­
izational arrangements in governance cases, comparing dif­
ferent patterns to analyze their relationships. Finally, through
grounded theory discrepancy analysis (Corbin and Strauss
1990), the study identified cases where arrangements and
responses diverged from theoretical expectations.
This three-stepped analytical process enabled us to for­
mulate propositions about organizational arrangements and
competing paths fostering flexible adaptation and proactive
innovation in robust governance. All propositions are theo­
retical generalizations drawn from our empirical cases, which
must be tested in future studies.
Findings
Phase 1: Which structures, coordination
mechanisms, and leadership appear in robust
governance?
The first analytical stage revealed both shared elements
and variations in the structure, coordination mechanisms,
and leadership configurations in the six case studies. The
emerging arrangements are presented by first highlighting
commonalities and subsequently addressing differences.
Structure.
All case studies turned to adhocracies to navigate the
challenges of the vaccination campaign. These temporary
structures, referred to as “Crisis Units” or “Regional Steering
Committees” (hereafter “control rooms”), were tasked with
defining the overall strategy, formulating guidelines, and set­
ting targets for local health authorities managing vaccinations
at the operational level. However, the nature of these tempo­
rary structures exhibited a notable variation across our em­
pirical data. In most cases, five out of six, they were both
formalized and internal to the regional governance structures,
comprising both political and administrative leaders of the
Health and Welfare Regional Directories. In contrast, one case
established an emergent informal control room operating ex­
ternally, albeit in coordination with the regional authorities
formally responsible for decision-making.
This consistent element across cases paved the way for an
increasing variation in the structural configurations in the six
cases. In all our cases, Italian Regions established network
structures, centered around the control room, involving local
health authorities and inoculation centers, albeit with varying
levels of autonomy. On one end of the spectrum, two cases
exhibited a vertically centralized structure, functioning as a
strategic nucleus and coordinating all actors in the response
network through a command-and-control system. In an in­
termediate position, three of the six cases showcased a flatter
network structure, where the control room was actively in­
volved in decision-making by local health authorities, who
were able to mobilize all available means and resources. The
last case was characterized by the choice of a core-periphery
network, where strategy and decisions resulted from
joint efforts by both regional and local health authorities.

Leadership.
All six cases demonstrated energizing leadership, aimed at
channeling the efforts of all partners in the response network
toward a shared goal: vaccinating the maximum number of
people in the shortest possible time. To achieve this, leaders
focused on team building, sustaining people’s morale, and
conveying passion, enthusiasm, emotions, and commitment

across the six cases was coupled with diverse leadership

leadership, represented by a strong leader with a clear vision,
providing well-defined instructions by assigning clear goals and

Phase 2: How do structures, coordination
mechanisms, and leadership perform to ensure
flexible adaptation and proactive innovation?
In the second phase, our objective was to gauge the level of
flexible adaptation and proactive innovation in robust gov­
ernance cases, as perceived by those directly involved with the
organization and management of the vaccination campaign.
To achieve this, we systematically identified and counted
instances explicitly referring to the perception of flexible ad­
aptation and proactive innovation (as well as those referring
to their opposites, i.e., standardization and planning) in the
interview transcripts.2
Our data indicated that 12 interviewees perceived mainly
flexible adaptation and proactive innovation in robust govern­
ance, 13 leaned more toward standardization and planning,
and 18 indicated a mixed approach, variously blending these
four different repertoires, with a sort of equilibrium between
instances referring to flexible adaptation and proactive inno­
vation and those referring to standardization and planning.
The analysis led to the identification of two cases where
there was a prevalence of flexible adaptation/proactive inno­
vation, one case where there was a prevalence of standardi­
zation/planning, and three cases with a balance between the
two. Despite these differences, all cases exhibited elements
of flexible adaptation/proactive innovation and standard­
ization/planning (i.e., no case exclusively displayed flex­
ible adaptation/proactive innovation or standardization/
planning).
On the one hand, standardization and planning seem to
require flexible adaptation and proactive innovation too. As
one interviewee recounted:
“The organization of the vaccination campaign was strongly
governed by the Regional Control Room. Every Monday
morning, we had online meetings held by the Health
Commissioner and his/her top executives, who assigned
tasks and evaluated results. Despite the tight ­regional con­
trol, our keyword was flexibility. For example, protocols
with companies were not entirely defined by the control
room. We were free to adapt them to specific demands.”

Phase 3: How do structures, coordination
mechanisms, and leadership combine in robust
governance for flexible adaptation and proactive
innovation?
To explore potential variations in perceptions of flexible
adaptation and proactive innovation based on the organi­
zational arrangements identified in the first phase, we cross-
tabulated our emerging patterns of themes in the third phase
(Table 2).
First, our data uncovered consistencies in organiza­
tional arrangements across the six cases, irrespective of the
perceived levels of flexible adaptation and proactive innova­
tion. Aligned with theoretical expectations, the establishment
of networks centered on adhocracies (Mintzberg 1989), the
activation of hybrid coordination mechanisms (Christensen,
Lægreid, and Rykkja 2015; Nowell and Steelman, 2019), and
the enactment of energizing leadership (Spitzer 2003) seem to
be the key elements contributing to robust governance in all
six cases. In essence, these elements characterize all our cases,
regardless of the different perceived levels of flexible adapta­
tion and proactive innovation. This leads to the formulation
of the first three propositions of this study:
Proposition 1. Network structures centered in adhocracies
always occur in robust governance.
Proposition 2. Hybrid coordination mechanisms,
mixing formality and informality, always occur in robust
governance.
Proposition 3. An energizing leadership always occurs in
robust governance.
Secondly, in line with theoretical expectations, all participants
who emphasized the significance of flexible adaptation and
proactive innovation belonged to the three cases employing
flatter network structures with medium/high autonomy,
relying primarily on informal coordination systems, and
characterized by a distributed leadership (by contrast, all
interviewees who primarily mentioned standardization and
planning were in a vertical network structure with little au­
tonomy, based on formal coordination mechanisms, with a
dual leadership).
In case A, for example, the control room fostered coordina­
tion and interaction with the regional government, local health
authorities, and hospitals. Daily meetings facilitated the anal­
ysis and revision of crucial decisions. Ample autonomy was
granted to local health authorities and inoculation centers
for on-the-ground implementation and revision of decisions
as needed. Leadership was shared across the response net­
work. Virtual meetings and WhatsApp chats facilitated
rapid information exchange across the network, ensuring
all actors stayed connected, shared information, socialized
practices, and revised them if necessary. Consequently, the
model designed by the control room, which ultimately proved

Based on these considerations, our data appear to indicate
that flat network structures combine with mainly informal
coordination mechanisms and distributed leadership in ro­
bust governance. This leads us to formulate the following
proposition:
Proposition 4: Flat network structures, mainly informal
coordination mechanisms and distributed leadership may
jointly occur in robust governance, with higher levels of
flexible adaptation and proactive innovation.
However, the data also reveal that five interviewees, despite
being in an opposite situation (in a centralized/vertical net­
work structure relying primarily on formality and dual
leadership), unexpectedly reported employing flexible and
innovative solutions. Similarly, four interviewees, belonging
to the case where a centralized/vertical network structure is
established, mainly formal coordination mechanisms are ac­
tivated and distributed leadership is enacted, or to the case
with a flat network structure, mainly informal coordination
mechanisms and dual leadership, reported a sort of equilib­
rium between flexible adaptation/proactive innovation and
standardization/planning. These inconsistencies warrant fur­
ther examination.
We hence conducted a discrepant analysis and identified
one fully discrepant case and three partly discrepant ones
(Table 2). Despite being characterized by a specific combi­
nation of arrangements (i.e., a vertical network structure,
mainly formal coordination mechanisms and dual lead­
ership), the fully discrepant case employs flexible and in­
novative solutions that do not logically fit the pattern of
arrangements they are associated with. Moreover, while the
fully discrepant case exhibits a certain level of coherence in
terms of structure, coordination mechanisms, and leadership
patterns, leading counter-intuitively to evidence of flexible ad­
aptation and proactive innovation, the three partly discrepant
cases present a more mixed picture. Therefore, the coexist­
ence of flexible and innovative solutions along with stand­
ardization and planning must be understood in the context
of vertical network structures combined with mainly formal
coordination mechanisms and distributed leadership, and flat
network structures combined with mainly informal coordi­
nation mechanisms and dual/hierarchical leadership. Fully
and partly discrepant cases will be analyzed in the following
paragraphs.

The analysis of partly discrepant cases: toward a hybrid
approach to robust governance.
While all our cases, including the fully discrepant one
discussed in the previous section, demonstrate some internal
consistency in the ways arrangements align, our partly dis­
crepant cases exemplify the interplay of seemingly divergent
organizational elements.
In case C, for instance, the control room collaborated
with the directors of regional departments, the Alderman
responsible for healthcare, and directors of local healthcare
authorities and hospitals. Decisions about the organization
and management of the vaccination campaign were collec­
tively made during daily online meetings. Each local health
authority then had the necessary autonomy to adapt these
shared decisions to local needs and implement them within
the relevant territory. Online meetings and WhatsApp
chats facilitated rapid decision-making and information
sharing, serving as tools to transfer practices and prototypes
experimented in one part of the region to other contexts. This
flat, open, and informal system was counterbalanced by a hi­
erarchical leadership: the Alderman in charge of healthcare
participated in all online meetings, provided well-defined
instructions by assigning clear goals and responsibilities to
the directors of local healthcare authorities, and monitored
their progress.
Similarly, in case E, the vaccination campaign was cen­
trally managed by the Regional Control Room, which
crafted the overall strategy and developed guidelines
to shape local health authorities’ responses. Decision-
making was confined to a closed institutional boundary
consisting of the heads of the Health and Welfare Regional
Directory, who sought the views of both political leaders
and technical experts. The operating model designed by the
Regional Control Room relied on plans and rules, used as
strategic assets to align all actors at the operational level.
Comprehensive planning regulated every aspect of the vac­
cination process, shaping targets, staff, and vaccine reser­
vation systems. Through scenario building, plans predicted
the average number of weekly vaccinations based on re­
source availability, vaccination points, and daily inocula­
tion rates. The composition of medical teams was tailored
to estimated timeframes for each phase, from citizens’
arrival to their discharge. This resulted in a structured,
rule-bound approach to vaccinations, relying on formal
coordination mechanisms to ensure standardization across
the system. Besides the plans, procedures, and formal acts,
weekly institutional meetings, involving members of the
Regional Control Room and heads of the Health Regional
Directory, facilitated information sharing and result-
monitoring. However, formal coordination mechanisms
had to be counterbalanced by instant messaging systems,
such as informal chats and video calls, to provide rapid and
feasible communication across the network, flexibility, and
responsiveness. Three interviewees clarified the issue:
“Fine-tuning took the form of virtual institutional meetings

Results show the emergence of different robust governance
models where structure, coordination mechanisms, and lead­
ership combine into a mix of order and disorder, stability,
and change. In the first case, the “disorder” introduced by
the establishment of a flat and informal network structure
is counterbalanced by the “order” brought in by hierar­
chical leadership. In the second case, the “order” introduced
by network centralization and formality of coordination
mechanisms is counterbalanced by the “disorder” introduced
by informality and distributed leadership. The last proposi­
tion of the study can be formulated thus:
Proposition 6: Vertical/flat network structures, (in)
formal coordination mechanisms, and hierarchical/dis­
tributed leadership may combine differently in hybrid
configurations, equally characterizing robust governance
(with a different mix of flexible adaptation/proactive inno­
vation and standardization/planning).
Discussion and conclusion
Responding to the growing interest in robust governance and
acknowledging the lack of an organizational perspective in
previous studies (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023; Trondal
2023), this article aimed to investigate the organizational
arrangements in robust organizations.
First, our analytical journey shows three consistent
arrangements that seemingly characterize robust govern­
ance: adhocracies and network structures, hybrid coordina­
tion mechanisms, and energizing leadership (Propositions 1,
2, and 3). Second, despite shared characteristics, our study
reveals empirical variations in how these arrangements man­
ifest across cases. Third, cases display varied levels of flexible
adaptation/proactive innovation, and standardization/plan­
ning, suggesting a nuanced combination of these elements
rather than a stark contrast, which may complement and
combine in varying “degrees” of robustness. Fourth, cases
show how organizational arrangements can combine in
different ways in robust governance, and this appears to
be related to various combinations of flexible adaptation/
proactive innovation and standardization/planning, thus
unveiling different pathways or “models” of robustness, as
further discussed later (Propositions 4 and 6). Lastly, our
study highlights an emerging feature of robust organiza­
tions, that is, a specific “mindset” spread through robust or­
ganizations, leading to experimentation and the emergence
of creative solutions (Proposition 5), which seems to intro­
duce flexibility even in the more “conventional” robustness
model, as we will see later. These findings lead to the formu­
lation of propositions for organizational arrangements and
pathways in robust governance that must be tested in future
research.
The following section discusses how these results con­
tribute to the existing literature on robust governance.
Designing for robust governance
In their recent work, Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing (2023)
have underscored the imperative of pushing the boundaries
of literature on robustness in new directions. They particu­
larly advocate for exploring how we can design for robust
governance, by introducing flexibility and innovation in in­
stitutional arrangements (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023,
pp. 10–11). By delving into the organizational underpinnings
of robust governance, our study answers this call.
Our study suggests that adhocracies, hybrid coordina­
tion mechanisms, and energizing leadership represent the
primary organizational facets of robust governance. On the
one hand, our results resonate with contemporary literature,
emphasizing the importance of hybrid arrangements in tur­
bulent scenarios (Christensen, Lægreid, and Rykkja 2015;
Nowell and Steelman, 2019) and incorporating elements of a
command-and-control system in network forms (Christensen,
Lægreid, and Rykkja 2016; Moynihan 2009). On the other
hand, they show how to equip robust organizations with
arrangements that can introduce flexibility and innova­
tion within institutional infrastructures (Ansell, Sørensen,
and Torfing 2023). Adhocracies are stable organizational
structures that introduce dynamism as well as flexibility,
embodying mechanisms for ongoing review and revision of
practices (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023). Given their
temporary nature, they offer the dual advantage of being
well-articulated yet flexible and multiform. Similar to the
control rooms examined by Schulman et al. in their study of
the management of high-reliability organizations (Schulman
et al. 2015), regional adhocracies were a central part of the
organization of the vaccination campaign, as they had to ad­
dress tight coupling and complex interactivity issues with the
external environment through alternative modes or sliding
modules of actions that balanced short- and long-term emer­
gency solutions, stability/zero equifinality and flexibility/
maximum deviance from the norm. Hybrid coordination
mechanisms create platforms at the intersection of formal

centralized control and informal self-organization (Ansell,
Boin, and Keller 2010). They allow the integration of knowl­
edge from all sources into a coherent picture before making
any decision. Characterized by a blend of formality and in­
formality, they amalgamate traditional communication tools
with emerging digital ones, which is emblematic of the on­
going digitalization of public administrations. Energizing
leadership promotes flexible adaptation and proactive inno­
vation by motivating individuals and mobilizing resources/
stakeholders (Bolden 2011; Crosby and Bryson 2010). It
explicitly addresses the human values and interpersonal dy­
namics of the leaders (Keys and Miller 1984), encompassing
their skills in helping individuals achieve goals.
In addition, Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing (2023) con­
tend that robust organizations must couple structure with
the process (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023, pp. 10–11).
Although prior contributions in robustness literature have
elucidated the specificity of robust governance, they have
often focused exclusively on structural features, such as re­
dundancy, diversity, modularity, and participatory decision-­
making architectures (Capano and Woo 2018; Ferraro,
Etzion, and Gehman 2015; Howlett and Ramesh 2023), or
on process dimensions, including outside-the-box thinking,
improvisation, fast learning, and strategies for managing mul­
tiple time horizons (Capano and Toth 2023; Pot, Scherpenisse,
and ’t Hart 2023). By concurrently examining various facets
of the organizational architecture of robust governance, our
study transcends the more siloed approach that has hitherto
characterized the literature on robust governance.
We shed light on how organizational arrangements can
combine structure with processes. Our analytical stance
aligns more closely with the work of Sørensen and Ansell
(2021), which integrates considerations of structure and
process to develop a framework of “political robustness,”
and with Trondal’s (2023) research, which illustrates how
intra- and inter-organizational vertical specialization, as well
as processes of organizational socialization, cultivate sta­
bility in the behaviors of public employees coping with tur­
bulence. However, we take a step further by revealing various
combinations of organizational arrangements that appear to
typify robust organizations, blending elements of stability/
change, order/disorder. For instance, the presence of hierar­
chical leadership (stability/order) may be offset by flat net­
work structures and informal coordination mechanisms
(change/disorder). Similarly, to instill flexibility/change in
a vertically centralized network structure reliant on formal
coordination mechanisms, distributed leadership becomes
crucial.
Different ways of balancing stability/change and
order/disorder
In addressing turbulence, scholars emphasize the impor­
tance of integrating change and stability in new ways
(Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023), which may be appli­
cable to both future crises and the public sector’s day-to-day
challenges. That said, how robust organizations accomplish
this task remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, our study
offers insights in this direction, by showing how stability and
change can harmonize differently in robust governance cases.
First, our data show how the balance between stability and
change can emerge from different combinations of organi­
zational arrangements, as previously argued. Second, it can
emerge from different mixes of flexible adaptation/proactive
innovation and standardization/planning that characterize
robust governance. As our data indicate, flexible adaptation/
proactive innovation and standardization/planning do not
necessarily oppose each other; rather they can coalesce and
generate different “degrees” of robustness (i.e., with higher
flexibility/innovation and lower standardization/planning, or
vice versa). Third, the balance between stability and change
can also originate from various combinations of organiza­
tional arrangements with different mixes of flexible adapta­
tion/proactive innovation and standardization/planning. For
example, cases emphasizing flat network structures, informal
and rapid communication, and participatory leadership ex­
hibit a higher propensity for flexible adaptation and proac­
tive innovation. Conversely, cases relying more on top-down
structures, predetermined plans, and hierarchy primarily show
standardization and planning. Consequently, it is conceivable
that diverse “models” of robustness exist, each characterized
by a unique balance of stability/change and order/disorder.
As a theoretical contribution to Ansell, Sørensen, and
Torfing’s (2023) conceptualization, our findings suggest that
robust governance is characterized by various organizational
arrangements associated with different mixes of flexible ad­
aptation/proactive innovation and standardization/planning.
In this perspective, the relationship between stability and
change appears to assume different forms, depending on how
organizational arrangements combine in different “degrees”
of robustness, as well as in unexpected ways. These different
combinations of stability and change can, in fact, be related to
the relationship between order and disorder that is mentioned
by Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing (2023): far from being in op­
position, these two dimensions in our cases seem to become
complementary. This leads to alternative pathways of robust
governance in different circumstances.
Our findings resonate with previous academic contributions
illustrating how public organizations adapt their behaviors
to address complex challenges (Rhodes and Donnelly-Cox
2014). In this respect, Eckhard et al. (2021) introduced the
notion of “latent hybridity,” highlighting how during the
German refugee crisis, public organizations temporarily
shifted from their typical administrative routines to embrace
principles more commonly associated with the private and
nonprofit sectors. This temporary adoption of different insti­
tutional logics led to a departure from entrenched bureau­
cratic practices and toward more flexible and innovative
approaches. This viewpoint suggests that our balance be­
tween stability and change/order and disorder may be contin­
gent on prior organizational social norms that make certain
values more available (Tversky and Kahneman 1973). From
this perspective, this balance is likely to be affected by the
organizational culture, which precedes both arrangements
and responses to turbulence by influencing early managerial
choices through path dependence (Kelman and Hong 2015).
The incorporation of innovative practices into rigid and
established protocols, however, is not automatic. In this per­
spective, public administration scholars have recently pointed
out that more flexible and innovative practices, introduced to
address public needs in the face of complex and unpredict­
able challenges, must harmonize with existing norms (Mergel
2023). In contexts where bureaucratic, rule-based approaches
predominate, such as in many public organizations, the adop­
tion of novel practices may encounter resistance, as they might
be perceived by public servants as obstacles rather than viable
alternatives (Gibson 2014). This undesirable effect becomes

ative to tackle societal issues confronts organizations with
the trade-off between exploration and exploitation (March

devise solutions that strike a balance between the costs as­
sociated with introducing new solutions, which better suit
external demands, and the tried-and-tested approaches that
ensure efficient execution.
Leading for robust governance: toward a better
understanding of energizing leadership
A further contribution of our study consists of an enhanced
understanding of what constitutes an “energizing lead­
ership” in robust governance. It is a form of leadership
that may be embodied by a single individual, but it is also
often distributed among two or more individuals. What is
common across our cases is the ability of the leaders to con­
verge all individual and collective efforts toward a common
goal, by leveraging elements such as people’s passion, com­
mitment, self-confidence, and engagement. Leaders in our
cases appear to play the role of Owens et al.’s (2016) “en­
ergy brokers,” who enhance followers’ work functioning
through the transfer of “emotional energy,” that is “the
capacity to do work where the source is affect—the sub­
jective component of a biobehavioral system of activation”
(Baker 2019, p. 380). This is in turn related to the con­
cept of “relational energy” (Baker 2019), which represents
the emotional energy generated (or depleted) in social
interactions,3 and that corresponds to “a heightened level
of psychological resourcefulness generated from interper­
sonal interactions that enhances one’s capacity to do work”
(Owens et al. 2016, p. 37). Whereas Owens et al. (2016) do
not explicitly define as “energizing” the leaders who they
find are involved in “the provision of energy in addition
to being information sources, extrinsic reward-brokers, or
visiongivers” (p. 46), we draw on their contribution to in­
terpret the changes in actors’ job engagement—resulting
from leaders/followers interactions and the related transfer
of psychological resources—which emerge also in our cases.
Energizing leaders drive actors to perform according to
plans as well as to experiment when necessary and to pro­
vide feedback that can flexibly adjust plans or change them.
It is, therefore, a type of leadership where frontline per­
sonnel may also “lead” toward change regardless of their
formal position in the hierarchy (Ospina 2017; Pearce and
Conger 2003) or within the response system (Bolden 2011).
Our cases therefore exhibit the relational dimension, the
significance of experimentation and adaptation, the hori­
zontal and distributional leadership approach, and open
communication that the literature emphasizes as major
constituents of a leadership for robust governance (Ansell,
Sørensen, and Torfing 2021, 2023).
Experimentation and adaptation, along with openness to
continuous change based on feedback and learning, are also
features of leadership agility (Joiner 2019). Other features
that an agile leadership shares with the leadership approach
emerging from our cases include open and bottom-up com­
munication; downward delegation of responsibilities and
involvement of employees, which require trust and the rec­
ognition of their problem-solving ability; appreciation of
experts; and an open error culture (Mergel 2023; Mergel,
Gong, and Bertot 2018). However, what seems to distinguish
agile leadership from energizing leadership is that despite
the former being an “employee-centric form of leadership”
(Mergel 2023), it is still associated with viewing leadership
roles linked to positions of authority. Public managers as­
piring to achieve agile governance are encouraged to del­
egate, support, and be open to change (Mergel, Gong, and
Bertot 2018), but they are not expected to relinquish or share
their leadership roles. Energizing leaders, instead, push rel­
evant stakeholders to take the initiative directly and imple­
ment solutions, thereby allowing distributed leadership roles
to emerge thanks to the transfer of psychological resources
that is implied by the diffusion of emotional energy (Owens
et al. 2016).
Moreover, whereas the concept of energizing leadership
shares some features with those of transformational and
charismatic leadership research (Conger 1999; Van Wart
2003), the latter research mostly sees the leader as the pri­
mary source of leadership, with followers being involved in
activities that generate motivation and commitment to pro­
duce the desired results (Ospina 2017). Despite the fact that
such literature regards the interaction between leader and fol­
lower as potentially transformative for both, influence runs
primarily from leader to follower, as it is linked to the au­
thority of the leader (Bellé 2014; Wright and Pandey 2010). In
a similar vein, although energizing leadership may appear to
overlap with the concept of empowering leadership, the latter
is linked to cognitive mechanisms (Pearce and Sims 2002)
rather than affective ones, as is the case of a leadership where
the transfer of emotional energy plays a key role (Owens et
al. 2016). Also, the empowering leadership concept is rooted
in a behavioral approach to leadership (Cheong et al. 2019)
rather than a relationship-based approach as in the case of
energizing leadership.
As we observe the concept of energizing leadership taking
shape in our cases, such influence dynamically flows in dif­
ferent directions. It reflects that different actors may at times
assume decision-making power and take responsibility for
specific tasks, as in a distributional leadership perspective
(Bolden 2011). It follows that an energizing leadership is
linked to processes rather than positions of authority, and
that it can be learned, both purposefully and unintention­
ally, through experience. Furthermore, the relational aspect
and the influence flowing in different directions suggest that
the relational and emotional energies (Baker 2019; Owens et
al. 2016) that are generated through interactions may lead
to a form of emotional contagion (Barsade 2002; Hatfield,
Cacioppo, and Rapson 1994) that, in turn, feeds into the de­
velopment of energizing leadership itself.
Solving the “puzzle” from the robustness literature
In our 2023 article (Cristofoli et al. 2023), we explored the
Italian Regions’ responses to the challenges of the vaccination
campaign, showing through QCA how an effective and timely
response to the turbulence of the vaccination campaign can
equally be reached through competing robustness models. In
particular, they highlight two alternative models of robustness,
which they label as dynamic and static, respectively. The former
is coherent with Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing’s (2021, 2023)

and leave us wondering how a “conventional” model can in­
corporate certain degrees of flexibility. Building on that study,
we developed a new research project to deepen and extend the
results of the previous analysis, by shedding light on the missing
organizational piece of the jigsaw that could explain how an ap­

results presented in this article, on the one hand, seem to confirm
our previous findings (Cristofoli et al. 2023), while, on the other
hand, help to make sense of the puzzle.
In fact, our data show how a different mix of flexible ad­
aptation/proactive innovation and standardization/planning
is possible in robust governance, thus confirming that var­
ious degrees or models of robustness can exist, characterized
by a different mix of order and disorder, and supported by
a different combination of organizational arrangements.
Moreover, our data help us to understand how a “static” ro­
bustness model (to recall Cristofoli et al. 2023) can incorpo­
rate some degrees of flexibility. It is thanks to a new “mindset”
that, acting as oil with the gears of a machine, leads people to
believe that things may be done unconventionally, and pushes
actors beyond managing defensively and toward acting
boldly. Such bold action is not the result of imprudence,
but rather of a mindset that acknowledges that emergency
involves planning as well as improvisation, top-down ration­
ality as well as the bottom-up capacity to re-invent, planning
and order as well as the capability to act without waiting for
all the administrative steps to be executed. Differently put,
it’s an immaterial and somewhat long-lasting resource that is
used to manage complex situations requiring not straightfor­
ward solutions, cultivated by actors during crises, and stored
in memory to be reused when a new emergency unfolds.
This is coherent with the ideas of adopting improvisation
and bricolage, creative problem-solving, and fast responses to
address crises and turbulence (Wiedner, Croft, and McGivern
2020; Zenk et al. 2020). It is also coherent with Capano and
Toth’s (2023) conclusions about the importance of unplanned
individual and organizational behaviors, characterized by
outside-the-box thinking, improvisation, and fast learning, to
solve unexpected problems. As they examined Italy’s initial re­
sponse to the pandemic, Capano and Toth (2023) highlighted
how healthcare personnel demonstrated flexibility, remained
creative even under pressure, and devised effective impromptu
solutions to emerging problems (Toth 2021).
However, our data suggest that such a mindset may be an or­
ganizational feature of robust organizations: despite high levels
of hierarchy and formality, frontline workers within robust or­
ganizations seem to be able and encouraged by the control room
to systematically adjust decisions to local needs and flexibly
adapt the instructions from the top. As highlighted by Trondal
(2023), the stability of an organizational architecture can be bal­
anced by structured flexibility introduced by civil servants. In
response to the puzzle identified in our previous study (Cristofoli
et al. 2023), the present work proposes that a balance between
stability and change within robust organizations can, also,
be reached through a combination of stable organizational
infrastructures (that provide order in response to turbulence)
and systematically flexible civil servants who invigorate the in­
frastructure from within (thus introducing that “pinch” of dis­
order necessary to creatively address unexpected events).
Managerial implications, study limitations, and
future avenues of research
Our study contributes to managerial practice by presenting
a diverse range of organizational arrangements that can be
combined in various ways to tackle turbulence. It provides
public organizations and managers the opportunity of a
versatile toolbox, capable of assembling and disassembling
organizational arrangements in a tailor-made approach for
specific contextual needs, opportunities, and constraints. We
show that there is no a one-size-fits-all robust organizational
model; instead, robust organizations have the flexibility to
choose from different alternatives.
Furthermore, public organizations do not need to opt ex­
clusively for either flexible/innovative or conventional mana­
gerial practices to be robust, as both have their strengths and
weaknesses, and blending the two can be an effective strategy.
Importantly, changes in practices and routines should not
completely diverge from taken-for-granted and established
norms and organizational values. Rather than a radical cul­
tural revolution, robust solutions should be contextually
relevant and capable of introducing changes without facing
resistance.
Lastly, in more conventional organizational environments,
fostering a robust mindset is crucial. This mindset empowers
individuals at all levels to take bold and unconventional
actions. Leaders who encourage such improvisation and
bottom-up innovation should be prioritized when selecting
managers or positions of responsibility for turbulent
situations, and they should also be given some degree of inde­
pendence from political and administrative pressures.
The limitations of this study are first related to its case
study-based nature, which does not allow generalization of
its results. In addition, whereas our results improve the un­
derstanding of organizational arrangements and pathways
in robust governance, especially by suggesting that unex­
pected configurations may also be effective, they do not pro­
pose which model works better in certain situations. Future
research, both qualitative and quantitative, should therefore
not only analyze additional cases, sectors, and countries but
also explore the factors influencing a certain configuration’s
emergence and its outcomes in terms of degree and com­
position (flexible innovation and proactive innovation, and
standardization and planning) of robust governance.
Future research could, first of all, test the propositions we
formulated through a QCA, in terms of necessary and suffi­
cient conditions for flexible adaptation and proactive innova­
tion in robust governance. In addition, it should also proceed
at the micro level of the analysis to explore the role that
individuals—with their behaviors, values, and emotions—
play in fostering robust responses to turbulence. Our results
suggest two possible directions for such investigation. The first
direction relates to individuals’ roles as they inject flexibility
into stable organizational infrastructures. The way they act
and react in response to events or other people’s actions and
encouragements is linked to organizational factors as well as
to individual-level characteristics, and a better grasp of such
links and related dynamics would also allow a superior un­
derstanding of their contribution to varying configurations
of order/disorder and stability/change. A second and related
direction involves focusing on the emotional and relational
energies that are induced by these actions and interactions,
which appear to be both a cause and an effect of energizing

leadership. Although our data do not provide sufficient evi­
dence of such causal relationships, we believe that their ex­
ploration—coupled with the possible role of an emotional
contagion—will significantly contribute to the development
of robust governance research.

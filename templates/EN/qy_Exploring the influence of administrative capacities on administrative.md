Abstract
This research explores administrative capacities to explain the variation in the public’s experience of administrative burdens. Through a qualitative
exploratory case study based on semi-structured interviews in Mexico, the paper argues that administrative capacities can structure or shape
administrative burdens. The effect can be either positive, where burdens are alleviated or transferred to the state, or negative, where burdens
are amplified or newly created. This study identified six administrative capacities that influence administrative burdens: the design of interaction
rules, government communication strategies, government resources, organizational structures that provide personalized assistance to citizens,
coordination schemes among government offices, and professionalization of street-level bureaucrats. This paper contributes to the growing lit­
erature on the governance of administrative burdens and strategies for burden reduction.
Key words: Governance of administrative burdens; burdens reduction; administrative capacities; case study; Mexico.
Introduction
The administrative burdens approach enables the study
of citizen-state interactions (also known as bureaucratic
encounters) from the citizens’ perspective. Administrative
burdens are the frictions that citizens experience when
interacting with public services (Herd and Moynihan 2025,
p. 46) and have been defined as “an individual's experience
of policy implementation as onerous” (Burden et al. 2012,
p. 741). Administrative burdens are a function of three costs
citizens face in their encounters with the state: learning costs,
compliance costs, and psychological costs (Moynihan, Herd,
and Harvey 2015). Learning costs arise from searching for in­
formation about public services and assessing their relevance
to the individual, while compliance costs refer to the burdens of
following administrative rules and requirements (Moynihan,
Herd, and Harvey 2015, pp. 45–46). Psychological costs are
the feelings of dissatisfaction experienced by citizens fol­
lowing arbitrary, unfair, or discriminatory interactions with
the government (Moynihan and Herd 2020, p. 37).
Academic studies have identified that administrative
burdens tend to compromise the objectives and materializa­
tion of social rights (Burden et al. 2012; p. 741; Moynihan,
Herd, and Harvey 2015, p. 44). Administrative burdens
imposed on citizens can be so significant that they present in­
surmountable barriers, preventing them from taking up their
social rights (Herd et al. 2013) and resulting in administra­
tive exclusion (Brodkin and Majmundar 2010, 2010; Jilke,
Van Dooren, and Rys 2018). Burdens reinforce and aggravate
social inequalities (Herd and Moynihan 2019; Nisar 2018;
Peeters et al. 2018), including gender inequalities (Herd and
Moynihan 2025); they delay the delivery of resources or serv­
ices (Kyle and Frakt 2021; Wiley and Berry 2018), and cause
disruptions or disconnections to social programs (Heinrich
and Brill 2015). Herd and Moynihan (2021) state that
“burdens exist partly because they are taken for granted: we
do not systematically evaluate their impact, assess their neces­
sity, or seek alternatives” (p. 751).
Over the past decade, researchers have studied the phenom­
enon of administrative burdens from various perspectives.
Political decisions (Moynihan, Herd, and Harvey 2015), or­
ganizational factors such as administrative capacity and be­
nign neglect (Moynihan, Herd, and Harvey 2015; Peeters
2019), and individual experience factors such as human cap­
ital (Christensen et al. 2020) have been identified as the main
mechanisms that generate variation in administrative burdens.
Current research on administrative burdens acknowledges
that administrative capacities can either worsen or alleviate
these burdens, as they are factors shaping the effective imple­
mentation of public policies and the delivery of services and
benefits (Burden et al. 2012; Heinrich and Brill 2015; Herd
and Moynihan 2019; Peeters 2019). However, the literature
has paid little attention to this relationship, and studies have
not specifically focused on identifying these capacities. Only
a few scholars have researched or analyzed the relationship
between burdens and capacities (for example, Peeters 2019;
Peeters and Nieto-Morales 2021; Selin 2019), and none have
done so in-depth, identifying specific capacities that shape
burdens.
Given evidence on the adverse effects of administrative
burdens on access to social rights (Herd and Moynihan
2019; Jilke, Van Dooren, and Rys 2018; Nisar 2018), this
paper aims to deepen the study of the origin of administrative
burdens based on a factor that governments can control: ad­
ministrative capacities.

This research paper addresses the question: How do
administrative capacities explain the variation in the ad­
ministrative burden carried by citizens at their point of
encounter with the state? Using a qualitative case study
approach, it examines a Mexican federal social pro­
gram called Microcredits (“Tandas para el Bienestar” or
“Microcréditos para el Bienestar” in Spanish). The research
involved 122 interviews with beneficiaries and street-level
bureaucrats (SLBs), commonly called “servants of the na­
tion.” This approach enabled an in-depth examination of
the program's access processes, capturing the experiences
of citizens (beneficiaries) encountering administrative
difficulties and SLB’s perspectives on the functioning of ad­
ministrative capacities.
This paper theorizes about the role of administrative
capacities in governing administrative burdens. It confirms
the theoretical assumptions in the literature that administra­
tive capacities can and do influence administrative burdens.
The research paper argues that there is a set of specific ad­
ministrative capacities that can impact burdens positively or
negatively at the point of citizens’ encounters with the state.
The paper contributes to filling a gap in the literature by of­
fering an initial theoretical framework for analyzing the rela­
tionship between burdens and administrative capacities and
providing evidence of the effects of the interaction between
these variables.
The paper is organized into five sections. The first sec­
tion addresses the theoretical and conceptual frameworks
of administrative burdens and administrative capacities.
The second section describes the methodology and presents
the context of the case study. The third section presents
the main findings of the research, identifying at least six
administrative capacities that influence the variation of ad­
ministrative burdens in three dimensions. The fourth sec­
tion discusses the findings and the study's limitations, and
the final section presents the conclusions and considers
future directions for researching the relationship between
capacities and burdens.
Theoretical and conceptual frameworks:
administrative capacities and administrative
burdens
In general terms, capacity is a “power, ability, or faculty for
something in particular” (Addison 2009, p. 11). According
to scholars, capacity in the context of policy-making requires
a combination of resources, abilities, and organizational
standards to assure effective policy design and successful im­
plementation (Domorenok, Graziano, and Polverari 2021;
El-Taliawi and Van Der Wal 2019; Hagemann 2019; Lindvall
and Teorell 2016; Lodge and Wegrich 2014).
An administrative notion of governmental or state capacity
has been discussed as “infrastructural state power” (Mann
1984; p. 189; Soifer 2015; p. 9; Brinks, Levitsky, and Murillo
2019, p. 26) and “a way of exercising power” (Berwick and
Christia 2018, p. 74), which means influencing civil society,
coordinating it, and carrying out policy choices in a territory
or political space (Mann 1984; Soifer 2015).
Administrative capacities are inherently contingent in na­
ture and multidimensional, emphasizing that each public
issue or intervention demands specific administrative
capacities; consequently, the discussion of capacities prompts
the question, “Capacities for what?” or “Power for what?”
(Chudnovsky 2015; Lindvall and Teorell 2016; Repetto
2004). Additionally, research shows that capacity levels can
differ significantly across states and offices, with limited ca­
pacity hampering the delivery and quality of public goods
(Imam and Temple 2023). Recognizing that capacity meas­
urement is a relevant but complex discussion, and following
Holland (2016, p. 233), capacity can be understood at two
levels: at or above the minimum required and below the min­
imum required.
The literature has recognized that administrative
burdens are inextricably linked to administrative capacities
(Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021), and that administrative
capacities shape the construction of burdens (Heinrich
2016; Heinrich and Brill 2015; Herd and Moynihan 2019;
Peeters 2019). Limitations in administrative capacities
and resources are factors that contribute to the experi­
ence of administrative burdens (Burden et al. 2012; p. 749;
Heinrich 2016; p. 8); they can limit the effectiveness of so­
cial programs (Heinrich and Brill 2015, p. 277) and are a
“source of institutional weakness” (Brinks, Levitsky, and
Murillo 2019; Peeters 2019).
Scholars such as Herd and Moynihan (2019) have suggested
that governments shift the burdens away from citizens onto
the state; however, “such a shift is not a free lunch” (p. 262).
This action “requires a well-functioning administrative state”
and demands a capable government that invests in informa­
tion and communication technology and administrative data
and allows professionals to manage burdens with autonomy
and resources (Herd et al. 2023; Herd and Moynihan 2019).
In impoverished nations, governments face limited capacities
to recognize and address these burdens, so the impact of ad­
ministrative burdens may be more significant in these coun­
tries (Herd and Moynihan 2019).
In this paper, administrative capacity is understood as
the state's organizational capacity to perform its mandate,
objectives, and programs (Ricciuti, Savoia, and Sen 2019).
It encompasses a combination of resources, abilities, and or­
ganizational standards of bureaucratic structures and public
servants (such as street-level bureaucrats) to manage burdens
and shift them from citizens onto the state or a third party or
eliminate them.
The literature review for this research found that authors
who study administrative burdens as a dependent variable
commonly indirectly report administrative capacities (of or­
ganizations or individuals) and their effect on administrative
burdens. The review found at least six general administra­
tive capacities that impact burdens, which are identified and
described below.
Policy design inherently shapes burdens (Baekgaard,
Moynihan, and Thomsen 2020; Herd and Moynihan 2020;
Peeters et al. 2018). Through the policy designs of citizen-
state interactions, governments can manage the rules gov­
erning interactions and gauge at least part of the compliance
costs, minimizing or avoiding them. The literature on admin­
istrative burdens indirectly calls for the ability to design less
burdensome citizen-state interactions. The design strategies of
bureaucratic encounters that influence burdens include: the
reconfiguration of the spatial point of the encounter (Nisar
2018), a carefully structured interaction that balances le­
gitimate state interests with citizens’ constitutional rights
(Doughty and Baehler 2020), administrative simplification

For instance, Linos and Riesch (2020) conducted a field
experiment in the US in which they simplified an administra­
tive process by removing two stages: standardizing formats
and using nudges. The new interaction design generated sev­
eral effects that were predominantly positive, resulting in
reduced administrative burdens overall; however, eliminating
one of the stages increased learning costs for some users.
This highlights the significance of interaction design as part
of a government's capacity to govern burdens, specifically in
identifying, managing, and shifting burdens away from citi­
zens onto the state or third parties.
Furthermore, material resources are another capacity
identified in the literature that can help alleviate adminis­
trative burdens. Technological resources (Linos, Quan, and
Kirkman 2020; Ntaliani and Costopoulou 2018), financial
resources for managing administrative procedures (Cook
2021; Lopoo, Heflin, and Boskovski 2020), and government
infrastructure, such as offices, facilities, mobile offices and us­
ability of bureaucratic spaces (Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021;
Nisar and Masood 2024), are key resources with the poten­
tial to influence burdens. Ntaliani and Costopoulou (2018)
studied how an electronic platform helped reduce administra­
tive tasks in Greece, Spain, and Latvia. They found that the
platform significantly reduced the time and effort needed for
three government procedures in rural areas. The administra­
tive reductions were 99%, 92%, and 10% across the three
countries, respectively. Governments had to invest financial
and technological resources in a new system to achieve these
results.
On the other hand, professionalism and the profession­
alization of government human resources are crucial for
minimizing, alleviating, or absorbing administrative burdens.
According to studies on administrative burdens, the profes­
sionalism demonstrated by street-level bureaucrats (SLBs)
during bureaucratic interactions involves their ability to ex­
ercise discretion in favor of citizens (Bell and Smith 2021)
and provide assistance and support to citizens (Carter, Scott,
and Mahallati 2018; Linos, Quan, and Kirkman 2020).
The professionalization of SLBs in managing bureaucratic
encounters entails ensuring a consistent and accurate appli­
cation of administrative rules and procedures (Carter, Scott,
and Mahallati 2018; Selin 2019) and involves using tools that
improve citizens’ informational positions (Widlak and Peeters
2020).
Numerous studies have highlighted the significance of
SLBs in managing administrative burdens (for example, Bell
and Smith 2021; Lotta, Nieto-Morales, and Peeters 2024;
Mikkelsen, Madsen, and Baekgaard 2024). Key capacities in­
clude the exercise of discretion (Moynihan, Herd, and Ribgy
2016; Nisar 2018; Selin 2019), the number of staff available
to assist service users (Peeters et al. 2018), and the knowledge
and skills of these staff members to perform their tasks effec­
tively (Halili 2020). This underscores governments’ need to
sustain a professional and adequate size of the SLB workforce
to address service demands.
Additionally, the ability of the government to communicate
is essential to managing citizens’ learning costs. In the litera­
ture on administrative burdens, some government skills have
been identified for this purpose. They include the design of
advertising to make known rules of citizen-state interactions
(Carter, Scott, and Mahallati 2018), the ability of the govern­
ment to provide complete, relevant, timely, understandable,
and accessible information (Ntaliani and Costopoulou 2018),
as well as clear, direct, transparent, and personalized commu­
nication to citizens (Linos, Quan, and Kirkman 2020). In these
contexts, digital alerts, prompts, and nudges for advancing in­
formation to citizens are also relevant for reducing burdens
(Lopoo, Heflin, and Boskovski 2020).
Linos, Quan, and Kirkman (2020) conducted three field
experiments in which they implemented nudges, one of which
involved sending advance communications to citizens through
a courtesy letter containing relevant information. This letter
not only requested action from the recipient but also provided
simplified guidance on how to comply. Both the sender (a gov­
ernment agency) and the recipient (the property owner) were
addressed by their full names, and the letter included detailed
and personalized information. The authors noted that the
courtesy letter reduced learning costs and improved citizen
compliance with the requested action. The level of person­
alization and the detail of information governments provide
citizens are closely linked to reductions in learning costs.
Collaborative or coordination mechanisms in bureaucratic
encounters serve as a capacity that can alleviate burdens or
transfer them to third parties, such as civil associations, ver­
ification units, advocacy groups, or indeed to other govern­
ment offices. This occurs through the institutionalization of
third-party participation in citizen-state interactions (Carter,
Scott, and Mahallati 2018; Linos, Quan, and Kirkman 2020;
Lopoo, Heflin, and Boskovski 2020; Moynihan, Gerzina, and
Herd 2022b; Wiley and Berry 2018). Non-state actors may
compensate for state actors’ and citizens’ lack of capacity,
though they themselves face their own capacity constraints
(Herd et al. 2023, p. 15). Furthermore, a well-designed collab­
orative mechanism integrated into the rules of interaction can
help identify system errors and facilitate corrections (Widlak
and Peeters 2020).
For instance, Wiley and Berry (2018) study how
governments hire non-governmental organizations (NGOs)
to perform specific tasks through contracts. These contracts
provide clear rules for NGOs to follow as “third-party
brokers.” NGOs partner with governments to secure re­
sources and implement policies supporting specific vulnerable
groups, absorbing some administrative burdens and easing
citizen-state interactions (Cook 2021; Heinrich 2016; Herd
et al. 2023).
Finally, the academic literature identifies the importance
of governmental structures for delivering citizen services as
a capacity to absorb administrative burdens effectively. This
is especially true when organizational structures align with
the specific needs of communities and individuals (Wiley and
Berry 2018), when they offer personalized assistance to citi­
zens (Herd and Moynihan 2020; Linos, Quan, and Kirkman
2020), and when the structure is both empowered and granted
autonomy in managing bureaucratic encounters (Selin 2019).
As a formal structure for managing bureaucratic encounters
with citizens, this capacity may overlap with governmental
communication and human capital capacity.

Research context and design
Context of the case: the microcredits for wellbeing
program
At the beginning of the Federal Administration 2018–2024,
the Mexican Government implemented the Microcredits for
Wellbeing Program as a microfinancing scheme (involving re­
payment). As a priority social program, it was implemented
through a new strategy and approach based on Mexican
President Andres Manuel López Obrador’s policy objective
to “offer and deliver social programs directly and without
intermediaries” (López Obrador 2021, p. 64).
To do this, the government conducted the “Welfare Census”
survey from October 2018 to April 2019 (México 2020e). An
“army” of street-level bureaucrats (SLBs), known colloquially
as servants of the nation (in Spanish servidores de la nación—
SN), was responsible for carrying out household visits for the
registration of potential beneficiaries to the social program
(México 2020e).
The implementation idea “to offer and deliver social
programs directly and without intermediaries” through the
Welfare Census and SLB household visits marked a signifi­
cant shift in citizen-state interactions of the federal Mexican
government. Instead of requiring a citizen-initiated encounter
involving a visit to governmental offices, it facilitated interac­
tion through government-initiated encounters, an underused
strategy in governmental social programs (Kahn, Katz, and
Gutek 1976).
Analyzing social programs in the context of bureaucratic
encounters facilitates the simultaneous study of administra­
tive capacities and burdens (Heinrich 2016; Kahn, Katz, and
Gutek 1976; Perales-Fernandez 2023). This empirical case
study observed five citizen-state interactions or bureaucratic
encounters. However, only three encounters are directly re­
lated to the program's access process and are the specific
focus of this research. The following paragraphs outline the
intended structure of these three bureaucratic encounters (BE)
as defined by the program's rules.
BE1. Promotion and registration of applications
This was planned as a government-initiated encounter (GIE).
The SLBs promoted the program and registered potential
beneficiaries during the Welfare Census through home visits
and electronic means (using real-time electronic systems, elec­
tronic devices, and apps with electronic questionnaires) into
the Integrated Regional Development System (SIDER, by its
Spanish acronym). Potential beneficiaries had to meet seven
eligibility criteria and requirements in 2019, which increased
to eight in 2020. These criteria included: (1) Owning a busi­
ness with at least six months in operation; (2) Aged 30 to
64 years old; (3) Residing within the Program Coverage; (4)
Support request (format 1); (5) Additional application form
(format 2) in 2020; (6) ID Card (INE); (7) Unique Population
Registry Code (CURP in Spanish); and (8) Proof of resi­
dence. Citizens presented these documents and answered the
questionnaires, while the SLBs filled them out online from an
electronic device.
BE2. Notification, verification of eligibility requirements and
criteria, and delivery of payment orders
This was planned as a government-initiated encounter (GIE).
In this second bureaucratic encounter, SLBs had to return to
citizens’ households to revise the eligibility criteria, inform
them about the results of their application, and deliver the
order folio to claim business loan support. The selection of
potential beneficiaries was based on a prioritization scheme
established by the Ministry of Economy (MoE), which was
not publicly disclosed. The MoE managed the program
and was in charge of defining the program rules, while the
Welfare Ministry was responsible for the Welfare Census
and program delivery. The verification process involved
compiling an applicant's electronic file following the specified
requirements. Once verification was complete and the pro­
gram criteria met, the SLBs delivered a payment order with
a reference number, along with instructions for beneficiaries
on how to collect their support, including details about the
location and timing of collection. The delivery of payment
orders did not guarantee the authorization of credit (México,
2019c, 2020a, 2020b). Prior review and validation by per­
sonnel from the MoE was required to ensure the electronic
files were correctly integrated. Following this review, the MoE
authorized and activated the payment orders (México 2020a,
2020b). Only after this process could the beneficiaries receive
the actual payment.
BE3. Social program payment
This was planned as a citizen-initiated encounter (CIE). The
encounter involved the disbursement of support payments
to the beneficiaries. Beneficiaries were required to visit a
financial institution designated by the government to col­
lect the payments, such as Telecomm—a federal agency
that offers financial services between individuals and gov­
ernmental entities nationwide. This third bureaucratic en­
counter required offices across the country to serve all
beneficiaries collecting social support from the microcredit
program.
Research method design
To explore how administrative capacities explain variation in
the administrative burden carried by citizens, an abductive
approach (Dubois and Gadde 2002) was employed within a
qualitative exploratory case study (Denzin and Lincoln 2000;
Gerring 2004; Stebbins 2001), focusing on a Mexican federal
social program, Microcredits for Wellbeing, during 2019 and
2020.
The abductive approach consisted of modeling the concept
of administrative capacities, which began with a literature re­
view on governmental capacities to identify types of admin­
istrative capacities. However, the findings were very general
and not necessarily linked to administrative burdens, so the
decision was made to conduct open-ended interviews with
beneficiaries and SLBs without predefined specific categories
of analysis of administrative capacities.
Following the interviews, a new and comprehensive liter­
ature review was necessary. This involved revisiting papers
on administrative burdens to identify indirect mentions of
administrative capacities, as researchers often report on
observed resources, abilities, and organizational standards
and their impact on burdens. With this information, it
was possible to model the capacities and identify them in
the patterns found in the interviews, leading to the results
presented in this paper  . For more details on the research
method design, see the appendices in the online supplemen­
tary material.

Empirical exercise
The empirical exercise consisted of 122 semi-structured phone
interviews, from September 2020 to June 2021 with program
beneficiaries and SLBs in two Mexican states: Mexico City
(CDMX by its Spanish initialism) and Hidalgo State.
The interview sampling frame—jointly established by
the researcher and personnel from the Welfare Ministry in
Mexico City and the State of Hidalgo—included individuals
who confirmed receiving the support and voluntarily agreed
to participate in the study. The welfare offices could have
considered those who were up to date with their payments,
had participated in program activities such as welfare fairs or
training, or had more mature businesses, but these criteria were
not directly or indirectly related to the administrative burdens
experienced during the beneficiaries’ access process. On the
other hand, the state delegations of the Welfare Ministry pro­
vided data for SLBs who met three conditions: (1) they had
worked in the Microcredits for Wellbeing Program, (2) they
had worked in the selected areas of the research, and (3) they
agreed to participate in the study.
A minimum number of beneficiaries and SLBs was estab­
lished prior to the study by the researcher and the government
team; however, the final number of interviews conducted in
both groups was based on the conceptual saturation achieved
in the categories of analysis (Charmaz 2014, p. 113). This

All interviewees provided informed consent and were
audio-recorded. The interviews afforded a triangulation of
beneficiary burdens' experiences with SLBs’ implementation,
examining functions, rules, policy instruments, and govern­
ment resources at each stage of the access process. This made
it possible to identify the administrative capacities used in the
program at the point of encounter between the SLBs and the
citizens.
This research approach enabled the study of burdens not
only from citizens’ experiences but also through the analysis
of formal rules. The Atlas.ti program was used to triangulate

Findings
The program's access process was designed to be completed
in three bureaucratic encounters. Two of these would be
initiated by the state through visits by the servants of
the nation to the citizens’ homes or businesses to offer
government programs, and a third initiated by the citi­
zens to receive the microcredit in person at an authorized
office. The access process involved five expected adminis­
trative burdens, or “burdens of design,” which refers to
deliberate interaction costs that policies impose on citizens
through officially authorized procedures or administrative
instruments published in official media (Perales-Fernandez
2023). These burdens are intentional and formal and are
part of the policy's causal theory (Peeters 2019). Also, they
were deduced from the wording established in the opera­
tional rules of the program.
The program's access process initially seemed straightfor­
ward during the policy design phase. The requirements and
eligibility criteria appeared impartial and achievable for all
potential candidates. However, during the transition from de­
sign to implementation, gaps arose that amplified and altered
the number of administrative burdens that citizens might face
and the nature of bureaucratic interactions. In both states,
the implementation phase increased administrative burdens,
complicating access to the program beyond the initial plans.
Table 2 summarizes key findings regarding planned versus
implemented encounters and burdens.
Mexico City implemented the encounters designed in the
program rules: the two initial encounters were initiated by
the state (GIE), and the last was initiated by citizens (CIE).
However, Mexico City generated fourteen burdens, not five as
planned. Hidalgo State could not complete the three planned
bureaucratic encounters, and citizen-initiated encounters
(CIE) were predominant throughout the program's ac­
cess process. Furthermore, Hidalgo State produced eighteen
burdens, suggesting multiple potential unforeseen issues
during the planning and implementation phases.

According to the literature, there are three different
explanations for the increase in administrative burdens.
States may implement deliberate political decisions (strategies
and tactics), utilizing administrative burdens as a form of
“policy making by other means” by making interactions on­
erous for opposing groups while promoting partisan-political
goals (Herd and Moynihan 2019; Moynihan, Herd, and
Harvey 2015, 2016). Additionally, states may unintention­
ally engage in benign neglect, where a lack of organizational
awareness of the potential impact on citizens of bureaucratic
procedures results in burdens arising “inadvertently, by acci­
dent, or through administrative incompetence” (Moynihan,
Herd, and Harvey 2015; Peeters 2019). Moreover, differences
in citizens’ human capital (particularly cognitive resources)
may have contributed to burdens, given its relevance to
individuals’ perceptions of burdens and their abilities to nav­
igate administrative challenges and engage with state actors
and SLBs (Christensen et al. 2020).
However, while each of these explanations is plausible in
influencing the differences in administrative burdens among
states, none were proven during the empirical exercise. For
instance, no discrimination or exclusion was reported among
beneficiary interviewees based on their political affiliations.
It was noted that beneficiary interviewees from each state re­
ceived comparable treatment based on unique circumstances
within their respective states. Therefore, intentional political

through official communications (CONAMER/19/0685
and CONAMER/20/0870), highlighting that the Ministry
had failed to specify the timeframes, both for beneficiaries
to complete the procedure and for the authority's decision.
CONAMER recommended that the Ministry of Economy
incorporate these terms into the Regulatory Proposal before
publication in the official gazette. However, the Ministry did
not act on this recommendation, leaving response times in
the program rules unspecified. This example suggests an in­
tentional decision rather than human error or benign neglect,
which is why this explanation was ruled out as a factor in
the variation in burdens.
Regarding the human capital explanation, beneficiary
interviewees reported an average of 10.6 years of schooling
in Mexico City and 10.3 years in Hidalgo State . Beneficiary
interviewees in both states and with varying degrees of mul­
tidimensional poverty stated that the program’s requirements
were straightforward and that they found the access process
manageable. Beneficiaries reported that SLBs helped them to
understand the eligibility criteria and the access process. No
significant differences were found in human capital or the
complexity perceived by beneficiary interviewees regarding
eligibility criteria and the access processes. For these reasons,
human capital was also ruled out to explain the burden var­
iations identified.
Instead, the research provides compelling evidence and data
that the main explanation for the differences in administrative
burdens between Mexico City and Hidalgo State was the
presence or limitation of the administrative capacities to im­
plement the program’s access process. The research found
that capacities have the potential to shape (create, reduce,
amplify, or transfer) administrative burdens. Six administra­
tive capacities were identified that shape burdens positively
or negatively. These capacities can be arranged into three ad­
ministrative burdens governance dimensions: the first dimen­
sion refers to administrative capacities to manage burdens
according to types of planned bureaucratic encounters; the
second dimension relates to capacities for managing burdens
resulting from implementation gaps or failures; and the third
dimension refers to capacities for managing burdens to imple­
ment policy design.
First dimension: Administrative capacities to
manage burdens according to types of planned
bureaucratic encounters
The disparities in the types of encounters implemented by
each State (Mexico City and Hidalgo) during the first two
planned interactions resulted in differences in burdens. The
people who experienced government-initiated encounters
avoided the learning and compliance costs (i.e., transfer costs
for visiting government offices) because SLBs informed them
about the access process and eligibility criteria during the
household visits. Mexico City showed more capacity to offer
its beneficiaries this experience than Hidalgo State. Three
administrative capacities were identified for implementing
government-initiated encounters.

Government communication strategies: clear, direct, and
personalized communication
Central to the Government’s idea to “offer and deliver so­
cial programs directly and without intermediaries” (López
Obrador 2021, p. 64) was the role of SLBs in visiting po­
tential beneficiaries and communicating all details of
the program's rules and benefits in a clear, direct, and
personalized manner, thereby enabling the transfer of learning
costs to the government. As a result of these encounters, in
Mexico City, 35% of interviewed beneficiaries faced learning
costs directly while researching the program, in contrast to
69% of interviewed beneficiaries from Hidalgo State. SLBs
in Mexico City performed the program's function more ef­
fectively by providing citizens with information about
the program and its details. Notably, 87% of interviewed
beneficiaries viewed the access process positively in Mexico
City, compared to 74% in Hidalgo State.
Organizational structures that provide personalized
assistance to citizens
In CDMX, the population is less dispersed, with a population
density of 6,163 inhabitants per square kilometer. From 2018
to 2020, the annual average of SLBs assigned in CDMX was
1,433. The number of SLBs and Mexico City’s population
density allowed for more direct engagement with citizens and
facilitated the effective implementation of GIE encounters.
By contrast, in Hidalgo State, which has a population den­
sity of 148 inhabitants per square kilometer, the annual av­
erage of SLBs assigned during the same period was 595.
The challenges faced by SLBs in Hidalgo were exacerbated
by greater distances to target areas, higher costs associated
with transportation, and sometimes a lack of available trans­
port, complicating their work and the implementation of
GIE encounters. The organizational structure established for
CDMX, therefore, facilitated the more efficient transfer of
learning costs and compliance costs to the government, as ab­
sorbed by SLBs.
Second dimension: administrative capacities for
managing burdens resulting from implementation
gaps.
When the intended outcomes of a policy do not match its
actual results, it is commonly termed an implementation
problem, gap, or failure (Aguilar 1993; Hill and Hupe 2002;
Hogwood and Gunn 1984:2018). Capacity limitations can
lead to implementation gaps (Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021,
p. 14). This research found five administrative capacities (as
implementation gaps) that can lead to the creation or expan­
sion of administrative burdens when they fall below the re­
quired minimum level.
1. Professionalization of SLBs
Building capacities of SLBs involved ensuring their under­
standing of program rules (and rule updates), procedures,
and systems, as well as how to avoid problems and interpret
errors. It also involved allowing SLBs to gain practical experi­
ence in applying rules correctly and consistently.
Some beneficiaries reported problems processing their
applications, which obligated them to submit their application
twice. This could be attributed to the fact that the servants of
the nation were newly created as an organizational structure,
and the program and its procedures were also newly created.
At the start of program implementation, there was little ad­
ministrative experience in applying rules and procedures, as
well as managing information and matching the programs
with the beneficiaries correctly (Moynihan et al. 2022a).
Consequently, as Fernanda explains, this resulted in addi­
tional compliance costs for the beneficiaries.
Interviewer: “You submitted all the documenta­
tion twice?”
Respondent: “Yes, twice…both times [SLBs] came,
unexpectedly, and both times …[SLBs] had to take
photographs of the evidence of my small business.”
(Fernanda, beneficiary, CDMX)
In CDMX, duplicate information was presented in 13% of
cases, and in Hidalgo State in 10% of cases, which created
new burdens of duplicate document submissions. While these
administrative burdens may decrease over time as SLBs ac­
cumulate experience and knowledge, it is important for
governments to professionalize their SLBs before program
implementation to minimize unnecessary burdens on citizens.
Ability to design less burdensome citizen-state interactions,
balancing the state's rational and legitimate interests with
citizens’ constitutional rights
In this case study, one of the most significant program access
burdens in both states was the excessive time citizens had to
wait for the government's response to applications. The gov­
ernment failed to forecast the response time in its program
rules and balance this with the citizens’ needs, which there­
fore generated compliance and psychological costs for citi­
zens. Some of the beneficiaries expressed their experiences as
follows:
“After [registration], it was about a year [when servants
of the nation came back to inform me about approval
of the application]. I had already forgotten that we had
joined this welfare program.” (Aletxe, beneficiary, Mexico
City)
“Well, yes, some time passed, I think it took months, and
I did not remember that I had signed up [...] about 5 or 6
months, or more, I mean.” (Adolfo, beneficiary, Hidalgo)
Did having to wait so long generate feelings?
“Yes, like frustration.” (Rocío, beneficiary, Mexico City)
“I was disappointed because I thought they would not
give it to me. I went to ask every week at their offices.”
(Carol, beneficiary, Hidalgo)
Failure to accurately calculate and communicate the response
time, which led to an average waiting time of six months,
damaged the government's image and amplified and created
burdens for citizens as psychological costs. In CDMX, the av­
erage response time over the analysis period was 5.9 months,
with a response time range of 0.5 to 18 months. In Hidalgo
State, the average response time over the analysis period was
6.1 months, with a response time range of 0.25 to 14 months.
In both states, beneficiaries experienced psychological costs
such as frustration, loss of autonomy (uncertainty), stress, dis­
satisfaction, and discomfort.

Coordination schemes among government offices
The MoE was responsible for managing the distribution of
resources and processing payment orders within the avail­
able budget. Once MoE staff had verified that beneficiaries’
electronic files were uploaded and were adequately inte­
grated by SLBs into the SIDER, payment orders were acti­
vated, and funds were transferred to payment entities (such
as Telecomm). While this process was part of the program
rules and required a waiting period for payment order ac­
tivation, there was no set maximum time for review and
no precise mechanism for updating the Welfare Ministry
and its SLBs on the activation status of payment orders.
Beneficiaries had to wait on average an additional month
to collect the support: 28 days in Mexico City and 30 days
in Hidalgo. Furthermore, beneficiary interviewees reported
that although SLBs provided an estimated activation date
for the folio, the absence of a clear timeframe caused some
beneficiaries to face additional compliance costs, as they
had to make up to three visits to the Telecomm offices in
Hidalgo State and four visits in CDMX to try to collect the
social support. Limited coordination capacities between the
MoE and the Welfare Ministry for authorizing the support
("tanda") payments in both Mexican states expanded ex­
isting compliance costs.
Government communication strategies
During the notification phase, the government provided
support payment information such as approximate dates of
payment, place of collection, and mechanisms for reimburse­
ment. However, this information did not correspond to the
reality that citizens faced. Compliance costs were generated
for travel to the place of the payment (Telecomm), with cit­
izens making an average of 1.6 visits to collect the tanda,
ranging from 1 to 4 visits in Mexico City and 1 to 3 visits in
Hidalgo, and using electronic means to contact SLBs asking
for the payment date. Given the lack of coordination between
the ministries of Economy and Welfare for the activation of
payment orders, the communication efforts of the SLBs were
undermined by their limited ability to provide complete, rele­
vant, timely, and reliable information to citizens.
Furthermore, beneficiaries in both states reported new
requirements at the payment institution (Telecomm) due to
a lack of advance information regarding paperwork required
for the payment. Beneficiaries needed to provide a copy of
their identification card (INE) and payment order, which
resulted in them having to leave the building to obtain the
copies required by the Telecomm payer.
“I was not informed that I had to bring a copy of my ID
Card [INE], nor a copy of the payment order to leave it at
the cashier's counter [in Telecomm], so I had to go out and
get the copies.” (Miguel, beneficiary, Mexico City)
“I had my payment order, but they [servants of the na­
tion] never told me I had to bring copies, so I had to go and
get them and return to Telecomm.” (Fabiola, beneficiary,
Hidalgo)
This situation shows a limited ability to inform the beneficiaries
in advance of the information needed to collect the loan. As
beneficiaries declared, this experience added new burdens,
such as compliance costs (for delivery of documents for proof
of identity) and psychological costs (stress, frustration).
Government resources: facilities
Telecomm is a decentralized public agency attached to the
Ministry of Communications and Transportation of the
Mexican Federal Government, which offers financial services
between individuals and governmental entities. It participates
in the Microcredits program, offering payment services to
beneficiaries via its offices. However, beneficiaries experi­
enced long waiting times for the financial services and in­
adequate facilities to handle in-person payments. Customer
service for beneficiaries was inconsistent, leading to a neg­
ative perception of Telecomm’s services and amplifying ex­
isting burdens in the form of compliance costs of in-person
waiting times.
“The only difficulty was the waiting times in line; it was
an hour's wait.” (Juan, beneficiary, Mexico City)
“There were many people, so I had to wait in line, half
an hour to an hour.” (Pepe, beneficiary, Hidalgo)
Beneficiaries reported average waiting times at Telecomm
offices of 30 minutes in both states (32 minutes in CDMX
and 28 minutes in Hidalgo State). These ranged from 5 to
120 minutes in CDMX and from 2 to 180 minutes in Hidalgo
State, resulting in significant additional compliance costs for
some beneficiaries.
Third Dimension: administrative capacities for
managing burdens to implement policy design
Governments require appropriate capacities to implement
policy design and prevent the emergence of new administra­
tive burdens. In this case study, two capacities were shown to
be fundamental to managing program implementation and
governance burdens.
Ability to design less burdensome citizen-state interactions
via automated registration processes
The standardization, formalization, and rationalization of
the program's access processes through the automatization
of the register avoided administrative burdens, principally
compliance costs. In both states, the use of the SIDER system
was consistent and homogeneous. This system was fed by an
electronic questionnaire App through which the SLBs filled in
the beneficiaries’ information and took photos of the required
documents. By automating the registration process, SLBs
could adhere correctly to program rules without requesting
unnecessary information. Out of eighty-seven beneficiaries
interviewed in both states, only 3.4% reported submitting
documents beyond what was required by program rules. This
indicates that the implementation of automated registration
prevented the request for additional documentation in 96.6%
of cases. It is noteworthy that the use of digital registration
processes by government employees (SLBs) rather than cit­
izens appears to have reduced beneficiaries’ administrative
burdens.
Academic studies have commonly discussed the advantages
and disadvantages of automation and digitalization when
these processes are carried out by citizens. These studies sug­
gest that automation boosts efficiency and lowers compliance
costs for citizens, but it is also possible that citizens may en­
counter administrative burdens when their interactions are
digital (for example, Fredriksson 2020; Madsen, Lindgren,
and Melin 2022; Peeters 2023; Peeters and Widlak 2018).

Government resources: financial and technological resources
The government implemented the SIDER system by providing
electronic devices and technology, such as cell phones and
Apps, to SLBs. Financial resources were also often necessary
to cover the costs of SLBs’ household visits and facilitate com­
munication with citizens through the Internet and phone lines.
The lack of these resources would impose additional burdens
on the program's access process. Indeed, a relevant finding
was that SLBs in Mexico City and Hidalgo State allocated
a considerable portion of their salary, averaging 24% and
33%, respectively, toward acquiring the necessary resources
when the government fell short of meeting these needs1.
Nonetheless, by utilizing the SIDER system and household
visits for registration and verification, the program was able
to avoid compliance costs associated with physical document
delivery and limit discretionary requests for additional doc­
umentation. This streamlined approach ensured consistent
requirements throughout the implementation process and
facilitated a smooth registration experience.

The administrative capacities can have a positive or nega­

Discussion
This paper presents evidence on how administrative capacities
lead to variations in the experience of administrative burdens,
offering an additional theoretical basis for understanding
why burdens may differ under a single policy implementation
plan. Administrative capacities add to the explanations that
already exist in the literature to explain variations of adminis­
trative burdens (deliberate political strategies, benign neglect,
and human capital), all of which, in particular ways, have the
power to impact the experience of policy implementation in
many contexts (Burden et al. 2012).
The primary contribution of this research is to demonstrate
that variations in administrative capacities can influence the
burdens citizens face. Numerous scholars have pointed to
the relationship between capacities and burdens, including
Heinrich (2016), Herd and Moynihan (2019 and 2020),
Peeters (2019), and Selin (2019). Notably, it has been estab­
lished that while access rules and procedures are intended to
promote equality, they do not always yield equal outcomes
(Peeters and Nieto-Morales 2021). Varied experiences can
arise from weaknesses in administrative capacities related to
service delivery (p. 20). The findings indicate that variations
in administrative capacities between the Welfare Ministry
delegation offices in Mexico City and Hidalgo State led to
differences in citizen burden levels across states, with both
states experiencing notable increases compared to the burdens
initially planned.
This paper's second contribution is the identification of six
types of administrative capacities that can impact administra­
tive burdens, either positively or negatively. This adds to the
emerging body of research on burden reduction (for example,
Cantarelli et al. 2024; Herd, Moynihan, and Widman 2024;
Kalucza and Sievert 2024). The findings suggest that the

The ability to design less burdensome citizen-state
interactions.
Policymakers’
decisions
on
structuring
citizen-state interactions directly impact the resources and
capabilities needed for their implementation and the ad­
ministrative burdens citizens face. Furthermore, balancing
the government's interests with the rights and interests of citi­
zens (Doughty and Baehler 2020) is an administrative capacity
that impacts the onerousness of program access. This can
potentially add new compliance and psychological costs or
help avoid burdens, ultimately achieving “Citizen Centricity,”
where citizens are at the center of the state's decision-­making
(OECD 2009; World Bank Group 2018).
Government
communication
strategies
are
often
overlooked, but they are essential for transferring burdens to
the government. Communicating the details of the interac­
tion and the next steps in advance reduces burdens on citizens
(Linos, Quan, and Kirkman 2020). Conversely, communica­
tion failures can trigger administrative burdens (Holler and
Tarshish 2022). In this case, government communication
was relevant for implementing the interaction design and
avoiding burdens (learning costs) for citizens. However, when
communication fails during the implementation of the access
process, it can generate new burdens or extend existing ones.
In this regard, the results highlight that to reduce burdens,
governments must be committed to providing citizens with
timely information on social programs and developing meas­
ures to ensure clear, direct, individualized, and transparent
communication about the program's rules (Linos, Quan, and
Kirkman 2020).
Additionally, government resources, such as technological,
financial, and facilities, are essential for implementing gov­
ernment actions (Linos, Quan, and Kirkman 2020) and, when
at or above the required level, can help avoid administrative
burdens. For example, Technological resources (Ntaliani
and Costopoulou 2018) and financial resources are neces­
sary to execute supervision and facilitate regulatory com­
pliance (Carter, Scott, and Mahallati 2018; Selin 2019). In
the case discussed in this paper, technological and financial
resources were necessary for conducting fieldwork, such as
identifying potential beneficiaries and registering them, per­
forming verifications, and delivering payment orders. These
resources helped alleviate the administrative burden on cit­
izens. Nonetheless, resource constraints may force organiza­
tions to rethink the interaction design, creating new burdens
or amplifying the existing ones.
Organizational structures that provide personalized assis­
tance to citizens, such as helping them to complete their en­
rollment applications through front-line staff (Linos, Quan,
and Kirkman 2020), can increase enrollment rates for social
programs (Herd and Moynihan 2020) and transfer some of
the learning and compliance costs to the personnel assisting
them, whether government or NGO staff. Papers on admin­
istrative burdens have highlighted the relevance of devel­
oping organizational structures that assist citizens in their
process of application (for example, Herd and Moynihan
2020; Linos, Quan, and Kirkman 2020; Lopoo, Heflin, and
Boskovski 2020; Wiley and Berry 2018). In the case study, at
least 2,028 servants of the nation were engaged in the Welfare
Census and oversaw the federal government's priority so­
cial programs in Mexico City and Hidalgo State. According
to reports from the federal government, there were 18,000
servants of the nation at the outset of the Welfare Census
in the whole country (México 2020e). Engaging SLBs, like
servants of the nation, or independent third parties, such as
NGOs, to support citizens in program access and application
follow-up is an administrative capacity that transfers admin­
istrative burdens, especially learning and compliance costs,
to the state or third parties as appropriate. It is important
to acknowledge, however, that delegating responsibilities to
third parties to alleviate citizen's burdens can impose signifi­
cant costs on those third parties, who must also manage their
own capacity constraints (Herd et al. 2023). This emphasizes
how, in cases of burden transfer, the burden remains, and the
party taking on the responsibility has to take necessary meas­
ures to address it.
Coordination schemes among government offices are cru­
cial in scenarios where implementing public policies and
programs involves different levels of governance (Lopoo,
Heflin, and Boskovski 2020). The participation of multiple
institutions fragments task execution. Proper coordination to
facilitate citizen actions, such as sending and forwarding ap­
plication forms or notifications with the support of the Postal
Office, can reduce administrative burdens (Linos, Quan,
and Kirkman 2020; Lopoo, Heflin, and Boskovski 2020).
By contrast, inadequate coordination among institutions
(such as the coordination failures between the MoE and the
Welfare Ministry) in performing actions like authorizations,
validations, or the execution of a social program could result
in new administrative burdens.
Finally, the degree of professionalization of SLBs
in implementing social programs can impact citizens’
experiences in their interactions with the state. The literature
has highlighted the importance of professionalizing SLBs to
effectively carry out tasks such as supervising administrative
rules and procedures (Carter, Scott, and Mahallati 2018),
ensuring the correct, uniform understanding and application
of rules (Selin 2019), and utilizing tools to improve the in­
formational position of citizens (Widlak and Peeters 2020).
These measures help SLBs to absorb or avoid administrative
burdens. This research found that low levels of professionali­
zation of SLBs can create new administrative burdens. When
governments fail to equip SLBs with the necessary capabilities,
administrative burdens inevitably increase.
This list of administrative capacities proposes a framework
for understanding the influence of capacities on burdens.
Findings indicate that administrative burdens are highly
sensitive to administrative capacities. When governments
lack the essential capacities to implement the planned in­
teraction, new administrative burdens can easily arise or be
exacerbated. Conversely, when capacities meet the minimum
requirements, and the design prioritizes burden reduction, the
intended objectives are fulfilled.

cance of governing administrative burdens through enhancing
administrative capacities. Nisar and Masood (2024) state
that material and virtual infrastructure—in this paper, office

The case study presented reveals that the Mexican govern­
ment aimed to foster closer connections with citizens through
bureaucratic encounters initiated by the government, spe­
cifically through household visits conducted by the servants
of the nation. While the government’s intentions appeared
sincere, the effectiveness of administrative capacities was
contingent upon specific contextual factors. In Hidalgo, for
instance, there were insufficient human and financial re­
sources—especially considering the state’s geographic and
socio-demographic landscape—to develop the proposed in­
teraction model effectively. This resulted in new and increased
burdens despite Hidalgo having a smaller population than
Mexico City.
Regardless of the type of citizen-state interaction
governments choose to pursue, it is imperative that they de­
velop the ability to identify and understand the nature of
administrative burdens, as well as the effects of administra­
tive capacities on administrative burdens within the specific
contexts of public policy implementation. Limited capacities
increase the uncertainty of citizens and SLBs and how public
policies are expected to work, increasing administrative
burdens and making bureaucratic encounters onerous and
complex. Developing a comprehensive categorization of ad­
ministrative capacities based on interaction design and cit­
izens’ profiles would offer governments valuable guidance
on the capacities needed to alleviate administrative burdens
on citizens, thereby reducing the bureaucratic barriers to
accessing social rights.
Ultimately, the findings from this research should be un­
derstood within the context of certain limitations. The col­
lection of information coincided with the health contingency
for COVID-19, so telephone interviews were conducted,
impeding the observation of the non-verbal communication of
interviewees. Also, due to the long duration of the pandemic,
the strategy of direct observation in the field was canceled.
In this regard, not all the administrative burdens might have
been documented. Additionally, snowball sampling could not
be employed in the failure cases (i.e., citizens who were inter­
ested in enrolling in the program but did not receive the neces­
sary support), so the administrative burdens they encountered
in their attempt to enroll and the exclusion mechanisms they
experienced were not identified. Likewise, it was impossible
to study all the possible administrative capacities required
for program implementation at middle and senior manage­
ment levels, meaning the identified capacities may not be all
the necessary ones. Furthermore, direct traceability between
the beneficiaries and the servants of the nation who assisted
them was not possible, so the individual capacities of SLBs
were not measured. Despite these limitations, this research
provides valuable insights into the relationship between ad­
ministrative burdens and administrative capacity variables at
the point of citizen-state encounter.
Conclusions
This article enriches the existing literature on administrative
burdens by delineating a core set of administrative capacities.
Knowing the specific administrative capacities that contribute
to the governance of administrative burdens is a first step
to advancing the study of reducing burdens on citizens and
optimizing the design of the citizen-state interaction.
This paper is a foundation for further studies on ad­
ministrative burdens to continue exploring citizen-state
interactions, understanding the nature of the interaction,
and how administrative burdens can become particularly
pronounced when governmental organizations face limited
capacities. The administrative capacities identified in this
research paper are not exhaustive. Further investigation is
needed to understand how administrative capacities and
burdens are linked. This research encourages scholars in fu­
ture studies to explicitly identify and describe the administra­
tive capacities observed, the type of burden impacted by the
observed capacity, the effects on burdens (whether avoided,
reduced, transferred, created, or amplified), and as far as
possible, the required levels of capacities. Further research
should also examine the effects of transferring burdens to
third parties, with a focus on impacts on the policy eco­
system and program management.
Further studies could develop logical algorithms to stream­
line and optimize administrative burdens by considering, for
example, the interaction design, the administrative capacities
of government offices, multi-contextual factors where interac­
tion will be implemented, and the citizen’s profile (including
their human, social, and average administrative capital).
Expanding the list of required administrative capacities that
influence burdens may contribute to reducing those burdens
and encourage scholars to improve methods for measuring
both capacities and burdens.

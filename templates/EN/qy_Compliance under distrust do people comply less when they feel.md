Department of Accounting, School of Business and Economics, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, Amsterdam, Netherlands

Abstract
Several studies indicate that people are less compliant when they feel distrusted. This can pose a challenge for public administration, as some
forms of control may signal distrust towards people and could undermine their motivation to comply. In this study, we question whether feeling
distrusted is necessarily negative for compliance. In two experiments on tax compliance (N = 239), we examine the case in which the individual
is distrusted by the authority. Mediation analyses indicate that distrust reduces opportunism, and this is in turn associated with higher compli-
ance. In a survey experiment on compliance with COVID-19 rules (N = 590), we examine the case in which the individual’s group is distrusted
by other members of society. A mediation analysis indicates that distrust increases opportunism, but only for participants who already see
themselves as less compliant than average, and this is in turn associated with a lower willingness to comply in the near future. These findings
challenge the notion that distrust necessarily leads to retaliation or negative reciprocity, and indicate that the cautious communication of distrust
may even be positive in some cases.
Key words: compliance; distrust in citizens; feeling distrusted; opportunism.
Introduction
Part of the objective of public administration is to generate com-
pliance (Baltodano 1997; May 2005; Weaver 2015). To achieve
this, public agencies commonly employ control mechanisms that
rely on supervision and the threat of punishment for noncompli-
ance (Leong and Howlett 2022; Piquero et al. 2011; Pratt et al.
2017). One potential problem with this type of coercive control
is that it can signal distrust (Bigoni et al. 2015; Bouckaert 2012;
Braithwaite 1998; Lee 2021; Lee and Lee 2022; Six 2013; Van
de Walle and Lahat 2017; Van Slyke 2007), and this has led to
the question of how feeling distrusted affects compliance.
This is not an obvious question. While several studies in-
dicate that feeling distrusted reduces cooperativeness and
compliance (e.g., Belot and Schr¨oder 2016; Falk and Kosfeld
2006; Rudorf et al. 2018), there are valid reasons to ques-
tion whether this is necessarily the case (Lee and Dodge
2019; Mooijman et al. 2017). On the one hand, distrust may
be perceived as antagonistic, reduce intrinsic motivations
to cooperate, and induce retaliatory responses (Enzle and
Anderson 1993; Frey and Jegen 2001; Weibel and Six 2013).
On the other hand, distrust may motivate ethical delibera-
tion and induce a sense of responsibility towards the collec-
tive interest (Feldman and Kaplan 2021; May 2005), or may
motivate strategic cooperation with the objective to restore
trust and escape the negative consequences of being distrusted
(Mendoza and Wielhouwer 2015; Mooijman and Graham
2018; Nurmohamed 2020). Despite this tension, we are not
aware of experimental studies designed to assess how feeling
distrusted affects compliance.
Our objective is to investigate whether the potential ef-
fect of distrust is necessarily negative. We conduct three
experiments in which randomized groups of participants are
identified as distrusted, and are subsequently asked to make
decisions and answer questions related to compliance. The
analyses indicate that distrust can induce positive as well as
conditionally negative effects. With these findings, we seek
to make two concrete contributions to the public adminis-
tration literature. First, we present evidence challenging the
notion that distrust is necessarily negative for compliance.
This can help reevaluate the premise that distrust undermines
a positive voluntary response (Bouckaert 2012; Bowles
2008; Bowles and Polania-Reyes 2012; DeHart-Davis 2009;
Ruscio 1996; Six 2013; Weibel and Six 2013) and suggests
that interventions signaling distrust are not always ineffi-
cient. Second, we present evidence indicating that distrust
makes people adjust their intention to respond opportunisti-
cally, and in this way, we identify a relevant mechanism in the
association between distrust and compliance (adjustments
in opportunism operate as a mediator). To evaluate the be-
havioral impact of distrust, it may be necessary to first as-
sess its effect on intentions, and this goes in line with the
idea that intentions play a key mediating role in explaining
norm-violating behavior (Bennett et al. 2024). Our findings
may help advance different lines of public administration
research, including the literature on distrust towards cit-
izens (Bouckaert 2012; Six 2013; Six and Latusek 2023;
Yang 2005), the literature identifying not only the negative
but also the positive aspects of distrust (e.g., Lee and Dodge
2019; Lee and Lee 2022), and more broadly, the literature
recognizing perceptions and feelings as relevant predictors of
socially desirable behaviors (Grimmelikhuijsen et al. 2017;
Tummers et al. 2016).

We begin by examining the case in which the individual is
distrusted by the authority, as this type of distrust has been
recognized as relevant in the public administration literature.
For example, it has been theorized that public administrators’
“lack of trust may be one of the biggest obstacles to au-
thentic citizen involvement” (Yang 2005, p. 273); that “the
communication of distrust” by regulators may destroy “vir-
tuous citizenship” (Braithwaite and Makkai 1994, p. 2); and
that there can be a “negative impact of regulator distrust on
trust building, regulatee self-determination and compliance”
(Six 2013, p. 180). We also examine the case in which the
individual’s group is distrusted by other members of society.
For example, immigrants, ethnic minorities, or young adults
may be distrusted by others, who expect these groups not
to comply with certain rules or in certain situations. Some
groups may be perceived as untrustworthy and to some ex-
tent unwilling to comply. This type of distrust matters in this
context, considering that lack of trust between groups may
influence cooperation (Balliet, Wu, and De Dreu 2014), and
social distrust may influence governance, collective action,
and civic behavior (Aghion et al. 2010; Balliet and Van Lange
2013; Draude, H¨olck, and Stolle 2018; Hardin 2004; Peters
2018; Van de Walle and Lahat 2017). Notably, particularized
social distrust, which is “directed towards members of a spe-
cifically defined group,” may influence the way in which the
distrusted group feels in terms of security and identity (Bilgic,
Hoogensen Gjørv, and Wilcock 2019, p. 1286), and this may
in turn influence their behavior. In either case (when the indi-
vidual is distrusted by the authority, or when the individual’s
group is distrusted by others), the conventional view is that
distrust is detrimental, and this is precisely what we seek to
test.
Theory
Distrust in the context of compliance
Distrust refers to expectations of intentions which are incon-
gruent or in conflict with the distrusting actor’s interests or
values (Rousseau et al. 1998; Schul, Mayo, and Burnstein
2008; Six and Latusek 2023; Ullmann-Margalit 2004).
Following this conceptualization, there are three points to
consider for the purpose of this analysis. First, distrust in this
context reflects expectations of intentions not to comply. A
distrusted individual is expected to have “the intention to
undermine the interests of the authorities” (Mooijman et al.
2017, p. 578), and therefore, is expected to be to some extent
uncooperative and break the rules (Mooijman et al. 2015).
Distrust in this sense reflects the belief that the individual
will not “act in a fashion that is helpful or beneficial” to the
authority’s performance or goal fulfillment (Yang and Holzer
2006, p. 276).
Second, if compliance is in the best interest of society, dis-
trust may come from anyone interested in compliance, not
only from the authority. Anyone interested in compliance
may distrust those who appear to have the intention not to
comply, and who appear to stand at odds with the collec-
tive interest. This is why compliance decisions can also be
analyzed as collective action problems or social dilemmas
(e.g., public goods and commons dilemmas; Kollock 1998),
as they commonly represent a conflict between short-term
self-interest and long-term collective interest (Van Lange
et al. 2007, 2013).
Third, although noncompliance can be attributed to in-
ability and incompetence (Kauppi and Van Raaij 2015; Six
and Latusek 2023) or biases and bounded ethicality (Feldman
2018), the strategic or calculative intention not to comply is
commonly associated with self-interest (Feldman and Kaplan
2021; Van Lange et al. 2013). This type of uncooperative,
short-term oriented self-interest is often described as oppor-
tunism (Das 2004; Lyons and Mehta 1997). Opportunism
can be defined as “self-interest seeking of a more strategic
kind,” and more specifically, as “self-interest seeking with
guile” (Williamson 1996, p. 49). Indeed, the public adminis-
tration literature recognizes opportunism as a key antecedent
of uncooperative behaviors and noncompliance (Feldman
and Kaplan 2021; Gilliland and Manning 2002; Kauppi and
Van Raaij 2015; Maurya and Srivastava 2019; Potoski and
Prakash 2004). The predominant calculative character of
opportunists makes them untrustworthy, as they can always
be tempted by the “golden opportunity” to defect and free-
ride (Nooteboom 1996). This is why “strongly calculatedly
motivated regulatees are worthy of distrust” (Six 2013, p. 49).
This also explains why, from a regulatory perspective,
higher distrust is commonly expected to be accompanied
by more intervention, which can take the form of increased
monitoring and stricter enforcement actions (Aghion et al.
2010; Bigoni et al. 2015; Braithwaite and Makkai 1994;
Ferrin, Bligh, and Kohles 2007; Mendoza, Wielhouwer, and
Kirchler 2017). Distrust is linked to more coercive regulatory
styles, which rely more on skepticism and deterrence or the
threat of punishment for noncompliance (May and Winter
2000; Mendoza and Wielhouwer 2015; Rousseau et al. 1998;
Six 2013). Seen from a different angle, distrust can be linked
to more restrictive or bureaucratic types of control, which are
designed to enforce obedience (Six 2013). It is important to
note that not all interventions and types of control are indic-
ative of distrust (Six and Verhoest 2017). Distrust may even
be absent in regulatory styles that focus on providing support
and facilitating compliance (P. May and Winter 2000), ena-
bling types of control that seek to promote the internalization
of values (Six 2013), and approaches that seek to establish
legitimacy through procedural justice and promote voluntary
compliance (Levi, Sacks, and Tyler 2009).
In sum, distrust conveys a message about expected op-
portunism and noncompliance. It reflects the perception of
conflicting interests, and even possible antagonism (Lynam
and Miller 2019; Parks, Joireman, and Van Lange 2013).
Regardless of where distrust may come from (authorities or
other members of society), the critical question is how people
respond when they feel distrusted. Noting that several studies
make distinctions between the concepts of distrust, mistrust,
and low trust (e.g., Sitkin and Bijlsma-Frankema 2018; Six
and Verhoest 2017; Van de Walle and Six 2014), our atten-
tion lies on the concept of distrust. Compared to these other
terms, distrust reflects a higher degree of confidence about the
negative character of the expectations (Six and Latusek 2023;
Ullmann-Margalit 2004), and this makes the message that it
conveys relatively clearer and more straightforward.
The negative side of distrust
Several studies indicate that distrust has a negative effect on
compliance (Belot and Schr¨oder 2016; Bowles 2008; Bowles
and Polania-Reyes 2012; Falk and Kosfeld 2006; Mooijman
et al. 2017; Rudorf et al. 2018; Weibel and Six 2013). The

underlying argument is that distrust induces an uncoopera-
tive response. It has been proposed that distrust fosters an-
tagonism, strengthens the perspective of opposing sides in
the interaction, and makes people less oriented towards co-
operation and more oriented towards individualism and
competition (Van Lange et al. 1997; Van Lange and Balliet
2015). Distrust can also indicate that people are perceived
as self-­interested and opportunistic, and this can hurt them
and undermine their willingness to cooperate on a volun-
tary basis (Falk and Kosfeld 2006; Mooijman et al. 2017;
Mulder et al. 2006; Sitkin and Roth 1993; Weibel and Six
2013). Distrust can even signal that voluntary cooperation is
unnecessary or has no value, displace or “crowd out” any in-
trinsic motivations to cooperate, and push people to respond
exclusively to external incentives (Bowles 2008; Enzle and
Anderson 1993; Frey 1993; Frey and Jegen 2001; Mooijman
et al. 2017). For example, distrust may undermine intrinsic
motivations to comply when it threatens people’s need for au-
tonomy, competence, and relatedness (i.e., self-determination;
Six 2013). The general premise is that distrust conveys a nega-
tive message which can trigger a reciprocal negative response
(Frey 1993; Bowles 2008; Bowles and Polania-Reyes 2012;
Falk and Kosfeld 2006).
The notion that distrusting people is costly or detrimental
is explicitly acknowledged in the public administration lit-
erature (Bardach and Lesser 1996; Ruscio 1996; Van Slyke
2007). It has been argued, for example, that distrust is antag-
onistic and obstructs collaboration (Ansell and Gash 2008);
that distrust evokes “resistance, evasion and dishonesty”
(Bouckaert 2012, p. 96); that distrust signaled by over-control
lowers “cooperation with rule implementation” (DeHart-
Davis 2009, p. 371); and that in general, regulator distrust
has a negative impact on regulatee compliance (Six 2013).
Is distrust necessarily negative for compliance?
Although it is intuitive to expect a negative effect of distrust,
there are two reasons to question if there is more to learn
about its potential influence on compliance. The first relates
to theory and the second to empirical validity. It is possible to
build an argument in favor of a positive effect, and there are
in fact recent calls for research in this direction (Mooijman et
al. 2017; Mooijman and Graham 2018; Nurmohamed 2020).
This can be seen from a normative or a strategic perspective.
From a normative perspective, distrust may be interpreted
as a reminder or invitation to behave in a socially desirable
manner. This follows from the idea that certain signals can
draw attention to the individual’s “responsibility to prevent
harm” and activate a “sense of duty to comply” (May 2005,
p. 33). These signals can raise awareness about compliance,
and promote deliberation when ethical considerations are
in the blind spot (Feldman and Kaplan 2021). Moreover,
responding to distrust with noncompliance would be in a nor-
mative sense undesirable, as it would legitimize the reasons to
be distrusted (Mooijman et al. 2017).
From a more strategic perspective, a positive response to
distrust could also be advantageous. The basic logic is that
distrust can have negative consequences for the individual
(which can be, e.g., emotional, cognitive, economic, social), so
compliance can be seen as a means to mitigate or ideally elim-
inate them. For instance, if people have a fundamental need to
maintain a positive self-concept and protect their self-esteem
(Higgins 1987; Leary 2007), distrust can threaten how they
perceive themselves or how they think they are perceived by
others (Baer et al. 2021; Lau, Lam, and Wen 2014; Salamon
and Robinson 2008). Distrust may motivate people to protect
these needs, and demonstrate that they are or can be coop-
erative. Moreover, a cooperative response may allow people
to disprove negative expectations about their intentions, and
in this way restore their reputation when their credibility is
in question (Mooijman and Graham 2018; Nurmohamed
2020). The general premise is that, if distrust is costly or
“hurts” in some way (Falk and Kosfeld 2006), compliance
can be the remedy.
Interestingly, this type of strategic response may be tem-
porary. In situations that involve repeated interactions (as
requests for compliance commonly do), compliance may
represent a means to restore trust and escape the nega-
tive consequences of distrust (James Jr 2002; Mendoza and
Wielhouwer 2015). After trust is restored, however, the value
of strategic compliance may be reduced. The important point
about this strategic response is that distrust can induce com-
pliance—at least in the short-run.
A second reason to question whether distrust is necessarily
negative for compliance relates to empirical validity. To the
best of our knowledge, the experience of being distrusted has
not been experimentally manipulated with the objective of
assessing its influence on compliance. This is relevant con-
sidering that the available evidence can be considered indi-
rect and fragmented. It mainly refers to a higher frequency of
perceived distrust when coercive control has a negative im-
pact on cooperation (Falk and Kosfeld 2006; Rudorf et al.
2018), a negative effect of “controlling surveillance” on in-
trinsic motivation (a type of surveillance assumed to be more
indicative of distrust; Enzle and Anderson 1993), and a neg-
ative correlation between perceived distrust and compliance
(Mooijman et al. 2017). Notably, the explanatory variables
in these studies relate to power or control, and these in turn
influence the extent to which people feel distrusted (distrust is
not treated as the prime causal factor). This also underscores
the importance of distinguishing the effect of distrust from
the effect of more coercive types of control.
Research question and hypotheses
While feeling distrusted is described in the literature as a
negative experience, it is not obvious whether it leads to the
escalation or de-escalation of the potential conflict between
short-term self-interest and the collective interest. Therefore,
our research question is whether distrust is necessarily nega-
tive for compliance, or put differently, whether distrust can be
also positive for compliance.
The arguments presented so far suggest that distrust conveys
a message, and that the corresponding response involves
adjustments in intentions. In line with classical social psycho-
logical theory, an indirect effect is implied, as intentions can in
turn influence norm-violating behavior (Bennett et al. 2024).
Concerning a possible negative effect, distrust is assumed to
not only reduce the willingness to respond cooperatively and
voluntarily to a request but also trigger uncooperative self-­
interest (Belot and Schr¨oder 2016; Bowles 2008; Bowles and
Polania-Reyes 2012; Falk and Kosfeld 2006). Therefore, the
negative effect of distrust would imply an increase in oppor-
tunism. Concerning a possible positive effect, distrust may
induce the internalization of values (through, e.g., ethical
deliberation and awareness of responsibility; Feldman and

can undermine the intention to pursue short-term self-­interest,
and induce cooperative intentions that go in line with the col-
lective interest. Therefore, the positive effect of distrust would
imply a decrease in opportunism.
If distrust induces changes in intentions that go in favor
or against the collective interest, then those intentions likely
capture changes in opportunism. This leads to our first hy-
pothesis: the association between distrust and compliance is
indirect, and is mediated by opportunism. Our research ques-
tion and first hypothesis are depicted in figure 1.
Our second hypothesis relates to a possible conditional
effect: when distrust is directed towards the group, the re-
sponse should vary depending on how individuals see them-
selves compared to others. In general, evaluations about
the ingroup can trigger comparisons between the self and
other group members (e.g., Mussweiler and Bodenhausen
2002; Vanhoomissen and Van Overwalle 2010). For ex-
ample, members of a distrusted group may question whether
their individual behavior is different from the average
group behavior, whether they should rather be evaluated as
individuals, or whether group-level distrust is in any way de-
served or justified. This type of consideration may vary be-
tween individuals, and should consequently induce different
motivations and attitudes within the group (Frey and Tropp
2006; Vorauer 2006).
We do not formulate hypotheses about differences between
distrust directed at the individual vs. the group. For example,
both types of distrust can be interpreted as hostile or antago-
nistic (inducing a negative effect), or can motivate people to
disprove negative expectations about themselves (inducing a
positive effect). This is why we abstain from developing ex-
pectations about the sign.
Experiments
We report three experiments which were designed to ex-
amine the potential association between varying distrust
manipulations (treatments), opportunism (mediator), and
compliance (outcome). Two experiments on tax compliance
examine the case in which the individual is distrusted by the
authority (Experiments 1 and 2). A survey experiment on
compliance with COVID-19 rules examines the case in which
the individual’s group is distrusted by other members of so-
ciety (Experiment 3). The experiments were not preregistered.
At the moment of preparing Experiment 1, preregistrations
were less common, and our motivation was to explore an
open research question, given that we had no clear hypothesis
about the sign of the effect. Experiments 2 and 3 followed as
extensions with modifications.
Each of the three experiments was approved by the Research
Ethics Review Board of the School of Business and Economics
(SBE) at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. The participants were
university students. All of them gave informed consent to par-
ticipate and were invited to contact the Ethics Review Board
in case they had any concerns. The Board was contacted only
by one participant, requesting confirmation that the payment
procedure would be secure. No participants reported concerns
to the researchers by email or during the debriefing sessions.
In Experiments 1 and 2, participants received credits as well
as cash (bank transfer) payments, which varied depending on
their tax compliance decisions. In Experiment 3, participants
chose between making a donation or participating in a raffle.
To test mediation, we followed a two-step approach known
as causal mediation analysis (for which we use the R medi-
ation package; Tingley et al. 2014). This involves the esti-
mation of (1) a regression in which the treatment explains
the mediator, (2) a regression in which the mediator explains
the outcome, and (3) a point estimate of the indirect associ-
ation between the treatment and the outcome (Cheung and
Cheung 2023; Hayes and Scharkow 2013). For reference, we
also report estimations based on structural equation mod-
eling (SEM; for which we used the R lavaan package; Rosseel
2012). In principle, both the two-step approach and SEM
are suitable for examining direct and indirect associations.
However, SEM may be considered relatively less appropriate
when the sample size is small (N < 200, as in Experiments 1
and 2; Kyriazos 2018; Wolf et al. 2013), and relatively less
informative when the structural model has no latent variables
and all possible paths have been identified (i.e., a saturated
model, which has zero degrees of freedom and a perfect
model fit; West et al. 2023).
Given that the analysis departs from a research question, we
follow the recommendations to (1) abstain from conducting
post-hoc power analyses, (2) explain the reasoning behind
the selection of the sample sizes, and (3) report 95 percent
confidence intervals to provide insight into the size of the
observed effects (Haile 2023; Zhang et al. 2019). Following
recommendations for mediation analysis, we report bias-
corrected confidence intervals based on 5,000 replications
(Hayes and Scharkow 2013; Shrout and Bolger 2002). In
the Supplementary material we report an outline of the ex-
perimental procedures and questionnaires, summary statis-
tics and correlations, and tests of alternative mechanisms (of
other potentially relevant mediators).
Experiment 1
Method.
If coercive control can make people feel distrusted (Falk and
Kosfeld 2006; Mooijman et al. 2017), it is critical to examine
if the potential effect of distrust can be distinguished from
the effect of coercive control. Tax compliance experiments are
suitable for this purpose. They have been conducted exten-
sively, are considered to have an adequate degree of external
validity, and can be used to isolate the potential influence of
coercive control (Alm 2019; Alm, Bloomquist, and McKee
2015; Mascagni 2018).
Experiment 1 followed the standard design of tax compli-
ance experiments (Mascagni 2018). Throughout a series of
ten rounds, participants received actual income (100 mone-
tary units equivalent to €1 per round), reported the amount of

The income reporting task was followed by a questionnaire

perceptions of others, and demographics. As attention checks,
participants had to solve one arithmetic equation at the be-
ginning of each round. Providing the correct solution would
allow them to receive 100 units of income and continue with
the reporting task.
Manipulation.
Participants were randomly assigned to either the control
group or the distrusted group. The income reporting task
included two practice rounds and 10 rounds with actual
payoffs. The participants did not receive information about
the number of rounds to complete. After completing the prac-
tice rounds, all participants were informed that the audit
probability was 20 percent (this would apply to rounds 1–5).
After completing the fifth round, participants were informed
that the audit probability had increased to 40 percent (this
would apply to rounds 6–10). This increased audit prob-
ability represents an increase in coercive control. When the
increased audit probability was announced, the participants
in the distrusted group were also informed that they had
been classified as distrusted (“you have been classified as a
distrusted taxpayer”). This design allowed us to assess the
potential effect of distrust over and above the influence of
increased control (which was captured by the increased audit
probability).
Participants.
This was a lab experiment. The participants were bachelor
students who were registered in the participant pool. Taking
prior tax compliance experiments as reference, a sample
size of approximately 30–40 participants per condition was
set a priori (Choo, Fonseca, and Myles 2016; Mooijman
et al. 2017). Eighty-eight participants attended the session,
but eleven were excluded from the analysis due to a tech-
nical error while capturing their responses (five participants
from the control group, six from the distrusted group). As
a result, the analysis was based on the responses of N = 77
participants (thirty-nine in the control group, and thirty-eight
in the distrusted group). In terms of demographics, most
participants were Dutch (78 percent), the average age was
20.3 (SD = 1.6), 52 were male (68 percent), and 25 were fe-
male (32 percent).
Measures.
Compliance was measured as the total amount of reported
income after the increased audit probability was announced
(the sum of reported income for rounds 6–10). Opportunism
was measured using the following item: “In the last rounds,
I declared income to get as much money as I can” (7-point
scale ranging from 1 = “completely disagree” to 7 = “com-
pletely agree”). The treatment was measured as a binary vari-
able taking the value of 0 for the control group and 1 for the
distrusted group.
Results.
Manipulation check.
An adapted three-item composite indicator was used to
measure the extent to which participants felt distrusted (e.g.,
“I felt distrusted by the Tax Authority”; Mooijman et al.
2017). The distrusted group felt significantly more distrusted
than the control group (t = 2.871, 95 percent CI = [0.247,
1.367], d = 0.652), indicating that the manipulation was
successful.

Discussion.
Distrust had a negative direct effect on opportunism, and this
was in turn associated with higher compliance. Influential re-
search has identified distrust as the “hidden cost of control”
(Falk and Kosfeld 2006). These results indicate that the effect
of distrust can be distinct from the effect of coercive control,
and may even be positive in some cases. Distrust also led to
the perception that the change in the audit probability was
unfair. Fairness perceptions can influence compliance (Tyler
2006, 2021), so in principle, they could also act as a medi-
ator in this analysis. However, the prediction would go in the
opposite direction: perceived unfairness should translate into
lower compliance (not higher, as we observe). In any case,
there was no evidence to indicate that this perception was as-
sociated with compliance.
Experiment 2
Method.
While Experiment 1 was designed to compare the effects of
distrust vs. no distrust, Experiment 2 was designed to com-
pare the effects of distrust vs. more salient distrust. Put dif-
ferently, the second experiment explored if more salient
manipulations could still be associated with opportunism and
compliance. The objective was to test if the observed effect on
opportunism can indeed be attributed to distrust (construct
validity), and to test if the expected indirect association can
be observed again, using a different sample (internal and ex-
ternal validity of the mediating mechanism).
The income reporting task and the audit probabilities were
the same as in Experiment 1 (20 percent for rounds 1–5, 40
percent for rounds 6–10). Experiment 2 included five addi-
tional rounds in which the audit probability was reduced to
20 percent, and in which all participants were trusted (“you
have been classified as a trusted taxpayer”; rounds 11–15).
This allowed us to explore if the potential effects of more
salient distrust remained present when participants were no
longer distrusted (once trust was restored). Observing no
group differences in these last rounds would indicate that the
response to distrust may be to some extent strategic, as it may
only last for as long as the person is distrusted. Participants
received 100 monetary units equivalent to €0.75 per round.
After completing the income reporting task, participants
responded a short version of the questionnaire used in
Experiment 1.
Manipulations.
The participants were randomly assigned to one of three
groups. The three groups were exposed to the same
manipulation as in Experiment 1. After completing the first
five rounds, all participants were classified as distrusted
(rounds 6–10). One group was the control group. Another
group was trusted in the first rounds (treatment 1). Before the
first round, the participants in this group were told they were
trusted (“you have been classified as a trusted taxpayer”;
rounds 1–5). The transition from trust to distrust can be
interpreted as the explicit loss of trust. The remaining group
received a distrust reminder (treatment 2). After completing
round 7, the participants in this group were reminded that
they were distrusted (“you will remain to be classified as a
distrusted taxpayer”).
The assumption was that both the explicit loss of trust
(treatment 1) and the reminder of being distrusted (treatment
2) make distrust more salient and accentuate the classification
(they confirm that distrust is present and clearly directed at
the individual).
Participants.
Experiment 2 was conducted online due to COVID-19
restrictions. The participants were bachelor students who
were registered in the participant pool. The number of
students who started to participate was 175, but 13 did not
complete the experiment. The analysis was based on the
responses of N = 162 participants (fifty-eight in the control
group, fifty-five in treatment 1, forty-nine in treatment 2). The
demographics were proportionally similar as in Experiment
1. Most participants were Dutch (74 percent), the average age
was 21.3 (SD = 1.8), 100 were male (62 percent), 45 were fe-
male (28 percent), and for the rest we did not obtain gender
information.
Measures.
For treatment 1 (the explicit loss of trust), compliance was
measured as the sum of reported income for rounds 6–10 (as
in Experiment 1). For treatment 2 (the distrust reminder),
compliance was measured as the sum of reported income
for rounds 8–10 (immediately after the reminder had been
announced). Opportunism was measured using the following
item: “When I was distrusted, I declared income to get as
much money as I can” (7-point scale ranging from 1 = “com-
pletely disagree” to 7 = “completely agree”). Given the dif-
ference in compliance measures (i.e., reported income during
different sets of rounds), each treatment was analyzed sepa-
rately. Each of the two treatment conditions was measured as
a binary variable taking the value of 0 for the control group
and 1 for the treatment group. The analysis followed the same
procedure as in Experiment 1.

Discussion.
Experiment 2 can be seen as an extension of Experiment 1. It
shows that, compared to the original distrust manipulation,
more salient distrust manipulations can still lead to reduced
opportunism. Experiment 2 also replicates the indirect asso-
ciation observed in Experiment 1. Overall, the results provide
support for the internal and external validity of our concep-
tual model (figure 1), and for the construct validity of dis-
trust as the predictor in this analysis. Experiments 1 and 2
support the expected indirect association in two ways. They
yield significant point estimates and do not provide evidence
in favor of other possible mediators. Interestingly, Experiment
2 indicates that the response to distrust is temporary, and that
reduced opportunism is only present when participants are
distrusted. This goes in line with the idea that the response
to distrust is to some extent strategic. Once trust is restored,
people may no longer see the need to be less opportunistic.

Before sending out the invitation to participate, the recipients

group was the control group. The second group was identified
as distrusted (treatment 1). For this group, both the invita-
tion to participate and the manipulation in the questionnaire

reports have indicated that students cannot be trusted to follow
Covid rules and recommendations,” and this was followed
by quotes from major news agencies (e.g., “the number of
Corona infections in Delft is increasing, thanks to students,”
“students seem to be less adhering to the rules”). The expres-
sion “cannot be trusted” was chosen, noting that the concepts
of no trust and distrust are distinct but can be to a large ex-
tent overlapping (Six and Verhoest 2017; Ullmann-Margalit
2004). Indeed, the manipulation check (described below)
indicates that the manipulation effectively captured distrust,
as it made participants feel significantly more distrusted. The
third group was identified as both distrusted and opportun-
istic (treatment 2). For this group, the invitation and question-
naire were the same as in treatment 1, but also included the
following statement: “In line with these news reports, some
people are questioning whether students are acting more self-
ishly or opportunistically than others.” In short, treatment 1
identified the group as distrusted, while treatment 2 identified
the group as both distrusted and opportunistic. Our design
follows an extensive record of survey and field experiments
manipulating letters and invitations (e.g., Bhanot 2021;
Butler, Karpowitz, and Pope 2012; Hallsworth et al. 2017).
The manipulation of the invitations was approved by the SBE
Research Ethics Review Board (we elaborate on the ethical
aspects of this experiment in the General Discussion section).
Treatment 2 was designed to examine the mediating role of
opportunism from a different angle. We were inspired by the
idea of a blockage manipulation, which is designed to exper-
imentally block or neutralize the effect of a mediator (Pirlott
and MacKinnon 2016). Treatment 2 aimed at discouraging
participants from making adjustments in opportunism,
and assess if this weakens the potential indirect association
(compared to treatment 1). This would provide additional ev-
idence in favor of the idea that opportunism acts as a relevant
mechanism. Our rationale was as follows. When a group is
subject to negative social evaluations, group members may
doubt their ability to change those evaluations on an indi-
vidual basis, and this can lead to withdrawal or disengage-
ment (Park et al. 2023; Wrosch et al. 2003). Compared to
treatment 1, the social evaluation in treatment 2 is more nega-
tive and explicit, and could in principle make participants rel-
atively more disengaged. Moreover, if distrust were to induce
a negative response, the need to make adjustments and esca-
late the conflict would be relatively lower for the participants
in treatment 2, as they are already explicitly recognized as
opportunistic and with conflicting interests. Conversely, if dis-
trust were to induce a positive response, disproving these ad-
ditional negative evaluations would be perceived as relatively
more difficult (a heavier burden of proof). Although distrust
is present in both treatments, opportunism may be less likely
affected in treatment 2, and this should weaken its potential
indirect association with compliance.

Discussion.
In line with Experiments 1 and 2, Experiment 3 shows that
distrust can influence opportunism, and can be indirectly as-
sociated with compliance. The opportunism measure was
validated by a significant correlation with a preference for
the raffle (opportunism was negatively correlated with
making an actual donation). In contrast with Experiments
1 and 2, Experiment 3 revealed a positive effect of distrust
on opportunism. This effect was conditional, suggesting that
the response to group-level distrust may involve additional
considerations, and may not be uniform across the distrusted
group. Distrust made participants more opportunistic, but
only when they already saw themselves as less compliant than
average.
Only treatment 1 led to changes in opportunism. Both
treatments 1 and 2 were effective in making participants feel
distrusted, but adding perceived opportunism (treatment 2)
appears to have thwarted participants’ motivation to react
and make adjustments. The contrast between treatments 1
and 2 provides additional support for the hypothesis that op-
portunism operates as a mediator. If opportunism is less likely
to change, as in treatment 2, the indirect association between
distrust and compliance may be less likely.

Limitations and ethical considerations
Although the three experiments reveal significant effects of
distrust on opportunism, opportunism was not randomly
manipulated, so we cannot claim with certainty that dis-
trust had an indirect influence on compliance (Acharya,
Blackwell, and Sen 2018; Green, Ha, and Bullock 2010).
Future experiments may consider manipulating distrust as
well as opportunism to properly assess if the expected indi-
rect association is indeed causal. In any case, independently of
whether opportunism operates as a causal mechanism or not,
the results suggest that distrust is not necessarily detrimental
for compliance.
This study did not account for other factors or mechanisms
that could also be at play. In particular, emotions may be rele-
vant, especially considering that distrust is assumed to “hurt”
and lead to an emotional response (Falk and Kosfeld 2006).
Future studies may explore if the response to distrust varies
depending on whether it induces, for example, guilt vs. shame,
as these emotions can be associated with approaching vs.
avoidance behaviors (Schmader and Lickel 2006). In addition
to emotions, perceptions of procedural and treatment fairness
may be relevant (Nagin and Telep 2017), especially consid-
ering that distrust led to perceived unfairness in Experiment
1. However, the response was positive in this case, which is
the opposite to what the theory would predict. This opens up
an interesting direction for future research, which could ex-
plore if perceived unfairness can motivate people to escape a
state of “unfair distrust.”
Concerning the generalizability of the responses and
behaviors of students, it is important to note that student
samples behave similarly to non-student samples when
completing tax compliance tasks (as in Experiments 1 and 2),
and that tax compliance experiments are considered to have
an adequate degree of external validity (Alm, Bloomquist,
and McKee 2015; Choo, Fonseca, and Myles 2016; Mascagni
2018). Experiment 3 exploited a more unique event, which
was related to the low levels of compliance that were observed
specifically in the student population. It is possible to identify
other settings in which certain groups (including young adults)
may comply relatively less with rules or recommendations
compared to other populations (e.g., driving under the influ-
ence, sexual safety; Weaver 2015). Future experiments may
use samples from other populations to examine the possible
effects of distrust in tax or other compliance settings.
The results indicate that the effect of distrust can be detected
when coercive control increases (Experiments 1 and 2) or re-
mains constant (Experiment 3). Our experiments were not
designed to assess and compare the effect of distrust against
the effect of coercive control (or any other effects) in terms
of sign or magnitude. Future studies may manipulate both
distrust and coercive control across conditions to assess with
more precision any possible differences between these effects
(e.g., using a 2 × 2 experimental design for distrust vs. no dis-
trust, and fixed vs. increased coercive control).
Finally, it is important to elaborate on the ethical aspects of
the present study. One point of discussion relates to the nega-
tive conditional effect observed in Experiment 3, considering
that the manipulation made a subset of participants more
opportunistic, and this was associated with a lower willing-
ness to comply with COVID-19 rules and recommendations
(e.g., self-testing, staying at home). In this sense, our survey
experiment represented a risk of societal harm (McDermott
and Hatemi 2020; Phillips 2021). While we were aware of
the risk of a negative effect of distrust (based on the theory
that we reviewed), we took the following points into consid-
eration: (1) COVID-19 restrictions had been reinstated but
were expected to be temporary, and (2) the news reports on
student noncompliance were frequent and came from var-
ious locations, implying that the observed behavior was likely
representative of the larger student population. For these
reasons, we expected the risk to be contained and relatively
low. The societal harm associated with a lower willingness to
comply would be limited in terms of time (as the restrictions
were not expected to be long-lasting) and in terms of scope
(if compliance was already low, the potential additional harm
would be minor).
Another point of discussion relates to manipulating the
invitations to complete the survey in Experiment 3, as the
recipients could be exposed to the manipulation before giving
consent to participate. This posed the risk of violating the
recipients’ autonomy to decide whether to partake in the
study (McDermott and Hatemi 2020; Phillips 2021). This
risk has been present in numerous field experiments testing
the effects of manipulated letters and invitations (e.g., Bhanot
et al., 2021; Butler, Karpowitz, and Pope 2012; Ferraro
and Price 2013; Hallsworth et al. 2017; Van Duin et al.
2018). One point of debate on this subject is that making
participants aware that they are participating in a field ex-
periment can compromise the validity of the results (Teele et
al. 2014). While this may apply to certain field experiments,
it is not directly applicable to survey experiments (our case),
as the invitations to participate already communicate that a
study is taking place.
In any case, we took concrete actions aimed at being
transparent and respectful towards participants. The first
sentence of the survey invitations stated that the recipient
was “invited” to complete a questionnaire and that the
objective was “to learn more about students’ experiences
with Covid rules and recommendations.” This was followed
by information about the possibility to participate in the
raffle or donating to UNICEF. This initial communication
of choices and incentives makes this survey experiment dif-
ferent from field experiments in which there is no initial
awareness of the research study, voluntary participation,
and data collection. Notably, the invitation was designed to
communicate that this was a study and that participation
was voluntary before introducing the manipulation. The
first part of the survey described in more detail that par-
ticipation was voluntary and anonymity was assured, and
then respondents were asked to indicate whether they gave
consent to participate.
Concerning the text in the manipulation, we made sure to
use direct quotes from well- known news outlets indicating
that students cannot be trusted (there was no deception). The
intention was to present widely available information that the

Contributions
This study aims at making two theoretical contributions to
the public administration literature. First, it challenges the no-
tion that distrust necessarily leads to negative reciprocity or
retaliation, or that it is strictly costly, detrimental or counter-
productive (Bowles 2008; Falk and Kosfeld 2006; B. S. Frey
and Jegen 2001; Van de Walle and Lahat 2017; Weibel and
Six 2013). In this sense, this study joins an emerging body
of public administration literature recognizing the positive
aspects of distrust, and which questions the widespread as-
sumption that trust is essentially “good,” while distrust is
essentially “bad” (e.g., Lee 2021; Lee and Dodge 2019; Lee
and Lee 2022). Our findings indicate that people’s response
to distrust is not immediately straightforward, but do not
imply that the response is random or unpredictable (after all,
Experiment 2 can be seen as an extension and replication of
Experiment 1). Identifying the conditions that make the re-
sponse to distrust positive or negative goes beyond the scope
of the present study, but our analysis may already provide
some hints. In Experiment 2, the positive effect of distrust
disappeared once trust was restored, indicating that the re-
sponse was short-lived and aimed at escaping the negative
state of distrust. In Experiment 3, an effect was observed
when participants were classified as distrusted (treatment 1),
not when they were classified as both distrusted and oppor-
tunistic (treatment 2). If treatment 2 is considered to be more
antagonistic than treatment 1, the difference in the effect hints
at the possibility that a negative response to distrust is not
necessarily driven by retaliation.
All this could suggest that the response to distrust follows
some logic and may be to some extent strategic. We specu-
late that the sign of the effect might depend on at least two
factors: the possibility to escape the distrusted state (Mendoza
and Wielhouwer 2015; Scholz 1984), and the costs associated
with being distrusted (which may be social, psychological, ec-
onomic). The basic premise is that compliance can be costly,
as it may reduce individual benefits and require time, atten-
tion, effort, or material resources (Becker 1968; Moynihan,
Herd, and Harvey 2015). Holding everything else constant, if
it is too difficult or not possible to escape the distrusted state,
or if people are distrusted regardless of their individual be-
havior (as in Experiment 3), to increase opportunism may be
advantageous, and therefore, the response to distrust may be
negative in those cases. Conversely, if it is possible to escape
the distrusted state, and distrust is in some way costly for the
individual (as in Experiments 1 and 2), to decrease oppor-
tunism may be advantageous, and therefore, the response to
distrust may be positive in those cases.
The second theoretical contribution refers to the identi-
fication of opportunism as a mediator in this analysis. This
also responds to a recent call for research on the mediating
role of intentions in explaining norm-violating behavior
(Bennett et al. 2024). The question of how distrust affects
compliance may first require the examination of how distrust
affects intentions, particularly with regard to self-­interest
and cooperation. Experiments 1 and 2 did not provide ev-
idence indicating that distrust influenced normative or pro-
social motivations. When participants felt distrusted, they
only made adjustments in opportunism, hinting once again
at the possible strategic nature of their response. Limiting
opportunism is a well-recognized challenge for public ad-
ministration (e.g., Gilliland and Manning 2002; Kauppi and
Van Raaij 2015; Maurya and Srivastava 2019), and the the-
oretical connection between perceived opportunism and dis-
trust has been explicitly accounted for (e.g., Lamothe and
Lamothe 2012; Van Slyke 2007). While prior studies describe
how opportunism influences distrust, our analysis describes
how distrust influences opportunism. This represents an in-
teresting contribution to the literature on distrust in citizens
(Bouckaert 2012; Six 2013; Van de Walle and Lahat 2017;
Yang 2005), which has received considerably less attention
than citizens’ distrust in government or distrust between
citizens.
In the context of regulation, it is sometimes said that
“trust is good, but control is better” (Kirchler, Kogler, and
Muehlbacher 2014, p. 87). This points at the common intu-
ition that public administrators have a dichotomous choice
between trust and control, or that they choose to distrust
actors when they implement control. While distrust in citi-
zens may be linked to stricter enforcement and more coer-
cive control, not all types of control signal distrust (May
and Winter 2000; Six 2013; Six and Verhoest 2017). Recent
studies reveal other interesting types of associations between
trust and regulatory control. For example, regulatory regimes
involving command-and-control may lead to higher trust by
citizens in firms than looser regimes involving pledges or self-­
regulation (Maman, Feldman, and Levi-Faur 2023), and reg-
ulation through the mandatory disclosure of information may
increase citizen trust in regulated sectors (Grimmelikhuijsen,
de Vries, and Bouwman 2024). The interesting point is that
implementing different forms of oversight and control does
not necessarily generate more distrust, and can even enhance
trust in less obvious manners.

Abstract
This paper proposes a theory of organizational memory predicting that task knowledge retained in organizational structures (i.e., agency cen-
tralization and task standardization) buffers the disruptive effects of employee turnover. Using a dataset of information technology (IT) projects
implemented by 94 US federal agencies between FY2015 and FY2021, estimates from fractional panel probit models reveal that both centralized
authorities under chief information officers and task standardization reduce project delays associated with IT workforce turnover; they are par-
ticularly effective in mitigating knowledge loss from voluntary and managerial position turnover. This evidence highlights the contingent nature
of the turnover–performance relationship, suggesting that government agencies with high turnover may experience fewer delays when organi-
zational structures retain task knowledge.
Key words: employee turnover; organizational performance; organizational theory; federal IT workforce.
Employee turnover is a phenomenon of considerable impor-
tance and scholarly interest (An and Meier 2022; Doherty,
Lewis, and Limbocker 2019; Hancock et al. 2013). Because
high-quality human capital is essential in all organizations
(Jakobsen, Løkke, and Keppeler 2023), turnover incurs both
direct (e.g., recruiting and training new employees) and indi-
rect costs (e.g., low morale). Additionally, the lengthy hiring
process and low entry-level pay of government agencies make
this task especially daunting (US Government Accountability
Office 2022). Recent articles forecast a coming human capital
crisis, as the US government attempts to replace the signifi-
cant portion of the workforce that is soon to retire (Freidman
2022; Robinson 2022). Concerns about high turnover are
not unique to the US; governments in other countries also
grapple with similar challenges, as evidenced by research on
organizational-level predictors of turnover intentions among
Korean government employees (Jung, Chan, and Hsieh
2017), Dutch frontline workers (van Loon et al. 2018), and
local government officials in Pakistan (Quratulain and Khan
2015), to name just a few.
Recent empirical studies have contributed few reliable
generalizations about the ways in which turnover affects or-
ganizational performance. Public management researchers
studying turnover intention view change in the government
workforce as a negative organizational outcome that should
be mitigated (Bao and Zhong 2021; Lee and Whitford 2008).
According to human capital theory, turnover is a disruptive
force that leads to the loss of organizational knowledge and
expertise (Moon 2017) and increasing retraining and hiring
costs (An and Meier 2022). However, turnover can also in-
troduce fresh knowledge and prevent rigidity (Campbell, Im,
and Jeong 2014; Connolly 2018; Lee 2018). Emerging litera-
ture in public administration has explored specific conditions
under which turnover can positively or negatively affect or-
ganizational performance, including optimal turnover rates
(Meier and Hicklin 2007; Wynen et al. 2019), managerial
turnover (Connolly 2018), voluntary and involuntary depar-
ture distinctions (An 2019; Lee 2018), and the influence of
prior organizational performance (Boyne et al. 2011; Wang
and Sun 2020). Additionally, some research has examined the
relative quality of new and departing hires (An and Meier
2022).
Prior research on turnover using aggregate turnover rates,
while valuable, fails to account for variations in key causal
factors that ultimately influence how the loss of organi-
zational knowledge impacts performance. A foundational
assumption in organizational theory is that organizations re-
tain past experiences, both within individuals and through
knowledge embedded in organizational routines (Cyert and
March 1963; Nelson and Winter 1982) and communication
structures (Rao and Argote 2006; Weick 1995)—a concept
known as “organizational memory.” From an organiza-
tional theory perspective, the aggregate number of employees
leaving or remaining fails to account for the full range of
knowledge resources that organizations utilize in practice,
nor can it accurately illustrate the extent to which the loss
of individual knowledge influences organizational outcomes.
To better understand how individual turnover affects organ-
izational knowledge and productivity, it is crucial to address
this gap, since knowledge held by individual workers only
represents one of several mechanisms through which organi-
zations acquire and access past experiences and lessons.

This paper proposes a theory of organizational memory
that explains both mechanisms––human capital and struc-
ture—for maintaining (or losing) organizational knowledge.
It aims to address a critical but understudied aspect of turn-
over in the government workforce: the extent to which or-
ganizational structure affects an agency’s ability to buffer the
impact of employee turnover on administrative performance.
This work argues that turnover (i.e., loss of human capital)
disrupts administrative performance, reflecting a loss of or-
ganizational memory through lost experiences and expertise
in organizational tasks. However, this negative impact can be
mitigated by other sources of organizational memory, beyond
individual employee departures, such as centralized commu-
nication structures and standardized routines.
Using a dataset of federal IT project delays between fiscal
year 2015–2021, this study analyzes the impact of organiza-
tional memory on the agency’s project performance in terms
of schedule delays, to test the hypothesis that personnel turn-
over and organizational structure jointly shape agency perfor-
mance. These effects, I maintain, are most acute when (1) more
experienced personnel depart, (2) decentralized communica-
tion structures (i.e., the Chief Information Officer’s [CIO’s]
lack of formal authority in project implementation and per-
sonnel decisions) fail to facilitate knowledge retention, or
(3) a lower proportion of short-term projects yields a lower
level of task standardization (i.e., fewer projects involve quar-
terly or more frequent iterative cycles). The empirical findings
generally support these proposed hypotheses. An inter-decile
increase in the proportion of more experienced managers
among the departures yields a 0.47% rise in project delays for
each 1% increase in the overall managerial turnover. In con-
trast, centralized agencies experience 0.88% and 1.19% fewer
delays per 1% increase in aggregate and voluntary turnover,
respectively, compared to agencies with a noncentralized CIO
authority. Task standardization also offsets organizational
memory loss, but only under limited conditions. In agencies
where more than 40% of projects are standardized, aggregate
voluntary turnover no longer has a significant marginal effect
on delays, and minor delays decrease by 1.85% for each 1%
increase in managerial voluntary turnover.
This new perspective on the turnover–performance rela-
tionship helps illuminate the phenomena by specifying or-
ganizational mechanisms that determine when and how
turnover rates affect agency performance. While quantity-
focused arguments explain why rising absolute turnover
levels affect performance, they often assume a standardized
loss of value across all departees. In contrast, organizational
memory theory offers an organizational-level approach to un-
derstanding the consequences of personnel turnover, the in-
terplay between individuals and organizational design shapes
the loss and retention of organizational knowledge, and thus
performance—a long-standing theme central to organiza-
tional theory. This comprehensive perspective allows for a
deeper understanding of how employee departures impact
the continuity and functionality of organizational operations,
highlighting how turnover disrupts organizational memory
and ultimately shapes organizational performance.
Project delays within the US federal IT
workforce
Effective management of IT services and equipment has be-
come a critical component of administrative performance,
significantly impacting the effectiveness of all agency programs
that rely on IT (Dunleavy et al. 2006). Project delays in IT
investments have become a significant concern within the
federal government, drawing increased attention from ex-
ecutive leadership. In February 2015, the US Government
Accountability Office (GAO) identified a new government-wide
high-risk area, addressed via an initiative entitled “Improving
the Management of IT Acquisitions and Operations” (US
Government Accountability Office 2023). Of the 4,300+ IT
projects that are worth $80 billion reported by federal gov-
ernment agencies in FY2016, 43% were listed as over budget
or behind schedule (CIO Council 2017).1 Consequences of
those delays in delivering technologies extend beyond the
IT unit, disrupting critical managerial functions of the entire
agency and the effective service delivery of agency programs
(Dunleavy et al. 2006; US GAO 2021a). For example, frequent
delays in updating the Department of Housing and Urban
Development’s financial management system in 2014 affected
access to essential records and data across the entire organi-
zation (US Department of Housing and Urban Development
2014). For this reason, IT workforce performance is closely
linked to the organizational memory of the parent executive
department, reflecting the quality of the department’s informa-
tion system and accessibility to important records and data.
This study focuses on project delays by federal IT
units within 94 US federal agencies subject to the Federal
Information Technology Acquisition Reform Act (FITARA) of
2015, which mandates annual reporting of their investments
in the development or maintenance of information technology
(IT) systems to the US Office of Management and Budget
(US Office of Management and Budget 2011).2 These agency
reports are compiled into the “IT Portfolio,” published as part
of the President’s budget, which details project descriptions,
projected costs, and timelines (US General Services
Administration nd). This government-wide IT performance
reporting system identifies the top three investment categories
as IT systems for (1) administering agency programs (e.g., the
US Forest Service’s (USFS) Disaster Preparedness and Planning
System, which supports the management and monitoring of
federal wildfire activities), (2) managerial support (e.g., USFS’
central personnel management system), and (3) IT infrastruc-
ture, cybersecurity, or office automation. These projects are
also categorized by the purpose of investment, either for the
development of new products (i.e., the development, mod-
ernization, and enhancement [DME] category) or for the
operation and maintenance of existing products (the O&M
category). (US GAO 2016).

Meier and Hicklin 2007). Turnover also increases mobility,

While quantity-focused arguments explain why rising abso-
lute turnover levels affect performance, they often assume a
standardized loss of value across all departees (Hausknecht
and Holwerda 2013). The negative effects of turnover are
likely to be most significant when departing employees pos-
sess higher-quality human capital (An 2019). This study
explores these variations by distinguishing between voluntary
and involuntary turnover and the scope of responsibility of
the departing employees, both of which signal the quality of
human capital.
Because highly skilled individuals are more likely to quit
voluntarily than involuntarily (Lee 2018; Meier and Hicklin
2007), the negative impact of turnover on timely project com-
pletion will be most pronounced in voluntary turnover rates.
Similarly, one study showed that English local governments ex-
perienced performance improvements after the departure of a
low-performing management team (Boyne et al. 2011). In ad-
dition to the nature of departures, their impact will be more
pronounced for managerial turnover rates compared to overall
workforce-wide turnover rates. Few studies take into account
the variations in employees’ scope of responsibility over agency
tasks among the departees (Hausknecht and Holwerda 2013).
Senior managers in the public sector highlight their significant
impact on strategy formulation (Boyne 2004), implementation
processes (Fernandez and Rainey 2017), and overall organiza-
tional performance (Boyne et al. 2011; Hill 2005). Employees
in managerial positions, with broader responsibilities, typically
possess a comprehensive understanding of agency tasks and
processes (Boyne 2004), maintain critical relationships both
within and outside the organization (O’Toole and Meier 2003),
and hold specialized knowledge that is not easily transferable
(Hill 2005). The loss of such employees can disrupt workflows
and hinder decision-making processes to a greater extent as
their knowledge and expertise become more agency-specific,
and, thus, harder to replace compared to nonmanagerial
positions (Connolly 2018).
Reassessing employee turnover: an
organizational memory perspective
The following sections apply the lens of organizational
memory to previous research on workforce turnover and
administrative performance, with a focus on how departee
length of service and alternative mechanisms of organiza-
tional memory (i.e., centralized reporting channels and task
standardization) can affect organizational productivity.
Organizations that are able to acquire, store, and retrieve
knowledge are more effective than those that are not (Rao
and Argote 2006; Stein 1995). Turnover research in public
management often characterizes turnover as a critical loss
of organizational knowledge or knowledge management,
such as the loss of “institutional memory” (Hass and Wright
1989; Rusaw 2004: 488) or “governmental human capital”
(Wesemann 2024: 149). However, few studies (e.g., Stark
2019) provide detailed insights on what constitutes such
organizational knowledge that is developed and retrieved
within organizations.
Walsh and Ungson (1991: 61) defined organizational
memory as “stored information from an organization’s his-
tory that can be brought to bear on present decisions.” That
is, organizations are information systems that develop mul-
tiple retention bins of memory from their past experiences,
including both individuals and structural factors (e.g., com-
munication structure and standardized procedures) Classical
organizational theories similarly emphasize that organizations
retain their past experiences through knowledge embedded in
their standard operating procedures (Cyert and March 1963;
Nelson and Winter 1982), communication networks (Weick
1995), organizational structure (Galbraith 1977), and culture
(Walsh and Ungson 1991). From this perspective, the aggre-
gate number of employees leaving or remaining fails to account
for the full range of knowledge resources that organizations
utilize in practice, nor can it accurately illustrate the extent
to which the loss of individual knowledge influences organ-
izational outcomes. In the context of US federal IT agencies,
organizational memory reflects accumulated knowledge and
understanding of IT projects, retained through prior experi-
ence with those projects or similar ones. Figure 1 illustrates
this conceptual framework, highlighting three primary reten-
tion facilities: (1) individuals, measured by service length; (2)
communication structure, reflected in CIO authority central-
ization; and (3) standardized procedures, captured through
task standardization.
The negative impact of departure experience on
organizational memory
Employees often hold tacit knowledge that is difficult to doc-
ument but crucial for organizational functioning (Argote and
Guo 2016). Scholars in organizational memory acknowledge
that only individuals possess the cognitive ability to fully un-
derstand the reasoning behind a decision within the context
of the organization’s history, something that routines and
networks alone cannot achieve (Walsh and Ungson 1991).
However, relying heavily on individual employees as knowl-
edge repositories can create vulnerabilities, especially if key
individuals leave the organization. This is particularly note-
worthy within the public sector context given the tacit nature of
public works (US Government Accountability Office 2021a);
further, those tasks often require specialized investments of
skills and expertise that are uniquely tailored to the agency’s
programs and policies (Gailmard and Patty 2013). For ex-
ample, when the Bureau of Land Management relocated its
headquarters from Washington, D.C. to Colorado, vacancies
rose by 169%. As a significant proportion of these departures

Stein (1995: 22) defined organizational memory as “the
means by which knowledge from the past is brought to bear
on present activities, thus resulting in higher or lower levels
of organizational effectiveness.” Therefore, the notion of or-
ganizational memory aligns with the preceding discussion of
individuals as important sources of retained information in
government agencies. From this perspective, the number of
departures in a workforce alone does not measure the accu-
rate amount of loss in organizational knowledge, because not
all employees share the same level of skills and experiences
(Hauscknecht and Holwerda 2013; Simón et al. 2022). This is
because turnover is a collective phenomenon that reflects the
“aggregate quantity and quality” (emphasis added, Nyberg
and Ployhart 2013: 109) of employee knowledge and skills
lost by the agency.
The most important individual attribute relevant to the
study of organizational memory theory may be the length
of service in the organization. As Pfeffer (1983, as cited in
Walsh and Ungson 1991: 78) noted, an understanding of
an organization’s practices and beliefs comes with tenure in
the organization. Because an employee with a longer tenure
possesses institutional memory that better represents the
full history of the agency, their departure will have a greater
impact on organizational productivity, knowledge-sharing
networks, and replacement costs than that of a novice. This
yields the following hypothesis.
H2 (Service Length Hypothesis): The positive association
between employee turnover rates and the proportion of
delayed projects is increasing in the proportion of turnover
among experienced employees.
Longer tenure in an organization typically indicates a higher
position and greater competence. Even if that is not the
case, employees with longer tenure often have a greater in-
stitutional memory that spans the organization’s history.
Consistent with the cost-benefit theory of turnover (An and
Meier 2022), employees with longer tenure should have
higher levels of agency-specific knowledge than employees
who are relatively new in the agency (Piper and Lewis 2023;
Stein 1995). In addition, highly experienced employees may
be more difficult and costly to replace in terms of recruitment
and training (Hancock et al. 2013).
How agency centralization offsets organizational
memory loss
Organizational memory is not only stored in individuals;
it is also distributed across different parts of an organi-
zation (Levitt and March 1988; Walsh and Ungson 1991).
Organizational memory, then, is preserved when an organiza-
tion can access its knowledge and expertise, even as members
come and go. That is, not all organizational processes are the
product of voluntary human enactment. An organization’s
structural features, such as centralized reporting channels
and standard operating procedures, store past experiences
and lessons, preserving knowledge when key organizational
members leave (Fiedler and Welpe 2010; Walsh and Ungson
1991; Weick and Gilfillan 1971).
In particular, the dimension of “centralization” refers to
“the locus of authority to make decisions affecting the organ-
ization” (Pugh et al. 1968: 76), which involves the extent to
which an organization uses roles and positions to prescribe
behaviors, such as how, where, and by whom tasks are to
be performed (Fiedler and Welpe 2010). Centralized decision-­
making authority streamlines the process of recalling previ-
ously stored records. Also, a centralized directory of “who
knows what” buffers against the disruptive effects of network
gaps created by leavers. That is, a centralized reporting channel
facilitates the remembering process, improving accessibility
for a wider scope of organizational members and diminishing
knowledge gaps among remaining and onboarding employees
(Anand, Manz, and Glick 1998; Argote, Aven, and Kush
2018). Bureaucratic organizations with a so-called “central
memory” may have a competitive advantage over networked,
post-bureaucratic structures (Pollitt 2009: 204). Similarly,
Krause and Jin (2020) found evidence demonstrating that
federal agencies with centralized budgetary authority are
better equipped to improve budgetary stability through effi-
cient coordination.
In the realm of US federal agency IT projects, easy ac-
cess to such information can be crucial for maintaining
continuity and minimizing disruptions during personnel
transitions. In that context, the FITARA of 2015 identified
two major areas of reform: centralization and standardiza-
tion of project implementation (US Office of Management
and Budget 2015: 1). Since the enactment of the Clinger-
Cohen Act of 1996, all federal agencies must establish a
CIO position to oversee the development, maintenance,
and implementation of an integrated IT architecture that

How task standardization offsets organizational
memory loss
As noted, centralization and standardization have shown
more observable changes across federal agencies since the
FITARA of 2015 compared to other structural features, such
as the degree of formalization and specialization (US Office
of Management and Budget 2015: 1). In addition to central-
ization, the dimension of standardization plays a crucial role
in shaping organizational behavior (Cyert and March 1963).
It refers to the extent to which an organization employs rules
and procedures to prescribe how, where, and by whom tasks
should be performed (Fiedler and Welpe 2010). The notion
that standardization influences organizational memory can
be dated back to Cyert and March (1963), who found that
standard operating procedures are related to the memory
of an organization. Standardization is a function of the fre-
quency with which the pattern of coordinated activity is
performed (Grant 1996; Nelson and Winter 1982), since or-
ganizational routines are reinforced through repetition and
learning (Levitt and March 1998; Nelson and Winter 1982).
Further, standardization of tasks forces organizations to ex-
plicate their processes, define unambiguous interfaces, and
communicate them to others in a meaningful way (Argote
and Guo 2016; Fiedler and Welpe 2010; Miller, Pentland,
and Choi 2012). Therefore, task standardization establishes
routines as a repository of the organization’s past experiences,
enabling consistency and efficiency regardless of individual
turnover. This aligns with a recent study on Belgian unem-
ployment agency employees, which found that the negative
effects of turnover were smaller for non-discretionary tasks
governed by well-defined rules than for discretionary tasks
requiring value judgment (Wynen et al. 2019). Conversely,
tasks requiring non-routine responses, such as emergency
response, tend to rely more heavily on personnel knowledge
(Stavick 2025).
H4 (Standardization Hypothesis): The positive associ-
ation between employee turnover rates and the propor-
tion of delayed projects is decreasing in the proportion of
standardized projects.
H4 predicts that increasing the share of highly standardized
projects will moderate the impact of turnover on project
delays within an organization. Standardized IT projects are
usually modularized into granular segments and timeframes
by design, often involving quarterly (or more frequent)
planning-to-release cycles, compared to a one-year project
wherein each developmental stage involves distinct, new
tasks (US GAO 2017). Variation in task standardization has
become particularly evident among federal agencies (US GAO
2004, 2017). An agency with a higher level of standardized
procedures typically features shorter, iterative project cycles
involving planning, development, and launching, making it
simpler for remaining employees to effectively navigate any
skill gaps following staff departures. In contrast, one-time
projects rely on cross-functional networks spanning multiple
years.3
Data and empirical strategy
Using a novel dataset of IT projects implemented by US fed-
eral IT workers between fiscal years 2015 and 2021, this
study evaluates the extent to which workforce turnover
affects agency project delays. This analysis employs an un-
balanced panel of 94 federal independent agencies and
subagencies under cabinet-level departments4 (hereafter
“agencies”), encompassing 540 observations. The dependent
variable, Project delays, was measured as the proportion of
projects delayed by a more than 10% schedule variance5
by each agency in a given fiscal year. According to the US
Government Accountability Office (2013), a high-risk project
is one whose projected duration differs by more than 10%
from the initially planned duration. Annually, agencies’ IT
project managers are mandated to provide multiple updates
by the end of the fiscal year, including projected durations
and current progress for each project (US General Services
Administration nd). The data was obtained from the Federal
IT Dashboard, which offers public access to a comprehensive
list of federal IT investments and associated project perfor-
mance information submitted by agencies. Figure 2 displays
the overall distribution of delayed-projects ratio by agency:
the dependent variable falls within the [0,1] range, with large
clusters at zero (no project was delayed for more than 10% of
the planned duration) and one (all projects were delayed for
more than 10% of the planned duration).

Considering the bounded nature of the dependent variable
and data structure, the following models employ generalized
estimating equation (GEE) methods, following the proposal
of Papke and Wooldridge (2008) to estimate fractional probit
models for panel data. The GEE model extends the generalized
linear model to the analysis of correlated panel data. For frac-
tional outcomes, standard linear panel data models are unsuit-
able: they may produce estimates outside 0 and 1, which do not
correspond to the actual probabilities (Papke and Wooldridge
1996). Previous studies, including Papke and Wooldridge
(1996), have attempted to address this problem by using
pooled quasi-maximum likelihood estimation methods, such
as fractional logit and probit, and explicitly included unit fixed
effects for panel data on fractional response outcomes to ac-
count for unobserved heterogeneity that is possibly correlated
with the explanatory variables. Although these methods are
promising, one of the major practical shortcomings is that
those estimating unobserved heterogeneity for each unit may
encounter the incidental parameters problem, leading to bias,
especially in this large-N, small-t study setting (Wooldridge
2019). Papke and Wooldridge (2008) proposed an alternative
approach that models population-average covariates, analo-
gous to fixed-effects regression but with a reduced bias for
such cases (Mundlak 1978; Papke and Wooldridge 2008;
Wooldridge 2019), by utilizing GEE to analyze correlated
panel data for fractional outcome variable. Therefore, the sta-
tistical models permit flexible estimation of an unstructured
within-group correlation matrix to account for these panel dy-
namics. Statistical model specifications also include means of
time-variant explanatory variables to control for unobserved
heterogeneity across agencies.6
Turnover covariates
This study examines project delays within the IT units of 94
federal agencies, with a focus on federal IT workforce turnover.
Turnover is the main independent variable, first divided into
aggregate turnover (the proportion of total separations) and
managerial turnover (the proportion of separations limited
to managerial positions), then further disaggregated into ag-
gregate voluntary turnover, aggregate involuntary turnover,
managerial voluntary turnover, and managerial involun-
tary turnover. Voluntary turnover includes separations due
to quits and transfers, while involuntary turnover includes
reductions in force due to poor performance, misconduct, or
terminations. Separations due to retirement were excluded
from these measures.
The General Schedule (GS) classification system, which
covers the majority of white-collar employees in the US fed-
eral government, features a pay scale with levels ranging
from GS-1 to GS-15 (US Office of Personnel Management
2011). In 2001, the US Office of Personnel Management
introduced the “GS-2210 Information Technology” occupa-
tion group to encompass IT specialists, project managers, and
program managers responsible for managing, supervising,
and supporting IT projects (CIO Council 2020). This study
defines managerial turnover as that affecting those at grades
equal to or higher than GS-13, who “manage the overall
planning, execution, and timely delivery” of IT services. This
analysis is motivated by the notion that variation might exist

Control covariates

proportion of new accessions of IT workers, obtained from
the OPM Accessions Database. New employment can either
increase or decrease project delays: it offers the advantage
of filling gaps left by turnover but also involves the cost of
training those new employees. Proportion of new projects,
measured as the proportion of newly initiated projects within

Conditional turnover effects on project delays by
service length
The Service Length Hypothesis (H2) predicts a positive mar-
ginal effect of turnover rate moderated by the departures’
length of service (Turnover × Service length), measured as the
proportion of departing employees with 15 or more years of
service, which corresponds to the median length of service
of employees within the sample. Although overall involun-
tary turnover does not yield a statistically significant effect
on delays (cf. fig. 3C), a notable pattern appears when all
those involuntarily departing employees have served fewer
than 15 years (and thus less experienced). In such cases,
each 1% increase in involuntary turnover is associated with
a 1.65% reduction in project delays (P = .092; fig. 4C). In
contrast, an interdecile increase in the departure of more ex-
perienced employees does not produce statistically signifi-
cant differences in the marginal effects of aggregate turnover
(−0.02%, P = .910; fig. 4A), voluntary turnover (−0.01%,
P = .956; fig. 4B), or involuntary turnover (0.61%, P = .149;
fig.4C). Evidence consistent with H2 is observed only for
managerial turnover. An interdecile increase in the proportion
of longer-tenured departing managers (i.e., from 0% to 5%)
is associated with a 0.47% increase in delays (P = .011; fig.
4D). However, the departure of more experienced managers
does not yield statistically significant differences for either
voluntary (0.25%, P = .162; fig. 4E) or involuntary turnover
(−5.74%, P = .185; fig. 4F).
Conditional turnover effects on project delays by
agency centralization
Evidence consistent with the Centralization Hypothesis (H3)
was obtained by observing the negative marginal effect of the
turnover rate in centralized agencies (Turnover × Agency cen-
tralization). Figures 5A–C provide estimates for the average
marginal effects of aggregate turnover in centralized and
noncentralized agencies. Figure 5A shows that the propor-
tion of delayed projects decreases by 0.89% in centralized
agencies for each 1% increase in the aggregate turnover rate,
compared to noncentralized agencies (P = .031). This differ-
ential in project delay rates is even more pronounced when fo-
cusing on voluntary turnover, where a 1% increase exhibits a
1.19% lower project delays in centralized agencies compared
to noncentralized agencies (P = .037, cf. fig. 5B). However, this
reduction in delays for centralized agencies was not observed
in the aggregate involuntary turnover model (P = .346, cf. fig.
5C). Figure 5D–F exhibit average marginal effects of turn-
over, limited to managerial positions, in centralized versus
noncentralized agencies. Contrary to the hypothesis, agency
centralization does not produce statistically significant
differences for any type of managerial turnover9.
Conditional turnover effects on project delays by
task standardization
Figure 6A–F display the estimates of average marginal turn-
over effects across different levels of task standardization
(H4: Standardization Hypothesis), measured as the pro-
portion of standardized projects in a given agency year.
In support of H4, the positive association between the
aggregate turnover rate and project delays (cf. fig. 3A) is
observed only in agencies with low task standardization,
where standardized projects do not exceed 50% (cf. fig. 6A).
The relationship between aggregate voluntary turnover and
project delays appears stronger in agencies with relatively
low task standardization, but no significant effect on those
where standardized projects exceed 40% (cf. fig. 6B). Figure
6D–F present the estimates for turnover effects limited to
managerial positions: total managerial turnover, manage-
rial voluntary turnover, and managerial involuntary turn-
over, respectively. Figure 6D and E show that an interdecile
increase in task standardization from the 10th percentile
(0% standardized projects) to the 90th percentile (71%
standardized projects) is associated with a 1.10% reduction
in delayed projects for every 1% increase in total manage-
rial turnover, and a 1.11% reduction for every 1 % increase
in managerial voluntary turnover. While the directions of
all estimates are consistent with H4, neither estimate is sta-
tistically significant (P = .170, P = .166). Additionally, no
meaningful difference is found in the effects of involuntary
managerial turnover on project delays (cf. fig. 6F).

tests

from the turnover measures in Supplementary Appendix

a comparable set of estimates using alternative performance

planned duration) or limiting to extreme delays (>30% of
planned duration). Several differences are worth noting.

support for H2. Departures of employees with 15 or more

Discussion
Employee turnover in government agencies reflects a loss
of crucial organizational knowledge and skills. Despite the
growing importance of this line of research, whether turn-
over promotes or hinders organizational performance re-
mains contested (An and Meier 2022; Lee 2018; Wang and
Sun 2020). This article presents a theory of organizational
memory, relatively neglected in existing turnover research,
to better understand the dual effects of turnover on perfor-
mance, highlighting both its functional and dysfunctional
outcomes (March 1991; Walsh and Ungson 1991). The key
to understanding the impact of turnover lies in the distrib-
uted nature of organizational knowledge, which is not only
retained in individuals, but also embedded in their commu-
nication structures of “who knows what” and standardized
procedures that together support effective task performance
(Argote and Guo 2016; Walsh and Ungson 1991).
The empirical findings generally support the study’s
hypotheses informed by the organizational memory theory.
An interdecile increase in the overall turnover rate among
IT professionals delays 4.93% more projects (H1). Agencies
experience an additional 0.47% more project delays when
the turnover involves more experienced managers (H2). The
impact of turnover also depends on organizational design:
centralized agencies experience 1.19% fewer delays for each
1% increase in aggregate voluntary turnover and 0.88% fewer
delays for each 1% increase in aggregate turnover, compared to
noncentralized agencies (H3). Task standardization mitigates
the adverse effects of managerial voluntary turnover, but only
under limited conditions, especially in agencies with standard-
ization levels of at least 40 percent for aggregate voluntary
turnover and 50 percent for aggregate turnover (H4).
Federal agencies rely heavily on professionals recruited from
various fields (e.g., software engineers, doctors, and scientists)
when facing complex policy problems or new technological
challenges (Meier 2000). Additionally, public managers tend
to struggle with employee recruitment and retention compared
to those at private firms. Given the highly professionalized
workforce in IT units, with naturally higher rates of turnover
and fewer means to retain professionals, understanding how
to maintain organizational expertise through agency structure
in addition to competent workers is of paramount importance
for managers. Though focused on the federal IT workforce,
the complementarity between turnover and agency structure
offers broader implications. First, the aggregate number of
departures does not accurately represent the retention and/
or loss of organizational knowledge of an agency. In contrast
to voluntary turnover, more involuntary turnover, which may
reflect lower human capital, is associated with less project
delays especially when the departing employees have service
lengths below the median. Further, different forms of retention
facilities of organizational knowledge—whether in individuals,
communication structures, or standardized procedures—offer
distinct, complementary benefits (Argote and Guo 2016;
Argote, Aven, and Kush 2018). While centralization buffers
aggregate and voluntary turnover, it does not substitute for
memory loss from managerial departures. Moreover, the pre-
ponderance of adverse effects of aggregate voluntary turnover
in agencies with low task standardization suggests that having
a well-defined procedure plays an important role in helping
organizations retain organizational memory.
The current study is limited in two ways. First, while project
delay is examined as a performance measure, it represents
only one aspect of organizational performance. Although the
empirical evidence supports the claim that turnover causes
negative disruptions to project timelines, the positive effects
of turnover—such as the renewal of existing knowledge and
the development of new knowledge through new hires—may
be better captured in other performance dimensions. Future
research could explore additional performance metrics to cap-
ture the broader impacts of turnover, such as the reliability of
the final IT system or the quality of program administration.
Second, the findings of this study may not be generalizable to
other sectors of the government workforce, particularly those
where information and communication are less amenable to
codification and more personalized. Future studies could ex-
amine how turnover affects organizations with varying levels
of knowledge intensity, focusing on how personalized com-
munication and information exchange influence the effects of
turnover in these contexts.

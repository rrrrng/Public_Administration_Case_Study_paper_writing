ABSTRACT
Stakeholder theory has been advocating the inclusion of affected parties in organizational processes to increase the legitimacy and effectiveness
of organizational governance. However, organizations can fail to achieve these objectives if there is no systematic link between stakeholders
and their constituency. Based on democratic notions of representation, we argue that democratic stakeholder representativeness is an essen-
tial virtue of stakeholder governance processes. We conceptually derive authorization and accountability as normative elements of stakeholder
representativeness and operationalize the construct by proposing empirical indicators of stakeholder representativeness as well as procedural
guidance on their adoption in a practical governance context. By doing so, we contribute to the advancement of practical stakeholder govern-
ance as well as to the public management and organizational theory literature by specifying and operationalizing a construct that had previously
been only vaguely defined.
Introduction
Over the past few decades, stakeholder theory has success-
fully advocated for the consideration of stakeholder interests
in organizational governance processes (Bryson 1995;
Dmytriyev, Freeman and Hörisch 2021; Freeman, Phillips
and Sisodia 2018; Harrison et al. 2015). Organizations have
widely responded by implementing stakeholder dialogues,
stakeholder hearings, stakeholder panels, and other forms of
stakeholder engagement with the goals of creating organiza-
tional legitimacy as well as public value (Freeman, Wicks and
Parmar 2004). However, stakeholder governance may fail to
achieve these goals if there is no link between stakeholders
and their constituency. If participants in a stakeholder di-
alogue have no or a poor connection with the stakeholder
group they belong to, they can hardly collect the interests of
their community or communicate organizational outputs to
them. In this context, we argue that democratic stakeholder
representativeness is an essential virtue of any stakeholder
governance process that seeks legitimacy through the in-
clusion of affected parties. But what exactly does the term
“stakeholder representativeness” mean?
The existing literature offers little guidance on what
constitutes a representative stakeholder and which criteria or-
ganizations could consider in order to identify representative
stakeholders. Basic principles of democratic representation
cannot simply be transferred to the stakeholder context. This
is because stakeholder governance frequently occurs beyond
the boundaries of a nation-state making traditional election
procedures difficult to implement. Furthermore, in a stake-
holder governance context, there is no clearly defined demos
but rather a fluid stakeholder constituency that requires or-
ganizations to understand issue by issue which stakeholders
to include in a governance process. Against this background,
our research interest and study’s purpose are twofold: First,
we intend to highlight the importance of stakeholder repre-
sentativeness in ensuring procedural legitimacy within stake-
holder governance. Second, we aim to conceptually define
and operationalize democratic stakeholder representativeness
and, thereby, contribute to the advancement of stakeholder
theory. Before further unfolding our research interest, we will
define some key terms and concepts that are relevant in this
research context.
Governance has been prominently defined by Keohane
as “the making and implementation of rules, and the exer-
cise of power, within a given domain of activity” (2002: 3).
It comprises “all those activities of social, political and ad-
ministrative actors that can be seen as purposeful efforts to
guide, steer, control or manage (sectors of facets of) societies”
(Kooiman 1994: 2). Due to the increasing complexity of so-
cial issues, collaborative governance approaches that bring
together diverse stakeholders to develop policy solutions have
become common in public and private management (Bingham
et al. 2005; Bryson et al. 2017; Jager et al. 2021; Jastram,
Otto and Minulla 2022; Karimo et al. 2022; Lee 2022). In
this context, collaborative governance can be referred to as
“a group of interdependent stakeholders, often from mul-
tiple sectors, who work together to develop and/or imple-
ment policies to address a complex, multi-faceted problem,
or situation” (Robertson and Choi 2012: 84). One key ad-
vantage of collaborative governance is that stakeholders have
more opportunities to participate in decision-making when
compared to traditional bureaucratic processes and we can
observe that public organizations increasingly employ these
so-called new modes of multi-stakeholder governance (Risse
2004; Robertson and Choi 2012).

Within this framework, we define stakeholder governance
as any purposeful engagement with stakeholders, which can
include stakeholder management, stakeholder dialogues, or
any other type of interaction with stakeholders. Stakeholder
governance often seeks to create organizational or proce-
dural legitimacy (Best, Moffett and McAdam 2019; Martin
Connolly and Wall 2017) and we argue that this goal is not
always achieved in current governance approaches. This is be-
cause current stakeholder governance is frequently based on
a blurry or absent concept of stakeholder representativeness,
and thus, lacks important democratic characteristics that can
undermine the legitimacy as well as the effectiveness of gov-
ernance (Risse 2004; Scharpf 2005). This issue is illustrated
by the following critical statement from an industry repre-
sentative who participated in a multi-stakeholder govern-
ance process hosted by ISO, the International Organization
for Standardization [the statement has been translated into
English by the authors]:
“It is simply not transparent who is in this [process]. [….]
I think that ISO’s claim of being participative and demo-
cratic, and gender balanced and regionally balanced, is ac-
tually a farce. Our new member, for instance, I don’t know
who this is, where is he coming from? Who has nominated
him? […] Governments are democratically elected […].
But who is participating in this process and how the se-
lection took place - you cannot call this participative or
representative […]. You could also ask who the colleagues
from the NGO sector actually represent. I have no clue, I
don’t know for whom Mr. x is here or for whom Mr. y is
here […] or if there aren’t ten other NGOs which are actu-
ally much more representative.” (Jastram 2012: 98, 105).
This statement exemplifies the existing criticism regarding
the lack of stakeholder representativeness and transparency
in collaborative governance (Fougère and Solitander 2020;
Gerrits and Edelenbos 2004). Moreover, it indicates the neg-
ative impact that unclear stakeholder selection criteria can
have on organizational legitimacy.
In light of this, we aim to advance Stakeholder Theory by
differentiating and operationalizing the concept of stake-
holder representativeness. We define representativeness as
a democratic property that any stakeholder can have at a
specific point of time, in a specific governance context and
actor constellation. Representativeness describes how well a
stakeholder represents the group of stakeholders with whom
he/she is associated, and it can be high or low, depending
on whether or not normative criteria of representativeness
are met. Thus, democratic stakeholder representativeness
describes the normative quality of how well stakeholders rep-
resent a specific constituency in a democratic sense. In the
following, we will discuss why stakeholder representativeness
is critical for enforcing the central advocacy of stakeholder
theory and will subsequently develop the construct and its
core defining criteria.
Stakeholder Theory (Freeman 1984) is one of the most in-
fluential theories in organizational research. It is based on the
idea that accounting for the concerns and well-being of an
organization’s key interest groups is conducive to the achieve-
ment of its goals (Dmytriyev et al. 2021; Freeman, Phillips
and Sisodia 2018; Phillips, Freeman and Wicks 2003). In
other words, including stakeholders’ interests is a critical con-
dition for the success of any type of organization as it allows
for adaptation to a changing and increasingly turbulent or-
ganizational environment. It can specifically prevent risk and
enable innovation within organizations, as well as support a
positive reputation and protect an organization’s license to
operate (Freeman 1984).
The empirical success of the stakeholder approach has
been immense, and stakeholder governance processes have
spread widely throughout businesses and into the public
sector (Freeman and Phillips 2002; Jones, Harrison and Felps
2018; Scott, Thomas and Magallanes 2019). Ministries and
international organizations are increasingly utilizing stake-
holder governance processes, such as involving stakeholders
in hearings, consultations, or policy development processes
(Edelenbos and Klijn 2006; Fung 2006; Herian et al. 2012;
Jastram and Schneider 2015; Weible 2007). Furthermore, a
growing number of multi-stakeholder initiatives (Banerjee
2018; Detomasi 2007; Utting 2002), global action networks
(Waddell and Khagram 2007), and transnational norm-
building networks (Mückenberger and Jastram 2010), such
as the UN Global Compact, the Global Reporting Initiative,
or ISO 26000, have been built on principles of stakeholder
inclusion and participation (Jastram and Klingenberg 2018).
Also, private (self-) regulation is increasingly reflecting the no-
tion that including stakeholder representatives enhances the
legitimacy as well as the effectiveness of governance (Arnesen
and Peters 2018; Martin et al. 2017; Sloan and Oliver 2013).
This is especially true when the process involves the develop-
ment and/or promotion of standards and policies that rely on
the voluntary compliance of their addressees.
With regard to the substantial amount of existing
stakeholder-related literature (e.g., Hörisch, Freeman and
Schaltegger 2014; Rasche and Gilbert 2011; Waddock 2008)
it is striking that there is no conceptual clarity about what
makes a stakeholder representative. While some authors use
the term stakeholder representativeness in their studies, the
construct remains largely vague and undefined (e.g., Leardini
et al. 2019; Mascarenhas et al. 2015). However, clear
constructs are important in order to facilitate scholarly dia-
logue (Suddaby 2010). Moreover, they are an essential com-
ponent of theory building as well as of theory testing, and
comparing and evaluating research findings (Sutton and Staw
1995). Aside from this conceptual gap, no practical guidelines
or established principles exist for identifying and selecting
representative stakeholders. When there is no formal, institu-
tional, or procedural link between representatives and those
being represented, accountability and legitimacy problems
arise and governance effectiveness suffers (Buchanan and
Keohane 2006; Risse 2004). Hence, public acceptance and
support are more likely to be achieved the more representa-
tive stakeholders are, and a lack of stakeholder representa-
tiveness can jeopardize these goals.
Against this background, we argue that stakeholder in-
clusion alone is not sufficient to achieve the full potential
of legitimacy and governance effectiveness, and may even
backfire when stakeholder representatives are perceived as
non-legitimate by their constituency. Therefore, the concept
of representation needs to be further discussed and theoreti-
cally developed in the context of stakeholder theory in order
to increase the impact and democratic quality of stakeholder
governance and management. Our core research question is:
how can democratic representativeness be normatively de-
fined and operationalized in a stakeholder governance con-
text? We aim to advance stakeholder theory by developing

First, we present the current state of stakeholder theory, fo-

of democratic representativeness from the literature.

tiveness. We then discuss our concept and its implications for

State of the Theory
For nearly four decades, stakeholder theory (Freeman 1984,
1994) has been used as a heuristic approach to understanding
an organization’s environment with a focus on its key interest
groups (e.g., Brown and Dillard 2015; Freeman et al. 2010,
2018; Jensen and Sandström 2011). The concept of stake-
holder management is based on the premise that accounting
for stakeholders and their interests allows organizations to
create value as well as legitimacy and enables them to pro-
tect their so-called license-to-operate (Freeman, Wicks and
Parmar 2004; Matten and Crane 2005; Phillips 1997a).
Stakeholder theory is distinguished by its inclusive ap-
proach, which advocates treating all stakeholders with fair-
ness, honesty, and generosity (Harrison, Freeman and Sá de
Abreu 2015). As written by Harrison and colleagues, “a firm
that manages for stakeholders allocates more resources to sat-
isfying the needs and demands of its legitimate stakeholders
than what is necessary to simply retain their wilful partic-
ipation in the productive activities of the firm” (2010: 58).
Donaldson and Preston (1995) distinguish between three
interrelated types of stakeholder theory: descriptive, in-
strumental, and normative. In its descriptive form, stake-
holder theory provides a model that views organizations
as a combination of cooperative and competitive stake-
holder interests and allows for the analysis of corresponding
organizational
responses,
strategies,
and
management
approaches (Carroll and Buchholz 2015; Donaldson and
Preston 1995). Instrumental stakeholder theory examines the
interrelationships between the practice of stakeholder man-
agement and the accomplishment of organizational perfor-
mance goals, whereas normative stakeholder theory allows
for a moral interpretation of an organization’s interaction
with its interest groups (Donaldson and Preston 1995).
Against this background, the normative as well as the
instrumental relevance of stakeholder representation in
governance processes have been recognized in the public ad-
ministration literature as well as in other related fields (Ansell
and Gash 2007; Conner 2015). Authors argue, that for col-
laborative governance to be effective, stakeholders must par-
ticipate (Ansell and Gash 2007; Scott et al. 2019), which is
consistent with the premise of stakeholder theory advocating
for stakeholder inclusion “in all stages of the decision-making
process” (Freeman 1997: 22). Moreover, representation is
considered as critical for balancing competing stakeholder
demands in decision-making, and can thereby be relevant for
gaining legitimacy (Crucke and Knockaert 2016; Moriarty
2014). Hence, the public administration literature emphasizes
the importance of stakeholder representation as a proce-
dural quality for instance in the context of policy networks
and network governance (e.g., Scott et al. 2019; Sørensen
2002, 2005; Wang and Ran 2021), public organizations such
as schools (Conner 2015), or during public crises (Steelman
et al. 2021). Nonetheless, until now, scholars have failed to
clearly define what makes stakeholders representative and
how organizations can identify representative stakeholders to
include them in their governance processes.
This conceptual gap in the literature is not marginal. In
fact, over the evolution of stakeholder theory throughout
the last decades, the identification of relevant stakeholders as
well as its theoretical foundation have been key challenges
for scholars (Gomes and Gomes 2008; Laplume et al. 2008;
Mitchell, Agle and Wood 1997; Weitzner and Deutsch 2015).
Core questions include how to identify relevant stakeholders
and what makes a stakeholder relevant. The ambiguity under-
lying stakeholder identification is articulated in the so-called
broad versus narrow debate over different characteristics,
attributes, and claims that qualify stakeholders as rele-
vant (Bundy, Shropshire and Buchholtz 2013; Freeman and
Reed 1983; Jones, Felps and Bigley 2007). In this context,
Freeman’s definition of stakeholders as “any group or indi-
vidual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the
firm’s objectives” (1984: 25) still serves as the main boundary
of the broad view on stakeholder theory. The proponents of
this definition argue that the term stakeholder should include
a possibly large number of actors and groups comprising, for
example, fringe groups (Freeman 1984), and even the nat-
ural environment (Starik 1995). Furthermore, an inclusive
definition of stakeholders is critical to the creation of social
and economic value as it allows to consider the interests of
all relevant stakeholders who can influence the organization
(Bryson 2004).
Other researchers, however, see this broad scope of the
stakeholder concept as problematic because it leads to
the identification of a large number of undifferentiated
stakeholders (e.g., Mitchell et al. 1997; Sharp, Finkelstein
and Galal 1999). According to Phillips (2003), the theory
has limited value if virtually everyone is considered a stake-
holder. If not more precisely defined, the broad concept of
stakeholder management may allow influencing groups with
power to disrupt an organization’s operations in ways that

prevent consideration of particular interests of other legiti-
mate stakeholders (Mitchell et al. 1997). Thus, stakeholder
theory can undermine “business accountability… because a
business that is accountable to all, is actually accountable
to none” (Sternberg 2000: 5) and therefore, the theory may
fail to provide guidance for day-to-day operations (Marcoux
2000). Thus, critics of the broad views have attempted to
limit the scope of stakeholder theory by introducing spe-
cific attributes that make stakeholders relevant (Mitchell et
al. 1997; Mok et al. 2015). Sharp and colleagues (1999), for
example, distinguish between influencers and stakeholders,
arguing that while some stakeholders can be both—
stakeholders and influencers—others with a legitimate stake,
may have no influence over the organization. Other scholars
distinguish between technical and institutional stakeholders,
with the former associated with operational processes and the
latter related to legitimacy issues (Gomes, Liddle and Gomes
2010a). Alternatively, according to Mok and colleagues
(2015), a social network approach can be used to reveal the
positions and interconnectedness of stakeholders and their
corresponding issues and Key (1999) highlights different con-
tractual relationships between the firm and its stakeholders
in this context.
In the seminal article by Mitchell and colleagues (1997)
stakeholder salience is defined as a function of the attributes
power, legitimacy, and/or urgency. They define power as the
extent to which one party can impose its will on another due
to having access to coercive, utilitarian, and normative means.
Building on Suchman’s approach legitimacy refers, in this
context, to “a generalized perception or assumption that the
actions of an entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within
some socially constructed system of norms, values, beliefs,
and definitions” (1995: 574). Finally, urgency describes the
extent to which stakeholder claims necessitate immediate ac-
tion due to time sensitivity or criticality. Through a combina-
tion of these attributes the authors identify seven stakeholder
classes where the number of attributes stakeholders possess is
linked with the level of managerial attention they are likely to
receive (Mitchell et al. 1997).
Legitimacy as a core criterion for stakeholder relevance has
also been referred to and discussed in further studies (Carroll
1993; Hill and Jones 1992; Phillips 2003) and often been
associated with morality (e.g., Clarkson 1998; Donaldson
and Preston 1995; Langtry 1994). Kaler, for example, argues
that stakeholders are constituents with morally legitimate
claims whose interests need to be served by an organization
(2002). Phillips grounds his notion of normative legitimacy
on stakeholder fairness by arguing that “obligations of fair-
ness” are created when parties voluntarily accept mutually
beneficial contributions (1997b: 57). As he puts it, norma-
tive stakeholders are “those groups to whom the organiza-
tion has a moral obligation, an obligation of stakeholder
fairness” (2003: 30). In contrast to normative stakeholders,
derivative stakeholders are those groups whose interests and
actions should be considered by managers because of their
potential power upon the organization (2003). Managerial
consideration of those groups is also legitimate; however, this
legitimacy is determined through their ability to impact the
organization and its normative stakeholders (2003).
With a view to the intense debates on stakeholder repre-
sentation and legitimacy, it is striking that the construct of
representativeness, which is enrooted in democracy theory
as a crucial criterion of legitimacy (Dryzek 2001; Grant and
Keohane 2005; Mansbridge 2009), has so far not been clearly
defined and operationalized in the academic literature. If the
central premise of legitimacy is the desirability and appro-
priateness of an organization’s actions based on how well it
serves its stakeholders’ interests (Mitchell et al. 1997; Phillips
1997b, 2003; Suchman 1995), can this be carried out when
legitimate individual stakes are not truly represented or even
excluded due to absent ties between representatives and their
constituents (Habermas 1990; Suddaby, Bitektine and Haack
2017)?
Having relationships with many interest groups it is cru-
cial for an organization to identify and respond to relevant
interests and stakes to be able to maintain stakeholders’ ac-
ceptance, support, and cooperation (Crane and Ruebottom
2011; Freeman et al. 2018; Perrault 2017). Stakeholder
acceptance is a pivotal indication of the quality of govern-
ance processes (Leach et al. 2005), as well as a necessary
precondition for trust between governance actors and their
constituents (Mok et al. 2015). This can, however, only be
achieved if stakeholders are given adequate opportunities to
express their opinions and participate in governance processes
(Foo et al. 2011). Yet, stakeholder relationships have hith-
erto often been managed under conditions of assumed or
perceived representativeness which lacks the core democratic
characteristics required to establish an institutional or pro-
cedural link between representatives and their constituents.
If individual stakes bearing a legitimate claim are excluded
or not properly represented due to an absent connection be-
tween stakeholders and their representatives, the perceived
legitimacy and appropriateness of the governance process
might be distorted (Gomes et al. 2010b; Martin et al. 2017;
Suddaby et al. 2017). Accordingly, Foo and colleagues (2011)
demonstrated that stakeholders will be hesitant to participate
in governance processes if they perceive a lack of genuine in-
terest in their opinions, and they will eventually refuse to vol-
untarily comply with the adopted policies and standards (see
also Bryson 2004).
To summarize, we argue that the mere inclusion of any
stakeholders is insufficient to implement the central premise
of stakeholder theory because it can facilitate the negligence
of stakeholder rights when their legitimate interests are not
adequately represented. In the following section, we discuss
the notion of representation in the context of democracy
theory before deriving fundamental criteria of stakeholder
representativeness as core elements of stakeholder legitimacy.
Democratic Representation and Indicators of
Representativeness
Representation is a key element of democracy (Pennock and
Chapman 1968; Pitkin 1967; Schwartz 1988) and the lit-
erature offers a variety of definitions for it. Pitkin provided
one of the classical explanations and argues that to represent
means to “make present again” (1967: 8). In other words,
political representation is the process of making the voices,
opinions, and viewpoints of citizens present in the political
arena (1967). In a largely self-contained Greek polis, each
community member can act as his/her own representative on
political matters. Yet, in modern societies with a high level of
complexity, a strict application of direct democracy to every
single political decision is impractical. Consequently, most

modern democracies are representative, because the costs
of coordinating large constituency deliberations, as well as
the reduced efficiency of consensus building, make the prac-
tical implementation of direct democracy impossible in most
cases. Hence, the representation of many people by a few
people becomes important when the active participation of all
members of a political community in their community’s polit-
ical deliberation is impracticable, undesirable, or unrequested
(Dovi 2014). Therefore, in contemporary politics, representa-
tion occurs when political actors speak, advocate, and act on
behalf of other citizens during the political discourse. And yet,
the questions of when an actor can be considered representa-
tive and what criteria define his/her representativeness pose
conceptual challenges (Dryzek 2001; Grant and Keohane
2005; Mansbridge 2009).
The significance of representativeness becomes clear in a
discourse in which an actor claims to represent other citizens
without their formal consent or authorized power. The actor
speaks, acts, and makes decisions on behalf of those citizens
on issues that affect their interests. Can a decision made in a
discourse, in which the voices and opinions of those affected
were not truly represented be justified, valid and, thus legiti-
mate? Urbinati argues that “democratic representation is fair
or just representation insofar as it involves issues of advo-
cacy and representativity; issues of a meaningful presence, not
simply presence alone” (2006: 42). But what is meaningful
presence?
The core idea of legitimacy is the acceptance of a partic-
ular decision based on specific normative criteria (Suchman
1995; Suddaby et al. 2017). The legitimacy of public power
is determined by whether decisions serve the interests of
those affected, meaning that the decisions reflect what those
represented desire (Grant and Keohane 2005). However, the
legitimacy of decisions is not solely based on their outcomes
but also on the input of those represented and the process by
which decisions are made (Arnesen and Peters 2018; Mosley
and Wong 2021; Scharpf 2005).
In this context, representatives are considered legitimate to
the extent to which they can speak and act credibly on behalf
of the people they represent (Grant and Keohane 2005). We
call this property, which representatives may possess, repre-
sentativeness and there is a large body of literate on criteria
deemed critical for effective representation (e.g., Disch 2011;
Hendriks 2009; Montanaro 2012; Saward 2014). Related
concepts include descriptive or passive representation (rep-
resentatives mirror their constituents in terms of certain de-
scriptive characteristics, experiences, and interests), symbolic
representation (symbolizing and standing for the constitu-
ency), substantive representation (the extent to which policy
outcomes reflect the constituents’ interests), or anticipatory
representation (representatives anticipate the expectations of
the audience) (Bradbury and Kellough 2011; Jankowski et al.
2020; Lim 2006; Mansbridge 1999, 2009; Meier 2019; Miller
and Keiser 2021; Mosher 1968; Petit 2010; Pitkin 1967). A
further relevant concept is functional representation which
is often contrasted with territorial representation. While the
later refers to the representation of a constituency in a specific
territory, functional representation is related to the represen-
tation of groups that share a functional characteristic such
as trade unions representing employees (Wolff 2013). The
concept has received considerable attention in the democracy
and political science literature (see e.g., Hirst 1994; Squires
1999) since Cole famously stated: “All true and democratic
representation is […] functional representation” (1920, 33).
However, as Wolff (2013) puts it: “functional representation
is not automatically democratic representation” (4).
In 2008, Dryzek and Niemeyer introduced the notion of
discursive representation which assumes the representation of
discourses rather than individuals and is particularly relevant
when a well-bounded constituency cannot be determined. To
fulfill the promise of deliberative democracy, discursive repre-
sentation entails identifying an appropriate actor best suited
for representing a specific discourse (Wolff 2013). In this con-
text, Saward (2010) presented the idea of the representative
claim which he defines as “a claim to represent or to know
what represents the interests of someone or something” (38).
Within this framework, Guasti and Geissel (2019) explain
that claim makers raise their voices on behalf of a certain
constituency and indicate a linkage between themselves and
the claimed constituency. However, the authors also point out
that in practical politics “claims of representation with ex-
plicit reference to a claimed constituency are very rare, most
claim-makers only insinuate implicitly that they speak for
someone” (Guasti and Geissel 2019: 101).
Aside from the relevance of each of these concepts in spe-
cific situational contexts, one of the most prominent and
widely applied types of representativeness relates to the
classic type of formal representation in which constituents
express their consent (or dissent) with a particular decision
or representative through voting. According to proponents
of formal representation, having an identity relationship
with constituents is not necessarily a proxy for good rep-
resentation (Gay 2002), and it may even lead to reduced
accountability when the descriptive characteristics of a repre-
sentative lead constituents to believe that their core interests
are being properly represented even when this is not the case
(Mansbridge 1999). Hence, Urbinati and Warren (2008) em-
phasize that to be representative actors must be responsive
to the interests of their constituents and address their needs
and viewpoints (Pitkin 1967; Weldon 2002). In this context,
Grant and Keohane underline the relevance of transparency,
arguing that responsiveness can only be achieved in the pres-
ence of continuous communication between representatives
and the represented (2005). Transparency and the exchange
of information advance all forms of representation and
increase the likelihood that ultimate decisions will be con-
sistent with the interests of the affected audience and thus
gain wider acceptance (Beisheim and Dingwerth 2008; Wang
and Guan 2022).
However, representation can formally not be achieved
if a specific audience does not recognize the representative.
Thus, free and fair elections are often considered as a key
condition of representation and a key driver of legitimacy
(Rehfeld 2006). Similar to Pitkin’s (1967) classic claim that
to be democratic, representatives must (a) be authorized by
the constituents and (b) have opportunities to hold their rep-
resentatives accountable for their performance, substantial
parts of the literature treat authorization and accountability as
core elements of representativeness (Dovi 2016; Mansbridge
2009; Pitkin 1967; Rehfeld 2006; Schwartz 1988). In other
words, the absence of formal authorization and accounta-
bility can eventually undermine the claims and activities of
representatives, regardless of any positive outcomes they may
achieve (Mansbridge 2009). In the following sections, we will,
therefore, discuss authorization and accountability in greater
depth and identify their core normative elements.

Authorization
As stated above, democracy theorists agree that authorization
is one of the core normative conditions of democratic repre-
sentation (Mansbridge 2003; Schwartz 1988; Young 1990).
According to Pitkin (1967), authorization is the means by
which a representative obtains his/her standing, status, or pos-
ition. Formal authorization gives representatives a legitimate
power to speak and act on behalf of citizens and represent
their interests during a political discourse (Montanaro 2012;
Volmert 2011). As such, authorization is a critical mechanism
for ensuring the recognition of representatives by the constitu-
ency. In this context, elections are typically the core institution
justified by the standard of self-determination (Young 2000,
see also Montanaro 2012). Without electoral institutions,
as Pitkin argues, “the ideal of representation would remain
an empty dream, or at most would occasionally recur as a
fitful, inexplicable blessing, which we have no power to pro-
duce or to prolong” (1967: 239). Therefore, she claims that
in representative democracies it is difficult, if not impossible,
to achieve systematic responsiveness from political actors
without elections. Similarly, in the delegation model of repre-
sentation (where the represented appoint their representatives
as delegates for their constituency), power is viewed as legiti-
mate only when it is authorized with the legitimizing consent
of those who delegate it (Grant and Keohane 2005).
Yet, a growing number of individual and collective actors
currently act on a self-appointed basis claiming to repre-
sent others outside of electoral institutions and apart from
state authority. Self-appointed representation usually occurs
in civil society and the public sector, and it is independent
of the state’s coercive political authority (Montanaro 2012).
However, self-appointed claims may remain ineffective until
recognized by the represented audience (Rehfeld 2006), while
representation will be considered legitimate only when the
democratic audience recognizes and accepts the representa-
tive. In this context, however, a lack of formal or informal
voting systems limits the constituency’s ability to reject non-
accepted representatives. Hence, self-appointed representation
systems can only supplement, rather than replace the demo-
cratic quality of voting procedures in allowing constituencies
to express authorization or disapproval of representatives. In
light of this, we define the following core empirical indicator
for authorization:
Indicator 1: All representatives have been elected and can
be de-elected by their constituency (Benner, Reinicke and
Witte 2004; Grant and Keohane 2005; Montanaro 2012;
Pitkin 1967).
Accountability
The second core criterion of democratic representation
is accountability (Dovi 2007; Grant and Keohane 2005;
Mansbridge 1999). According to Keohane, “an accounta-
bility relationship is one in which an individual, group, or
other entity makes demands on an agent to report on his
or her activities and has the ability to impose costs on the
agent” (2002: 12). As put by Pitkin (1967) it comprises (a)
the representative’s responsiveness to the principal and (b) the
constituents’ ability to punish or sanction their representa-
tives for failing to act in their interests (e.g., by de-electing
a representative). Thus, accountability is concerned with the
control of the agent by the principal, the prevention of abuse
of power, and the possibility of imposing sanctions on power
wielders (Grant and Keohane 2005; Lee 2022; Romzek and
Dubnick 1987).
In this context, Page and colleagues (2015) differentiate
between vertical and horizontal democratic accountability
with the former referring to the extent to which decisions are
legal and responsive to authorizers, while the latter refers to
how much decisions respond to collaboration partners and
other stakeholders. With regards to stakeholder governance,
the authors explain that horizontal accountability improves
the effectiveness of collaboration processes by facilitating
deliberation, knowledge exchange and consensus building
among relevant stakeholders (Lee 2022; Page and colleagues
2015; Schillemans 2008; Stoker 2006). Rehfeld (2006) fur-
ther distinguishes between participation and delegation-
oriented forms of accountability. In the participation model
of accountability, the actions of power wielders are judged
by those affected by the outcomes through their participation
in the decision-making process, whereas, in the delegation
model the democratic audience evaluates the actions of repre-
sentatives whom they have entrusted with power. In this vein,
accountability implies a relationship between power wielders
and the democratic audience. Between these two parties, there
must be a general consensus on the legitimacy of the opera-
tional standards for accountability and the formal roles of
the parties in the relationship (Rehfeld 2006). As such, a core
function of accountability is to expose and punish two types
of power abuses: the illegitimate or unauthorized use of power
and decisions by representatives that are evaluated by the
constituency as unfair or unwise (Rehfeld 2006). Therefore,
fostering accountability implies the establishment of insti-
tutional mechanisms that consistently provide information
to the relevant audience empowering them to hold power
wielders accountable and impose sanctions (Rehfeld 2006).
According to Dovi (2007), a key virtue for any represen-
tative is the ability to gain the constituents’ critical trust.
To foster this, representatives must sufficiently inform
constituents of all costs and benefits associated with a deci-
sion, respond to the constituent’s claims, and serve as good
role models (Dovi 2007). This is in line with the broader
perception of accountability which is shifting away from
monitoring and sanctioning representatives toward them ac-
tively accounting for or explaining their actions (Behn 2001;
Mansbridge 2009; Philp 2009). We can, thus, distinguish be-
tween two main drivers of accountability: responsiveness and
transparency. Responsiveness measures the scope to which
representatives consider the needs, problems, and viewpoints
of the represented during their decision-making process
(Weldon 2002), while transparency describes the accessibility
of information to ensure the proper functioning of all forms
of representation (Grant and Keohane 2005). This implies
mutually transparent communication involving the principal
and the agent both raising questions and providing answers,
thereby determining whether or not they remain aligned
or whether the grounds of their relationship have changed
(Mansbridge 2009). Furthermore, the representative’s re-
sponsiveness to the constituents’ needs and problems serves
as an important measure of how much the constituency’s
perspectives influence the representative’s decisions (Weldon
2002). Therefore, in this context, a crucial element of political
democracy is transparency.

Transparency can be defined as open access for anyone
affected by a decision to all information relevant to his/her
own related decision-making (Grant and Keohane 2005). A
high level of transparency not only ensures that accounta-
bility mechanisms function properly, but it also increases the
likelihood that the final decision is one that the represented
are likely to accept (Beisheim and Dingwerth 2008). To sum-
marize, transparency about relevant information, as well as
the representative’s responsiveness to the needs and interests
of their constituency are critical to the proper functioning of
an accountability relationship. Therefore, we define the fol-
lowing two accountability indicators:
Indicator 2: Representatives regularly share all informa-
tion relevant to decision-making with their constituency
(transparency) (Grant and Keohane 2005; Mansbridge
2009).
Indicator 3: Representatives regularly report on how they
have represented the interests and claims of their constitu-
ency (responsiveness) (Weldon 2002).
In the following, we will transfer these principles to the stake-
holder governance context and discuss possible mechanisms
for their application.
Stakeholder Representativeness and its
Practical Application
In this section, we discuss how the above developed criteria
of political representation can be transferred to and applied
in a stakeholder governance context. In line with the previous
section, we argue that legitimate stakeholder governance
processes need to consider the following indicators of demo-
cratic stakeholder representativeness:
Indicator 1: All stakeholder representatives have been
elected and can be de-elected by their stakeholder groups.
Indicator 2: Stakeholder representatives regularly share all
information relevant to decision-making with their stake-
holder groups.
Indicator 3: Stakeholder representatives regularly report
on how they have represented the interests and claims of
their stakeholder groups.
In the following sections, we will discuss how these ideal-
typical, normative principles can be implemented during
practical governance processes.
Authorization
With respect to the practical application of the authorization
principle, we argue that stakeholder democracy benefits from
new opportunities for digital participation. Thus, in a prac-
tical governance process, this indicator could, for instance, be
addressed by applying a procedure like the following:
1.	 All affected stakeholder groups and an equal number of
representatives for each party are identified by the gov-
erning organization according to transparent criteria that
are published, for example, on a globally accessible on-
line platform. To identify missing stakeholder groups, a
grievance mechanism is installed allowing stakeholders,
who do not feel properly represented, to file a complaint.
2.	 In the next step, stakeholders nominate themselves or
other candidates on the platform and provide informa-
tion regarding the qualifications of themselves or their
nominees. All stakeholders must have equal chances to
nominate themselves or others, and representatives may
only represent one stakeholder group.
3.	 Next, represented stakeholders elect their representatives
via the online platform.
The primary challenge arising at this stage is defining the
stakeholder constituency. A constituency can be defined as
a group of people or political entities sharing similar polit-
ical viewpoints and aspirations, who are entitled to elect a
representative (Lockyear and Cunningham 2017). Defining
the stakeholder constituency, however, is not a trivial task,
because individuals can belong to multiple stakeholder
groups at the same time. Scientists, for instance, can also be
Non-Governmental Organization (NGO) members and are
consumers as well. Depending on the governance issue, there
may be several stakeholder groups which individuals could
possibly be associated with. So which stakeholder group
should they belong to? Which stakeholder group should they
be eligible to vote in and who makes these decisions?
We agree with Dahl (1989) in that individuals themselves
are best qualified to determine which stakeholder group they
belong to with regard to a specific governance issue. Thus,
we propose a self-attribution mechanism through which
stakeholders self-select one of the stakeholder groups defined
in step 1. A similar mechanism has been employed by the
UN in the process of determining NGOs suitable for consul-
tative relations (United Nations 1996). We argue that self-
attribution mechanisms enable more legitimate governance
processes because they are based on the principle of equal
consideration of interests (Dahl 1989), which ensures that all
affected stakeholders have equal chances to participate and
express their preferences. Self-attribution, however, creates
a second potential challenge if stakeholders attribute them-
selves based on strategic considerations rather than their as-
sessment of the best issue-related fit. Such strategic behavior
may jeopardize the democratic nature of the voting process.
For example, an individual may see her/himself primarily as
an NGO member. However, assuming or knowing that his/
her preferred NGO candidate will receive a sufficient number
of votes, the individual could strategically assign herself/him-
self to another stakeholder group where his/her voting could
potentially have destructive motives. This issue may worsen
if larger groups of actors use strategic self-attribution. To
mitigate such problems, we again propose a mechanism that
allows other constituencies to file a complaint when necessary.
Another issue to consider in this context is the possible ex-
clusion of relevant parties from digital governance processes.
As the literature shows, new forms of digital governance are
rising (Feeney and Welch 2016), and recent sources indi-
cate that the digital divide is gradually closing (International
Telecommunication Union 2021). Furthermore, scholars
found that digital platforms can be a useful tool for including
actors who lack the resources to participate in governance
processes in person (e.g., Anduiza et al. 2009; Hoffmann and
Lutz 2015). Nonetheless, depending on the governance con-
text and issue at stake, governance bodies need to consider
how to support and provide access to otherwise excluded rep-
resentatives, particularly stakeholders from developing coun-
tries. ISO, for instance, in their ISO 26000 multi-stakeholder

to support the participation of stakeholders from developing

Overall, the illustrative procedure described above
demonstrates that authorization in a stakeholder governance
process is generally possible. Yet, organizations may modify
and/or choose a procedure that is best suited to their specific
governance context. If organizations are looking for simple
procedural provisions for stakeholder representativeness to
begin with, they should concentrate on three first “light” steps
regarding stakeholder authorization: (1) communicating their
stakeholder selection criteria to the public, and/or (2) allowing
stakeholders to nominate themselves as participants in a
governance process, and/or (3) allowing stakeholders to ex-
press potential concerns about an existing selection of stake-
holder representatives. Offering such options would increase
the transparency of stakeholder selection mechanisms and
provide space for stakeholders’ voices in this procedure.
Normative democratic criteria regularly need to be translated
into practical action, and the specific context of the govern-
ance discourse influences how close the process will get to the
normative ideal.
Accountability
Stakeholder accountability implies that representatives
participating in a governance process regularly report in-
formation relevant to the decision-making of their stake-
holder group. This could, for instance, include the following
steps:
1.	 Stakeholder representatives regularly publish reports,
meeting agendas, and/or memos and other relevant
documents on an online platform to keep their stake-
holder parties educated and capable of making informed
decisions.
2.	 Stakeholder representatives regularly publish reports
related to how they have represented their stakeholder
constituency’s interests.
3.	 All stakeholders have equal chances to access the
documents published by their representatives.
4.	 Any stakeholders who do not feel properly informed or
sufficiently represented may file a complaint.
To prevent unnecessary bureaucracy, stakeholder account-
ability must provide enough information for stakeholders
to understand decision-making outcomes while avoiding
impeding transparency by providing too many documents.
The appropriate balance depends on the complexity and
gravity of the governance issue at stake. In complex govern-
ance network structures bureaucracy can further be limited
because not all participating stakeholders need to be repre-
sentative. An illustrative example in this context is GRI (the
Global Reporting Initiative), which is an organization with
over 500 member organizations from over 70 countries that
facilitates dialogue on sustainability reporting. Moreover, it
develops and disseminates the most prominent and widely
applied reporting standard in the field of sustainability (GRI
2022a). In general, any organization can become a member
of GRI and participate in mutual learning about sustaina-
bility reporting (GRI 2022b). However, when GRI develops
or updates its core reporting standards, it commissions a
distinguished group of 15 stakeholder experts. The group is
described by GRI in the following way: “The 15 members of
the GSSB [Global Sustainability Standards Board] represent
diverse sectors, backgrounds and regions around the world.
Their range of technical expertise, diversity of experience,
and multi-stakeholder perspective support and strengthen
the development of the GRI Standards” (GRI 2022c). As
demonstrated by this example, not all participants in multi-
stakeholder governance networks must be representative.
However, for certain activities or subtasks, such as stand-
ardization, it can have positive effects on legitimacy, norm
acceptability, and compliance if representatives of affected
stakeholder groups are included. In other words, while a
couple of hundred stakeholders may be members of a gov-
ernance network, a subgroup for critical tasks may consist of
only a few representative stakeholders.
Moreover, in other situations, highly complex governance
tasks can make substructures necessary in order to ensure ad-
equate, widespread stakeholder representation. ISO norms and
standards, for example, aim to enable and facilitate interna-
tional trade which has implications for a large number of in-
dustry players and other stakeholders. Therefore, ISO engages
stakeholders from various sectors to increase the quality and
perceived legitimacy of its governance outputs (Mückenberger
and Jastram 2010; Tamm Hallström 2006). When the nature of
the task is more complex, ISO gathers more diverse stakeholder
expertise. This happened, for example, in the case of ISO 26000
where approximately 2000 stakeholder experts from more than
90 countries were involved in the development of an international
guideline related to Corporate Social Responsibility. To manage
the process, ISO established international as well as national
multi-stakeholder committees comprised of stakeholders from
various sectors (Jastram 2012). This increased level of stake-
holder participation aimed to achieve high levels of input as
well as output legitimacy and to allow for widespread voluntary
compliance (Scharpf 1997, 1999).
Another example relates to the development of a National
Action Plan (NAP) on Business and Human Rights, in which
the German government invited a wide range of stakeholders
from different sectors to engage in consultations and provide
their input to the governance process. To reduce complexity,
the process was divided into 12 thematic subtopics and in each
meeting about 40 stakeholders were invited to participate and
share their expertise and perspectives on a specific focus topic
(National Action Plan 2016). These examples show that in-
ternational and national governance bodies do not hesitate to
engage large numbers of stakeholders in complex governance
processes in order to achieve legitimacy and acceptability of
governance outputs. To reduce complexity, it can be construc-
tive to create topical or geographical subgroups as the examples
of ISO or the German NAP process demonstrate. However, a
weakness of these and many other governance processes is that
clear and transparent stakeholder selection criteria had been
missing and it was, thus, often unclear why specific stakeholders
were included while others were not (Jastram 2012). Thus, in
order to further increase the legitimacy of these processes, we
recommend the consideration of principles of democratic stake-
holder representativeness as developed above.
Having presented these recommendations on procedural
provisions for democratic stakeholder representativeness it
is crucial to remember that normative democratic criteria
such as Habermas’ “ideal speech situation” (1984), or Dahl’s
criteria of a “perfect democratic process” (1989) describe
ideal-typical standards that are, or can most often, not be

insufficient resources or capacity. Nonetheless, they serve as

ernance processes regarding the proximity to the ideal norm
and provide guidance for practitioners and stakeholders by
creating awareness of normative best practices (Jastram and

Discussion: Implications for Stakeholder
Theory
We have argued that stakeholder governance approaches
can be fundamentally misconceived and misleading if prin-
ciples of representativeness are neglected. Particularly the
goal of inducing legitimacy through stakeholder inclusion
(Barney and Harrison 2020; Freeman et al. 2018; Harrison
et al. 2010) may not be achieved if stakeholder representa-
tives are not regarded as representative by their constituency.
While authors tend to agree that the inclusion of stakeholders
matters for fostering legitimacy (Gomes et al. 2010b; Phillips
2003), the quality of governance processes (Foo et al. 2011;
Gomes and Gomes 2008), stakeholder support (Beach et al.
2012; Robertson and Choi 2012) and joint value creation
(Best et al. 2019; Bryson et al. 2017), we argue that in the
absence of stakeholder representativeness those objectives
are constrained. Against this background, the consideration
of representativeness will advance existing discourses on
stakeholder identification (Gomes and Gomes 2008; Phillips
1997b) and stakeholder relevance (Mitchell et al. 1997;
Phillips 2003; Weitzner and Deutsch 2015).
Our new construct adds, for instance, to Mitchell and
colleagues’ (1997) stakeholder salience framework by
contributing a new dimension that, as we argue, should be
included as a relevant constituent of stakeholder legitimacy. If
legitimacy is primarily associated with output related aspects,
such as the desirability and appropriateness of actions, the
important democratic link between constituents and repre-
sentatives becomes neglected. For actions and decisions to be
considered as desirable and appropriate is it crucial for the
constituents to have an institutional connection with their
representatives. These ties will facilitate the development
of constituents’ trust that their viewpoints and needs are
being properly represented and fostered during a governance
process. If stakeholders participating in governance processes
are not representative, key claims, interests, or issues in rele-
vant communities may be missed which could lead to a lack
of legitimacy and/or ineffective governance processes.
While these aspects are particularly important in the public
management sector, stakeholder representativeness can also
be relevant in private stakeholder management in order to
increase the acceptability of governance and decision-making
and to protect the license to operate of private organizations.
In private organizations, however, other strategic business
considerations and economic goals may come into play and
businesses may identify their relevant stakeholders such as
customers, shareholders, employees as well as NGOs based
on other selection criteria such as economic influence and
power. However, today private organizations also increas-
ingly engage as political actors (Scherer and Palazzo 2007,
2011; Scherer, Palazzo and Baumann 2006), whether indi-
vidually or as participants in multi-stakeholder governance
initiatives. In this regard, the concept of stakeholder repre-
sentativeness becomes important for private organizations as
well, because they share the interest in legitimate and accept-
able governance outputs.
Therefore, for both public and private organizations it is
pivotal to satisfy stakeholder expectations through their ac-
tive engagement (Beach et al. 2012; Martin et al. 2017) and,
as a result, to gain public support for the introduced projects
and policies (Bryson 2004). In this context, new approaches
to stakeholder governance based on the idea of knowledge
coproduction have gained attention in research and practice
(Wiewiora, Keast and Brown 2016). The active participa-
tion of affected stakeholders in the development of services,
standards, or policies has particularly been advocated for its
potential to achieve shared solutions that are aligned with the
opinions and perspectives of the targeted audience (Alford
2002; Bovaird 2007; Monfardini 2010). This type of en-
gagement is postulated to transform the relationships with
stakeholders and ultimately gain their trust and cooperation
(Cepiku and Giordano 2014). Yet, also in these forms of gov-
ernance, representativeness is important because otherwise
satisfaction and input of the targeted audience may not be
reached.
Aside from these considerations, one further crucial pur-
pose for engaging with stakeholders is to identify and pre-
vent risks (Freeman 1984; Freeman et al. 2018; Phillips et
al. 2003). This goal, however, may not be achieved if rep-
resentatives do not have close ties with their constituency
and may, thus, not be fully aware of any disapproval or
concerns prevailing among their peer stakeholders. Thus,
representative stakeholder dialogues are also crucial for ef-
fective risk identification and timely public relations with
an organization’s core audience (Freeman et al. 2010; Lock
2019). Continuous information exchange is critical for
establishing and maintaining relationships with stakeholders
(Stohl and Stohl 2005), forming alliances (Shumate and
O’Connor 2010) and fostering stakeholder cooperation
(Taylor and Doerfel 2005). However, if the stakeholders
chosen are not representative and have poor ties with their
core constituents, the intended messages may not reach the
relevant audience. This can lead to communication failures,
create knowledge gaps, and can eventually result in stake-
holder dissatisfaction.
Democratic stakeholder participation, however, does not
imply that all stakeholders must participate or be represented
in a governance process. Yet, from a democratic perspective, it
is important that a general chance of participation is granted
to all affected parties. Whether or not stakeholders choose
to participate remains their own decision. As in general poli-
tics, when participating in governance processes, stakeholders
may take different approaches to accountability, sharing in-
formation with their audience, and creating value for their
constituency. It is, thus, important, that representatives can
also be de-elected by their constituency if trust and support
are no longer given.
Conclusions
In this article, we have argued that democratic stakeholder
representativeness is an important asset for any stakeholder

governance process that strives for the creation of legitimacy.
We have discussed that representativeness is a core element of
democratic processes and can increase both the legitimacy and
effectiveness of governance. This is especially relevant in a public
management context, but it can also become crucial for private
organizations that aim to protect their license to operate.
We have defined stakeholder representativeness as a prop-
erty that any stakeholder can have at a specific point in time
and in a specific governance context and actor constellation.
Furthermore, we have operationalized stakeholder represent-
ativeness based on normative criteria and indicators derived
from democracy theory (authorization and accountability).
Finally, we have provided procedural guidance on the appli-
cation of stakeholder representativeness in practical stake-
holder governance contexts.
In doing so, we do not intend to replace the fundamental
stakeholder definition by Edward Freeman (1984) but
rather to suggest representativeness as one possible addi-
tional characteristic a stakeholder can have. We sought to
advance stakeholder theory by conceptually differentiating
stakeholder representativeness and thereby contributing
a clarified construct to the stakeholder management litera-
ture. If stakeholders are not representative, stakeholder gov-
ernance may fall short of its goal of creating legitimacy and
could also miss other objectives relating to risk management,
public value creation, public relations, and communication.
By contributing the construct of representativeness, we enrich
the theoretical debate on stakeholder inclusion and identifi-
cation and demonstrate how representativeness can improve
the democratic quality of a stakeholder governance process.
Without the systematic integration of principles of stake-
holder representativeness, public and private stakeholder
governance processes may be perceived as less legitimate if
those affected do not feel adequately represented. This may,
as we have argued, result in an absence of public support and
compliance deficits if governance outcomes are not accepted
due to a perceived lack of stakeholder representativeness.
With the concept of stakeholder representativeness and the
development of its core criteria and indicators, we hope to
stimulate a discourse about representativeness in stakeholder
governance, and we suggest applying our framework as a
normative benchmark of legitimate stakeholder governance.
We see our framework as relevant not only for the literature
on public management and organizational theory, but also for
scholars in the political sciences, international relations, law,
and sociology.
We suggest future research on the challenges of
implementing stakeholder representativeness in a practical
governance context. It would be interesting, for instance,
to analyze, which types of practical support are most ef-
fective in facilitating digital access to governance platforms
for stakeholders with limited chances for participation.
Moreover, research on stakeholder responses to new govern-
ance procedures as described in this article would be worth-
while. It would be desirable, for example, to explore how
stakeholders implement principles of authorization and ac-
countability and whether there are behavioral differences
between stakeholders from different sectors or cultural
backgrounds. While stakeholder inclusion has increasingly
become mainstream in current governance processes, best
practice approaches to facilitating representativeness can still
be largely explored.

ABSTRACT
This research examines how actors responsible for leading organization-­wide efforts use “influence tactics” in pursuit of in-
traorganizational, or functional, collective action. We draw from intraorganizational influence theory and propose a revised
taxonomy of tactics that vary along two dimensions: coerciveness (soft and hard) and orientation (relational and rational). We
test factors associated with their use in the context of municipal sustainability, an objective that requires input from multiple
distinct units for which it is not a core mission. Analysis of a sample of over 500 United States cities shows that the hard–rational
approach of citing formally adopted ordinances or plans in an attempt to compel participation was the most frequently used tac-
tic. The results of a multivariate probit regression further suggest that features of city governments' organizational structure and
the support received from elected officials are associated with the use of different influence tactics to achieve collective action
around sustainability initiatives.
Harmonizing the varied and often siloed efforts needed to at-
tain broad organizational objectives is an enduring challenge in
organizational science research. Similarly, in public administra-
tion scholarship, identifying the means through which public
organizations can effectively implement expansive policy ini-
tiatives is a fundamental focus. As they become more complex,
societal challenges and their potential solutions increasingly
fail to fit neatly within the hierarchical and decentralized struc-
tures that characterize traditional government organizations
(Kettl 2002). Moreover, the devolution of policymaking in the
US over the past several decades has resulted in general-­purpose
local governments taking on new, expansive issues (Craw 2015).
Objectives including diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI), cli-
mate protection, and public safety require input from multiple
departments in a typical local government organization. As a
result, understanding the dynamics around intraorganizational
collaboration in city governments has become particularly im-
portant in the management of issues that span policy domains
and necessitate joint action from multiple functional units.
From this perspective, issues related to sustainability stand out
because of the explicitly multi-­disciplinary/multi-­functional ap-
proach they call for. Although not traditionally a local respon-
sibility, over the past two decades, city governments around
the world have adopted it as an objective (UN Environment
Programme 2023). As a general concept, sustainability calls for
the long-­term balance of environmental, social, and economic
well-­being, the relevant expertise for which is often associated
with distinct educational and professional backgrounds (Purvis
et al. 2019). Moreover, in most general-­purpose organizations,
including local governments, staff with expertise around each
of these foci have been compartmentalized into different units.

Recent scholarship on “functional collective action” (FCA)
has illuminated the transaction costs, risk, and buy-­in chal-
lenges that may arise when units within a single organization
are asked to engage in additional work or adopt new practices
to advance an organization-­wide goal (Kim et al. 2022; Yi and
Cui 2019). This vein of research, much of which focuses on local
governments and environmental-­related issues, has also exam-
ined the interplay between organizational arrangements and
the inter-­departmental implementation of functionally trans-
boundary policies (Krause and Hawkins  2021a; Laurian and
Crawford 2016).
Local governments have an important role to play in advanc-
ing global Sustainable Development Goals (UN Environment
Programme 2023). Over the past several decades, cities have been
credited with innovating policies around climate and sustain-
ability, often pursuing aims more ambitious than those identified
by higher levels of government (Solecki and Rosenzweig 2022).
Their implementation of these aims, however, has often failed
to live up to expectations (Betsill and Bulkeley 2007; Stokes and
Breetz 2020). In light of this, scholarship examining local sus-
tainability efforts is shifting toward understanding the relevant
management tools and processes that are able to assist imple-
mentation (Park 2021).
The implementation of comprehensive local sustainabil-
ity initiatives requires assistance and buy-­in from a range of
community organizations, which shine a spotlight on the inter-­
organizational collaborative process. In addition, meaning-
ful sustainability action on the part of most general-­purpose
governments depends on contributions from multiple internal
departments and/or administrative units. Thus, local sustain-
ability initiatives also require substantial intraorganizational
integration to be successful (Deslatte and Stokan 2020; Krause
and Hawkins 2021a). Thus far, this internal dynamic has been
relatively unexplored by scholarly literature. Despite a small
number of studies looking at how sustainability managers facili-
tate buy-­in (Krause and Hawkins 2021a; MacDonald et al. 2020),
generalizable assessments of the interpersonal strategies used to
achieve the cross-­departmental implementation of city sustain-
ability initiatives are lacking.
This research examines how actors responsible for leading
broad organization-­wide efforts use different types of “influ-
ence tactics” in pursuit of intraorganizational collective action.
The administrative settings of city government, coupled with
the silo-­spanning characteristics of sustainability, provide a
useful context for study. Guiding our analysis is the observa-
tion that both formal hierarchical structures and decentralized
implementation concurrently shape the management strate-
gies used within public organizations (Kettl 2002; O'Toole and
Meier 2011). Within this context, intraorganizational influence
theory provides a useful perspective to help organize the types
of strategies an actor can use to motivate semi-­independent
functional units to engage in collaborative behavior and achieve
greater integration. Intraorganizational influence theory has
been applied primarily in the business management literature
to explain the types of strategies managers use to influence be-
havior and align the actions of other company employees toward
a particular goal or objective (Kipnis and Schmidt 1982; Falbe
and Yukl 1992; Yukl 2012). Its perspective is likewise relevant
to public sector organizations, where collaborative intraorgani-
zational relationships are often needed to achieve desired policy
results.
The following section describes the integration challenges posed
by functional fragmentation and relates them to existing FCA
research. We next describe intraorganizational influence theory
and use it as a means to (1) organize strategies for facilitating
collaborative behavior and (2) identify factors that may influ-
ence the types of strategies that a city sustainability leader de-
cides to employ. We then describe the data collection process,
variable measurement, and model specification, and present the
results of the empirical analysis. We conclude with a discussion
of the implications for public administration and management
research.
1   |   City Structure and FCA Challenges
In response to what was perceived as undemocratic and cor-
rupt government systems in the latter half of the 19th century,
reform-­minded activists and scholars promoted new approaches
to organizing and managing government affairs. Through the
mid-­20th century, a ‘rational-­centered’ perspective led to wide-
spread reorganization of governments to improve efficiency,
deepen technical expertise, and enhance the professionalism
of the public sector workforce. Of particular significance for
the current research are the classic studies on government bu-
reaucracy that called for organizational structures, procedures,
and rules that promoted clarity and consistency in operations
(Weber 1947). In many local governments, these calls were re-
sponded to with the creation of hierarchical systems that cen-
tralized administrative authority, and structures that organized
core government responsibilities into separate departments,
each with its own functional missions and work procedures.

The typical city government continues to exhibit functional
fragmentation whereby policy expertise and specialized respon-
sibilities are compartmentalized into distinct service areas, and
relationships and reporting structures between units are for-
mally delineated. This can be a relatively efficient structure for
implementing narrow sets of policies or managing daily tasks in
a predictable and stable environment (Kettl 2009; Provan and
Lemaire 2012; Rainey 2014). However, departmental boundar-
ies have been observed to act as “semi-­permeable membranes,”
slowing and reducing the flow of communication and infor-
mation important to collective action (Egeberg 2007). Scholars
also observe that variation in the structural characteristics and
administrative practices between units in an organization can
inhibit cohesiveness and stymie joint work (Saz-­Carranza and
Ospina  2010). In addition, differences in the professional ori-
entation of staff clustered in key departments, such as between
planners and engineers, can further exacerbate challenges to
collective action (Johnson 2021; Kettl 2002).
For many objectives, the semi-­permeable boundaries existing
between departments pose no meaningful problem. However,
issues like sustainability, which are complex and functionally
transboundary, depend on complementary input from diverse
sets of specialized and administratively separate actors to be ef-
fectively addressed (Park et al. 2021). In their seminal work on
organizational theory, Lawrence and Lorsch (1967, 4) described
integration as “the process of achieving unity of effort among
the various subsystems in the accomplishment of the organi-
zation's task”. Functional Collective Action (FCA) is related to
this idea of integration. It arises when there is sufficient con-
nectedness of mission, policies, and workflows across units in
an organization. FCA was originally introduced in the urban
administration and public policy literature as one component
of the larger Institutional Collective Action (ICA) framework.
The ICA framework addresses the challenge posed by frag-
mented governmental authority in metropolitan regions and
identifies governance mechanisms that can be employed to fa-
cilitate greater vertical and horizontal coordination between
government entities (Feiock  2013). Within this overarching
perspective, FCA focuses on the fragmentation of bureaucratic
responsibilities within a single government and the types of or-
ganizational and administrative strategies that facilitate the in-
tegration of individual departments or units for the purpose of
achieving organization-­wide goals (Kim et al. 2022).
The increasing failure of US higher-­level governments to achieve
satisfactory policy solutions has resulted in many local govern-
ments taking action around complex and non-­traditional is-
sues, such as climate protection (Solecki and Rosenzweig 2022),
immigration (Kuge  2022), and safe pharmaceutical disposal
(Hubbard and Fowler 2021). These are not issues that any single
local government can handle on its own, much less any single
department. As a result, urban policy and public administration
scholars have drawn from FCA research to understand the use
and efficacy of different management approaches for facilitating
integration and collaborative effort in the pursuit of an overar-
ching goal. For example, based on the FCA framework, Krause
and Hawkins (2021a) point to the importance of having an ex-
plicit and empowered leader “orchestrating” the implementa-
tion of transboundary initiatives.
In this analysis, we couple research on FCA, which emphasizes
administrative and organizational design, with intraorganiza-
tional influence theory, which emphasizes behavioral strategy,
to better understand how sustainability leaders within local
governments work to build organization-­wide participation in
relevant efforts.
2   |   Intra-­Organizational Theory and City
Sustainability Implementation
Intraorganizational influence theory has been applied primar-
ily in the business and operations management literature to de-
scribe the various tactics that can be employed by managers to
align the actions of peers, subordinates, and supervisors within
an organization (Yukl 2012). This theory is described as consist-
ing of an agent who uses (or avoids) one or more tactics to affect
the attitude of another, the “target,” and influence their behavior
to produce a particular outcome (Yukl and Falbe 1990; Gattiker
and Carter 2010; Lu et al. 2019). Much of the scholarship on in-
traorganizational influence theory stems from the Profiles of
Organizational Influence Strategies (POIS) instrument devel-
oped by Kipnis and Schmidt (1982); see also Kipnis et al. (1980),
and later refined by Yukl and Falbe  (1990) and Falbe and
Yukl (1992). Together, they outline a series of influence tactics
that are commonly used to shape professional behavior in the
workplace. See Table 1 for a comprehensive list.
Although commonly applied in business settings, the use of
influence tactics has been studied in varied organizational
contexts, including universities (Yukl et al. 1993; Hochwarter
et al. 2000) and non-­profit settings (Farmer et al. 1997). In pub-
lic administration research, Jensen (2007) explores seven influ-
ence tactics used to shape policy decisions in formalized group
decision-­making exercises, finding a link between the type of
decision and the tactic selected. In particular, he notes an in-
crease in the use of rational tactics as decisions become less po-
litical and more administrative in nature.
Multiple taxonomies have been developed to categorize these
influence tactics along a dimension of interest, for example,
by whether their direction of intended influence extends up-
ward, downward, or laterally within an organization (Higgins
et al. 2003; Madigan et al. 2020) or whether tactics place more
focus on oneself (i.e., the asker) or others (Feser 2016). Others
suggest categorizing influence tactics in workplace settings by
linking them to intended aims (Bass 2008; Lee et al. 2017). One
of the more widely referenced taxonomies in workplace settings
is means-­based, separating tactics into hard, soft, and rational
types depending on the instrument deployed (Farmer et al. 1997;
Gattiker and Carter 2010; Madigan et al. 2020). Hard influence
tactics generally rely on the invocation of authority and may
take the form of demands or expressed requirements. They may
implicitly or explicitly be accompanied by the threat of sanctions
for noncompliance. Soft influence tactics, in comparison, seek
to persuade but leave the decision about whether to cooperate at
the discretion of the target. They are often personal and aim to
influence by appealing to the target's values and aspirations or
by integrating their input into decision-­making (Schwarzwald
et al. 2004). Finally, rational influence tactics use reason and

Though some researchers have utilized these categories to orga-
nize influence tactics and to examine their relative effectiveness
on various types of outcomes (Kipnis and Schmidt 1982; Farmer
et al. 1997; Tepper et al. 1998; Somech and Drach-­Zahavy 2002;
Yukl et al. 2005; Gattiker and Carter 2010), other scholars have
reconfigured influence tactics within these categories or devel-
oped alternative category descriptions. For example, whereas
inspirational appeal and consultation are described as soft in-
fluence tactics in previous studies (Kipnis and Schmidt  1982;
Yukl and Falbe 1990), they are categorized by Fu et al. (2004) as
a “persuasive” strategy, which encompasses tactics that focus on
logical arguments and that connect requests to the larger good.
Compared to Yukl and Falbe (1990), who categorize exchange as
a hard tactic, Fu et al. (2004) reposition the exchange influence
tactic as a relationship-­based strategy. Theoretical orientation
and research context have also led scholars to present alterna-
tive descriptions of tactics. For example, based on the gender
roles of organization managers, Smith et al. (2013) categorize
intimidation and legitimating as “agentic tactics” and personal
appeal and ingratiation as “communal tactics.”
In line with previous research, we use the definitions of the indi-
vidual influence tactics described by Gattiker and Carter (2010).
We also retain the distinction between hard and soft influence
tactics presented in the literature (Kipnis and Schmidt  1982;
Deluga 1991; Falbe and Yukl 1992; Farmer et al. 1997; Tepper
et al. 1998; Gattiker and Carter 2010), but depart from previous
research by presenting a second dimension based on the extent
to which requests are justified “rationally” by invoking fact or
logic, as opposed to justified by invoking relationships and/or
normative responsibilities to others.
The motivation behind our revision is to account for the endur-
ing influence of two foundational theories in organizational
studies: rational-­choice institutionalism and social institution-
alism. These theories offer alternative insights into understand-
ing what shapes individual behaviors and decisions within an
organization. They provide a basis from which sets of manage-
rial tactics can be developed to facilitate a target's cooperation.
Incorporating these perspectives provides a nuanced under-
standing of the interplay between cost–benefit calculation and
socially influenced behavior in deciding whether to cooperate or
not within organizational settings.
Rational-­choice institutionalism posits that professionals in or-
ganizations are goal-­oriented actors, with actions predominantly
guided by instrumental rationality (Hall and Taylor 1996). This
perspective is well represented in existing taxonomies, particu-
larly through the discussion of rational types of influential tactics
that emphasize appeals to a target's reasoning and evaluations of
means-­end efficiency. The flexibility of tactics based on rational
influence allows them to be used for many types of objectives.

However, presenting only facts and data, even when done in
a logical manner, may not be sufficient to influence a target
(Gupta and Case 1999; Yukl et al. 2005). Rational persuasion
may have to be coupled with other strategies, such as cultivat-
ing collaborative relationships and encouragement, that tend
to be positively associated with a target's attitude and enthusi-

Yukl 1992; Gupta and Case 1999; Yukl et al. 2005; Sussmann
and Vecchio 2007). Attempts at influencing a target are more
effective when an agent is trusted by the target (Yukl and
Tracey 1992), when the target perceives the agent to be a cred-
ible source of information (Berson and Sosik 2007; Yukl 2012),
and when an agent's requests align with the intrinsic motivation
of the target (Falbe and Yukl 1992; Somech and Drach-­Zahavy
2002; Moss et al. 2005; Yukl et al. 2005; Clarke and Ward 2006).
Reflecting this, Fu et al. (2004) include, in their taxonomy of
influence tactics, a relationship-­based strategy that focuses on
utilizing positive social relationships, such as personal appeals,
to influence target behaviors.
From this perspective, sociological institutionalism posits that
organizational behavior is not solely determined by purposive
calculations but is also socially constructed through a variety
of factors, including norms, symbolic systems, and social cues
derived from interpersonal interactions (Hall and Taylor 1996;
Lounsbury and Ventresca  2003). Relevant to this perspective,
and in the context of intraorganizational influence, are theories
of social dependency and exchange that recognize influence as
a reciprocal process whereby a target's response to an influence
attempt may alter the variety, frequency, and intensity of future
influence attempts by an agent (Somech and Drach-­Zahavy
2002; Sussmann and Vecchio 2007). High-­quality relationships
built on trust and mutual affection are likely to lead a target
to reciprocate by demonstrating a commitment to the requests
made by the agent (Hassan et al. 2014). This notion of social
reciprocity has been offered, for example, as an explanation for
appealing to targets' values, ideals, or aspirations (e.g., ingratia-
tion) to influence their behavior (Vecchio 2007).
The relational orientation of the influence tactics further ac-
counts for an agent's expectations of a target's response to their
request based on their experiences with the target in different
decision contexts and their interactions and exchanges with the
target over time (Stenstrom and Haycock 2015). By developing a
relationship with a target, an agent is better able to understand
their target's relevant predispositions and more accurately pre-
dict how they may react towards a request (Yukl 2012). The social
context of an organization provides guideposts for the agent's se-
lection of an influence strategy that aligns with their established
professional interpersonal relationship with a target (Ferris and
Judge 1991; Ammeter et al. 2002; Treadway et al. 2013). To be
successful, an agent must understand the characteristics of the
social context, along with the situation that necessitates action
be taken, and subsequently translate that knowledge into select-
ing the appropriate influence tactic to motivate change (Ferris
et al. 2007; Yukl 2012). Previous studies of local sustainability
leaders, for example, highlight the extent to which their success
is tied to the relationships that result from the direct interac-
tions that occur across staff with diverse professional foci and
work-­related responsibilities (MacDonald et  al.  2020; Krause
and Hawkins 2021a).
Reflecting these theories, the first dimension of the revised ty-
pology indicates the different degrees of coercion used to influ-
ence action (“coerciveness”). Hard influence tactics aim to make
a target feel compelled to adhere to a request by presenting the
locus of decision-­making as external to the target1, whereas soft
influence tactics seek to persuade compliance while acknowl-
edging the target's authority over the decision. The second
dimension reflects the approach used to justify why a target
should cooperate, and ranges from using objective rational ar-
guments to fundamentally normative and relational ones. This
“orientation” reflects whether an influence tactic is being used
to leverage a target's sense of reason or their normative sensibil-
ities, often by invoking connections to other people.
According to this taxonomy, an agent can use reason to explain
why a target must adhere to a certain directive (hard-­rational).
Alternatively, the agent may leverage their interpersonal rela-
tionship with the target or emphasize the target's responsibilities
to others to underscore the importance of aligning their behav-
iors with directives from the top leadership (hard-­relational).
The agent may use reason to educate a target about the value of
an initiative for which their cooperation is sought (soft-­rational)
or create interpersonal conditions able to advance target behav-
ior, such as cultivating champions in departments to help secure
buy-­in (soft-­relational). While some taxonomies implicitly group
tactics that use relational orientation into the larger category of
soft tactics (Gattiker and Carter 2010), we argue that norms and
relationships can be invoked with varying degrees of coercive-
ness to justify both encouragements and orders. Reflecting these
two dimensions, we place the ten previously identified influence
tactics into four quadrants of a rearranged taxonomy of intraor-
ganizational influence tactics (Table 2).
3   |   Explanations for Use of Influence Tactics in
Local Sustainability
What explains the types of influence tactics that local sustain-
ability professionals use to achieve cooperation from relevant
city units? To address this question, we combine the insights on
influence tactics developed in the business leadership literature
with research on the administrative structures and mechanisms
cities employ to overcome FCA challenges.
Based on a mixed-­methods analysis of U.S. city governments,
Krause and Hawkins  (2021b) identify four key factors that
shape a sustainability leader's ability to advance the cross-­
departmental collective action around the implementation of
sustainability initiatives: (1) the extent of their authority to re-
quire actions of others within the organization; (2) the degree of
mission support received from city leadership; (3) the frequency
of their access to and interactions with department directors;
and (4) the sustainability lead's personal and professional
characteristics.

Administrative structures determine, in part, how city gov-
ernment functions and delivers core services by determining
where departments, divisions, and agencies are located within
an organization and by delineating their formal relationship
with each other. As such, administrative structures may affect
how interactions between functional units occur, including the
means through which the sustainability lead distributes infor-
mation and resources between units and how it approaches
other units to participate in city initiatives. Differences in roles
and responsibilities and the formal authority of a unit relative
to others reflect, to a degree, the positioning or arrangement of
units within the overall design of the organization. In this study,
we operationalize administrative structure as the arrangement
of functional units within a city government. Depending on
the characteristics of the city administrative structure, and in
particular the position of a sustainability lead within it, some
influence tactics may prove more appropriate and/or effective
in getting units to collaborate on implementing sustainability
initiatives.
Most cities maintain a hierarchical structure reflected in stan-
dard organizational charts where the mayor and council sit at
the top, followed by the chief administrative officer, depart-
ment directors, division or office managers, and frontline staff.
Authority flows downward from one's position within the orga-
nization (Rainey 2014). Sustainability units that are structurally
situated nearer the top of this hierarchy, particularly if proximate
to the mayor or top-­level managers, are expected to have greater
formal authority over other units (Krause and Hawkins 2021b).
Related to this, having support from local political leaders is an
established essential ingredient for successful sustainability im-
plementation (Hughes 2015). When sustainability is a priority
of elected officials, “their backing offers a shadow of authority
that enables and enhances the lead unit's ability to coordinate
others” (Krause and Hawkins 2021a, 185).
We hypothesize that the organizational features of sustainabil-
ity functions within a city government and the support they re-
ceive from elected officials are both associated with the degree
of coercion the sustainability agent employs in its interactions
with other units. When formal reporting requirements give sus-
tainability agents direct access to those at the top of the organi-
zational hierarchy, their targets are more likely to be situated
as subordinate units. This, in turn, opens the door to the use
of harder tactics. Additionally, the expressed support of elected
leadership and/or any ordinances they may have passed allows
the agent to be more assertive in its interactions with its tar-
get units.
H1.  Sustainability agents reporting to units near the top of
a city hierarchy are more likely to use hard-­rational and hard-­
relational influence tactics as means to get other units to partici-
pate in initiatives.
H2.  Sustainability agents in cities in which there is more sup-
port from the city council around sustainability are more likely to
use hard-­rational and hard-­relational influence tactics as means
to get other units to participate in initiatives.
Krause and Hawkins  (2021a) highlight the advantages that
sustainability leaders gain from having frequent and regular
interactions with department directors. Frequent interactions
help build trusting relationships and enable parties to better un-
derstand each other's priorities and constraints. It also enables
sustainability leaders to provide information to department
heads on relevant new initiatives in a timely manner. Knowing
decision makers across the city government on a personal level
facilitates the effective use of relational approaches. We thus hy-
pothesize that when a sustainability agent has regular access to
department heads, their use of relational influence tactics will
increase.
H3.  Sustainability agents with more frequent interactions with
department directors are more likely to use hard-­relational and
soft-­relational influence tactics as means to get other units to par-
ticipate in initiatives.
Having a person or unit formally and explicitly charged with
managing the implementation of sustainability in a city's gov-
ernment may increase the use of all types of influence tactics.
Staff in this role have a particular incentive and ability to as-
sess the organizational environment to determine and employ

staff to cultivate personal relationships across the city govern-

4   |   Empirical Analysis of Influence Tactics
In the context of this study, a city's sustainability leader is the
agent who uses influence tactics to shape the relevant behavior
of others. Depending on the city, the sustainability lead may be a
single individual, a small staff, or a distinct unit or department.
The target is any other employee, department, unit, or combina-
tion of units in the same city government whose contributions
are needed to advance comprehensive sustainability efforts. The
agent's aim is to increase cross-­departmental collective action in
their government around sustainability. In this process, they are
likely to deploy a range of influence tactics to motivate the target
to contribute to the organization's overarching sustainability vi-
sion, even if doing so is not a part of the target department's core
mission (Table A1 in Appendix A).
4.1   |   Data Collection
To examine the hypotheses, this study uses a unique dataset
compiled in late 2020 and early 2021 from a nationwide survey
of US local governments. All local governments with a popula-
tion of at least 20,000 (N = 1850 per the 2019 Census estimates)
received a survey that asked a series of questions about how they
organize and implement sustainability-­related efforts. The sur-
vey was first distributed via email and included an invitation to
participate and a link to the survey instrument in Qualtrics. Up
to two reminder emails were sent to non-­respondents. Finally,
paper surveys, with a pre-­stamped return envelope, were sent
via first-­class mail to all recipients who did not respond to the
email invitations. Of the 1850 cities that were surveyed, a total of
591 surveys were returned, representing a 31.9% response rate.2
Because this study focuses on the interpersonal influence tactics
that local sustainability staff employ to gain cross-­departmental
cooperation, our survey respondents themselves are import-
ant. We followed a systematic, multi-­step process to identify
the appropriate survey recipient from each city, for example,
the individual “most responsible” for its sustainability-­related
efforts. This involved going to each city's website and search-
ing the term “sustainability” to locate an explicitly identified
sustainability unit or manager. In the absence of matches, the
search terms “green,” “energy,” “climate protection,” and “envi-
ronment” were used. As available, the description of the units'
service responsibilities and position titles was reviewed, and the
unit's policy documents and/or meeting agendas were used to
confirm the appropriate survey recipient. In cases where there
was limited information available online, calls were made to
identify the appropriate person and obtain their direct contact
information.
4.2   |   Measures
4.2.1   |   Dependent Variables
Dependent variables are drawn from the dimensions outlined
in Table 2, which align soft and hard coerciveness tactics with
relational and rational orientations. Specifically, we employ sur-
vey questions representing the following approaches, each as-
sociated with one of the four quadrants: legitimating, upward
appeal, rational persuasion, and consultation. They are opera-
tionalized with a question asking recipients whether, in the past
year, staff from the lead sustainability unit used the tactic to “get
other units in the city government to actively participate in sus-
tainability initiatives” (see Table 3 for full survey wording). The
use of each of these strategies is measured as a binary variable.
Based on a review of research on intraorganizational influence
tactics, Gattiker and Carter (2010) note most studies examine
a subset of tactics for theoretical reasons and because of meth-
odological constraints. For example, Blickle  (2003) examines
three types of influence tactics, and Hochwarter et al. (2000),
Blickle (2000a, 2000b), Clarke and Ward (2006), and Gattiker
and Carter (2010) examine between four and six tactics in their
studies. Similarly, our selection of four of the ten influence tac-
tics listed in Table  1 is purposeful for two reasons. First, we
considered the degree to which the existing FCA literature sug-
gests the tactic is potentially useful in the context of integration
around sustainability implementation at the local level. In par-
ticular, although they do not refer to them as such, Krause and
Hawkins (2021a) note the use of each of these four influence
tactics in their case study of eight U.S. cities' sustainability im-
plementation efforts. Second, we are motivated by the need to
take a parsimonious approach in examining the range of tactics

4.2.2   |   Predictor Variables
To estimate the likelihood that a sustainability agent uses each
influence tactic in Table 3, we specify multivariate probit regres-
sion models with a set of predictor variables associated with the
previously described hypotheses.
The first factor (hypothesis 1) is indicated by a proxy variable
that accounts for the organizational position of the sustainabil-
ity agent in the city organization hierarchy. More specifically,
this variable indicates the agent's reporting structure relative
to the city's locus of authority. To measure this variable, survey
respondents were asked to identify the unit to which the sus-
tainability agent directly reports. The response categories con-
sisted of division manager, department director, assistant city
manager, city manager/CAO, and city council/mayor. These five
response categories were subsequently coded to form a measure-
ment scale that ranged from 1 (reporting to a unit with the least
authority in the city hierarchy) to 5 (reporting to the unit with
the greatest authority)3.
To account for the second factor (hypothesis 2)—support from
elected city leadership—survey respondents indicated, from
1 (none) to 5 (high), the amount of support sustainability ini-
tiatives received from the city council. Access to department
directors (hypothesis 3) is measured with a question asking re-
spondents to indicate on a scale of 1 (never) to 4 (regularly as
part of a standing committee) how frequently sustainability staff
meet with the directors of other departments in the city govern-
ment. The fourth factor (hypothesis 4)—whether the city has a
unit explicitly charged with managing sustainability implemen-
tation—is provided by survey respondents and is measured as a
binary variable.
In addition to these focal variables, we control for contextual
factors that may influence the use of influence tactics. The
first relates to research that points to the importance of inter-
personal trust in shaping the behaviors of actors in organiza-
tions (Bardach 1998; Yang and Maxwell 2011). Different levels
of trust have been associated with reliance on different mecha-
nisms to facilitate collaboration and increase the likelihood that
partners of a joint endeavor will follow through on their stated
commitments (Whetsell et al. 2021). This variable is measured
by combining the answers to two survey questions that asked
respondents to identify, on a 1 to 4 scale, the extent to which they
perceive representatives from different units trusting one an-
other and fulfilling their commitments to each other (Cronbach
alpha = 0.79).
The analysis also controls for cities' use of a performance mea-
surement system around sustainability. Performance measure-
ment, when well-­designed and implemented, has the potential
to motivate action and change behavior by providing employees
with a rationale for how both their individual efforts (Van der
Hauwaert et al. 2022) and their participation in collaborative en-
deavors relate to successfully achieving the organization's goals
(Bisbe et al. 2019; Park 2021). To measure this variable, survey
respondents indicated whether the city collects performance in-
formation on none, some, most, or all sustainability actions.
Larger organizations tend to be more structurally complex
than smaller ones, with more levels, departments, and job titles
(Kalleberg et al. 1996). As such, they likely have a larger “foot-
print” and a greater number of units requiring integration. The
size of the government organization is proxied by the city popula-
tion. The city's financial capacity is measured by the percentage of
its revenue generated from local sources and whether the city has
a dedicated budget for sustainability initiatives. Finally, the analy-
sis includes whether the city has a stand-­alone sustainability plan.
4.3   |   Method of Analysis
A city's sustainability agent may use multiple tactics described
in Table 3, and as such, their use of one tactic can affect their
subsequent use of others. Reflecting this, we estimate four mul-
tivariate probit regressions, one for each type of influence tac-
tic displayed. Whereas a univariate probit model assumes the
outcome of one equation is not influenced by or related to the
outcome of another equation, the multivariate probit method
simultaneously estimates the outcomes of multiple models with
binary dependent variables. Violating the assumption of indepen-
dence may underestimate the standard errors of the coefficients
and increase the risk of Type 1 errors, for example, incorrectly
rejecting the null, and lead to inaccurate conclusions about the
precision of the point estimates. The multivariate probit method

5   |   Results
5.1   |   Descriptive Results
Of the 591 survey respondents, most (61%) indicated that the sus-
tainability agent in their city has used legitimating as a tactic
to compel target units to act around sustainability. They invoke
formally adopted ordinances, codes, or plans both to increase
the legitimacy of their instruction and to indicate to the target
that cooperation is required. Like legitimating, the next most
frequently used approach—upward appeal—is also a “hard tac-
tic” in that participation is cast as an instruction rather than a
request. However, rather than pointing to formal documents,
when sustainability agents use upward appeals, they justify
their directives by pointing to the preferences of members of top
leadership, giving the tactic a relational orientation. Soft tactics,
which leave the choice of whether to comply or not with the tar-
get's request, are used by sustainability staff in a smaller portion
of cities. Less than half, 46%, of sustainability agents provide
education to city staff about sustainability as a means of persua-
sion, and only 31% utilize consultation as a soft/relational tactic
to motivate participation (Figure 1).
The use of influence tactics is not mutually exclusive, and a sus-
tainability agent may use more than one attempt to motivate a
target to engage in collective action around this goal. Figure 2
shows that sustainability agents in just over 30% of cities used
two influence tactics, whereas only 18% used all four of the
tactics.
5.2   |   Regression Results
Table 4 presents the results of the multivariate probit regression
analysis. We find that the sustainability agents' formal reporting
structure within the city government is significantly associated
with hard-­rational and hard-­relational tactics, as hypothesized
(hypothesis 1). Interestingly, however, the associations are in
opposite directions; it is positively associated with the hard-­
relational influence tactic and negatively associated with the
hard-­rational influence tactic. In other words, when the sus-
tainability agent reports to units higher in the city hierarchy, it
is, contrary to our initial expectation, less likely to utilize legit-
imating, that is, attempting to compel participation by pointing
to formally adopted ordinances, codes, or plans. In comparison,
as expected, an agent reporting to units higher in the organiza-
tional structure has a positive and significant association with
the use of the hard-­relational tactic of upward appeal, that is,
pointing to directives from top leadership.
The results for the second focal variable—city council support—
are largely consistent with hypothesis 2; agents in cities with
strong political support for sustainability are more likely to use
both hard tactics of legitimating and upward appeal. Agents are
also more likely to use consultation tactics to influence targets'
participation when support from elected officials is perceived
as strong. Results partially support hypothesis 3, which pre-
dicts that when sustainability agents have frequent interactions
with department directors, they are more likely to use relational
influence tactics. As seen in Table 4, the results suggest an in-
creased use of hard-­relational tactics (upward appeal), but no
significance is seen in using the soft-­relational tactic of consul-
tation. The fourth hypothesis predicts when agents have been

emphasizing the idea that targets do not have the autonomy to
do otherwise. Pointing to ordinances or other formal city docu-
ments was one way of doing this, while linking directives to the
desires of city leadership was another.

tween the sustainability agents' formal reporting structure
within the city government and the use of legitimating as a

same time, in line with our hypotheses, sustainability agents
are significantly more likely to point to formal documents when
they perceive stronger support from elected officials.
While somewhat unexpected, these findings resonate with re-
cent research indicating that sustainability leaders who oper-
ate within a “shadow of authority” have an advantage in their
efforts to advance intraorganizational integration around sus-
tainability (Krause and Hawkins 2021a; Park et al. 2021). Public
managers can surmount the limitations of their own authority
by strategically operating within and leveraging the influence
of those in positions with greater authority (Scharpf 1994). Our
results suggest that the nature of that shadow affects how agents
operate to get other units to act collectively, particularly when
it comes to the use of the hard-­rational tactic of legitimating.
Sustainability agents that are organizationally closer to top man-
agement, via their reporting structure, benefit from the gravitas
of that proximity. As a result, they may have less of a need to
rely on formal ordinances or rules to establish the legitimacy of
their requests. This is particularly so when fostering collabora-
tion, as the use of formal tactics invoking enforcement may risk
interrupting the process of building trust and mutual respect
that are essential in collaboration (Agranoff and McGuire 2004;
Willem and Buelens 2007). Concomitantly, agents that perceive
more support from their city councils appear more likely to en-
gage legitimating tactics, suggesting that links to formal policy
may leverage leadership support. Alternatively, cities with high
council support for sustainability may also be the ones that have
adopted stronger policies, giving agents more to point to as they
attempt to influence the targets' behavior.
Upward appeal is the other hard tactic examined, but unlike
legitimating, it relies on interpersonal relationships and com-
munication, rather than “rational” tools and “objective” logic.
Upward appeal highlights a call for action from city leadership.
While support from the city council, as predicted in hypothesis
2, has a modestly significant positive association with the use of
this tactic, having more regular access to department heads is
more significant. One possible explanation for this is that when
an agent has regular interactions with department directors,
the agent can more effectively articulate relevant directives and
clarify that what is being asked of the target is consistent with
the expectations of those with greater authority. An alternative
explanation is that regularly meeting with and getting buy-­in
from department directors enables the sustainability agent to
convey to department staff that their director approves of their
involvement in a particular sustainability initiative.
As expected, the presence of an explicit sustainability lead (hy-
pothesis 4), either a person or unit, in charge of coordinating and
managing the city's initiatives is significantly associated with
the increased use of three of the four influence tactics. The one
exception is the use of legitimating, a hard-­rational tactic. When
a city has an explicitly recognized sustainability lead, there
may be less need to rely on formal mechanisms, such as written
codes, ordinances, or plans, to induce collaborative behaviors.
Moreover, because they do not align well with the conceptualiza-
tion of collaborative networks as fluid, open, and often voluntary,
the sustainability agent might eschew the use of hard-­rational
tactics, especially when other tactics are readily available.
Sustainability agents often engage professional organizations
that promote strategies for implementing and managing local
initiatives that correspond to the types of tactics examined here.
For example, the Urban Sustainability Directors Network's high
impact practices include strategies around building “commit-
ment, collaboration, and accountability across local government
departments” and encouraging local leaders to break down pol-
icy silos by incorporating varied disciplines into their work, such
as health and economic development, and thoughtfully engag-
ing their related programs, plans, and processes (USDN 2024).
Such efforts require “boundary spanning” across the organiza-
tion (van Meerkerk and Edelenbos 2014) and a long-­term rela-
tional building approach that integrates factual information and
reason into dialogue. Getting other units to act may require re-
ducing information asymmetries between sustainability science
and other disciplines in the organization and lead sustainability
agents to utilize rational persuasion (i.e., providing education to
city staff) as an influence tactic.
Last, the results associated with the control variables provide in-
sights into the importance of long-­term planning and informa-
tion collection for decision-­making and management purposes.
When a city government regularly measures the performance
of its sustainability efforts, it indicates a dedication to achiev-
ing its sustainability goals, beyond mere symbolic adoption.
Measuring and gathering information on the progress of its ac-
tivities demonstrates a serious commitment to accomplishing
stated sustainability goals. Performance measurement activities
can also indicate the overall capacity of a city government since
the institutionalization of a performance measurement system
entails a substantial investment of resources (Park 2025). The
significant and positive relationship between the use of most
tactics and the presence of a sustainability budget and strategic
plan also points to organizational commitment influencing the
way staff attempts to build collective action around the imple-
mentation of organization-­wide goals.
7   |   Conclusion
Within the realm of public management research, a prevailing
notion underscores the importance of intraorganizational effort
in realizing strategic objectives and overarching organizational
aims. However, the structure of many general-­purpose govern-
ments is at odds with this outcome, and the typical compart-
mentalization of government functions into semi-­autonomous
administrative units does not align with the boundary-­spanning
nature of many contemporary issues. Given the prevalence of
these dynamics, there is a noteworthy absence of research on the
strategies public managers use to achieve functional collective
action around shared organizational goals.
We draw from research on functional collective action (FCA)
and intraorganizational influence theory to examine city sus-
tainability agents' use of different strategies aimed at getting
staff from target units to actively participate in sustainability
initiatives. Building from this literature, we propose a revised
taxonomy of influence tactics and advance functional collective


## Page 12

Public Administration Review, 2026
121
action research by testing hypotheses about the organizational
factors that are associated with their use. The analysis comple-
ments existing qualitative studies by providing generalizable ex-
planations about the use of integration mechanisms.
The results of multivariate probit regression models suggest cit-
ies' organizational structure, their administrative arrangements
for managing sustainability initiatives, the support received from
elected officials, and the regularity of the agents' interactions
with department directors correspond with the use of different
types of influence strategies. Our results indicate that agents
lower in the city hierarchy are more likely to use legitimating as
a tactic than those that are higher up. This suggests that sustain-
ability agents outside the “shadow of authority” are more likely
to rely on codified ordinances and institutionalized procedures
to change the behaviors and actions of other units. Decentralized
implementation creates FCA challenges, particularly for sustain-
ability agents that are farther from points of authority. Pointing
to formal documents and expectations appears to provide a use-
ful strategy for agents facing more difficult FCA dilemmas.
The results also point to the political dynamics around the use
of different tactics; agents increase their use of hard-­rational and
hard-­relational strategies when there is greater support from
elected officials but also seek to influence the actions of others
by cultivating sustainability champions throughout the orga-
nization (soft-­relational tactic). Taken together, the support for
sustainability objectives by the city council is associated with the
use of influence tactics that include elements of both reason and
relationships. The use of more coercive tactics does not preclude
sustainability agents from also engaging targets through personal
connections, as indicated by the positive association between
agents' access to department heads and using upward appeal as
an influence tactic. Meeting with staff in other departments may
provide opportunities for agents to leverage existing relations and
to “soften” directives from top leadership. Finally, when an agent
is formally and explicitly in charge of sustainability objectives in a
city, they are more likely to use multiple types of influence tactics.
Although we do not explore the direct linkage between plan
quality and functional collective action, characteristics of the
formal documents that agents reference in their requests of
the target have the potential to shape outcomes. Pointing to a
formal document, such as a plan or ordinance, to motivate ac-
tion may be successful in so far as that document articulates
objectives, activities, and responsibilities for implementation.
Multiple studies point to a positive association between the qual-
ity of strategic plans and better outcomes, including enhanced
organizational performance (Vandersmissen and George 2024;
George et al. 2019). Clear identification of the functional units
that should be involved in sustainability efforts, or description of
tasks associated with a specific unit, may be particularly import-
ant features of planning documents that are used by agents with
less authority. As our findings indicate, sustainability agents
lower in the city hierarchy in terms of reporting structure are
more likely to rely on legitimating as a tactic, but the ability to
point to more robust city documents may be more effective in
justifying their requests of a target.
An additional implication of this study concerns the positive
association between sustainability leads' access to department
heads and their use of the hard-­relational tactic of upward ap-
peal. This finding points to understanding the purpose and
institutional design of “decision spaces” or venues where staff in-
teractions are likely to occur in city organizations (Hawkins and
Krause 2023). For example, in venues where there is clear polit-
ical or administrative authority, rules may determine potential
participants' access, their roles, and their abilities to make and
shape decisions about issues being debated (Baumgartner and
Jones 1993; Pralle 2010). Sustainability leads' access to depart-
ment heads may also occur through informal venues whereby
exchanges and interpersonal interactions over time generate
trusting and reciprocal relationships that reduce the transac-
tion risk of collaborative activities (Fisher and Leifeld  2015;
Pralle 2010). The characteristics of an interaction space may con-
strain (or facilitate) the extent to which an agent can effectively
engage department heads, or other functional unit leaders, and
thus shape their use of different types of influence tactics be-
yond upward appeal. Understanding whether there is a greater
likelihood of an agent using upward appeal compared to more
rational tactics—whether hard or soft in their coerciveness—de-
pending on the type of venue or other points of access to engage
department directors is a worthy research endeavor.
Altogether, this study provides new evidence about the factors that
shape agents' use of influence tactics in city governments, where
the aim is to engage departments in functional collective action
around an overarching organizational aim. Data limitations, how-
ever, preclude us from identifying how frequently agents utilize
each of the influence tactics we examine, and as a result, we are
unable to apply analytical strategies to explain this type of varia-
tion that may exist. Future research can potentially address this
question. An additional data limitation concerns the professional
characteristics of the sustainability lead. This includes our inabil-
ity, with the existing data, to control for respondents' years of ser-
vice in the city organization or their years of experience working
on sustainability issues at the local government level. The next
step in this line of research is to examine the extent to which the
different types of influence strategies we explore in this study are
effective in getting other units to work collectively around sustain-
ability initiatives. In addition, future research should explore the
implications of employing a greater variety of tactics and deter-
mine whether sustainability leads who utilize all the influence
tactics examined in this study operate under different adminis-
trative structures or decision-­making environments compared to
leads who utilize none or fewer tactics. There is also potential for
examining the selection of strategies based on the targets' respec-
tive roles, functions, and responsibilities and whether they are the
sustainability agent's peers, subordinates, or superiors.

ABSTRACT
Governments face significant challenges in attracting and retaining younger talent, leading to a workforce increasingly skewed
towards older employees. This study examines the impact of public sector fellowship programs as alternative pathways into gov-
ernment roles for early career professionals. Leveraging data from 17 cohorts of applicants across four US fellowship programs
over 19 years (N = 2141; 31,153 individual-­year observations), we employ a staggered difference-­in-­differences approach to com-
pare the career trajectories of fellows and similarly motivated finalists. We find that fellowship participants are 30 percentage
points more likely to pursue government careers, with a significant employment effect persisting up to 8 years after the launch
of the fellowship. These findings suggest that scholarship can look beyond motivation-­based theories of who enters and stays in
government to better understand how to bring new and different people into the public sector.
1   |   Introduction
Governments are struggling to attract and retain younger gen-
erations in public service as the workforce ages, affecting the
stability and capacity of government to address pressing chal-
lenges (Acheampong 2021; Bright and Graham Jr. 2015; Lane
et al. 2003). In the United States, the federal government work-
force has seven times more employees over 50 than under 30,
with one-­third eligible to retire soon, yet younger employees are
not filling these roles quickly enough (Partnership for Public
Service 2020). This trend is also observed among public admin-
istration graduates and others who may have viewed govern-
ment as their primary career pathway, but are now more likely
to opt for careers in the nonprofit or private sector (Bright and
Graham Jr. 2015; Chetkovich 2003; Fowler and Birdsall 2020;
Rose 2013).
The question, then, of how to recruit and retain talent in gov-
ernment is both practically and academically important. Extant
literature often centers around motivation-­based theories, and
in particular public service motivation, which suggests that
individuals with high public service motivation are drawn to
government roles due to their prosocial drive to contribute to so-
cietal well-­being (Perry and Wise 1990; Perry et al. 2010). The de-
bate surrounding recruitment strategies often examines whether
employee behavior is predominantly influenced by intrinsic
motivations or extrinsic rewards and how these dynamics dif-
fer between the public and private sectors (Feeney 2008; French
and Emerson 2014; Houston 2000; Kjeldsen and Jacobsen 2013;
Lewis and Frank 2002; Lyons et al. 2006; Park and Rainey 2008;
Steijn 2008; Vandenabeele 2008; Wright 2007). In line with this
approach, empirical evidence on how to improve public sector
recruitment also focuses on addressing different types of mo-
tivation, highlighting distinct employer value propositions or
shaping perceptions of candidates' fit with the job or organiza-
tion, in an effort to motivate individuals to apply (Asseburg and
Homberg 2020; Keppeler and Papenfuß 2021; Linos 2018).
In this article, we argue that while motivation in general,
and public service motivation in particular, may influence
job choices (Holt  2018), a more comprehensive understand-
ing of how to attract and keep talent in government must

consider other factors that affect career trajectories (Gabris and
Simo 1995). On the recruitment side, we argue that career out-
comes are not solely dependent on encouraging more people to
want a government job, but also depend on those individuals
navigating the process of getting a job. In the public sector, this
process may include the administrative burdens of applying for
a government job, the opaque nature of recruitment and selec-
tion in parts of government, as well as the difficulties of being
placed in a government position that allows for career mobility
when the candidate holds little to no experience (e.g., Linos and
Riesch 2020; Sievert et al. 2022). For instance, hiring in the pub-
lic sector takes an average of 98 days, more than twice as long as
the typical hiring process in the private sector (Partnership for
Public Service 2020). This extended timeline is compounded by
associated challenges such as unclear and complex civil service
exam requirements, long waits for exam access, and security
clearances (U.S. Merit Systems Protection Board 2006; Yeager
and Krasna 2024). These cumbersome processes challenge the
government's ability not only to attract talent, but also to moti-
vate candidates to persist through the selection process. On the
retention side, we consider the role that initial job choices play
in shaping future career paths. Previous studies argue that occu-
pational trajectories are influenced by earlier vocational choices
(Dlouhy and Biemann  2018; Miller  1984; Sydow et  al.  2009;
Rothstein 2023). Working in a particular occupation contributes
to the development of occupation-­specific human capital, which
makes transitioning to a different occupation costly (Cho and
Lewis 2012). It may also create path dependencies due to profes-
sional networks, status quo bias, organizational commitment, or
other factors that lead initial job placements to impact long-­term
career choices.
Public sector fellowship programs have emerged as an alterna-
tive pathway into government roles for early-­career profession-
als, bridging the gap between education and work experience
and creating a standardized application and selection process
for those who want to enter government with little to no expe-
rience. In so doing, they may reduce administrative hurdles to
entering government by creating a “fast track” alternative to tra-
ditional recruitment strategies, but may also affect retention in
government if they attract employees at early stages of their ca-
reer that produce path-­dependent job choices in the future. The
United States is home to a vast number of public sector fellow-
ship programs. While they share similar objectives—to bring
specialized talent into government—they can vary in terms of
size, target audience, geographic area, or policy domain. For
instance, the Presidential Management Fellows Program, the
largest and oldest of its kind, places around 400 fellows annually
(Corrin et al. 2024), whereas the Presidential Innovation Fellows
program admits between 20 and 35 (Presidential Innovation
Fellows, n.d.). Similar programs have emerged at the state and
local level and match individuals with state or local government
agencies based on their skills and career interests across a range
of agencies and roles, while others might specialize in areas such
as STEM or legal policy. While certain fellowships target mid-­
career professionals, almost all are designed for individuals who
have little to no previous experience in the public sector, and
many specifically target early career professionals. During the
program, fellows work in government positions alongside career
civil servants for one-­ to two-­year appointments and, depending
on the fellowship, may also be directly hired by the government
agency, simplifying post-­fellowship retention efforts. To our
knowledge, there is limited rigorous evidence on the impact of
such programs on career outcomes. As such, our study asks: do
public sector fellowship programs increase the likelihood of a
career in government among similarly motivated individuals?
Drawing on data from four US-­based public sector fellowship
programs and following 17 cohorts for up to 19 years (N = 2141;
31,153 individual-­year observations), this study uses a staggered
difference-­in-­differences approach to estimate the effects of fel-
lowship participation on short-­and long-­term career trajectories
of fellows, compared to similarly motivated finalists. Across
different model specifications, we find that fellows are 30 per-
centage points more likely than finalists to work in government
post-­fellowship. This effect persists over time, with a statistically
higher share of fellows remaining in government roles up to
8 years after beginning a fellowship.
Our study holds significant implications for practitioners and
scholars alike. First, the findings point to a need for more re-
search on the non-­motivational factors that impact public sector
recruitment and retention, contributing to existing theories of
administrative burden in the context of government hiring while
also expanding our knowledge on what works in building career
pathways for aspiring public servants. Second, it is the largest
study to date on the impact of public sector fellowship programs,
contributing directly to a broader conversation on the impact
of national service programs, fellowship initiatives, and other
bridge programs between training and employment (Dobbie
and Fryer Jr. 2011; Frumkin et al. 2009; Mo and Conn 2018; Mo
et al. 2019). This is especially important given the scale of public
and private investments in these initiatives. Last, the study adds
to the broader literature on how early career experiences affect
long-­term career outcomes (Arellano-­Bover 2024; Dlouhy and
Biemann 2018; Margolis et al. 1999; Miller 1984; Rothstein 2023;
Sydow et al. 2009), making the case for how public sector fellow-
ship programs, much like other forms of work experience, play
a crucial role in building human capital, fostering an environ-
ment conducive to path dependence, and motivating individuals
to continue pursuing government careers in their subsequent
professional endeavors.

trust in institutions in the United States continue to fall, with
only 22% of Americans trusting the government to do what
is right most or all of the time (Pew Research Center 2024),
but Gen Z—the generation that would need to fill positions
left by the silver tsunami of turnover in government—remains
underrepresented in government jobs. Gen Z comprises 18%
of the US workforce (Page et al. 2024), but only 1.6% of the
federal workforce (Hyman et al. 2022). This need not be the
case. Gen Z stands out for its belief in the government's role
in problem-­solving: 70% think the government should take
greater responsibility for addressing societal issues, com-
pared to 64% of Millennials and 49% of Gen Xers (Parker
and Igielnik 2020). As such, it may not be the “value propo-
sition” of government that is keeping this generation out of
government, in the way that has been documented for previ-
ous generations of public administration graduate students
(Adams 2000; Chetkovich 2003; Rose 2013).
Extant literature points to the critical role of motivation-­
related factors in defining job choice, including public ser-
vice motivation, person-­organization fit, and person-­job fit
(Chapman et al. 2005; Feeney 2008; Houston 2000; Kjeldsen
and Jacobsen 2013; Lyons et al. 2006; Perry and Wise 1990;
Perry et al. 2010; Steijn 2008; Vandenabeele 2008). In turn, a
growing number of studies aim to induce more applications
by focusing on light-­touch interventions that may shift moti-
vation or perceptions of fit, with mixed success and relatively
modest effects (e.g., Linos 2018; Keppeler and Papenfuß 2021).
Yet, career decisions are influenced by a host of other factors
related to the process of getting a job (Uggerslev et al. 2012).
Social ties, for instance, play a crucial role in workers' labor
market entry (Kramarz and Skans 2014), suggesting that ei-
ther information about a specific sector or easy entry into an
occupation can impact job choice. Indeed, the availability of
job-­related information further impacts sector choice. For
instance, Lee and Jilke (2023) show that individuals become
less sensitive to whether a job is in the public or private sec-
tor when more information is provided. Other studies show
that complex job advertisements, including extensive lists of
requirements, discourage applications (Sievert et  al.  2022).
Even beyond the initial application, onerous selection pro-
cesses may lead to higher drop-­off in selection processes. In
line with this, Linos and Riesch (2020) find that reducing fric-
tion costs—such as simplifying forms or application instruc-
tions—significantly enhances the likelihood of retention in a
selection process.
This study conceptualizes public sector fellowship programs
as a higher-­touch recruitment intervention that can not only
simplify the process of getting a job in government—by pro-
viding a clear selection pathway and information about gov-
ernment positions, thus reducing uncertainty—but that could
also provide employees with the information, social ties, and
experience that contribute to greater retention in government
over time. The recruitment and selection process of fellow-
ship programs is often distinct from hiring directly into the
agencies in which fellows work. In fellowship programs, can-
didates generally apply for participation in the fellowship the
year before, and rather than applying for a specific position,
they submit a broad application for a range of potential roles.
They outline their skillset and interests, and, if they advance
in the selection process, they are matched with an agency or
role that aligns with their profile. By structuring selection in
this way, fellowship programs may reduce some of the hurdles
related to recruitment and selection into government, such
as uncertainty about qualifications, a lack of relevant con-
nections, and unfamiliarity with the application process. As
such, they can play a critical role in bridging the gap between
academic education and the professional work environment,
providing a “fast track” into government through an applica-
tion and selection process that is at least clearer, if not simpler,
than traditional HR practices. At the same time, given that
many of these programs are also highly competitive, partici-
pation through a pipeline program of this kind may also offer
a signal of competence to future employers.
Importantly, fellowship programs often provide additional sup-
port for fellows, including mentorship, leadership or other skills
training activities, and opportunities for networking and collab-
oration with peers, all of which contribute to their professional
and personal development. Employees at the lower levels of work
experience (GS 1–4) are the most difficult to retain in govern-
ment roles, with their turnover rate more than double the overall
average across government roles (14.5% vs. 6.1%) (Partnership
for Public Service 2022), suggesting that the first few years of
service may be critically important to overall workforce reten-
tion. By facilitating skill development, expanding networks,
and providing meaningful work experiences—often for one to
2 years—fellowships can help mitigate barriers to pursuing fu-
ture jobs in the public sector. Additionally, there is path depen-
dence stemming from initial job choices, built upon networks
established, credentials obtained, or a sense of belonging created
around certain types of organizations, all of which can influ-
ence long-­term career trajectories (Dlouhy and Biemann 2018;
Rothstein 2023; Sydow et al. 2009). Evidence suggests that sec-
tor preferences tend to remain stable over time, in contrast to
job roles or employers (Bright and Graham Jr. 2015; Tschirhart
et al. 2008). As a result, prior experience in the public sector may
be associated with a greater likelihood of pursuing work in that
sector.
Thus, for all three reasons—easing recruitment and selection
barriers, providing retention-­oriented supports, and path de-
pendence—we argue that public sector fellowships can promote
further employment in the public sector.
Some programs monitor and report on the percentage of
fellows who work in government post-­fellowship. The
Presidential Management Fellows Program reports that 83%
of its fellows secure and accept a position in government
immediately post-­fellowship (Corrin et  al.  2024), while ap-
proximately 40%–50% of fellows in the AAAS Science &
Technology Policy Fellowships continue to work in a policy-­
related field in the immediate year after the fellowship (AAAS
Science and Technology Policy Fellowships, n.d.). Estimates
from other programs examining government employment ei-
ther 2 years or 2 jobs after the fellowship show more modest
effects: 16% of Code for America fellows, 20% of Presidential
Innovation Fellows, and 16% of Fuse Corps fellows ultimately
occupy government roles (Schacht 2024). These reports, while
promising, present some empirical challenges. First, some of
these studies do not track applicants long enough to look at

overall impact of these programs is on careers, rather than

where responses may be skewed towards those who end up
staying in government. Last and most importantly, in existing
reports, the counterfactual is unclear. What employment out-
comes would these fellows have, in the absence of participat-
ing in these programs? If motivation is the critical barrier for
entry into the public sector, then even in the absence of such
programs, highly motivated individuals who are interested in
government jobs would use other pathways into government.
If, on the other hand, motivation is only one component of job
selection, and fellowship programs close some of the other
barriers related to getting and keeping a job in government,
then we should expect a higher level of employment in the
public sector post-­fellowship, compared to individuals who
are equally motivated at baseline to get a job in government.
We hypothesize that this is the case. More formally, we hy-
pothesize that:
Hypothesis 1.  Participation in a public sector fellowship pro-
gram increases the likelihood of individuals being employed in the
public sector after completing the program, compared to similarly
motivated applicants.
The most relevant available evidence regarding the impact of
public sector fellowship programs comes from studies of service-­
learning and national service programs, such as AmeriCorps
or Teach For America, which provide individuals with oppor-
tunities to participate in community service through volunteer
or paid roles. Like public sector fellowship programs, these
programs offer an alternative recruitment pathway into the
profession by offering early career experiences that allow skill-­
building opportunities and the chance to build initial social cap-
ital. However, while both national service programs and public
service fellowships seek to cultivate a spirit of service and con-
tribute to societal needs, their stated purpose is to foster civic
engagement and a sense of social responsibility across diverse
career paths, as opposed to directly affecting job choice. As a re-
sult, while the findings from service-­learning and national ser-
vice programs can offer insights into how such programs might
influence participants, they are less informative on how they
shape career trajectories. Research indicates that participation
in these initiatives enhances civic awareness (Lee et al. 2007;
Seider et al. 2012), civic engagement (Frumkin et al. 2009; Mo
et  al.  2019) and political ambition (Mo et  al.  2019). Evidence
suggests they also improve students' racial tolerance (Mo and
Conn 2018; Morgan and Streb 2001), and shift beliefs towards
viewing poverty as a systemic issue rather than an individual
failing (Mo and Conn 2018), reinforcing the idea that disadvan-
taged children can achieve parity with their more advantaged
peers (Dobbie and Fryer Jr. 2011). There is limited evidence on
the impact of national service and service-­learning programs on
employment outcomes. A notable exception is Dobbie and Fryer
Jr. (2011) who found that Teach For America fellows are more
likely to pursue careers in education than otherwise, although a
series of studies note that teacher turnover is higher for teachers
who entered through alternative pathways into teaching, like
Teach For America, compared to traditional routes (Ronfeldt
et al. 2013). In this study, we primarily aim to answer how pub-
lic sector fellowship programs affect fellows' career trajectories,
offering a deeper exploration of this understudied area within
public administration.
3   |   Methods
We follow 17 cohorts of individuals (N = 2141) who applied be-
tween 2016 and 2021 to at least one of the following four US
fellowship organizations: Foster America, Govern For America,
Presidential Management Fellows Program, or Public Rights
Project. For further details on each organization, refer to
Appendix A.
We track each individual's employment history up to 10 years
prior to the program start and up to 8 years after its launch to
examine how fellowship participation influences employment
over time for fellows compared to similarly motivated finalists.
We define “similarly motivated finalists” based on the group
that is the closest in recruitment stage to the group that received
a fellowship offer. For Foster America, Govern For America and
Public Rights Project, fellows are defined as individuals who
were offered the fellowship, regardless of whether they partic-
ipated. This includes those who declined the offer, those who
started but did not complete the fellowship program, and those
who fully completed it. Finalists are defined as candidates who
advanced to the final stage of the selection process but were not
offered a fellowship. The largest organization in our sample, the
Presidential Management Fellows Program, uses a slightly dif-
ferent definition. In this program, all finalists are placed on a list
that spurs a matching process with agencies seeking to fill posi-
tions. For the purposes of the study, fellows from this program
are defined as individuals selected to participate in the program
who have secured a position at a federal agency. Finalists are
applicants who were equally successful in the application and
selection process, and were deemed eligible for a fellowship by
the selection committee, but did not match with a specific fed-
eral agency role.
This comparison leaves open the possibility that finalists could
differ from fellows in key unobservable or observable but un-
measured ways. To address this potential limitation, our pri-
mary analysis employs a staggered difference-­in-­differences
approach that relies on the assumption that finalists and fellows
have similar career trends rather than similar characteristics.
We provide evidence that, conditional on observables, finalists
and fellows indeed follow similar trends in employment out-
comes prior to the fellowship period. As a secondary analysis,
we use a linear probability model to offer a simpler and more
intuitive perspective on the overall effects of fellowship partici-
pation on employment during the post-­fellowship period. Using
a linear probability model also allows us to assess the robustness
and consistency of our results across different model specifica-
tions. Below we provide more details on the exact specifications
and robustness checks we use.

Of the 2750 finalists and fellows from past cohorts, we are able
to match employment and education records for 2141 individuals
(78%). Compared to finalists, fellows are slightly (but not signifi-
cantly) less likely to have a public profile (77% vs. 79%). For those
who are matched, we categorize individuals' jobs by work sector
(e.g., public sector, private sector and non-­profit sector) and by nar-
rower industry/policy area (e.g., government; educational services;
professional, scientific, and technical services, etc.). We define
“government” as any position within a government department
or agency at the federal, state, or local level, while “public sector”
also includes jobs in publicly financed institutions, such as public
schools, state universities, and public hospitals. Jobs included in
the sample have a minimum duration of 4 months, and similarly, a
job is counted as a post-­fellowship job if it lasts for at least 4 months
after the end of the fellowship period, regardless of when it began.
We then construct a panel dataset that summarizes individuals'
employment outcomes at the calendar-­year level. For each indi-
vidual, the dataset may thus include data for the years spanning
before, during, or after the fellowship period, depending on the
availability of education and job records. For each individual, the
year in which the fellowship started (or would have started, in the
case of finalists) is considered the cohort year. We limit the sam-
ple to the 10 years prior to the cohort year and up to 8 years after
the cohort year, resulting in a dataset of 31,153 individual-­year
observations. This dataset is used for the staggered difference-­in-­
differences estimations that constitute our primary analysis. Using
the start and end dates of the fellowship for each organization and
cohort, we additionally construct an individual-­level dataset that
includes fixed demographic characteristics as well as aggregated
pre-­ and post-­fellowship data. This dataset includes 2141 individu-
als and is used to estimate linear probability models that constitute
our secondary analysis. See Appendix B for more details on sample
and outcome construction.

5   |   Empirical Specification
We use a series of empirical specifications and robustness checks
to estimate and triangulate our findings.
Our preferred specification applies a staggered difference-­
in-­differences approach to estimate the effects of fellowship
participation on employment outcomes over time. Since indi-
viduals in our data are part of distinct treatment cohorts, each
spanning different years, the commonly used two-­way fixed
effects model, which assumes homogeneous treatment effects,
is not a good fit for our context, as it tends to give more weight
to earlier cohorts, resulting in biased estimates (Callaway and
Sant'Anna 2021; de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille 2020; Sun
and Abraham 2021). Therefore, we follow the method proposed
by Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) to estimate our average treat-
ment effect, which involves estimating a series of group-­time
effects—or average treatment effects on the treated ATT(g,t)—
each estimated using methods analogous to the traditional 2 × 2
difference-­in-­differences approach.
In our study, we use the outcome regression approach described
in Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021), and adapted from Heckman
et al. (1997). For any given cohort and time period, we are inter-
ested in the ATT(g,t) parameter:
where g indicates the cohort year, that is, the year in which an
individual (would have) entered the fellowship program, and t
represents each time period at which we observe the individual
pre-­ and post-­cohort year. We estimate ATT(g,t) parameters by
first linearly modeling the evolution of outcomes in the group of
individuals who never enter the fellowship, using these models
to estimate the evolution of outcomes for those who do partici-
pate in the fellowship had they not participated, and comparing

Most of the fellowship programs in our sample last for two years,
but the Presidential Management Fellows Program provides an
additional year for finalists to match with a federal agency. During
this extra year, some individuals match at the beginning, while
others match later. Therefore, we conservatively consider the first
post-­fellowship year to be e = 4, as this is the earliest period when
we can be confident that all individuals in the sample (would)
have completed the fellowship program. While it is reasonable to
assume that fellows will hold a government position during the
fellowship period (e = 0 to e = 3), our primary interest lies in evalu-
ating the likelihood that they will remain in government employ-
ment in the post-­fellowship period (e = 4 and beyond).
Our estimation method relies on a parallel trends assumption
based on a never treated group: conditional on observable co-
variates, we assume that the outcomes over time for fellows
would have followed the same trajectory as those for finalists
had the fellows not participated in the fellowship. We provide
evidence in the Results section, both visually through plots and
formally through statistical tests, that suggest this assumption
is satisfied.

7   |   Results
7.1   |   Effects on the Likelihood of Being Employed
in Government
Figure 1 shows the main results of the study. In line with our
hypotheses, participating in a fellowship program significantly
increases the likelihood of working in government by 30 per-
centage points in the first year post-­fellowship (Table  A3 in
Appendix C). When expanding to all public sector jobs, which
include all government jobs as well as jobs in publicly funded
institutions like public schools, state universities, or public hos-
pitals, the effect is mitigated slightly but remains very large:
24 percentage points in the first year post-­fellowship. This gap
reduces over time but remains significantly positive for at least
8 years after the start of the fellowship, for those we can track
for our full observation window. Importantly, this effect is not
driven by an overall change in the likelihood of having a job per
se. Moreover, while we note some immediate substitution into
education, the magnitude of the effect on education is minimal
compared to the overall effect on public sector jobs.
Figure 1 provides visual evidence that the conditional parallel
trends assumption is satisfied for each of the main outcomes.
In addition, we conduct formal pre-­trend tests of the hypoth-
esis that all pre-­treatment effects are zero. Joint F tests fail to
reject the null hypotheses of parallel pre-­trends for our models
of public sector employment, overall employment, and grad-
uate education for the full 10-­year period prior to the cohort
year (p = 0.29, p = 0.12, p = 0.35, respectively). We likewise
fail to reject the null hypothesis of parallel pre-­trends for our
model of government employment for the 4-­year period imme-
diately prior to the fellowship (p = 0.38) but note that fellows
are marginally less likely to hold a government job 5–6 years
before the cohort year (relative to the year before the cohort
year). Further analysis suggests that this dip may be due to
imputed zeros for fellows who are slightly less likely to report
any job in those years. The imputation itself does not change
any of our main results, and we present the complete stag-
gered difference-­in-­differences results for these models with
and without imputation in Appendix C.

7.2   |   Where Do Finalists Go?
To descriptively explore what the counterfactual is for fellows,
we examine the employment patterns for fellows and finalists
in various job industries. Notably, it is still the case that the
modal finalist works in government at some point during the
study period—about 65% hold a government job at some point
in the 8 years following the cohort year. However, Figure  2,
which presents results from analyses of employment trends
post-­fellowship across selected industries for fellows compared
to finalists, indicates that finalists spread out across multiple
industries outside of government to a larger extent than fellows.
For instance, finalists are 12 percentage points, on average,
more likely to work in education/academia (e.g., state or private
universities) in the post-­fellowship period relative to fellows, a
gap that does not shrink even 8 years after the start of the fel-
lowship program. On average, finalists are also 6 percentage
points more likely to work in professional, scientific, and tech-
nical services (e.g., management consulting, legal services, etc.)
and 7 percentage points more likely to work in other private
sector firms (e.g., software companies, banks, etc.) in the year
immediately following (what would have been) their fellowship
completion year (Table A6 in Appendix C). However, this ef-
fect fades away in both industries over the following 2–3 years.
And while it appears that finalists may initially work for civil
society organizations (e.g., charitable foundations, advocacy
organizations) at a greater rate than fellows during the fellow-
ship period, this gap quickly disappears and there are no differ-
ences in civil society employment by the time all fellows have
completed the fellowship. Appendix  C displays the complete
staggered difference-­in-­differences results for these models.

linear probability model specification, with alternative conser-

25% of missing fellows and 100% of missing finalists hold jobs
in government in the post-­fellowship period, our main effect is
a 5-­percentage point increase in public sector employment and
remains statistically significant.
Another way our findings could be influenced by data quality
issues relates to the classification of individuals as fellows or
finalists. For the Presidential Management Fellows Program,
we collect this information using the Wayback Machine, which
identifies finalists who were matched as fellows on a given date.
For two cohorts, our data suggests significantly fewer fellows
compared to other years. In our main analysis, we adopt a con-
servative approach by keeping these cohorts. Excluding these
cohorts from the analysis does not alter the direction or signif-
icance of the effect, and its magnitude remains largely consis-
tent. In the full sample, fellowship participation is linked to a 27
percentage point increase in the likelihood of working in gov-
ernment post-­fellowship (Table 2). When these two cohorts are
excluded, the effect rises slightly to 30 percentage points in our
linear probability models (Table A10 in Appendix D). Looking
specifically at the year immediately after fellowship completion
in the event study estimates of our staggered difference-­in-­
differences models, we likewise see that fellows in the full sam-

(Table A3 in Appendix C), compared to 33 percentage points
when excluding cohorts subject to data quality issues (Table A9
in Appendix D). This suggests that our full sample estimate
leans towards being conservative, as these cohorts likely in-
clude some fellows mistakenly classified as finalists.
9   |   Discussion
The human capital crisis in government has largely been con-
ceptualized in the public management literature as a crisis of
motivation. This study offers a new perspective by asking what
drives career trajectories in government among similarly moti-
vated individuals. Following 2141 individuals over 19 years, we
find that fellowship programs that target talented individuals
to serve in government over 1–2 years significantly increase the
likelihood of staying in government for up to 8 years. This does
not seem to be driven by their impact on the likelihood of hold-
ing any job, nor a substitution away from education. Rather,
evidence suggests these fellowships seem to be leading fellows
to substitute into government away from careers in education/
academia, professional services, and the private sector.

This study contributes to three main areas of research. First, it
advances an emerging strand of public administration literature
that extends beyond the focus of public service motivation as a
primary driver of public sector careers (e.g., Linos 2018; Sievert
et al. 2022), emphasizing that non-­motivational recruitment and
retention factors can influence career trajectories in govern-
ment for equally motivated individuals. Second, it contributes to
theories of administrative burden (Moynihan et al. 2015), sup-
porting arguments that removing uncertainty and complexity
in recruitment processes in government hiring can lower barri-
ers to public sector employment. Finally, it adds to the economic
literature on path dependence in career development (Arellano-­
Bover 2024; Dlouhy and Biemann 2018; Rothstein 2023; Sydow
et  al.  2009) by studying how early-­career employment influ-
ences both short-­ and long-­term sector choices.
These contributions notwithstanding, the study also has multi-
ple limitations. First, the study relies on the assumption that fi-
nalists and fellows are comparable—specifically, that finalists
are similarly qualified and motivated as fellows, with the only
difference being whether they were offered a fellowship. While
we address these concerns by examining parallel trends in
government employment before the start of the fellowship pro-
gram, it remains possible that some unmeasured observable
or unobservable characteristic could influence the observed
differences. For example, there may be ex-­ante differences in
motivation between finalists and fellows in their interest in
government. Given the size of the effects and the nature of the
selection process where we are comparing fellows to those who
have persisted through a competitive process and made it to
the final round, it is unlikely that a difference in underlying
motivation would explain these effects fully.
Second, while we find suggestive evidence that path depen-
dence plays some role in determining career trajectories, our
data cannot provide a detailed understanding of the underlying
mechanisms at play. That is, we cannot disentangle whether
this persistence in government employment relates fully to sta-
tus quo bias, a time-­varying change in motivation related to
more experience and information about government, organiza-
tional identification with the given government agency, or the
development of agency-­specific human capital that increases
the cost of finding another job. Future studies could aim to dis-
entangle the underlying mechanisms of these large effects.
Last, the nature of this analysis explores career effects relative
to an existing recruitment and selection process in government.
If traditional pathways into government become easier or more
streamlined, it is possible that the additional recruitment and
retention value of a government fellowship program will be mit-
igated. Future research could explore the impact of fellowship
programs in countries where traditional pathways into govern-
ment are more streamlined than in the United States.
10   |   Conclusion
The evidence presented in this article collectively suggests that
public sector fellowships successfully improve both recruitment
and retention of talented early-­career professionals in public sec-
tor careers.
While the effects of fellowships are straightforward, the impli-
cations for policy are, of course, more nuanced. Even if govern-
ment fellowship programs quadrupled in size and scope, they
would still be a drop in the ocean compared to the size of the
government workforce. As such, while one potential policy pre-
scriptive of this work is to expand fellowship programs, a broader
policy implication of these findings is that adjusting the tradi-
tional pathways into government in line with the approaches
used by fellowship programs could increase recruitment and
retention in government. This could include expanding cohort-­
based hiring and skills-­based selection, adjusting onboarding
and early-­years training to include guidance on navigating pub-
lic sector jobs, or offering early support to facilitate integration
into the government workforce. We leave it to future research
to evaluate whether this is, in fact, true.
We also leave to future research critical questions about the rel-
ative effectiveness of fellows who stay in government, compared
to civil servants in similar roles who enter government through
more traditional pathways. This could be studied indirectly by
measuring how mindsets differ over time between these groups,
as well as compared to finalists. It could also be studied by
measuring how other civil servants perceive the effectiveness
of their colleagues by fellowship status. Other policy-­relevant
questions for further exploration include whether retention in
government is inherently a positive outcome or merely reflects
the high costs faced by individuals who began their careers in
the public sector when considering a transition to other sectors.
Ultimately, this study provides important insights for public
human resource management, especially at a time when at-
tracting talent has become increasingly difficult. While aspiring
public servants are often viewed as motivated individuals eager
to serve, the impact of that motivation will not be fully realized
if the current recruitment strategies do not succeed in helping
them “get their foot in the door.”

Appendix A
Description of Fellowship Organizations
This study examines the career trajectories of individuals who applied
to four US fellowship programs: Foster America, Govern For America,
Presidential Management Fellows Program and Public Rights Project.
In the tables below, we summarize key information about these fellow-
ship programs (Tables A1 and A2).
While each organization has its own recruitment process, they gen-
erally follow similar stages. The process begins with a written appli-
cation reviewed by a designated selection board. Depending on the
organization, this may be followed by group and individual inter-
views. Applicants who advance through these stages are invited to
match with a government partner. However, not all invited applicants
ultimately secure a placement and become fellows, either due to a lack
of fit, their removal from consideration, or other reasons. In the case
of the Presidential Management Fellows Program, participating fed-
eral agencies have up to 12 months from the finalist announcement
to hire them.
All organizations offer some combination of skill-­based trainings, pro-
fessional development opportunities and access to a network of profes-
sional peers during the fellowship.

B.2.1. Initial Exclusion Criteria
We apply the following exclusion criteria to the initial set of administra-
tive applicant data:
1.	 We exclude all fellowship program applicants who did not ad-
vance to the finalist stage, that is, those who were screened out of
the selection process prior to a final interview or selection stage.
2.	 We exclude individuals from the current cohort, that is, individuals
whose fellowship was not yet completed as of December 2024, as
these individuals lack outcome data for the post-­fellowship period.
3.	 We exclude individuals if the specific fellowship program to
which they applied does not involve a placement within a govern-
ment agency.
B.2.2. Fellowship Status
Across all four fellowship programs, we occasionally observe individ-
uals who applied more than once. In such cases, we consider an indi-
vidual a fellow if, in any of these applications, they were selected as a
fellow, and we assign them to the cohort year associated with their suc-
cessful application. Similarly, we consider an individual a finalist if, in
any of these applications, they reached the latest stage of the selection
process, and we assign them to the cohort year associated with their
advancement to the finalist stage. Individuals who have both finalist
and fellow applications are categorized as fellows.
B.2.3. Cohort Start and End Dates
Our primary analyses following Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) consider
data at the calendar-­year level, whereby year 0 corresponds to the year
in which an individual (would have) started the fellowship program, re-
gardless of the exact date in that year that the official program (would
have) started. In comparison, secondary analyses using linear probabil-
ity models rely on the distinction between the pre-­ and post-­fellowship
periods. These periods are defined using the specific start and end dates
associated with each fellowship program cohort, which may vary by orga-
nization and by year. In the case of the Presidential Management Fellows
Program—in which selected finalists have a one-­year period to match
with a federal agency and effectively commence their two-­year fellowship
(as described in Appendix A)—cohort start and end dates are defined
based on the minimum fellowship program start date and the maximum
(implied) program end date present in the data. Conservatively, we define
the first year post-­fellowship as 4 years after the cohort year (i.e., e = 4).
B.2.4. Procedure for Matching Fellows and Finalists to
Professional Profile Data
After the initial exclusion criteria are applied, fellowship program fel-
lows and finalists are matched to individual professional profile data
included in the Revelio Labs dataset. The administrative data fields
available in each fellowship program determine what information
can be used in identifying possible matches from the universe formed
above. Generally speaking, possible indicators of a match are based
on:
•	 Individual first and last name
•	 Educational background, including undergraduate college and
graduate school
•	 Employment background, including prior employment
•	 Location data, for example, individuals' hometown or state based
on their application
Using these data, we identify a pool of potential matches. This pool is then
filtered to include those individuals who are designated the best match
for an individual based on the similarity of the administrative data to the
profile data. In the case of multiple potential matches, a manual review
of publicly available profiles is used to discern the true match when pos-
sible. In cases where we are unable to confidently select one of many po-
tential profiles, we exclude the individual from the matched sample used
in analyses.
B.3. Constructing Model Covariates and Outcomes
Covariates and outcome variables used in primary and secondary anal-
yses are constructed based on available administrative data and pub-
licly available profile data obtained from matched Revelio Labs profiles.
B.3.1. Predicted Gender and Race/Ethnicity
Administrative data for Foster America, Govern For America, and Public
Rights Project include individual applicants' gender and race/ethnicity,
with race/ethnicity missing for 15% of individuals and gender missing for
28%. However, this data is entirely missing and not publicly available for
the Presidential Management Fellows Program applicants. In cases where
data is missing for any of the four organizations, we rely on values from
two sources: (1) Revelio Labs data, which includes predicted gender and
race/ethnicity based on individual first and last names using a proprietary
model; (2) R's predictrace package (Kaplan 2023), which likewise uses in-
dividuals' first and last names to predict gender and race/ethnicity, draw-
ing on data from the US Social Security Administration and US Census
Bureau. We use a combination of these two sources to impute gender and
race/ethnicity variables where administrative data are missing or unavail-
able. Given the use of these predicted values, we caution against direct in-
terpretation of gender and race/ethnicity covariates included in the models
presented in this article.

individuals' likely age at the time the fellowship (would have) started
by assuming that all individuals are 18.0 years old when they started
the earliest undergraduate degree (Associate or Bachelor's) included on

•	 Analyses exclude employment and education records with a dura-

•	 Analyses exclude individuals who do not have at least one employ-

least one employment or education record in the cohort year or later.
B.3.4. Pre-­Fellowsh	ip Work Experience
Years of pre-­fellowship work experience are calculated as the difference
(in calendar years) between the start date of the fellowship program in
which an applicant (would have) participated and the earliest start date of
any post-­undergraduate employment record. For those individuals who
do not have an associated undergraduate education record, we use the
earliest start date of any associated employment record. In this subset,
individuals who have negative work experience—that is, the fellowship
program start date precedes the earliest start date of eligible employ-
ment—are considered to have 0 years of pre-­fellowship work experience.

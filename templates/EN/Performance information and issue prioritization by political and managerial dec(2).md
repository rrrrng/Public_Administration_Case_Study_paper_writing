Abstract
Issue prioritization is the first stage of attention-based theories of decision-making, but remains theoretically and empirically uncharted territory
in public administration research. We propose and test how issue prioritization is informed by the characteristics of the performance information
on which decision-makers rely, in particular its source (internal or external information), nature (objective or subjective information), aspiration
level (historical, social, or coercive aspirations), and required cognitive effort (attention costs). Furthermore, we theorize how these characteris­
tics of performance information determine issue prioritization decisions of political and managerial decision-makers in different ways. We empir­
ically examine issue prioritization decisions in road maintenance and primary school education using a discrete choice experiment among 2,313
local government officials. The experiment reveals that decision-makers are more likely to prioritize issues that are signaled through objective
performance measures and that are articulated relative to coercive aspirations, but that the effects of the information’s source and attention
costs differ between policy domains. Comparison of observational variation regarding decision-makers’ roles indicates that public managers
more strongly prioritize road maintenance issues that are articulated in objective performance information, but not in primary school education.
The study advances public administration research and theory with a “horizontal” behavioral perspective on decision-makers’ information proc­
essing to prioritize between simultaneous performance issues.
Key words: issue prioritization; performance information; discrete choice experiment; local governments; public officials.
Introduction
In the wake of developments such as evidence-based policy-
making, performance management, and big data, public sector
decision-makers possess historically unrivaled information
on societal problems, their origins, and potential solutions
(Moynihan 2008; Van Dooren and Van De Walle 2016).
Paradoxically, this abundance of information leads to an
ever-increasing scarcity of attention, as the available informa­
tion greatly exceeds decision-makers’ information-processing
capacities (Simon 1971). Attention is the sine qua non of
­decision-making: What is attended to can be addressed in
policy responses; what is overlooked or goes unnoticed cannot.
Behavioral theory—rooted in the Behavioral Theory of
the Firm (Cyert and March 1992), and the Carnegie School
more generally (March and Simon 1993; Simon 1997)—is
the main attention-based theoretical perspective on organiza­
tional decision-making, and has been widely applied in public
administration research (Holm 2018; Hong 2019; Nielsen
2014; Rutherford and Meier 2015; Salge 2011; Van der Voet
2023; Wei et al. 2022). Behavioral theory portrays organiza­
tional decision-makers as actors that seek to make rational
decisions by relying on performance information, but whose
information-processing capacity is ultimately “bounded” be­
cause of cognitive constraints. As decision-makers are not
omniscient, when performance falls short of aspiration levels,
they must actively process problem- and solution-related in­
formation to generate alternative courses of action (Salge
2011; Van der Voet 2023). Despite its popularity, empirical
evidence in support of behavioral theory is mixed (Gavetti
et al. 2012; Posen et al. 2017; Rutherford and Meier 2015).
In particular, this article seeks to address two limitations that
currently bar the application of behavioral theory to public
sector organizations.
The first limitation is that behavioral theory provides a
purely “vertical” perspective on decision-making: decision-
makers attend to an issue when performance is perceived
to be below aspirations (Cyert and March 1992). Public
sector applications of behavioral theory widely acknowledge
that performance feedback in public sector organizations
is frequently multidimensional (e.g., Kelman and Friedman
2009; Nielsen 2014; Rutherford and Meier 2015; Zhu and
Rutherford 2019), and that decision-makers face perfor­
mance relative to multiple, simultaneous goals (i.e., “aspira­
tion levels”). For instance, Holm (2017) shows how public
managers’ responses to identical performance feedback di­
verge as a result of multiple aspiration levels, and Hong (2019)
provides evidence of aspiration switching when managers ad­
here to one but not all simultaneous performance goals. In
light of prior research that emphasizes multidimensionality
of performance feedback and multiplicity of aspiration levels
in the public sector, this study aims to inform how decision-
makers prioritize between simultaneous performance issues
that require attention (Gavetti et al. 2012, 25; cf. Andersen
and Nielsen 2020; Rimkutė and Van der Voet 2023).

We define issue prioritization as the processing of perfor­
mance information in order to identify and rank the elements
within a policy domain that require a response (Jones and
Baumgartner 2005). As not all issues can be addressed at
the same time, issue prioritization thus involves the selection
of certain performance problems that require an immediate
response over other problems that may be postponed until
later or safely ignored altogether (Jones and Baumgartner
2005, 33). Although issue prioritization is recognized as the
first stage of the behavioral models of attention, it largely re­
mains uncharted territory in attention-based investigations of
­decision-making. Understanding issue prioritization requires a
“horizontal” perspective on attention allocation: when public
sector decision-makers are confronted with multiple instances
where performance falls short of aspirations, which issue is
prioritized and why? We argue that behavioral theory’s si­
lence on issue prioritization is a particularly critical omission
for public sector organizations, as these typically simultane­
ously address a wide range of performance objectives (Rainey
and Bozeman 2000). To address this knowledge gap, this ar­
ticle examines how characteristics of performance informa­
tion inform decision-makers’ issue prioritization decisions.
The second limitation of behavioral theory is that it does
not explicitly account for the diverse decision-making roles
that co-exist in the public sector. Studies have applied behav­
ioral theory to decision-making by politicians (Desmidt and
Meyfroodt 2021; George et al. 2020; Nielsen and Baekgaard
2015; Nielsen and Moynihan 2017; Van der Voet and Lems
2022) as well as public managers (2017, 2018), but there has
been a void of theorizing concerning the potential differences
in how political and managerial decision-makers use per­
formance information to make decisions. This is surprising,
given that classics in public administration depart from a
dichotomy between politics and administration (Goodnow
1900; Svara 1998). Moreover, the dominant principal-agent
model of political-administrative relationships puts forward
the view that the interests of political and bureaucratic ac­
tors are ultimately opposed (Baekgaard, Blom-Hansen, and
Serritzlew 2020). Similarly, classic comparisons of political
and bureaucratic elites in democratic systems (Aberbach et
al. 1990; Aberbach, Putnam, and Rockman 1981) report
that politicians and top-level bureaucrats operate based on
strongly divergent preferences and objectives.
Strikingly, however, the position that “public managers are
from Mars and politicians are from Venus” is not reflected
in the few empirical comparisons of performance informa­
tion use by political and managerial actors. Prior studies that
rely on self-reported performance information use (e.g., 2013,
2014), as well as experimental research based on factual per­
formance feedback (e.g., Van der Voet 2023), report similarity
in the degree of performance information use by politicians
and public managers. Other experimental studies show that
politicians and managers’ use of performance information is
equally subject to cognitive bias (e.g., Florczak, Nielsen, and
Klemmensen 2019; Lerusse and Van de Walle 2022a, 2022c).
Departing from the notion that decision-makers’ use of per­
formance information may be driven by a variety of goals
(Sharot and Sunstein 2020), we aim to contribute to this
emerging stream of literature by investigating how politicians
and public managers’ issue prioritization preferences are
driven by performance information in different ways (cf.
Moynihan, Nielsen, and Kroll 2017). To that end, we bridge
the literatures on political-administrative relationships and
performance-based decision-making to theorize and em­
pirically test how the relationships between performance
information characteristics and issue prioritization differ be­
tween political and managerial decision-makers.
The main theoretical expectation of this article is that issue
prioritization decisions by decision-makers are informed by
characteristics of the performance information on which
decision-makers rely, and that these characteristics shape the
prioritization decisions of political and managerial decision-
makers in different ways. We conduct a discrete choice
experiment (DCE) that distinguishes between three charac­
teristics of performance information: the source of perfor­
mance information, the nature of performance information,
and the nature of the aspiration level to which performance
is compared. In addition, the article incorporates a focus on
attention costs, defined here as the expected time investment
required to sufficiently process information about the issue
before a prioritization decision can be taken. The main re­
search question is: How do characteristics of performance in­
formation affect issue prioritization, and to what extent do
differences exist between public managers and politicians?
Theoretical framework
Building on various theoretical perspectives, this study aims
to construct and test a comprehensive model of decision-
makers’ issue prioritization. We argue that decision-makers’
decision to prioritize one issue over another depends on the
performance information that they assess. Based on our re­
view of the literature, we identify four relevant characteristics
of performance information that may influence decision-
makers’ issue prioritization. First, decision-makers examine
the source of performance information, as previous studies
show that decision-makers differentiate between informa­
tion from internal and external sources (Cyert and March
1992). Second, decision-makers analyze the nature of per­
formance information. The literature usually distinguishes
between objective and subjective performance information
(Andersen, Boesen, and Pedersen 2016). Third, performance
information has limited value if not compared against a ref­
erence point or aspiration level (Nielsen 2014; Rutherford
and Meier 2015). Decision-makers therefore compare perfor­
mance metrics against past performance (historical aspiration
level), their peers (social aspiration level) or a predetermined
target (coercive aspiration level) (cf. Cyert and March 1992).
Additionally, since decision-makers’ cognitive capacity is
limited (Simon 1971), we introduce a novel emphasis on at­
tention costs as the time investment required to process per­
formance information.
Performance information is assessed by both (elected)
politicians and (appointed) public managers (e.g., Moynihan
et al. 2017). In the traditional model of public administration,
there is a clear distinction between politics and administra­
tion (Weber 1946). Whereas politicians are expected to de­
fine the political missions and plans of their constituencies,
public managers are responsible for neutrally implementing
and executing these missions and plans (Goodnow 1900;
Weber 1946). We therefore theorize how performance in­
formation will inform issue prioritization by politicians and
public managers in different ways. Throughout the theoret­
ical framework, we first formulate general hypotheses about

Source of performance information
Performance information about public services is not ex­
clusively generated by public organizations, but can also be
derived from a large range of external actors, including the
media, private organizations, scientific institutions, nongov­
ernmental organizations, and international organizations
(James et al. 2020). As this study examines performance
information use by public officials within government, we
classify this variety of potential sources of performance in­
formation into two categories: evidence generated by actors
within government (i.e., internal source of performance in­
formation) and evidence that originates from actors outside
of government (external source of performance information).
A range of studies provide evidence that the source of per­
formance information determines individual decision-makers’
performance information use. Generally, research in the tradi­
tion of the Behavioral Theory of the Firm (Cyert and March
1992; Posen et al. 2017) asserts that information processing
in response to a performance shortfall is primarily “simple-
minded” in the sense that it emphasizes information in close
vicinity to the symptom and the current alternative-in-use:
“Thus, if the problem is the failure to attain the sales goal,
the search begins in the sales department and with the sales
program” (Cyert and March 1992, 170). Decision-makers’
emphasis on local search thus suggests an initial reliance on
internal performance information in issue prioritization.
Additionally, decision-makers may evaluate the trustwor­
thiness of the evidence based on the source of performance
information (James et al. 2020), indicating that decision-
makers will pay more attention to the content of evidence
depending on its source. Past scholarship has identified that
the trustworthiness of the message was namely affected by the
expertise, competence, honesty, and helpfulness of the source
(Priester and Petty 1995). As a consequence, the source of
information is employed by decision-makers as a heuristic
in order to bypass the barriers related to information search
and processing, indicating that decision-makers tend to use
performance information that has been derived from sources
which they trust (James et al. 2020).
Other studies draw on behavioral insights to show how
decision-makers’ interpretation of the source of informa­
tion functions as a cognitive shortcut to evaluate its useful­
ness, as well as cognitive biases therein. James et al. (2020)
highlight that the source of performance information can
be associated with decision-makers’ ideological preferences,
indicating that decision-makers may use motivated rea­
soning to evaluate performance information. The theory
of motivated reasoning states that decision-makers tend
to deny or downplay evidence that contradicts their prior
beliefs (Kunda 1990; Lodge and Taber 2000; Taber and
Lodge 2006). When information emanates from a source
that is not ideologically aligned with the decision-maker’s
preferences, this may result in sub-optimal ­decision-making
where pressing issues or optimal solutions are not (fully)
considered (Baekgaard et al. 2017; Christensen and
Moynihan 2020; Lerusse and Van de Walle 2022a). Yet,
the influence of the source of performance information on
decision-makers’ use of performance information is not
limited to recipients’ motivated reasoning (James et al.
2020). The content of performance evaluations may include
stakeholders’ ideological or partisan motivations, thereby
leading to strategic communication via performance eval­
uation reports (James et al. 2020). Decision-makers are
therefore expected to “use information strategically to fa­
cilitate their goals and are wary of others that they suspect
of doing the same” (James et al. 2020, 49). We formulate
the following hypothesis:
H1: Public officials are more likely to prioritize issues that
are signaled by internal performance information than is­
sues that are signaled by external performance information.
As previous studies have exclusively focused on citizens’
judgment of performance information based on the source
of performance information (Schmidthuber, Willems,
and Krabina 2023; Van den Bekerom, Van der Voet, and
Christensen 2021), limited evidence exists concerning
the extent to which politicians and public managers may
react differently to the source of performance information.
Nonetheless, we argue that differences between politicians
and public managers can be witnessed given their distinct
role in democratic systems. The role of politicians is to
select, translate, and integrate societal interests into the
policy agenda of government (Goodnow 1900; Jones and
Baumgartner 2005), whereas public managers navigate bu­
reaucratic questions related to management and implemen­
tation of the political agenda (Meier et al. 2019). We argue
that politicians are under the pressure of their electorate
and other societal stakeholders, and are therefore expected
to be responsive to a broader range of demands. As a con­
sequence, we expect public managers to more strongly pri­
oritize issues based on internal performance information
compared to politicians who may relatively place more
emphasis on external information in issue prioritization
decisions.
In addition, bureaucrats will rely more strongly on
facts, technical analysis, and expertise, whereas politicians
will to a greater extent emphasize political advocacy and
interests (Aberbach et al. 1990, 123). Based on their study
of bureaucrats and politicians in Western democracies,
Aberbach, Putnam, and Rockman (1981, 254) assert that
“the ‘sensors’ of politicians key in on signals from the grass
roots and are more likely to detect sociopolitical problems.”
We expect that external performance information will to a
greater extent contain information that is motivated toward
distinct political objectives, and that this provides politicians
with greater opportunity to use information that supports
their own ideological preferences. Instead, public managers
are inclined to rely on technical information that has been
provided by government organizations and are therefore ex­
pected to be more neutral. Based on these observations, we
formulate the following hypothesis:

Nature of performance information
Performance information has increasingly become multi­
faceted to facilitate decision-makers’ need to form more
comprehensive views of the various dimensions of public
performance (Jacobsen, Snyder, and Saultz 2015). Public ad­
ministration research frequently distinguishes between ob­
jective and subjective performance information. A review by
Andersen, Boesen, and Pedersen (2016) posits that this dis­
tinction may concern the conceptual as well as operational
level of performance. Citizen satisfaction, for instance, is an
inherently subjective concept that captures an aspect of public
performance, whereas effectiveness may be measured using
indicators of either a subjective or objective nature.
On this operational level, the distinction between indicators
with an objective or subjective nature serves as a key clas­
sification in research on performance information, and the
public administration literature provides further guidance
on the distinction between these terms. Andrews et al. (2006,
16) state that “an objective indicator of performance should
[…] be impartial, independent, and detached from the unit
of analysis.” Andersen et al. (2016, 856) add that objective
indicators are based on “exterior” phenomena that are ex­
ternally observable. Objective performance measures thus as­
sess a dimension of performance to an explicit and formalized
criterion, potentially ensuring reliability and external verifi­
cation to validate the accuracy of the performance measure.
Consequently, objective performance measures are considered
“the gold standard in public management research”
(Andrews, Boyne, and Walker 2006, 16) because objective
indicators are viewed as accurately representing “actual” per­
formance (Meier, Brudney, and Bothe 2014). Students’ grades
are frequently utilized as an objective performance measure
because it measures a performance dimension—effective­
ness—that has been independently determined by teachers
as external assessors (Andrews, Boyne, and Walker 2006).
Moreover, students’ grades are publicly accessible and have
been generated based on predetermined evaluation criteria.
Nonetheless, the generation of objective performance meas­
ures can be subject to interpretation and bias, as the example
of student assessment by external assessors makes clear.
In contrast to objective performance indicators, subjec­
tive performance measures have been defined in the litera­
ture as potentially biased and not detached from the unit of
analysis. Similar to objective indicators, subjective indicators
may measure a specific performance dimension, but subjec­
tive performance indicators measure stakeholders’ subjective
judgments, perceptions, or experiences concerning the per­
formance of public services (Andrews, Boyne, and Walker
2006). Subjective performance indicators usually refer to
individuals’ satisfaction measures such as parents’ satisfac­
tion with their children’s schools or municipal residents’
satisfaction with road maintenance services. Moreover, sub­
jective measurements have also been identified as citizen-
generated performance indicators (Schachter 2010) or
as internal experiences (Andersen, Boesen, and Pedersen
2016). Subjective performance measures have been criticized
for being prone to social desirability bias and are not
straightforwardly open for external verification (Andrews,
Boyne, and Walker 2006). Nonetheless, decision-makers may
justify the use of subjective performance measures for two
main reasons. First, objective performance measures do not
always perfectly represent the performance dimension which
is relevant for public organizations. Second, objective per­
formance measures are not always available and subjective
indicators may represent a suitable alternative (Andrews,
Boyne, and Walker 2006).
From the above discussion, we conclude that objective per­
formance information is seen as the “gold standard” because
objective information is perceived to be more accurate and
reliable, whereas subjective performance information is used
to complement or substitute objective information when it is
unavailable. We therefore argue that, if available, objective
performance measures will be preferred over subjective per­
formance measures when public officials take issue prioriti­
zation decisions, and we formulate the following hypothesis:
H3: Public officials are more likely to prioritize issues
which are described by objective performance information
than issues which are described by subjective performance
information.
Although we expect that decision-makers will generally
prefer objective over subjective performance information to
inform issue prioritization, the literature suggests that this
preference may depend on the role of the decision-maker
(cf. Van der Voet 2023). Andersen, Boesen, and Pedersen
(2016) argue that opting for subjective or objective perfor­
mance measures depends on the conceptual perception of
performance by stakeholders. Moreover, decision-makers
often have very different interpretations of what constitutes
successful and unsuccessful performance based on the way
performance information is presented (Andrews, Boyne,
and Walker 2006). This article differentiates between public
managers’ and politicians’ processing of objective and sub­
jective performance information. Because “bureaucrats tend
to emphasize the technical and de-emphasize the advocacy
and partisan considerations within their role understanding,
whereas politicians tend to do exactly the reverse” (Aberbach
et al. 1990, 3), we argue that, compared to politicians, public
managers will be more likely to prioritize issues based on ob­
jective performance measures. Moynihan (2008) argues that
the use of objective performance information may be less pro­
nounced among politicians than managers, as politicians have
fewer incentives to give up established control mechanisms
for number-driven performance monitoring practices (cf.
Moynihan, Nielsen, and Kroll 2017). Moreover, politicians
are expected to be attentive to the demands of their elec­
torate in order to seek their re-election (cf. Hansen 2023;
Van der Voet and Rimkutė 2022). We therefore expect public
managers to remain neutral by relying on objective perfor­
mance indicators, while, in contrast, politicians are account­
able to citizens and feel they should respond to citizens’ needs
by more strongly prioritizing issues that score low on subjec­
tive performance measures such as citizen satisfaction. As a
consequence, we formulate the following hypothesis:
H4: Compared to politicians, public managers are more
likely to prioritize issues which are described by objective
performance information.

Aspiration level of performance information
Aspiration levels guide the way decision-makers evaluate
and respond to performance information (Cyert and March
1992; March and Simon 1993). In their original concep­
tion of aspiration levels, Cyert and March (1992) state that
decision-makers may compare current performance to the
organization’s past performance, to the performance of com­
parable organizations, or to a performance goal that was
set in the past. By comparing differences between the cur­
rent performance level and the aspiration level, decision-
makers are capable of identifying performance information
in terms of negative performance (the current performance
level is inferior to the aspiration level) or positive perfor­
mance (the current performance level is superior to the aspi­
ration level). Public administration studies have shown that
decision-makers in public organizations frequently rely on
aspiration levels when evaluating performance information
(Holm 2017, 2018; Hong 2019; Nielsen 2014; Nielsen and
Moynihan 2017; Rutherford and Meier 2015; Salge 2011;
Van der Voet 2023; Zhu and Rutherford 2019).
Public administration scholars traditionally make a dis­
tinction between historical and social aspiration levels. In
historical aspiration levels, decision-makers compare the cur­
rent level of performance to the past level of performance
(Cyert and March 1992; March and Simon 1993). The
second form of aspiration level, the social aspiration level,
compares the performance of the organization to the per­
formance of a comparable organization (Cyert and March
1992; March and Simon 1993). The criteria used to iden­
tify “comparable” organizations are various and can include
criteria such as geographical or strategic proximity (Nielsen
2014). More recently, public administration scholarship has
begun paying attention to coercive aspiration levels, in which
decision-makers evaluate performance information based
on predetermined targets or centrally determined standards
(Holm 2017). Coercive aspiration levels can be referred to
as political aspiration levels because they typically reflect po­
litical goals that have been set by the political principals of
organizations (Holm 2017).
Past public administration scholarship empirically examined
decision-makers’ behavioral reactions to performance below
historical, social, and coercive levels by examining decision-
makers’ innovative search (Salge 2011; Van der Voet 2023)
or managerial action (Hong 2019; Zhu and Rutherford
2019). Specifically, a small number of previous studies have
addressed decision-makers’ goal prioritization of perfor­
mance below historical, social, and coercive levels. Nielsen
(2014) finds that, when the performance of a goal is below
historical or social aspiration levels, public managers tend
to prioritize this goal while, in contrast to Nielsen’s (2014)
findings, Holm (2017) observes that public managers do
not respond to negative aspiration levels. Holm (2017) also
examines public managers’ behavioral reactions to situations
where historical and political aspiration levels are in conflict
and showed that public managers tend to prioritize goals that
are below historical aspirations levels, suggesting that public
managers are more responsive to performance information
below historical aspiration levels.
Although previous research has developed theoretical
reasoning explaining how decision-makers react to perfor­
mance information that is below aspiration levels, the rela­
tive influence of aspiration levels on decision-makers’ issue
prioritization has been overlooked in the literature. Given
that public organizations typically operate within a con­
text of performance oversight and centrally set performance
standards, it has been argued that coercive aspiration levels
may be of significant importance for public officials (George
et al. 2020; Salge 2011; Van der Voet 2023). Moreover, when
taking issue prioritization decisions, public officials may be
more likely to rely on the goals that have been previously
set within the organization in order to enhance the consist­
ency between the goals of the organization and the issues that
need to be prioritized. We therefore formulate the following
hypothesis:
H5: Public officials are more likely to prioritize issues
where performance is below coercive aspiration levels than
issues where performance is below historical or social as­
piration levels.
Although not previously addressed in public administration
research, we propose that politicians and public managers
may emphasize the importance of historical, social, and co­
ercive aspiration levels differently. A difference between both
decision-making roles is that public managers operate within
a framework of performance accountability to their political
principal, whereas elected politicians operate based on a di­
rect democratically legitimated mandate. Moynihan, Nielsen,
and Kroll (2017) argue that especially public managers may
be expected to be highly responsive to negative performance
information, precisely because managers are aware that po­
litical principals and their broader constituencies will be
attentive to negative results. Politicians may be expected to
be generally concerned with historical and social aspiration
levels, as these may provide electorally relevant comparisons
to preceding office holders and other political constituencies.
We argue that public managers, compared to politicians, face
more stringent accountability pressures and therefore in par­
ticular more strongly prioritize issues that are below coercive
aspiration levels. We consequently formulate the following
hypothesis:
H6: Compared to politicians, public managers are more
likely to prioritize issues where performance is below coer­
cive aspiration levels.
Attention costs
Public administration research on performance information
departs from the view that decision-makers strive to be ra­
tional actors who attempt to use performance information to
make optimal choices concerning public services. However,
the abundance of performance information that is available
to decision-makers increasingly exceeds decision-makers’
information-processing capacities (Simon 1971). A well-
documented challenge is thus that an abundance of informa­
tion results in a scarcity of attention. Recent studies have
shown that decision-making biases may underlie the selective
use of performance information, including, for instance, neg­
ativity bias and politically motivated reasoning (Baekgaard et
al. 2017; Christensen 2018; Hong 2019; Lerusse and Van de
Walle 2022a, 2022c).
As decision-makers are overloaded with performance in­
formation concerning an abundance of societal problems, we

propose that decision-makers strategically consider “attention
costs” in their prioritization decisions. We define attention
costs as the expected time investment required to sufficiently
process all problem-related and solution-related information
about the issue before a decision can be taken. Attention costs
of different issues are likely to vary due to differences in com­
plexity, scale, and novelty of issues. The model of behavioral
decision-making stresses a linear process in which issue prior­
itization precedes problem-definition and solution-generation
(Cyert and March 1992; Jones and Baumgartner 2005; Van
der Voet 2023). We argue that decision-makers—all else equal
(for instance in terms of levels of issue salience, urgency or
size of the performance gap)—will seek to minimize their at­
tention scarcity by prioritizing those issues with minimal at­
tention costs. As such, we propose that attention costs are
negatively related to issue prioritization. We therefore formu­
late the following hypothesis:
H7: Public officials are more likely to prioritize issues with
lower attention costs than issues with higher attention
costs.
Methodology
Research setting
We test our theoretical expectations among the population of
elected politicians (including mayors, aldermen, and munic­
ipal councilors) and civil servants with a management posi­
tion in Belgian municipalities. We focus on local governments
as local politicians and public managers regularly take impor­
tant decisions that have “a real and visible impact on the daily
life of the citizens” (Deschouwer 2012, 178). In Belgium, local
governments are composed of an executive body (the munic­
ipal college) and a legislative body (the municipal council).
The municipal college, composed of the mayor and the
aldermen, is responsible for executing the decisions taken
by the municipal council. The mayor and the aldermen have
been nominated to the municipal college by the municipal
councilors and are usually the politicians who have received
most of the electoral support. The municipal council, com­
posed of the municipal councilors, is responsible for taking
decisions about public service arrangements. The members of
the municipal college and the municipal council are directly
elected for a period of six years by the municipal residents
(Deschouwer 2012). While elected politicians give the polit­
ical direction of the municipality, public managers support
them in their daily tasks by implementing the decisions taken
by the municipal council. Yet, as some decisions do not need
the approval of the municipal council, public managers often
take decisions independently.
Of the different public services delivered at the Belgian local
level, we focus on road maintenance and primary school ed­
ucation, which represent both a technical and a social public
service. While social services, in particular primary school ed­
ucation, have received substantial attention from the perfor­
mance feedback literature (e.g., Holm 2018; Nielsen 2014),
technical services have remained relatively overlooked. We
therefore believe that it is relevant to include both public serv­
ices in our analysis in order to generate a more comprehensive
understanding of public officials’ issue prioritization decisions
in different contexts. Additionally, road maintenance and
primary school education are highly salient in Belgian local
governments. Belgium has one of the densest road networks
in the European Union (IWEPS 2020). Road maintenance is
also one of the public services where citizens are least sat­
isfied. In 2018, only 25 percent of the citizens stated to be
satisfied with the road conditions in Flanders (Statistiek
Vlaanderen 2019). Furthermore, satisfaction with road main­
tenance has substantially decreased over the past 10 years.
With regard to primary school education, the results of the
certificate of basic studies obtained by primary school pupils
at the end of primary school have been seriously declining
in recent years. These declining results have led to concerns
from public authorities and citizens about the performance of
Belgian primary school pupils. In light of these observations,
we argue that it is crucial to understand how public officials’
issue prioritization is affected by the characteristics of per­
formance information, possibly affecting the quality of road
maintenance and primary school education.
Experimental design
We conduct a preregistered DCE to investigate decision-
makers’ issue prioritization. DCE is a stated preferences
method where participants need to select, based on a hypo­
thetical scenario, their preferred option across several choice
sets. These choice sets may include two or more options which
are described by a set of attributes that can take several levels
(Lancsar and Louviere 2008). DCEs are often considered a
special case of conjoint experiment, but we argue that both
experimental methods are different in relevant ways. While
DCEs rely on random utility theory, a behavioral theory,
conjoint experiments are based on mathematical theory,
indicating that discrete choice and conjoint experiments are
not built on the same theoretical assumptions (Louviere et al.
2010). Random utility theory states that latent preferences,
which are present for all decision-makers, are correlated
with decision-makers’ choices (Louviere, Flynn, and Carson
2010). The theory postulates that decision-makers’ stated
preferences consist of a random and an observed compo­
nent. While the observed component includes all the variables
that are visible to the researcher such as decision-makers’
sociodemographic variables, the unobserved component
consists of the variables that the researcher cannot observe.
These unobserved variables can for instance include, but are
not limited to, unobserved attributes, unobserved taste vari­
ations, measurement errors, or instrumental variables (Ben-
Akiva and Lerman 1985; Louviere, Flynn, and Carson 2010).
In our experiment, participants first read a hypothetical
scenario stating that the municipality they work for has re­
cently received performance evaluations of two municipal
roads (schools) and that, based on these evaluations, decisions
about the prioritization of some roads (schools) over others
should be made (see online Appendices A and K for Dutch
and French translations). Second, participants are randomly
assigned to one of six blocks, in which a series of four con­
secutive choice sets is presented in random order. For gen­
eralizability purposes, each block concerns either scenarios
about road maintenance, or primary schools. The choice sets
describe two different performance issues (i.e., a compar­
ison of road A versus road B, or a comparison of school A
versus school B), and participants are asked to select which
one of these two issues they would choose to prioritize (see
online Appendix B). Each issue is described by four perfor­
mance information characteristics: the source of performance

Because the number of potential combinations of perfor­
mance information characteristics results in too many choice
sets for participants to consider, we conduct a fractional facto­
rial design to reduce the number of included choice sets. This
technique has been defined as “a sample from the full facto­
rial selected such that all effects of interest can be estimated”
(Lancsar and Louviere 2008, 667). We conducted the frac­
tional factorial design in R using the statistical package “sup­
port.CEs,” developed by Hideo Aizaki (2012). This package
is widely used to design DCEs, including in the public admin­
istration literature (Lerusse and Van de Walle 2021, 2022b;
Rimkutė and Van der Voet 2023). We computed an orthog­
onal main effect array by employing the mix-and-match
method in order to generate the fractional factorial design
(Aizaki 2012). The mix-and-match method “modifies the ro­
tation method by introducing the randomizing process. After
placing a set of N alternatives created from the orthogonal
main-effect array into an urn, one or more additional set(s)
of N alternatives is/are created using the rotation method
and placed into different urn(s). A choice set is generated
by randomly selecting one alternative from each urn. This
selection process is repeated, without replacement, until all
the alternatives are assigned to N choice sets. These N choice
sets correspond to a CE design” (Aizaki 2012, 5). This type
of fractional factorial design respects the orthogonality (the
attributes are statistically independent from each other) and
the level balance (each level evenly appears throughout the
choice sets) assumptions (Ryan et al. 2012). The property of
orthogonality ensures that the independent effect of attributes
can be identified (Ryan et al. 2012). The outcome of the frac­
tional factorial design indicates that participants should col­
lectively assess a number of twelve choice sets (see Table 1).
In fractional factorial designs, researchers are able to block
the number of choice sets per study participants in order to
reduce the learning fatigue of the study participants. The dif­

it is commonly done in DCEs (Ryan et al. 2012). It is impor­

in each block, as the inferences are based on analysis across
blocks. In order to facilitate participants’ assessment of the
choice sets, we have therefore divided the number of choice

needs to assess four choice sets. Based on the outcome of the

Despite its numerous advantages, DCEs have some limi­
tations that need to be discussed. One limitation involves
the potential for omitted variables bias, where participants
may rely on a set of “invisible” factors when evaluating the
alternatives (Lancsar and Louviere 2008), leading to poten­
tial endogeneity issues. To address this concern, we instruct
the participants to assume that the nondescribed character­
istics are the same for every issue. This approach diminishes
the risk that participants will rely on unobserved attributes
(Guevara and Polanco 2016). Another limitation, known
as hypothetical bias, arises when participants tend to over­
state their stated preferences due to the hypothetical nature
of the scenario. Yet, the hypothesis bias is more prevalent
when participants have to make complex, unusual, or remote
choices (OECD 2018). Our interviews indicate that public
managers and politicians are relatively familiar with issue pri­
oritization choices. This familiarity mitigates the risk of hy­
pothetical bias.
Operationalization of the attributes
We first operationalize the performance information charac­
teristics based on previous research. Second, we conducted
two semi-structured interviews with two randomly selected
local government public officials in order to discuss the
operationalization of the attributes and their respective
levels. The interviews were divided into two parts. The first
part of the interview concerned general questions about
the way issue prioritization is conducted within the mu­
nicipality, as well as the role of performance information
in this process. The second part of the interview addressed
the operationalization of the attributes and their respec­
tive levels by asking the interviewees to evaluate a choice
set of the DCE, as well as the full operationalization of the
attributes based on our literature review (see Table 2; see on­
line Appendix L for translations in Dutch and French). Both
interviewees remark that the focus of the experiment is sub­
stantively reflective of prioritization decisions in practice, as
the choice sets contain considerations that are deemed rele­
vant and reflective of day-to-day practice. The main revision
that was implemented based on the interviews was to oper­
ationalize attention costs as percentages of weekly working
time instead of hours per week, as interviewees indicated that
this was more intuitive.
Data collection and sample
We distributed the survey experiment to all elected local
politicians and local civil servants with a management pos­
ition in Belgium. The DCE was distributed among 21,423
local public officials (13,035 politicians and 8,388 public
managers). The contact details of the public officials were
gathered via an online database and complemented manu­
ally. The DCE was distributed electronically via Qualtrics on
the 22nd of June 2022 and lasted until the 30th of September
2022. We sent two reminders to the study participants. A
total number of 2,313 public officials fully participated in
the DCE (response rate 10.8 percent). The sample consists of
1,292 politicians and 1,021 public managers. The number of
participants per municipality as well as the response rate per
municipality are displayed in figure 2. In order to determine
the appropriate sample size for the DCE, a post hoc power
analysis was conducted using G*Power 3 software (Faul et
al. 2007). This post hoc power analysis exclusively pertains
to main effects and does not consider interaction effects. In
our post hoc power analysis, we specified our sample sizes
separately for road maintenance and primary schools, and an
effect size of .10. The analysis indicated that a .99 percent
power to detect an effect size of .10. This procedure indicates
that our study has sufficient statistical power and is in line
with recommendations by Lakens (2022) to decrease the
probability of type I error rates, and can be deemed more
conservative than common practice.

Analysis and results
We conduct a conditional (fixed effects) logistic regression
analysis in order to examine the theoretical expectations of the
study. First, we examine the main effects of the characteristics
of performance information on issue prioritization to test
hypotheses 1, 3, 5, and 7. Second, because participants’ role
(politician or manager) within their municipality remains
fixed across the alternatives, we include it as an interaction
term in the regression models in order to test hypotheses 2,
4, and 6 (Train 2002). We interpret the interaction terms as
multiplicative effects (i.e., odds ratio) rather than as an addi­

politicians and managers
Hypotheses 2, 4, and 6 state that the source, nature, and aspi­

prioritization of politicians and public managers differently.
We examine these theoretical expectations by means of in­
teraction effects, of which the results are reported in online
Appendices I and J. Hypothesis 2 states that public managers
are more likely than politicians to prioritize issues that are
signaled by internal performance information (compared to
external performance information). Yet, the analysis shows
that there are no significant differences between politicians
and public managers with regard to the source of perfor­
mance information for both road maintenance (P = .627) and
primary education (P = .135). We therefore fail to reject the
null hypothesis that there are no significant differences be­

source of performance information. Hypothesis 4 states that
public managers more strongly than politicians prioritize is­

politicians are more likely to prioritize issues when measured
by subjective indicators (compared to objective indicators)
for road maintenance (OR = 1.268, P < .000), but we did not
find this effect for primary education (P < .348). The analysis
thus provides partial support for hypothesis 4. Hypothesis 6
states that public managers are more likely than politicians to
prioritize issues that are signaled through coercive aspiration
levels (compared to social and historical aspiration levels).

education (P = .388).2
Discussion and conclusion
Issue prioritization is at the heart of contemporary govern­
ance, as public sector decision-makers are confronted with an
abundance of societal problems yet have limited capacity to
address all problems adequately or simultaneously. Crucially,
public administration research has yielded limited insight into
processes of issue prioritization. Behavioral theory provides a
strictly “vertical” explanation of how public sector decision-
makers prioritize goals that are below aspirations, yet offers
no guidance about prioritization between simultaneous
instances of negative performance. To that end, this article has
analyzed how the characteristics of performance information
that decision-makers have at their disposal affect issue prior­
itization decisions. Our analysis of a DCE among local gov­
ernment officials provides abundant support for behavioral
theory’s key premise that decision-makers actively process
performance information, as we find that several characteris­
tics of the information through which performance issues are
signaled affect prioritization decisions. Critically, our study
generates insight in the types of information that are most
likely to drive issue prioritization.
Analyzing road maintenance and primary school edu­
cation, and in line with our theoretical expectations, we
conclude that public officials are more likely to prioritize per­
formance issues that are signaled through information that
is expressed in objective performance measures and that is
articulated relative to coercive aspirations. Yet, we also find
differences between road maintenance and primary school
education. While public officials are more likely to priori­
tize road maintenance issues that are described via internal
performance information and that require lower cognitive
efforts, public officials are more likely to prioritize primary
school issues that are signaled by external performance in­
formation. In contrast to road maintenance, public officials
do not appear to take attention costs into consideration when
prioritizing primary school issues.
A second point of attention in this article is to what ex­
tent performance information affects issue prioritization by
politicians and public managers in different ways. Although
behavioral theory has been applied to both political and
managerial decision-makers (Desmidt and Meyfroodt 2021;
Holm 2018), public administration research has provided
scarcely any theoretical elaboration and empirical compar­
ison of political and managerial decision-makers. Whereas
classic qualitative studies have argued that political and man­
agerial decision-makers display strongly divergent behaviors
(Aberbach, Putnam, and Rockman 1981), quantitative and
experimental evidence has documented that the extent to
which politicians and managers use performance informa­
tion is highly similar (Lerusse and Van de Walle 2022a,
2022c; Saliterer and Korac 2013; Van der Voet 2023). Our

Our study advances behavioral theory by informing a
“horizontal” view on public sector decision-making processes
(Gavetti et al. 2012). Whereas empirical research has pro­
vided plentiful evidence that decision-makers are attentive to
singular performance issues in their organization (e.g., Hong
2019; Nielsen 2014), this study is among the first to inform
decision-making when confronted with multiple simulta­
neous issues that require attention. This article reveals how
the information that decision-makers have at their disposal
affects their prioritization decisions, thereby informing how
the processing of problem-related information is an impera­
tive—yet largely overlooked—phase in the behavioral frame­
work (Posen et al. 2017; Van der Voet 2023).
Our comparison of political and managerial decision-
makers contributes to a public administration literature in
which a void of empirical research concerning differences be­
tween politicians and public managers exists. Our comparison
of prioritization preferences in a controlled experimental set­
ting reveals little differences between the two ­decision-making
roles. In practice, however, politicians and public managers do
not operate in isolation from each other, and decision-making
processes depend on the interactions between both groups.
For instance, the study by Baekgaard, Blom-Hansen, and
Serritzlew (2020) shows how the provision of information by
bureaucrats may affect politicians’ priorities and preferences.
By emphasizing the effects of performance information,
this study provides a “rational” view on issue prioritization
processes. However, bureaucrats notably operate in a system
where a political reality exists, which may be loosely coupled
to problem-related considerations based on information, ex­
pertise or experience. A recommendation for future research is
therefore to experimentally vary political conditions in which
bureaucratic processes of issue prioritization take place, in
order to study how political priorities (or the absence thereof)
affect prioritization decisions by bureaucrats.
Most public administration research on performance in­
formation use and aspirations levels has focused on social
services, in particular schools (e.g., Holm 2018; Nielsen
2014). Limited evidence exists about decision-makers’ use
of performance information in technical services. Some
exceptions notwithstanding (e.g., Baekgaard et al. 2017), few
empirical studies have simultaneously examined technical and
social services. This study has therefore focused on a tech­
nical service (road maintenance) and a social service (primary
school education). Our analysis uncovers similarities as well
as three pronounced differences between technical and social
services concerning the characteristics of issues that decision-
makers are likely to prioritize. First, compared to road mainte­
nance, issue prioritization in primary schools is more strongly
driven by performance information of an objective nature. A
potential explanation is that the objective performance indi­
cator for schools (standardized test scores) is a strongly insti­
tutionalized and legitimate measure of school performance,
thereby more strongly driving prioritization decisions. Second,
whereas issue prioritization in road maintenance is driven
by internal information, decision-makers base prioritization
decisions for schools more strongly on external information.
A potential explanation is that municipal organizations have
limited expertise to collect or analyze performance data about
schools, and that decision-makers therefore prefer to rely on
information provided by specialized external organizations.
For instance, public organizations often rely on internal or­
ganizations such as PISA (Program for International Student
Assessment) scores to assess the performance of their pupils.
Third, while public officials who assessed road maintenance
prioritize performance issues with lower attention costs, at­
tention costs did not significantly determine prioritization
of primary school education issues. A potential explanation
concerns the salience of the performance issue, suggesting
that attention costs may more strongly determine prioriti­
zation for issues with low salience (as compared to high sa­
lience) due to lower potential negative consequences of not
attending to an issue. Our findings thus imply that the role of
performance information characteristics on public officials’
issue prioritization decisions is contingent on the type of
public service issues that are being assessed. Further research
should therefore be more vigilant in generalizing their study
findings beyond the public service they are examining, and
we strongly recommend future studies to collect evidence on
more than a single public service.
This analysis of issue prioritization is subject to a range
of strengths and limitations. Strengths of this study include
its use of the DCE as a method that fits the challenge for
decision-makers to prioritize between simultaneous per­
formance issues. For the purposes of our study, we believe
the DCE offers high ecological as well as internal validity.
In addition to comparing politicians and public managers,
a strength of the study is that it offers a systematic analysis
of issue prioritization for technical as well as social services.
In doing so, we have analyzed how decision-makers priori­
tize between issues with relatively similar characteristics (i.e.,
issue prioritization within the domain of road maintenance
and primary school education). A so far unexplored research
avenue is to analyze how decision-makers prioritize between
issues with strongly heterogenous characteristics (i.e., issue
prioritization between policy domains such as road main­
tenance, education, safety or public health). Limitations of
our study include that service delivery in local government
was utilized as a specific research context for the experi­
ment. Generalizability of results toward different governance
contexts may therefore not be warranted (i.e., policy for­
mulation at the central government level). A limitation with
regards to sampling is that we cannot fully determine repre­
sentativeness of our random sample beyond gender, function,
and region, as well as that the daily job responsibilities of
our respondents encompass a wider array of policy domains
than road maintenance and primary school education. A
limitation of our experimental design is that the choice sets
within the three blocks of the experimental design were not
presented to participants in random order. We therefore
cannot exclude the presence of potential order effects. Our
recommendation to future application of DCEs in public

In examining the moderating role of public officials’
function, our analysis relies on a nonexperimental compar­
ison in which covariation with participants’ demographic
variables (i.e., age, gender) bars the identification of causal
effects. While our analysis of experimental variation is
characterized by high internal validity, we acknowledge
that a potential limitation for generalizability to practice
is that the experimental task is relatively abstract and may
not fully resemble the context-embedded nature of real-
world decision-making. We believe that such concerns are
partially mitigated by our reliance on a sample of real-
world local government officials who assessed the exper­
imental choice sets as realistic and recognizable. Further
comparative research, as well as qualitative analysis, may
inform decision-makers’ considerations that underlie
their use of performance information to make prioritiza­
tion decisions. Substantively, we acknowledge that issue
prioritization decisions may draw upon a wider range of
considerations. Future research on issue prioritization can
therefore consider a wider range of explanations for issue
prioritization decisions, including for instance (political) sa­
lience. Furthermore, as Herbert Simon (1997, 305) states
“almost no decision made in an organization is the task
of a single individual,” future studies may fruitfully go
beyond the individual level of analysis by studying issue
prioritization decisions on the level of decision teams in
order to strengthen generalizability to practice. We thus
encourage public administration researchers to contribute
to further developing our understanding of issue prioriti­
zation decisions, drawing on a wider range of theoretical
explanations, research settings and methodologies.
We conclude that our study informs how public sector
decision-makers are responsive to societal challenges and that
their processing of performance information explains prior­
itization decisions. As the characteristics of performance in­
formation through which societal challenges are articulated
may vary in practice, our study has important implications
for public administration practice. First, in order to stimu­
late information use in decision-making, public organizations
can attempt to obtain, generate, or present performance in­
formation according to the preferences of decision-makers.
Our study informs that decision-makers are particularly at­
tentive to issues that are expressed in objective information
and are compared to a formal target. Second, decision-makers
themselves must be aware of their potentially unconscious
patterns of attention. Such awareness is of particular impor­
tance when prioritization decisions concern societal problems
for which objective information or coercive aspiration levels
are unavailable or inappropriate. An attention-based research
agenda on decision-making in the public sector may prove
pivotal to uncover how the information-processing patterns
of decision-makers ultimately shape the capacity of public
organizations to successfully prioritize and address societal
challenges.

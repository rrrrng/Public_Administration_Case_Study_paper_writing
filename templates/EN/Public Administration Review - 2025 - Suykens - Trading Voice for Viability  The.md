ABSTRACT
Propelled by the New Public Management reforms, the infusion of market values in the public-­nonprofit interface is argued to
have increased nonprofit organizations' (NPOs) capacity to influence public policy through increased access to government, yet
often at the cost of abandoning their critical stance toward the said government. Drawing on cross-­country survey data collected
from NPOs across three different welfare states (N = 779), this study examines to what extent key aspects of nonprofit market-
ization are associated with NPOs refraining from criticizing their funding government. Corroborating the critical nonprofit
marketization literature, we find that compromised advocacy goes hand in hand with high(er) levels of resource competition,
output-­based public control, and recruitment of business-­like staff. These results add to a growing body of evidence of a so-­called
closing or shrinking space for NPOs across different welfare state regimes.
1   |   Introduction
Around the world, nonprofit organizations (NPOs) vie for public
service contracts in quasi-­market settings. Propelled by the New
Public Management (NPM) reforms, quasi-­markets are best un-
derstood as competing grounds for public service contracts, with
contract performance closely monitored by the funding govern-
ment once awarded (Amirkhanyan 2010). Quasi-­market settings
are argued to encourage NPOs to operate in a business-­like way
by, among other things, engaging in commercial venturing (e.g.,
Suykens et al. 2021), hiring business-­like profiles (e.g., Battilana
and Dorado  2010), or introducing corporate management
tools in their day-­to-­day functioning (e.g., Hvenmark  2013).
Interchangeably referred to as nonprofit rationalization
(Hwang and Powell  2009), commercialization (Backman and
Smith 2000), NPOs becoming business-­like (Maier, Meyer, and
Steinbereithner  2016) and nonprofit hybridization toward the
market (Suykens et  al. 2020), we use nonprofit marketization
as our overarching concept to discuss the incidence of business-­
like logics, knowledge, and praces in the nonprofit sector (cf.
Eikenberry and Kluver 2004).
A key point emerging from this body of literature relates to
the equivocal impact on NPOs' engagement in political ad-
vocacy. Commonly understood as NPOs' efforts to influence
public policy (Mosley, Suarez, and Hwang 2023), nonprofit ad-
vocacy can be assumed to be deterred or encouraged by mar-
ketization. From a critical perspective, several studies show

policies. LeRoux and Goerdel  (2009), for example, find that
US human service NPOs limit their advocacy activities when
faced with increasing competition in quasi-­market settings.
Baines, Charlesworth, and Turner  (2014) describe how a
growing managerial focus on documenting human service in-
terventions led social workers across Australia, New Zealand,
and Canada to feel that their advocacy efforts were no longer
considered part of their organizational outcome targets. In a
similar vein, Zhang and Guo (2021) show that, under particu-
lar configurations, NPOs' advocacy efforts are endangered by
commercial venturing.
Other studies, however, suggest that it is not the extent of but the
way in which NPOs engage in advocacy efforts that has changed
due to the NPM reforms. Here, the argument is that public ser-
vice contracting increased nonprofit-­public collaboration and,
more importantly, provided nonprofit executives with increased
access to government. Increased interaction with policymak-
ers may help “nonprofit leaders learn to speak the language of
their legislators, gain a more nuanced understanding of the is-
sues that are important to them, and identify where legislators'
positions are fixed and where they might be swayed” (Johansen
and LeRoux 2013, 358). Furthermore, research shows that the
infusion of business-­like knowledge and practices in NPOs' or-
ganizational functioning encourages a non-­confrontational ap-
proach to advocacy (Onyx et al. 2010). From this perspective,
rather than limiting their advocacy efforts, NPOs are argued to
increasingly advocate by safeguarding a constructive relation-
ship with their funding government (Bloodgood, Tremblay-­
Boire, and Prakash 2014; Mosley 2011).
These arguments in mind, a dearth of studies have exam-
ined to what extent nonprofit marketization goes hand in
hand with NPOs refraining from criticizing their funding
government. Both granting of new-­ and extension of ongo-
ing public contracts may become more uncertain if NPOs
become critics of their funding government (Eikenberry and
Kluver 2004). Hinting at this direction, Arvidson, Johansson,
and Scaramuzzino  (2018) identify resource competition and
NPOs' perception of public funding as critical resources for
organizational viability as potent explanations as to why
NPOs may hold back their criticism toward the government.
This is important to study, as the preference for so-­called con-
structive consensus over more critical attitudes speaks to the
growing concern of a closing or shrinking space for NPOs,
which in turn is argued to harm democracy at large (Toepler
et al. 2023). Mostly examined in authoritarian states, obvious
manifestations of NPOs' shrinking space include the disrup-
tion of international funding flows and restrictions on the
freedom of association rights (Toepler et al. 2020). From this,
reducing NPOs' critical voice in western democracies argu-
ably constitutes a more subtle yet powerful mechanism con-
tributing to the shrinking space frame of reference. Building
on this, we ask:
To what extent is nonprofit marketization associated
with NPOs abandoning their critical voice towards
their funding government?
Drawing on survey data collected among NPOs across
Belgium, the United States, and Sweden (N = 779), we use or-
dinal regression analysis to examine the association between
different manifestations of nonprofit marketization (inde-
pendents) and the likelihood that NPOs refrain from their
critical voice function toward the government (dependent).
The choice for these countries was not arbitrary, as these,
respectively, correspond with the post-­corporatist-­, liberal-­,
and social-­democratic welfare state archetype (Salamon and
Anheier 1998). In addition to strengthening our external va-
lidity, comparing different welfare state types is highly salient
to the topic at hand, as studies show that both nonprofit mar-
ketization (e.g., compare Suykens et al. 2020; Eikenberry and
Kluver 2004; Wijkström 2011) and NPOs' engagement in po-
litical advocacy (e.g., compare Lundberg 2020; Mosley 2011;
Verhoest, Thiel, and Sandra 2016) holds true to different ex-
tents in different welfare state types.
This study contributes twofold to the public administration
literature. Empirically, it verifies to what extent nonprofit
marketization compromises NPOs' advocacy efforts across dif-
ferent institutional contexts. Doing so, our study sheds light
upon subtle shrinking space mechanisms in western democra-
cies. Practically, our study provides insight on which nonprofit
marketization aspects are (un)likely harmful to a more critical
stance toward the government and thus provides actionable
knowledge for nonprofit practitioners on how to navigate non-
profit marketization dynamics without compromising their ad-
vocacy efforts. In the remainder, we formulate our hypotheses
and elaborate on our method. We then present our findings and
conclude with a discussion on the implications for public admin-
istration research and practice.

2.1   |   Outside the Organization
Propelled by the NPM reforms, governments across the world
have established quasi-­markets where NPOs are required to
compete for public service contracts (Amirkhanyan  2010;
Krachler, Greer, and Umney  2022). Once awarded, contract
performance is typically monitored by public officials via stan-
dardized performance management tools to ensure efficient and
effective implementation (Bode 2006). Looking in detail at the
interplay between competition, contracting and control several
similar, yet different mechanisms come to light when it comes to
NPOs' advocacy work.
First, in terms of resource competition, two mechanisms can be
differentiated. On the one hand, NPOs with little slack resources
are likely to draw back from political advocacy when faced with
mounting resource competition. For instance, LeRoux and
Goerdel (2009, 531) find in their study on Michigan NPOs that
“strategically prioritizing organizational tasks, nonprofits reduce
their advocacy activities in the face of increasing competition as
they turn their attention inward to focus on the core mission.” On
the other hand, NPOs that hold sufficient professional capac-
ity are likely to resort to consensual than conflictual advocacy
strategies when faced with resource competition to safeguard
organizational funding (Macindoe and Whalen 2013). Arvidson,
Johansson, and Scaramuzzino  (2018), for instance, finds that
Swedish NPOs confronted with high levels of competition for
public service contracts are twice more likely to hold back their
criticism toward the government.
Second, turning to contract regimes, Frumkin and Andre-­
Clark (2000) argue that NPOs are increasingly forced to focus on
contractor's needs rather than client needs, and doing so, shift
from a value-­driven to an efficiency-­based rationale. Adding to
this, Garrow and Hasenfeld (2012) find that nonprofit service
contractees are less likely to initiate activities in response to
social needs that have a low probability of receiving funding.
Consequently, it is typically argued that NPOs bound by gov-
ernmental contracts seldomly engage in conflictual strategies as
a means to influence political decision-­making as one does not
bite in the hand that feeds them (Mosley 2006).
Third, public control of contract performance is equally argued
to put NPOs' critical stance toward the government at the back
pedal. To demonstrate contract compliance, NPOs typically
need to report on their intermediate performance by means
of standardized performance management tools (Suykens
et al. 2022). The bureaucratic burden tied to these accountability
procedures makes it less likely that NPOs fulfill functions be-
yond the tasks stipulated in the contract. For instance, studying
the impact of NPM-­like reforms on social care agencies across
Australia, New Zealand, and Canada, Baines, Charlesworth,
and Turner (2014) found that community activism and social
change work were virtually abandoned due to NPOs' adherence
to contractual obligations. Hence, on the whole, to cope with a
changed policy environment, NPOs are theorized to focus on
public service provision at the cost of voicing criticism toward
the government in order to safeguard organizational resources
(Arvidson, Johansson, and Scaramuzzino 2018). Following from
this, we hypothesize that: the likelihood that nonprofits refrain
from criticizing the government increases when nonprofits per-
ceive (H1) high levels of resource competition, (H2) themselves as
public contractors, and (H3) high levels of output-­based public
control.
2.2   |   Inside the Organization
In addition to quasi-­market engagement, NPOs can become
more similar to business enterprises at the organizational level.
A key manifestation of marketization at this level of analysis is
arguably managerialism, which is best understood as the belief
that organizations are best governed through corporate man-
agement knowledge and practices (Hvenmark  2016). Among
others, clear indications of nonprofit managerialism consti-
tute the recruitment of employees because of their corporate
management background (e.g., Battilana and Dorado 2010) as
well as the introduction of corporate management tools (e.g.,
Hvenmark  2013). Essentially, the argument is that corporate
knowledge and practices induce a rational approach to advo-
cacy (Suykens, De Rynck, and Verschuere 2022). In this con-
text, NPOs are expected to behave as responsible partners that
chose compromise and consensus over dissent and conflict (cf.
Bloodgood, Tremblay-­Boire, and Prakash 2014; Polanska 2023).
This change of attitude can be subtle. In his auto-­ethnographic
account, King (2017, 254) described how he felt himself chang-
ing from “an ‘idealistic dreamer’ concerned with social inequal-
ity to (as a nonprofit professional) seeing the course clients as
self-­regulating subjects, responsible, and responsibilized for their
own conditions” due to the process of making his organization
more business-­like. Indeed, rather than resisting the neoliberal
premises shaping present day inequality, business-­like nonprofit
practitioners are more likely to take these for granted. Following
from this, we hypothesize that the likelihood that nonprofits re-
frain from criticizing the government increases when nonprofits
(H4) extensively use business-­like management tools, and (H5)
intentionally hire employees because of their business expertise.
A second key manifestation of nonprofit marketization at the or-
ganizational level constitutes nonprofit commercialism, which
typically materializes through the development of profitable
selling schemes (Hung 2020; Suykens et al. 2021). In the past de-
cade, researchers have engaged in a vivid debate on the impact
of different sources of income on NPOs' advocacy efforts and
strategies (e.g., Lu 2018a; Zhang and Guo 2021). Studies elabo-
rating on the effect of commercial income paint a diffuse picture.
Whereas some find a negative association between commercial
income and nonprofit advocacy (e.g., Suárez and Hwang 2008),
others provide evidence for a null (e.g., Suykens, De Rynck, and
Verschuere 2022), a positive (e.g., Fyall and Allard 2017), or a
curvilinear relationship (e.g., Dong, Lu, and Lee 2022). Keeping
the empirical focus of this study in mind—i.e., not the extent to
which NPOs engage in advocacy efforts, but the likelihood that
NPOs abandon their critical voice—one could argue that com-
mercial income allows NPOs to decrease financial dependence
from the government and, thus, have more room for adopting
a critical stance toward the government. Mosley (2012, 861) for
instance finds that public funding serves as an incentive for

3   |   Materials and Methods
3.1   |   Data Collection
This study draws on cross-­country data collected from NPOs
active in Belgium (N = 559), US (N = 349), and Sweden (N = 535).
Compared to international comparisons on public agen-
cies (Verhoest, Thiel, and Sandra  2016), top public managers
(Hammerschmid, de Walle, and Steven  2016), and local gov-
ernments (Kim et al. 2013), NPOs are arguably a more volatile
unit of analysis. Here, international comparison is hampered
by a lack of (a) cross-­national agreement as to which organiza-
tions are counted as “nonprofit” (Salamon and Sokolowski 2016)
and (b) valid population parameters (Searing, Grasse, and
Rutherford  2022). Taking this into account, we aimed to
strengthen population equivalence twofold.
First, all NPOs included in our sampling conform to the oper-
ational–structural definition of Salamon and Anheier  (1992),
which essentially argues that key-­defining traits of NPOs consti-
tute (a) being formally organized, (b) founded by private citizens,
(c) self-­governing, (d) voluntary participation, and (e) adherence
to (a limited) distribution constraint. Second, in line with previ-
ous cross-­country comparisons in the field of public administra-
tion (e.g., Verhoest et al. 2018), this data collection was executed
by a research consortium consisting of one researcher per par-
ticipating country. This enabled us to take advantage of existing
population databases:
•	 For Belgium, we departed from an existing database of
Flemish NPOs active in the human well-­being (N = 603),
sociocultural (N = 1713), and social economy sector
(N = 159). This database was the result of a systematic
mapping via desktop research and elite interviews in 2017.
These NPOs are predominantly active at the regional
level, meaning that they (a) provide services beyond the
local community, (b) engage in politicization or advocacy
efforts aimed at the supra-­local level, and/or (c) are um-
brella organizations covering the Flemish region. Human
well-­being NPOs include care initiatives targeting mi-
nors, people with a disability, or underprivileged people.
The social economy sector consists of work integration
social enterprises, which provide vocational training to
disadvantaged workers. The sociocultural sector, on the
contrary, is more diverse. Here, professional associations,
patient associations, social rights movements, political
organizations, sociocultural associations, youth associa-
tions, ethnic-­cultural associations, and faith-­based asso-
ciations are included. The choice to utilize this mapping
database—and not public databases—has arguments for
and against. On a critical note, several fields of activity

four digital reminders were sent. In addition, printed surveys
were sent out in both Belgium and the US with a $1500 budget
each.2 Ultimately, we achieved a full response rate of 18.29%
(Belgium), 16.04% (Sweden), and 11.91% (US).
3.2   |   Measures
3.2.1   |   Dependent Variable
The dependent variable of this study is compromised ad-
vocacy. Building on the study of Arvidson, Johansson, and
Scaramuzzino  (2018), respondents were asked on a 9-­point
Likert scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree) to indicate to
what extent the following dynamic occurred at their organiza-
tion: “Sometimes, my organization holds back criticism towards
the government for the purpose of not jeopardizing economic sup-
port.” Here, descriptive analysis learns that our data are right-­
skewed, indicating that the majority of organizations do not
display this behavior (see Figure 2). Underlining the relevance
of our research question, however, we find that 18.4% of the re-
spondents agreed to varying degrees with this statement.
3.2.2   |   Independent Variables
In line with our theoretical framework, nonprofit marketization
is measured twofold. At the public-­nonprofit level, we focus on
resource competition, public contracting, and output-­based pub-
lic control. Resource competition was measured by asking re-
spondents to indicate on a 4-­point Likert scale (“no competition”
to “strong competition”) to what extent their organization com-
peted for income with (a) “other nonprofit organizations”, (b)
“government agencies”, and (c) “business enterprises.” To get a
more general measure for resource competition, we constructed
an index by calculating the sum scores (Cronbach Alpha = 0.70).
For public contracting and control, we asked the respondents
to indicate on a 5-­point Likert scale (“not at all” to “a lot”) to
what extent the government most important to them (a) “views
my organization as a public service delivery contractor” and (b)
“controls the output of my organization.”
At the organizational level, we focused on management tool
use, recruitment of business-­like professionals, and commer-
cial income. Management tool use was measured by asking the
respondents to indicate on a 5-­point Likert scale (not applicable
to often used) to what degree they utilized the following man-
agement tools: SWOT analysis, SMART analysis, lean man-
agement, benchmarking, pay for performance, quality norms,
key performance indicators, business process reengineering,
total quality management, and the balanced scorecard. In

3.2.3   |   Control Variables
To control for potential spurious relationships, we added several
control variables in our model. First, we included country dum-
mies to account for the different welfare state types. Although
both insider (e.g., lobbying in parliament) and outsider strategies
(e.g., organized protests and strikes) can be observed across, dif-
ferent nonprofit advocacy traditions can be discerned in differ-
ent welfare state types. An emblematic case of a post-­corporatist
welfare state, Belgian NPOs are historically intertwined with
the state in terms of funding, policy formulation, and public ser-
vice delivery (Suykens et al. 2021). In this context, a large pro-
fessionalized nonprofit sector typically combines public service
delivery with policy advocacy through non-­conflictual insider
strategies (Verschuere and De Corte 2015). Resonating with the
social-­democratic welfare state type, the majority of the Swedish
nonprofit sphere consists of volunteer-­driven, member-­based as-
sociations that bring citizens together around shared interests
and leisure activities. Traditionally, larger federative bodies rep-
resent the interests of these associations and their members to
policymakers (Hvenmark  2008; Lundberg  2012). Another im-
portant, yet smaller segment constitutes large professionalized
nonprofit human service providers. In Sweden, both the feder-
ative bodies and large nonprofit human service providers are
structurally consulted via the remiss procedure through which
NPOs—and for that matter public agencies and market actors—
are able to provide written considerations to policy proposals
(Lundberg 2020). This institutionalized pathway contrasts with
how nonprofit advocacy unfolds in the United States. A typical
example of a liberal welfare state type, the US nonprofit sector
can be described as large, professionalized, and operating at
arms-­length of the government (Salamon and Anheier  1998).
Here, nonprofit advocacy unfolds more ambiguously. Often,
nonprofit service providers incorporated under the 501(c)(3)
legal status tend to refrain from advocacy efforts as they fear to
be “found in violation of IRS regulations which restrict them from
attempting to influence legislation as a ‘substantial part’ of their
activities” (Macindoe and Whalen  2013, 121). Consequently,
NPOs primarily focusing on political advocacy typically incor-
porate as 501(c)(4), thereby relinquishing the tax-­exemption on
donations made to their organization (Beaton, MacIndoe, and
Wang 2021).
Second, in terms of sector, respondents were asked to indi-
cate their primary field of activity within the International
Classification of Nonprofit Organizations (Salamon and
Anheier 1996). Given that one of the key concerns related to mar-
ketization goes out to the idea that NPOs are reduced to public
service providers that largely forsake on advocacy (e.g., Baines,
Charlesworth, and Turner 2014; Eikenberry and Kluver 2004),
we included a dummy indicating a primary focus on social

NPOs set out to influence public policy by asking respondents
on a 5-­point Likert scale (“not at all” to “a lot”) whether “A
role of my organization is (to try) to influence policy makers.”
Here, the argument is that advocacy-­driven organizations are
less likely to refute their critical voice function than NPOs
for which advocacy is a secondary activity. In a similar vein,
influencing public policy demands organizational capacity
(MacIndoe  2014). To account for differences in professional
capacity, respondents were asked to indicate the number of
volunteers and professionals their organization had. In order
to alleviate respondents' burden, we used the ordinal classes
for both questions (1 = 0; 2 = 1–2; 3 = 3–10; 4 = 11–50; 5 = 51–
150; 6 = 151–500; 7 = Over 500). Subsequently, we calculated
a professionalization ratio by dividing the number of profes-
sionals by the number of volunteers. Lastly, we controlled for
public and private income streams by asking to what extent
the respondent's organization depended on “income derived
from the governments” (%) and “income derived from private
contributions” (%). Here, the rationale is that the critical voice
function is more likely performed when NPOs are less depen-
dent on public income and more dependent on private income
(Arvidson, Johansson, and Scaramuzzino 2018).
3.3   |   Statistical Analyses
Our dependent variable, compromised advocacy, is measured
by a 9-­point Likert scale. Therefore, we run ordered logistic
regression models in STATA to predict NPOs' advocacy role
toward governments. However, ordinal variables are often
treated as interval in data analysis practice, so we also run
ordinary least-­squares regression models to test if the re-
sults persist. Moreover, we applied multiple imputation (MI)
technique to handle missing data (Enders 2022) because our
six focal variables have a higher share of missing data ranging
from 10.1% (compromised advocacy) to 22.34% (management
tool use). We specify chained equations, with 40 imputations,
to perform the models, due primarily to our dependent vari-
able that takes on specific values from 1 to 9 (White, Royston,
and Wood 2011). According to Newman (2003), the MI ap-
proach yields smaller error in model estimations among var-
ious missing data techniques. Common method bias (CMB)
is a risk in our study design as our dependent and indepen-
dent variables are measured through the same survey. We
adopt two remedies suggested by Podsakoff, MacKenzie, and
Podsakoff  (2012) to minimize potential CMB. First, our re-
spondents did not knew that the survey aimed to study non-
profit marketization and advocacy. Instead, we introduced
the survey to them by stating that “we aim to examine under
what conditions practices like revenue diversification, entre-
preneurship and performance measurement are (not) a good
idea for nonprofit organizations.” Second, we applied (a) spa-
tial separation (i.e., buffer items between the variables at in-
terest) and (b) variating scale properties (different scales used
to measure the dependent and independent variables).

finds that compromised advocacy is (a) displayed by 18% of
the surveyed respondents and (b) explained by key aspects of
nonprofit marketization. In specific, three points of discussion
emerge. First, looking at the public-­nonprofit relationship, our
study lends credence to the critical nonprofit marketization
camp as we find that both resource competition and output-­
based public control increase the likelihood that NPOs refrain
from criticizing the government (cf. Arvidson, Johansson, and
Scaramuzzino  2018; Eikenberry and Kluver  2004). Adding
substance to this observation is the notion that this finding
holds true across three different welfare states and is modeled
as a linear relationship, meaning that the more intense re-
source competition and output-­based public control becomes,
the more likely NPOs are to refrain from governmental crit-
icism. In contrast, public service contracting is unrelated to
compromised advocacy. Whereas both resource competition
and output-­based public control are best understood as a di-
rect pressure to nonprofit viability, public service contract
terms are often subject of negotiation and, thus, may not by
default compromise NPOs' advocacy efforts. Indeed, public-­
nonprofit collaboration is best viewed from a stewardship—
and not an agency—perspective, as NPOs are often considered
interesting governmental partners because of their expertise,
community reputation, and/or proven track record on human
service delivery (Gazley 2008). We encourage future research
to examine which contractual configurations are (un)likely to
compromise nonprofit advocacy. For example, both contract
duration and NPOs' room to negotiate are likely negatively as-
sociated with the idea that NPOs refrain from criticizing their
funding government.
Second, looking at the organizational level, we find that man-
agerial hiring increases the likelihood of compromised advo-
cacy. This resonates with the idea that business-­like recruitment
serves as a carrier for business-­like norms, ideas, and practices
(Battilana and Dorado 2010; Hwang and Powell 2009), and, as
such, managerial hiring may seep in the idea that political ad-
vocacy is most effectively performed by rational professionals
seeking compromise over conflict (Brusseel, Raeymaeckers,
and Verschuere 2024). This said, and similar to nonprofit con-
tracting, the use of corporate management tools in itself may
not be sufficient to explain NPOs' compromised advocacy, as
insights resulting from said tools can be used in a constructive
or conflictual way. Turning to organizational income, and echo-
ing with the equivocal findings of existing research (e.g., com-
pare Dong, Lu, and Lee 2022; Fyall and Allard 2017; Lu 2018b;
Suárez and Hwang 2008; Suykens, De Rynck, and Verschuere
2022), we find that nonprofit commercialism is unrelated to the
likelihood of NPOs criticizing the government. This may suggest
that nonprofit commercialism is perhaps best understood as an
interaction term with both organizational and environmental
conditions affecting nonprofit advocacy rather than serving as
a one-­on-­one explanation (cf. Zhang and Guo 2021). Here again,
not commercialization in itself, but how and to what extent and
in what type of environment an NPO decides to commercialize
likely determine the outcomes of commercialization, making it
an opportunity for some and a bad idea for others (Hung and
Suykens  2023). Accordingly, future research could examine
how contextual variables at the organizational and field levels
are associated with different outcomes of nonprofit manageri-
alism and commercialism, thereby providing a more situated
understanding under what conditions these phenomena are
(un)likely to purposefully to work in NPOs (cf. Chandra and
Kerlin 2021).
Third, our control variables shed light to what extent “context
matters.” While findings at the organizational level are unsur-
prising (e.g., advocacy-­oriented NPOs are less likely to com-
promise on advocacy) and insignificant at the field level (e.g.,
membership of the social service sector), the notion that NPOs in
the US are more inclined to refrain from criticizing the govern-
ment in comparison to Swedish and Belgian NPOs is intriguing.
While further qualitative examination is necessary to unravel
the underlying causal mechanisms, we suspect that the his-
torical role and position of NPOs in liberal welfare states—i.e.,
provision of welfare services without extensive public funding
(Salamon and Anheier 1998)—induce NPOs to hire professional
expertise focused on service provision and not advocacy. Adding
to this, the US is known for having complex regulations on non-
profit lobbying (MacIndoe 2014), making it arguably more diffi-
cult for NPOs without specialist staff dedicated to advocacy to
adopt a critical stance toward the government.
6   |   Limitations
Before concluding, it is important to acknowledge the limita-
tions of our study. First, compromised advocacy is likely a sub-
tle organizational reflex that is strategically displayed by NPOs.
To capture this subtility, we modified the survey item proposed
by Arvidson, Johansson, and Scaramuzzino (2018) twofold, i.e.,
(a) addition of “sometimes” to the statement and (b) the use of
a 9-­point—instead of a 4-­point—Likert scale. Although this
study shows that some key aspects of nonprofit marketization
increase the likelihood of NPOs compromising their advocacy,
we welcome future studies to unravel under what circumstances
business-­like NPOs decide to do so. Second, we realize that our
survey measures do not grasp nonprofit marketization in full
(for an overview, see Maier, Meyer, and Steinbereithner 2016).
The conceptual focus of our study was incentivized by the
idea that despite the cross-­country contextual variation, all re-
spondents could reflect on their relationship with the govern-
ment, as well as their organizational income and management
practices. Second, although counter-­measures with regard to
cross-­cultural equivalence (Tsai et  al.  2022), social desirabil-
ity bias (Larson 2019), and CMB (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, and
Podsakoff 2012) were taken, these issues can never be ruled out
completely in a cross-­sectional study design based on perceptual
measures. In addition, the use of cross-­sectional data does not
allow causality claims nor to assess how the observed relation-
ships evolve over time. Here, repeating the survey over time to
create panel data and/or employ experimental research designs
may offer a promising way forward. Lastly, although our cross-­
country research design strengthens external validity, our sur-
vey samples are best understood as NPOs embedded in, and not
representative for, different welfare state types.

Increasingly, public administration scholars point to a so-­called
closing or shrinking space for NPOs (Toepler et al. 2020, 2023).
In essence, the idea of a shrinking space refers to a wide range
of different ways to constrain NPOs' political influence in so-
ciety. In this study, we focus on one shrinking space dynamic,
i.e., the idea that NPOs hold back their criticism toward the gov-
ernment to safeguard public funding. The take-­home argument
of our study lies in the realization that (a) this dynamic is not
widespread across different welfare state types in Western de-
mocracies, yet (b) is associated with key aspects of nonprofit mar-
ketization (cf. Arvidson, Johansson, and Scaramuzzino 2018).
Moreover, as none of the nonprofit marketization manifestations
were found to increase NPOs' critical voice, our study cautiously
corroborates the notion that “the incorporation of private-­market
values by either or both [the public and nonprofit sectors] appears
to be detrimental to democracy” (Eikenberry and Kluver 2004,
138). Cautiously, as our findings evoke intriguing questions for
further research. What is precisely to be understood by criticism
and how is it best voiced? If societal space is likely to shrink for
business-­like NPOs, does this imply political opportunities for
non-­business-­like NPOs? Accordingly, we welcome more fine-­
grained analysis of the large-­N patterns presented here. Until
then, our study commends caution for both public administra-
tion practitioners when engaging with nonprofit marketization.

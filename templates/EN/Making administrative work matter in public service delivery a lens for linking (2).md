Abstract
Among the general public as well as in the scientific literature, administrative work is widely associated with heavy bureaucratic procedures
that are disconnected from serving clients. Less is said and written about the importance of administrative work in delivering public service.
Drawing on a relational theoretical approach and based on an ethnographic field study in two municipal child welfare units in Denmark (including
38 days of observations and 30 interviews), this study shows how administrative work plays three key functions in various accountability rela­
tions and that these functions aid street-level bureaucrats in mastering the complexities of their work. The study offers a theoretical framework
that delineates the functions of administrative work in complex street-level practice. By demonstrating how administrative work may contribute
positively to fulfilling the purposes of street-level work, this study contributes to developing our understanding of administrative work as an in­
valuable part of street-level work and provides a more nuanced foundation for future studies on the virtues and issues of administrative work.
Key words: relational theory; functions of administrative work; ethnographic fieldwork; street-level bureaucrats; Danish child welfare service.
Introduction
Administrative work in the form of record-keeping and
filing has long been recognized as a key element in ensuring
democratic accountability (Brehm and Gates 1999; du Gay
2000; Weatherley et al. 1980). Yet, often connected with a
prevailing critical view on bureaucracy (see du Gay 2000;
Gouldner 1954; Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022), adminis­
trative work evokes aversion among professionals, the public,
and in the scholarly literature. In the scholarly literature, the
critical view on administrative work is seen in the literature
on administrative burdens (Halling and Baekgaard 2023).
Further, from the perspective of street-level bureaucrats, the
generation of red tape (unnecessary rules and procedures) has
been connected with experiences of alienation (DeHart-Davis
and Pandey 2005), with meaninglessness (van Loon et al.
2016), and with decreasing job satisfaction because red tape
takes time from working with the client (Steijn and van der
Voet 2019). Further, record-keeping has been shown to be at
odds with best practice (Møller and Hill 2021; Smith 2010).
Building on these insights, Tiggelaar and her colleagues
(2023, p. 1) investigate administrative tasks as an “unavoid­
able yet burdensome” part of street-level work that street-
level bureaucrats must cope with. The present study offers a
theoretical framework that turns this picture around by shed­
ding light on the virtues of administrative work from the per­
spective of street-level bureaucrats.
Even though effective rules and regulations (DeHart-
Davis, Davis, and Mohr 2014) and the purposeful and en­
abling elements of record-keeping related to performance
information (Høybye-Mortensen and Ejbye-Ernst 2018;
Raaphorst 2023) have recently received some attention, only
a few studies have closely examined the everyday adminis­
trative practice of record-keeping at the street-level (Weller
2012) and its functions. The context in which such record-
keeping is practiced is complex; any street-level bureaucratic
office must fulfill its instituted purpose and primary tasks
(Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022) while balancing competing
objectives and values under circumstances of uncertainty and
emotional proximity (Zacka 2017). To understand the com­
plex nature of public service delivery more fully, I argue that
we need to further examine the role of administrative work in
practice and its relationship with the purpose of office.
To develop our understanding of administrative work,
I draw on a relational approach (Emirbayer 1997) that
anticipates that administrative tasks derive their significance
from the functions they play in everyday transactions be­
tween street-level bureaucrats and the web of accountability
relations they are situated within (Hupe and Hill 2007). The
relational approach provides a theoretical lens that allows
me to look beyond individual behavior and to challenge the
dualism (Feldman and Orlikowski 2011) between adminis­
trative tasks and working with the client that is assumed in
much of the previous research on administrative work and
public service delivery. This enables me to develop and add
nuances and complexity to our understanding of the purpose
of administrative work. Using this theoretical lens, I analyze
empirical material from an ethnographic field study among
caseworkers in two Danish child welfare units and address
the research question: Which functions do administrative
tasks play in the everyday practice of street-level bureaucrats,

On this background, I contribute to existing theory on ad­
ministrative work in public administration as I develop a theo­
retical framework that delineates the functions administrative
work plays in the complex everyday practice of street-level
bureaucrats. I define administrative work as record-keeping
and filing but not as tasks such as coordination and HR
processes. The insights gained in developing the theoretical
framework have three implications for existing theory on
public administration: (1) by showing how administrative
work is a vital part of fulfilling the instituted purpose of of­
fice, this study’s insights challenge the widespread dualism
expressed in the literature, among professionals, and among
the public that frames administrative work as disconnected
from serving clients; (2) the insights contribute to theory on
regulation by suggesting that we distinguish between mana­
gerialism and bureaucracy; and (3) they stimulate discussion
of the concept of coping by proposing the potential of devel­
oping a self-contained concept of “mastering” the complex
nature of street-level work, in order to expand our theoretical
and empirical means of identifying mechanisms and practices
in how street-level bureaucrats succeed in balancing the com­
plexity of street-level work without compromising either their
vocational and democratic obligations, or citizens.
In the following section, I discuss the current literature on
administrative work in public service delivery and introduce
the relational approach as a tool to explore the functions of
administrative work. I then present this study’s research con­
text along with the study’s ethnographic research methods,
followed by the findings. On the basis of these findings, I
present a theoretical framework of the functions of adminis­
trative work and discuss three theoretical implications of this
framework for existing public administration theory.
Theoretical background
The literature on street-level bureaucracy highlights the mul­
tifaceted complexities of public service delivery that street-
level bureaucrats should master rather than merely tolerate
or reduce (see Tummers et al. 2015). Granted a considerable
amount of discretion (Lipsky 2010), street-level bureaucrats
must work in accordance with an increasing number of
policies and rules (Hupe 2019) and balance multiple bureau­
cratic values such as respect, responsiveness, efficiency, fair­
ness, and transparency (Hood 2007; Zacka 2017) in a context
often characterized by insufficient resources (Brodkin 1997;
Hupe and Buffat 2014). Moreover, public service delivery is
often characterized by moral and emotional proximity (Nisar
and Masood 2020; Zacka 2017). In practice, street-level
bureaucrats face multiple ambiguities and uncertainties; they
continuously face problems in relation to how they should in­
terpret and act upon the information they receive (Raaphorst
2018). They must conscientiously prioritize between dif­
ferent clients and equally desirably but sometimes conflicting
values (Møller and Grøn 2023). Street-level bureaucrats must
also categorize clients in accordance with abstract rules and
regulations while seeing the individual client and responding
holistically to their situation (Lipsky 2010; Maynard-Moody
and Musheno 2012; Nisar and Masood 2020).
Much research on street-level bureaucrats’ perspectives
on administrative work in public service delivery rests on
an assumption of dualism. Such research either assumes ad­
ministrative work does not help in actually delivering public
service and is therefore disconnected from public service de­
livery (Steijn and van der Voet 2019; Tiggelaar, Groeneveld,
and George 2023), or shows how administrative work and
service delivery are often disconnected because administrative
tasks deter street-level bureaucrats from responding to and
doing what works for the client (Dubois 2010; 24; Møller
and Hill 2021; Riccucci 2007; Smith 2010).
In contrast to the dualism assumed in the studies above, one
can argue that administrative work serves multiple purposes
in helping to deliver public service. This is a guiding ethos in
the present study; that is, administrative work may facilitate
the delivery of public services. Legally, case records ensure
transparency and establish which services a client is entitled
to receive (Brehm and Gates 1999; Weber 1995). Further,
from the perspectives of professionals, record-keeping forms
the basis for assessing the need for interventions (Møller and
Hill 2021), and from the perspectives of both professionals
and clients, records serve as a tool for preserving knowl­
edge and memory on cases (Hoyle et al. 2019; Prince 1996).
Moreover, from a political-administrative perspective,
record-keeping enables performance monitoring and man­
agement (Møller and Hill 2021). The theoretical framework
presented in this study is further inspired by recent literature
that, building on the emphasis Weberian bureaucracy places
on “the bureaucratic ethos” (du Gay 2000), proposes an
ethics-of-office approach to street-level work, highlighting
that professional knowledge and expertise are prerequisites
for delivering public service in accordance with such ethics-
of-office (Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022). This perspec­
tive emphasizes that specific street-level offices have distinct
purposes and tasks and that street-level bureaucrats are ex­
pected to engage in casuistry, such that, when handling in­
dividual cases, they integrate their skills, competences, and
experiences with generalized forms of knowledge, rules,
and procedures (Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022, p. 8; see
also Zacka 2017, p. 167). On this background, an ethos is
constituted that “is guided by the specific duties, obligations,
and ethical norms necessary for casuistically integrating
rules and discretion and balancing empathy and distance
in the particular situation at hand” (Møller, Pedersen, and
Pors 2022, p. 8). In line with current streams in the liter­
ature on street-level bureaucracy (Evans and Hupe 2020;
Maynard-Moody and Musheno 2012; Zacka 2017), this
perspective highlights how street-level bureaucrats handle—
or master—the complexities of street-level work. Regarding
administrative work, this mastery includes an obligation to
conduct administrative work in manners that comply with
the distinct purposes of the street-level bureaucrat’s office,
and which include a requirement to articulate, explicate, and
document decisions and actions (Møller 2019), and mobi­
lize knowledge (Møller 2022) that may support professional
reflection on cases (Raaphorst 2023). Consequently, admin­
istrative work may serve as a factor that aids street-level
bureaucrats in mastering the complexities of their work.
Building on these insights, I draw on a relational theoretical
approach (Emirbayer 1997) to develop our understanding of
the functions of administrative work in public service delivery
from the perspective of street-level bureaucrats. With its
focus on the constitutive role of relations and dynamics that
make up everyday practice, the relational approach offers

a particularly well-suited analytical tool for theorizing the

From a relational point of view, “the units involved in a
transaction derive their meaning, significance, and identity
from the (changing) functional roles they play in a transaction.
The latter, seen as a dynamic, unfolding process, becomes the
primary unit of analysis rather than the constituent elements
themselves” (Emirbayer 1997, p. 287). Ontologically, then,
this approach embarks on the assumption that phenomena
are not independent existences that can be ‘given’ in iso­
lation, but rather phenomena that are always embedded
within their actual situational context (Emirbayer 1997).
Likewise, in using a relational lens and following the work
of Feldman and Orlikowski (2011), phenomena are assumed
to be produced through a process of mutual constitution. As
such, relationality should not be confused with interpersonal
(Feldman and Orlikowski 2011). Rather, relationality implies
that I conceive the functions of administrative work as “col­
lective accomplishments” (Wagenaar 2004, p. 644).
For this study, the relational approach implies that the
functions of administrative work exist relationally and are
produced through processes of mutual constitution between
caseworkers and significant others. Hence, the key unit of
analysis in this study is not the individual caseworkers but
rather the multiple formal and informal accountability rela­
tions that the caseworkers act within and in relation to and
through which administrative work unfolds in everyday ac­
tivity. These include the state, the market, the profession, and
citizens (society) (Hupe and Hill 2007; Thomann, Hupe, and
Sager 2018). Moreover, I distinguish peer-level accountability
from professional accountability, in accordance with Zacka’s
work (2017, 2019). The concept of peer-level accounta­
bility emphasizes differences in style and moral sensibilities
among colleagues within a unit; this accountability is
generated through the colleagues’ criticism, deliberations,
and confrontations in relation to one another. In light of the
concepts of the bureaucratic ethos, I also distinguish between
accountability to a profession and accountability to the office
guided by the bureaucratic ethos and vocation. Finally, the
accountability relation to citizens may be understood through
the principles of participatory accountability and direct ac­
countability that stem from the citizens’ rights to complain
(Hupe and Hill 2007; Zacka 2019).
In line with the relational theoretical approach, this study
perceives administrative work as administrative practices
(Feldman and Orlikowski 2011) that are constituted in the
interface between rules and regulations, peers, managers
and encounters with clients that together make up everyday
public administration (Wagenaar 2004). By taking such a
relational practice approach to studying administrative work,
I am able to explore the nuances of record-keeping and sit­
uate different practices of record-keeping within the web of
accountability relations and complex contexts through which
record-keeping unfolds in everyday activity.
Furthermore, this study’s approach to investigating admin­
istrative work as a relational practice allows us to challenge
and move beyond the dualism (Feldman and Orlikowski
2011, p. 1242) of working in a bureaucracy with administra­
tive obligations and working with the client in a responsive
and caring manner and thereby allows us to develop a more
nuanced understanding of everyday practice in street-level
bureaucracies. Further, by emphasizing meaning, the theoret­
ical framework connects with narrativity and the creation of
meaning through stories as fundamental for human behavior
(Andersen 2020, p. 320). As such, the analysis has two layers:
administrative work as created in narratives among street-
level bureaucrats and administrative work as created in the
functions it plays in everyday practice.
Data and methodology
Research context and data collection
Caseworkers in child welfare perform one of the most rad­
ical tasks of the state. They have the statutory duty to en­
gage with families, the most private sphere of the citizen,
and ultimately have the authority to remove children from
their parents against their will. In the words of Lipsky, these
characteristics make these caseworkers “the ultimate street-
level bureaucrats” (Lipsky 2010, p. 233). Caseworkers in
child welfare are engaged in high-stake decisions in a context
characterized by complex rules and regulations (Hestbæk et
al. 2023), close and frequent interactions (Harrits 2016), un­
certain information on families’ situations, and lack of con­
trol of the sometimes uproarious lives of these families. Thus,
the caseworkers’ assessments and decisions bear the imma­
nent risk of serious errors that can potentially harm children,
which in addition may trigger public scandals (Lipsky 2010).
Child welfare workers are deeply committed to the care of
children (Pösö and Forsman 2013), while they simultaneously
have to complete “a large amount of redundant paperwork
within strict timelines” (Westbrook, Ellis, and Ellet 2008, p.
48), which has been criticized for limiting caseworkers’ pro­
fessional freedom and for burdening them at the expense of
client contact (Posborg, Nørrelykke, and Antczak 2009). Yet,
considering the extensive nature of the authority delegated
to the child welfare services, the administrative obligation of
record-keeping would seem a necessary part of protecting cit­
izens’ legal rights. Hence, child welfare services represent an
extreme case (Flyvbjerg 2006) of the duty of record-keeping,
which makes it a case well-suited for generating theoretical
insights on the purposeful functions of administrative tasks
in practice.
The provision of child welfare in Denmark has its roots in
the Scandinavian welfare state model (Berrick et al. 2017).
The overall aim of the Danish legislation on child wel­
fare is to provide all children and young people with equal
opportunities for self-expression, health, and personal devel­
opment (Hestbæk et al. 2023). Whereas the US and the UK
systems primarily are risk- and service-oriented and highly
regulated, the Danish system is more needs-oriented. This
means that at-risk families receive protection services that are
predominantly voluntarily received based on a cooperative
approach (Hestbæk et al. 2023). The main responsibility for
administering child welfare legislation lies with Denmark’s 98
municipalities and their child welfare units.
Within the last 10 years, the central government in Denmark
has focused on the debureaucratization of public welfare serv­
ices and simplifying regulations in this area (Askim, Hjelmar,
and Pedersen 2018). In connection with this, the central gov­
ernment has launched a pilot project in selected municipalities
that has exempted their municipal child welfare units from a
number of rules and regulations on child welfare work. This
has thus changed the regulatory frame for child welfare work

This study draws on five months of fieldwork, approxi­
mately two and a half months in each municipality, which
took place from August to December 2019 (see Appendix 1
for more details and an overview of the fieldwork). The field­
work comprised 38 days of observations using the shadowing
observation technique (Czarniawska 2007), interviews with
23 caseworkers and seven interviews with managers of
the caseworkers. I shadowed 10 caseworkers during their
daily routines, one social worker at a time, which meant I
could follow them during their administrative tasks, in­
ternal meetings, and interactions with clients over e-mail,
telephone, and physical meetings. The caseworkers were
situated in different units and included both experienced and
less experienced caseworkers in both municipalities. Field
notes were written during observations or immediately after,
which allowed me to document conversations almost ver­
batim. At the end of the day, field notes were checked, and
jottings were expanded. In a separate document, I noted my
own responses and reflections on different situations and
experiences (Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw 2011). All shadowed
caseworkers were interviewed along with 12 caseworkers
whom I recruited as I met them in the hallways, in their
offices, or through e-mails I sent them. I prioritized including
caseworkers with different numbers of years of experience
and from different groups within each child welfare unit. All
interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and coded in
NVivo. Special ethical considerations had to be taken in terms
of the data used, as the data included highly sensitive informa­
tion on clients, vulnerable children and their families. These
considerations relate especially to the aspects of consent, con­
fidentiality and sensitive topics. I continually negotiated the
consent, for instance, by always making sure that my partic­
ipation and the purpose of my participation, in both internal
meetings and meetings with parents and children in partic­
ular, was clear to all participants. Further, all data are stored
according to the guidelines set by the Danish Data Protection
Agency. All participant names in the article are pseudonyms.
Data analysis
The analysis presented in this article is provided on the back­
ground of an analysis of field notes and interviews with
caseworkers and their managers. The material was analyzed
in accordance with an abductive approach through multiple
phases of open and focused coding (see Appendix 2). Initially,
I did an open reading of the data material (Schwartz-Shea and
Yanow 2013) to discover elements within everyday work that
caseworkers associated with satisfaction and dissatisfaction.
The initial reading of the material resulted in a number of
subthemes, among which bureaucracy was one. Reading
through the data on this theme both in interviews and in field
notes on conversations among the participants, I noticed a
tense relationship between the caseworkers and their admin­
istrative work, which simultaneously induced “negative” and
“positive” feelings in the caseworkers. The “positive” elements
of administrative work were less explicitly formulated, such
as in this quote from a caseworker in municipality A: “But,
there are all these rules and frameworks, which I think can be
useful in a way. You just have to be careful that administrative
tasks don’t take over and become the only thing we do.” To
make sense of this tension and the “usefulness,” or functional
role, I decided to investigate the caseworkers’ administrative
practice more closely through a relational theoretical frame­
work, and therefore I returned to the interviews and espe­
cially the field notes and read them again with this relational
focus in mind.
The analysis of the functions of administrative work in
everyday interactions and practice presented here is prima­
rily based on field notes from observations of caseworkers
doing administrative work and from their interactions
in conversations and discussions among caseworkers,
caseworkers, and their managers. I used the interviews to nu­
ance my interpretations of my observations and as a trian­
gulation to check and critically confront my interpretations
(Schwartz-Shea and Yanow 2009). I have selected “proof
quotes” and “power quotes” (Pratt 2008) that serve as
illustrations of the findings of my analysis, see Appendix 3.
Proof quotes show the prevalence of a point, whereas power
quotes are quotes that are “poetic, concise, or insightful”
(Pratt 2008, p. 501) in regard to illustrating the patterns
found in the data.
Findings
The observations of caseworkers’ everyday practice in the
two municipalities show that administrative work continu­
ously derives significance and meaning at two different levels:
One, at a general reflective level, including narratives on ad­
ministrative practices of record-keeping among caseworkers;
Two, at a daily practice level, including administrative work
with record-keeping, where administrative work plays mul­
tiple functions in the accountability relations that constitute
everyday work.
Coexistence of positive and negative narratives on
administrative work
Across the two child welfare units, the level and function of
administrative work were continually voiced and discussed,
both in informal fora in the hallways and at formal meetings.

core narrative describing development of increasing bureauc­
racy and administration, as illustrated below in a discussion
among a team of caseworkers in municipality A:

Three functions of administrative work in everyday
practice
In both child welfare units, caseworkers continually produced
and used administrative documents in their ongoing practice
in relation to clients, peers, managers, and administrative
authorities related to central government—both at the desk
and in physical contact with these actors. In these interactions,
administrative work was observed to serve three functions.
These functions are presented in the following.
Qualifying street-level work through gaining,
judging and maintaining knowledge
The first function that administrative work serves is to qualify
social work through the process of gaining, judging, and
maintaining knowledge about cases. This circular process is
closely related to caseworkers’ discretionary responsibilities
and casuistry. Through this process, they follow and adjust
to the dynamic lives of families while simultaneously accom­
modating bureaucratic values of fairness, transparency, re­
sponsiveness, and the rule of law, and specialized professional
values of explicating and documenting decisions and making
room for mobilizing their professional and experience-based
knowledge. Hence, this function is closely related to the
ethics-of-office and encompasses an integration of guidelines
and values stemming from the caseworker’s office, peers, field
of professional training, clients, and the state.
Gaining knowledge in encounters with other professionals
and clients is an almost unconscious practice related to the
statutory practice of recording and filing information on
cases. Every time the caseworkers answer the phone or par­
ticipate in a meeting, they immediately grab some paper and
a pen or open a Word document and scribble down jottings
from these conversations, which they afterwards file on the
case. Further, formalized meetings with clients are framed
by administrative regulations to systematize the knowledge
gained from the meetings. For instance, one caseworker
explains how four questions decided upon in his organiza­
tion support his social work practice: “Once you’ve asked
these questions, you’ll already have a pretty good picture. I
think it’s good to always try to have the complete picture,
(…) so you don’t forget some of the other things that seem
like they’re working.” (Interview, caseworker, municipality
B). Even though many caseworkers sometimes complained
about the duty to “file everything,” the observations also
show how they continuously use this knowledge in parts of
the social work process.
Judging knowledge reflects the caseworkers’ administra­
tive process at the desk, piecing together, reflecting upon and
analyzing to judge the knowledge gained in encounters with
other professionals and clients. This part of the knowledge
process in particular plays the function of prioritizing time
for the caseworkers to draw upon their specialized knowl­
edge in their discretionary practice. The importance of this
process is stressed in both units, like when the caseworker
Tanya explains to her team manager at a team meeting, as
they talk about a specific intervention, that she sometimes
reaches an assessment on an intervention while writing the
case report, “instead of having to know before you write”
(field note, municipality B). The judging knowledge process is
also illustrated below:
Laura is sitting at her desk working on a document on the
forcible removal of a child to out-of-home placement for
the municipal committee that should decide on this matter.
Laura shows me the long document she is writing. It is a
template with a range of subheadings, including sections
on the child, his parents, the nature of the interaction
between parents and child, analyses and an assessment.
Laura tells me that in these sections she draws on theory or
knowledge, for instance about what is expected of a nine-
year-old. While she works, she continuously consults other
documents on the case, for instance observations from the
child’s teacher. There are many perspectives and documents
to be taken into consideration, Laura explains, but while
stating this, she emphasizes that “when it is about force,
it is very intrusive on the family, so it’s important that we
are very thorough, and do a decent piece of work.” (Field
notes, municipality A)
In this case, the administrative process becomes a means
for Laura to spend time reflecting on and justifying her
recommendations in a case of high complexity that could end
in a major intervention in the life of this family.

issue is. She takes notes about the case on a pad of paper.

Lisa notes the circumstances of the case and the central
challenges that the family needs help with. With this knowl­

challenges, and she processes the new knowledge by writing
a new note on the case, which further deepens her under­
standing of the challenges in the family and her foundation
for offering targeted interventions.
The function of knowledge was also reflected in caseworkers’
discussions and decisions on specific interventions. The gained
and judged knowledge was continually part of the deliber­
ative processes among caseworkers and their managers, as
well as among other authorities in cases involving the use of
force. For instance, in municipality B, in cases of out-of-home
placement of children and other cost-intensive interventions,
caseworkers can bring cases to an internal conference, where
managers from different departments within the municipality
with expertise in child welfare are gathered to discuss the
case. The following field notes stem from one such confer­
ence, where participants had read a description of the case
that the caseworker had sent in advance, and in their discus­
sion, they continually used the documented elements to dis­
cuss and analyze the case:
The caseworker Beth and her manager take their seats at
the table. Beth asks whether they want a graph showing
the development in the case up on screen. They do, and
everyone can see the graph. Beth tells them about the case
while they have their eyes on the graph. Beth is a new case­
worker on the case that has been reopened in the child
welfare unit. She begins by saying that when she reads the
casefile [written material from the previous caseworker], it
is interesting to note what had happened in the early stages
of this case. Beth goes on to say that no memo as such
was written explaining why the case was closed. She then
draws the picture of the case. She refers to the graph, and
explains that it has started improving, and she thinks that
she will soon, just before summer holidays, be able to close
the case again, until she calls the mum after the summer
holidays. A long discussion follows where the group nego­

to it and the things they know about the case in order to
reach a decision. (Field notes, municipality B)
In this process, the administrative work done by Beth in the
earlier parts of the knowledge process acts as a fixed reference
point for the deliberative discussion about which intervention
to offer to the family. Further, when presenting the case, Beth
emphasizes the insufficient record-keeping done previously in
the case, which challenges her work in terms of supporting the
family since it restricts her interpretation of the case, the situ­
ation of this specific family and their needs. This type of frus­
tration about colleagues’ insufficient administrative work was
also articulated by other caseworkers while shadowing them.
For instance, the caseworker, Olivia, explained that insuffi­
cient record-keeping by colleagues may complicate the coop­
eration with the family and that it is unsatisfying to defend
colleagues’ insufficient work in front of other professionals
and authorities. In this way, the importance of the administra­
tive work is constituted in relational processes both internally
in the unit, with the bureaucratic office of child protection,
and externally with other professionals and clients. The ad­
ministrative practice of gaining, judging, and maintaining
knowledge plays a significant role in ensuring the profes­
sional quality of the judgmental process in specific cases and
in prioritizing resources across cases, thus aiding caseworkers
in mastering the complexity of their work.
Enhancing responsiveness by representing the
client’s voice in administrative documents
A second function of administrative work that I identified
was the ability and use of administrative practice to represent
the client’s voice in the written case material. This function is
closely related to the bureaucratic values of responsiveness,
respectfulness and proximity, and professional norms of in­
volvement and empowerment (Mik-Meyer 2017). Hence, the
“representing client function” counterbalances the formal
tone often associated with documents from public organi­
zations, the power over clients’ future these documents en­
capsulate, and the potential distance or even conflict they
may orchestrate in future interactions. As one team man­
ager pointed out at a meeting that was to review the unit’s
50 standard letters informing clients about the municipality’s
decisions: “How do standardized letters affect clients? There
needs to be a balance between efficiency and ensuring that
clients feel seen and heard” (Field notes, municipality B).
In the interviews, many caseworkers simply emphasized
their legal obligation to do administrative work to protect
clients’ rights. However, in the observations of daily prac­
tice, the administrative documents were seen to play a much
more nuanced role in relational processes that negotiated
and mediated state-centered obligations with moral and
emotional considerations oriented towards the client. At the
caseworkers’ desks, the clients were metaphorically contin­
uously present alongside professional authorities—for in­
stance, when the caseworker Lisa is writing an assessment
report on a child. Lisa had received comments from her team
manager, who wrote that she wanted Lisa to delete a para­
graph on the mother’s positive competences. Lisa said out
loud: “But I just think it all sounds so negative, and that
can be very hard for the mother to read.” While working on
this document, Lisa and her manager were negotiating the
balance between efficiently building a case and convincing
the authorities that Lisa and her team needed to authorize
their decision on forcible removal of the child while simul­
taneously showing empathy and respect towards the mother
who was to have her child placed in care against her (the
mother’s) will.

continuously tries to involve the child in this administrative
process in her attempts to represent the child’s voice rather
than just her (Alice’s) own voice. Hence, even though it is a

In this situation, Lisa chose dialogue with the children to in­

the need to represent their viewpoint as accurately as possible
in the administrative documents on their case while keeping
them safe because the father could read along. So, we see how
the administrative practice of the function of representing
citizen’s voice aids the caseworkers in mastering state-oriented
objectives while simultaneously considering the caseworker’s
moral and emotional proximity to clients. However, the two
situations of caseworkers Alice and Lisa draw attention to
the complex normative dilemmas related to street-level office
holding, where the contact and relation to the client can com­
plicate the duty to stay neutral and document the facts of the
case as accurately as possible.
Protection from the insecurity of street-level work
A third function of record-keeping that I identified was that
of protecting the caseworkers and their unit against the in­
security of their vocation in terms of the vocation’s inherent
risk of conflicts with clients, criticism from higher authorities,
and critical media coverage. In interactions with clients, this
insecurity can influence caseworkers’ administrative actions
and how they express themselves. This was demonstrated in
the case of the caseworker Tanya, whom I observed texting a
parent in one of her cases to prepare the parent for receiving
some administrative documents Tanya had just dispatched.
Tanya told me that she is always in doubt about whether to
write “me” or “the undersigned.” She chooses “the under­
signed” and explains that it is a little more formal “in case any
issues arise in the future” (Field notes, municipality B). In this
case, Tanya chose the more formal formulation to shield her­
self against the unpredictability related to the interaction with
this client. This use of more formal legal language in record-
keeping also characterized caseworker Olivia’s approach
when she asked the legal expert in her unit for help with how
to formulate an e-mail in a legally correct manner in a high-
conflict case with one of her clients (Field notes, municipality
A). Further, prioritizing record-keeping and following all ad­
ministrative regulations was a way for the caseworkers to
protect themselves in case of complaints. Caseworkers could
regret not having done so, as demonstrated in the field note
below:
In a conversation with the caseworker Ida during transpor­
tation time, she tells me that she has received a complaint
about her work to the mayor from a client. She tells me
that it’s draining because of all the time she has to spend
on it, even though she doesn’t think it’s well founded. On
the contrary, Ida says that she has spent more time talking
to the mother in the case than the case warrants. She
adds that she would wish that she’d documented all her
conversations with the client, so she’d be able to show how
much contact there’d been. “But we don’t have time for
that when we have so many cases,” she says. (Field note,
municipality A)

Discussion
The above analysis demonstrates how administrative work
plays three functions in the myriad accountability relations
guided by different value prescriptions that caseworkers
navigate in their everyday practice. Further, the analysis
demonstrates how the administrative practices, manifesting
in the three functions, enable the caseworkers to master
the complexities of street-level work. On the basis of these
insights, I propose a new theoretical framework that allows
us to delineate these three functions of administrative work
in complex street-level practice (Table 1). The theoretical
framework provides a lens for determining whether and how
each function aids the caseworkers in fulfilling the instituted
purpose of office (child protection) while mastering the
complexities of public service delivery.
Linking administrative practice to the purpose of
office
The function of gaining, judging, and maintaining knowl­
edge on specific cases serves to qualify the casework process.
Administrative work serves this function of knowledge
through the caseworkers’ accountability relations with peers
and managers close to the caseworkers, with internal and ex­
ternal professionals, with clients, and with the bureaucratic
office. Through the function of knowledge, administrative
work plays a key role in ensuring casuistry. In the administra­
tive practice that the function of knowledge is manifested in,
the caseworkers draw on generalized knowledge from their
professional training and on the rules and procedures that
guide their work, all of which they integrate with their prac­
tical experience and “sense” of the specific case. This supports
the caseworkers in mastering the complexity of their work
since it lowers the uncertainty related to gaining information
on the often uproarious lives of their clients and interpreting
and assessing this information. Additionally, this function
enables the caseworkers to prioritize between cases on a sys­
tematically informed basis and thus ensure fairness and the
rule of law while being responsive towards individual cases
and clients. Consequently, through the function of knowledge,
the dualism between administrative work and work with the
client vanishes. Instead, when administrative work plays this
function, there is a clear relationship between undertaking
record-keeping tasks in everyday work and ensuring the best

The function of representing the client’s voice serves to
convey respect and responsiveness towards the client in
the administrative practice. Administrative work serves
this function in the accountability relation with clients as
participants primarily because this function is a way for
the caseworkers to show that they listen to clients and seek
to respond with a level of flexibility to their specific case.
Furthermore, the representation of a client’s voice in admin­
istrative documents is couched in both bureaucratically and
professionally based virtues of responsiveness to, respect
for, empowerment of, inclusion of and cooperation with
clients in the casework process. In this way, the function of
representing the client’s voice supports the caseworkers in
mastering moral and emotional closeness to clients within
the administrative procedures. Simultaneously, though, the
analysis draws attention to complex normative dilemmas
related to building relations with clients and ensuring re­
sponsiveness to individual cases while ensuring impartiality
and taking account of the obligation to document as accu­
rately as possible. Nevertheless, the function of representing
the client’s voice in records can support the client in un­
derstanding their case and in taking part in and accepting
the assessments and judgement made in their case. In this
manner, the function of representing the client’s voice creates
a relationship between the administrative practice and the
fulfillment of the instituted purpose of office.
The function of protection in administrative work serves
to shield caseworkers against potential criticism of and
sanctions on their local unit in hierarchical accountability
relations both downwards towards citizens and upwards
towards higher managerial levels and the state level. This
function unfolds in the direct accountability relation to
clients that provides them, as democratic citizens, the op­
portunity to complain. Such citizen complaints may some­
times be supported by the wider society through the media,
which may cause the political level to respond with addi­
tional rules, regulations, and managerial systems to set and
measure certain standards of street-level work, including
sanctions if standards are not met. In the accountability re­
lation to clients, the analysis demonstrates that the function
of protection can create a distance towards clients; that is,
the function is potentially a way for street-level bureaucrats
to cope with the lack of control over clients by moving away
from them (Tummers et al. 2015). In the accountability re­
lation to the state, the caseworkers and their managers seem
to prioritize administrative work to document compliance
with the increasing amount of rules and regulations piling
up at the street level. As such, the function of protection
can be viewed as a coping mechanism to deal with such
pressures (see also Sabbe, Schiffino, and Moyson 2021).
While the function of protection unfolds in relation to the
state’s legitimate right to ensure democratic accountability
and transparency, the administrative practice it results in
at the street level tends to reconstitute the dualism between
administrative work and working with the client to deliver
public service in everyday practice. In other words, the func­
tion of protection may only to a limited extent support the
caseworkers in mastering the complexities of street-level
work and fulfilling their purpose of office. Hence, from this
perspective, especially in relation to higher authorities, the
function of protection in administrative work may create
perceptions of meaninglessness that resonate with a collec­
tive “negative” narrative that posits administrative tasks are
disconnected from delivering public service.
The three functions of administrative work and the ad­
ministrative practices that they are manifestations of are not
isolated from each other but rather closely intertwined. Both
the function of representing client’s voice and the function
of protection (particularly in the direct citizen accountability
relation) can be viewed as specific ways of writing down the
knowledge that is gained, judged, and maintained. In combi­
nation, the administrative practices constituting the function
of representing client’s voice and the function of protection
may help caseworkers balance empathy and distance to live
up to fundamental values of equality, equity, and fairness
(Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022). Simultaneously, the formal
language, distance, and potential conflict in the relation with
clients that is constituted in the function of protection may
negatively influence the caseworker’s relation to their client
and, consequently, the caseworker’s access to knowledge,
which would be detrimental to lowering the uncertainty
characterizing street-level work and to making professional
diagnosis and judgement (Abbott 1988). Contrastingly, the
practices that constitute the function of representing client’s
voice may positively influence the practice of gaining, judging
and maintaining knowledge. The relational practices that
are inherent to the function of representing client’s voice can
be viewed as a strategy for supporting a more trustful rela­
tion with clients, which supports the process of gaining the
knowledge about clients that is critical for making qualified
assessments and judgements.
Theoretical implications
The insights gained in developing the theoretical framework
in this study have three implications for existing scholar­
ship on public administration. First, the insights from the
present study contribute to our understanding of the re­
lationship between the bureaucratic obligation of record-
keeping and the delivering of public service in a caring and
responsive manner. Using the function of knowledge as an
analytical concept resonates well with the increasing focus
on different kinds of knowledge in the literature on public
service delivery as key to case-based reasoning (Cecchini
and Harrits 2022; Lotta, Maynard-Moody, and Musheno
2023; Møller 2022; Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022).
Further, the concept of the function of knowledge supports
Raaphorst’s (2023) finding that record-keeping enables the
generation of these different kinds of knowledge within
(hybrid) performance management regimes. The present
study also extends this work by showing that the function
of knowledge is constituted within and integrates multiple
accountability regimes. Moreover, by linking the adminis­
trative practice to the purpose of office, the present study
highlights and nuances how record-keeping enables street-
level bureaucrats to deliver public service in a manner
responding to clients’ needs. In addition, using the function
of representing the client’s voice as an analytical concept
gives us a means to nuance our understanding of the ad­
ministrative work involved in documenting conversations
with clients, as street-level bureaucrats strive to include
the client’s own words in such documents as a means of
protecting the client’s legal rights, which bears all the

Further, the approach to record-keeping that manifests in

the present study suggests that the administrative tasks of
record-keeping and the delivering of public service in a
caring and responsive manner—often described as isolated
from each other—are closely integrated and inseparable
parts of public service delivery.
The second implication of this study pertains to the lit­
erature on regulation. I show and argue that the function
of protection presented in the theoretical framework can be
used to understand the contrast, or dualism, between ad­
ministrative and client-oriented work that prevails both in
the general societal narrative on administrative work and in
street-level bureaucrats’ daily practice where record-keeping
plays the function of protection, especially in their accounta­
bility relation with higher authorities of the state that super­
vise street-level bureaucracies’ compliance with regulations.
Here, record-keeping may primarily be undertaken tacti­
cally (Raaphorst 2023) with the purpose of proving that
the clients have received what they were legally entitled to
in order to avoid sanctions from these higher authorities,
while the relationship between the administrative tasks and
the specific purpose of the street-level office may vanish.
Previous studies concur with this point of view on record-
keeping and show that rules and regulations aimed at man­
agerial needs for monitoring bear the risk of street-level
bureaucrats losing sight of the instituted purpose of their
work when they endeavor to comply with these systems and
avoid mistakes (Møller and Hill 2021; Sabbe, Schiffino, and
Moyson 2021; Steijn and van der Voet 2019; Thomann,
Hupe, and Sager 2018). To the extent that administrative
work primarily functions as a way for managers and out­
side authorities to monitor the efficiency of street-level
bureaucracies, it can be defined as managerialism (Ward
2011). In contrast, the conceptualization of the bureau­
cratic ethos (Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022) emphasizes
the street-level bureaucrats’ obligation to accommodate the
virtues of their office, including the multiple values guiding
street-level work and the particular purpose of the public
service task in question (Pedersen and Pors 2023, p. 82).
However, both theoretically and empirically, confusion
about managerialism and bureaucracy seems to be wide­
spread, which seems to manifest itself in a broad criticism of
bureaucracy and administrative work. Hence, using a more
clear distinction between administrative tasks stemming
from rules and regulations based on a managerial need for
monitoring work and administrative work stemming from
endeavors to underpin core bureaucratic values may help
the political level to take the relationship between adminis­
trative tasks and the purpose of particular street-level offices
into account in their decision-making processes. Future the­
oretical and empirical research could contribute to making
such a distinction.
In a third implication for existing scholarship on public
administration, this study advocates a discussion of the
conceptualization of coping. Coping has been defined by
Tummers et al. (2015) as “behavioral efforts street-level
bureaucrats employ when interacting with clients, in order
to master, tolerate, or reduce […] demands and conflicts
they face on an everyday basis.” While the concept of
coping includes behavior that is positive for individual
clients and helps street-level bureaucrats tolerate or re­
duce the pressures they face (van Loon and Jakobsen 2018;
Tummers et al. 2015), the coping mechanisms are primarily
characterized by what Zacka (2017) shows are reductive
ways of dealing with the multiple demands on street-
level work by meeting only one or few of these demands.
Accordingly, this, in the literature broadly accepted con­
ceptualization of coping, does not capture the administra­
tive practices that manifest in concepts introduced in this
study: the functions of knowledge and representing the
client’s voice. These functions and practices, especially in
combination, enable street-level bureaucrats to take mul­
tiple accountability demands into account and master
the complexities of public service delivery. Likewise, the
concept of coping does not fully capture the productive
forms of coping (Møller 2021, p. 478) that street-level
bureaucrats use as they strive to balance the complexity
and uncertainty of their context (Evans and Hupe 2020;
Maynard-Moody and Musheno 2012; Zacka 2017) and as
they struggle to translate standardized policy demands into
appropriate case-based service delivery through means of
casuistry (Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022; Pedersen and
Pors 2023; Pors and Schou 2021; Zacka 2017). Considering
that the literature on street-level bureaucracy has had its
primary focus on negative forms of coping and street-level
behavior more broadly (Lipsky 2010, p. xvii), I suggest
that we elevate the concept of mastering from Tummers
et al.’s (2015) concept of coping, such that we develop a
self-contained concept of “mastering” the complexities
of street-level bureaucracy in addition to the concept of
coping. By introducing the concept of mastering, we can
enhance our possibilities, both theoretically and empiri­
cally, of identifying practices and mechanisms that capture
how street-level bureaucrats actually succeed in balancing
the complexities of street-level work in ways that enhance
the legitimacy of street-level work—both through admin­
istrative practices and through street-level behavior more
broadly.
Limitations and future studies
In the present study, I did not explore the interaction between
the functions of administrative work in practice and the con­
struction of narratives on administrative work in the collec­
tive discussions about administrative work, even though such
an analysis could provide significant insights into the discrep­
ancy between administrative narratives and practice and pos­
sibly also about their influence on street-level bureaucrats’
perceptions of administrative work. However, the theoretical
framework developed in this study is put forward as a foun­
dation for discussing how each function of administrative
work could influence caseworkers’ perceptions of adminis­
trative work. Future research could explore these dynamics
and mechanisms, for example, by considering sensemaking
processes (Weick 1995) and the role of relational leadership
with a focus on “activating professional knowledge” (Møller
and Grøn 2023).
Another limitation of this study concerns the scope and
transferability of the findings, as this study reflects the dy­
namics of child welfare work in a specific local context in
a specific country. Child welfare services involve a relatively

based on these documents. As such, the case was well-suited

The transferability of the findings to other settings must, how­
ever, be expected to depend on similarities and differences re­
garding the characteristics of administrative work in those
settings as well as similarities and differences regarding the
nature of client interactions (Gofen, Shelly, and Gassner
2019; Harrits 2016), different welfare state regimes (Møller
2019), different levels of “discretion as granted” (Hupe, Hill,
and Buffat 2016), and on differences in workload and digital
documentation systems as well as organizational culture. The
theoretical framework proposed in this study might serve as
a building block for future comparative studies on how such
contextual conditions influence the functions of administra­
tive work.
Conclusion
Administrative work is an important and unavoidable ele­
ment of street-level work. Yet, administrative work is deeply
contested and often set in contrast, both in practice and in
theory, to working with the client and actually fulfilling the
purpose of delivering public service. Using a relational the­
oretical lens, this study advances existing scholarship on
administrative work in public service delivery by adding ana­
lytical concepts that allow us to understand some previously
hidden nuances in the purpose of administrative work from
the perspectives of street-level bureaucrats and their practice.
The study demonstrates how administrative work plays three
different functions in the myriad accountability relations that
street-level bureaucrats navigate in their everyday practice:
the functions of knowledge, representing the client’s voice,
and protection.
On this background, the study proposes a theoretical
framework that provides a lens for identifying whether and
how different administrative practices may support street-
level bureaucrats in mastering the complexities of public
service delivery in accordance with their purpose of office.
Findings show that street-level bureaucrats are able to master
these complexities through the functions of knowledge and
representing the client’s voice in their administrative prac­
tice, constituted in accountability relations with the bureau­
cratic office, the profession, peers, and participant-directed
relations with citizens. This finding highlights that admin­
istrative work is not necessarily contradictory to delivering
public service in a responsive manner—rather, it is also a
valuable and inseparable part of this work. Conversely, the
findings suggest that the function of protection, constituted
in formal accountability relations to citizens and the state,
may only to a limited extent enable street-level bureaucrats
to fulfill their purpose of office, and to some extent, can
become counteractive to the endeavor of mastering the
complexities of delivering public service and fulfilling the
purpose of office. Hence, while administrative work is not
necessarily desirable in itself, hopefully, the theoretical lens
proposed in this study—relating administrative practices
to mastering the complexities of public service delivery—
will stimulate, nuance, and advance further discussions
and explorations among researchers, policy makers, and
practitioners about both the dilemmas and virtues of admin­
istrative work and its regulation.

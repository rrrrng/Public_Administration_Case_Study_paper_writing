Adapting to Sea-­Level Rise: Centralization or
Decentralization in Polycentric Governance Systems?
Mark Lubell
and Matthew Robbins
How do polycentric governance systems respond to the emergence of new collective-­action problems?
We study this question in the context of the evolution of polycentric systems of sea-­level rise adaptation
in the San Francisco Bay Area. We focus on how the structure of polycentric systems changes over time
to support cooperation and learning and whether those changes represent a process of centralization or
decentralization. The ecology of games framework provides the theoretical background for developing
hypotheses about the structure of the system over time. We test the hypotheses by analyzing the
polycentric system as a two-­mode network where actors are linked to policy, divided into five time
periods from 1991 to 2016. The results suggest that the polycentric system for sea-­level rise adaptation
started with a centralized set of actors, which evolved over time to a more decentralized structure. The
research has general implications with respect to how polycentric systems manage the trade-­off between
maintaining local autonomy and coordinating decisions at the regional level across fragmented policy
communities.

Introduction
How do polycentric governance systems respond to the emergence of new
collective-­action problems? At least since the formative work of Ostrom, Tiebout,
and Warren (1961), policy scholars have been interested in how new collective-­
action problems stimulate institutional change and cooperation in polycentric sys-
tems. Elinor Ostrom (1990, 1998; 2010) and institutional scholars (Aligica & Tarko,
2012; Berardo & Lubell, 2016) have expanded this question from the local level to
more complex polycentric systems with many different political actors and policy
forums operating at multiple levels of geographic scale (Cash et al., 2006). Change
over time is also an important topic in the broader literatures on collaborative and
network governance, with some evaluating performance relative to preferred devel-
opmental trajectories (Mandell & Keast, 2007, 2008; Sydow, 2004), while others sug-
gest cycles of network growth and interest over time (Scott, Ulibarri, & Scott, 2020;
Ulibarri et al., 2020). However, despite the importance of analyzing change over
time, there is a dearth of empirical research on how polycentric systems change over
time in response to new collective-­action problems (Berardo, 2014b). Yet considering
that polycentric systems are a type of complex adaptive system, responding to new
issues is one of the most important facets of resilience and adaptive capacity (Levin
et al., 2013).
This paper focuses on the new collective-­action problem of regional sea-­level rise
adaptation in the San Francisco Bay (SF Bay) region of California.1. Globally, sea-­level
rise is one of the most serious climate change threats and will have major social, eco-
nomic, and political consequences (Hauer, Evans, & Mishra, 2016; Hinkel et al., 2014;
Strauss, Kulp, & Levermann, 2015). The Intergovernmental Panel Climate Change
(IPCC, 2013) estimates that relative to the 1986 to 2005 period, average sea-­level
rise by 2100 could range from 0.26 m (10.2 inches) to 0.98 m (38.6 inches) depend-
ing on different emissions scenarios. Even if all carbon emissions are eliminated,
there is enough climate change locked-­in so that sea-­level rise will continue to occur
(Meehl et al., 2005; Strauss et al., 2015). Sea-­level rise adaptation provides the oppor-
tunity to study how polycentric systems respond to a new collective-­action problem
within the path-­dependent constraints of governance arrangements developed for

Our study focuses on an important theoretical question—­does the evolution of
cooperation and polycentric governance follow a process of centralization or decen-
tralization? The direction of institutional change depends on how polycentric sys-
tems navigate the “tension between regional and local costs and benefits (Gerber
& Gibson, 2009),” where local actors consider ceding autonomy and preferences
to regional priorities. This tension could be resolved via centralization if regional
actors use their political authority and resources to create institutional rules that
either compel or provide positive incentives for local actors to cooperate. In contrast,
decentralization would mean translating regional priorities and resources to local
circumstances and encouraging more voluntary action among local stakeholders.
These processes require analyzing the relationship among policy forums and actors
at multiple levels of geographic scale (Cash et al., 2006). Each of these processes
entails different types of transaction costs for negotiating political agreements about
how to address regional problems. The tension between decentralization and cen-
tralization also has important policy implications for pursuing regional solutions to
collective-­action problems like sea-­level rise adaptation.
The recently updated ecology of games framework (EGF) provides a theoret-
ical lens for analyzing learning and cooperation in polycentric systems. The EGF
conceptualizes polycentric governance as a multilevel system where diverse policy
actors participate in many different policy forums in order to negotiate agreements
about how to address regional collective-­action problems (Berardo & Lubell, 2016;
Lubell, 2013).2. The EGF considers the decisions of many different types of policy
actors, rather than the more specific question of cooperation among local govern-
ments that has been the primary subject of the literature on “institutional collective-­
action” (Feiock, 2009, 2013). The effectiveness of polycentric governance depends on
cooperation and learning throughout the entire complex system, not just one policy
actor or policy forum in isolation. The EGF links with neo-­institutional economics in
arguing that cooperation is more likely when institutional arrangements lower the
transaction costs of searching for mutually beneficial solutions, bargaining over the
distribution of costs and benefits, and monitoring and enforcing the resulting agree-
ments (Libecap, 1989; Lubell, Mewhirter, Berardo, & Scholz, 2017; Lubell, Schneider,
Scholz, & Mete, 2002; North, 1990).
We use network theory and analysis to develop and test empirical hypotheses
about the structure of the polycentric system for climate adaptation and sea-­level
rise in SF Bay. Specifically, we analyze the structure of the sea-­level rise governance
network that is operationalized as a two-­mode network consisting of 647 policy
actors (mode 1) participating in 103 policy forums (mode 2) in SF Bay between
1991 and 2016. Networks capture complex interdependencies at the system level
rather than relying on individualistic assumptions about the behavior of atomized
social actors (Bodin & Crona, 2009; Bodin, Crona, & Ernstson, 2006; Lubell, Scholz,
Berardo, & Robins, 2012; Scott, 2016). The incidence of different types of structural
configurations within the network, which are indicators of different types of social

tions and SF Bay is a critical research site. Sea-­level rise is widely recognized as the
most critical climate change adaptation problem in SF Bay, which features a high
level of coastal development. Figure 1 shows the flooding expected to occur in SF Bay
with 48” (4 ft or 1.2 m) of sea-­level rise not including king tides or storm surge, along
with some of the fragmented political boundaries and transportation infrastructure
of the 9-­county, highly urbanized region.3. The Bay Conservation Development
Commission (BCDC) is a powerful regional agency that issues permits for coastal
development in SF Bay and pursues planning for regional issues. In 2011, BCDC
amended its SF Bay Plan to require all development projects to consider sea-­level
rise and associated coastal flooding. Many local government and business stake-
holders reacted negatively to this attempt at centralization, so the resulting policy
was considerably weaker than originally proposed. Recognizing this tension, BCDC
created the Adapting to Rising Tides (ART) program, which takes a more decentral-
ized approach by supporting local partnerships to increase the capacity of policy
actors to assess vulnerability and identify infrastructure solutions that enhance resil-
ience and adaptive capacity. In 2020, BCDC initiated Bay Adapt: Regional Strategy
for Rising Bay to develop an overall regional plan; lead author Lubell’s member-
ship on the Bay Adapt leadership advisory group provides an ongoing opportunity
for participant observation. But BCDC is not the only game in town—­the adaptive
capacity of the region depends on learning and cooperation among the many other
regional and local actors participating in sea-­level rise governance at multiple scales.
The next section describes the interdependencies and associated cooperation
problems involved with the evolution of the polycentric system for sea-­level rise
adaptation. We then describe our specific network hypotheses in the context of two-­
mode governance networks of actors participating in forums in SF Bay. The meth-
ods and analysis section describes network data collection via online sources, the
complementary qualitative research, and descriptive statistics and statistical models
that test our hypotheses. The conclusion discusses the relevance of our findings for
understanding the idea of regionalism in polycentric systems and the future research
that is needed to develop a more general and comparative understanding of spatial
and temporal dynamics of institutional change.

Sea-­Level Rise Adaptation, Interdependence, and Cooperation
Collective-­action problems are rooted in interdependence: the benefits and costs
that individuals experience from their own decisions depend on the decisions of
other individuals in a community (Ostrom, 1990, 1998). Interdependency creates in-
centives for strategic behavior as analyzed in game theory and, depending on the
structure of the game, may lead to inefficient equilibrium outcomes. Individuals
have no incentive to consider social costs in their private decisions, leading to the
well-­known problems of the underprovision of public goods (receive nonexcludable
benefits without paying the costs) or overexploitation of common-­pool resources
(receive private benefits while social costs are shared across the group) or the impo-
sition of positive or negative externalities. Solving these problems requires cooper-
ation among individual actors, where mutual benefits are available from changing
behaviors in ways that incorporate social costs and benefits into the individual de-
cision making calculus.
Existing research recognizes the important role of interjurisdictional external-
ities as a rationale for regional governance. In their foundational paper, Ostrom,
Tiebout, and Warren (1961, p. 842) note that “the patterns of local government in a
metropolitan area can only be understood by attention to the variety of formal and
informal arrangements that may exist for settling area-­wide problems.” This idea is
a central motivation for the extensive research on “institutional collective-­action,”
which analyzes how different regional institutional arrangements affect the transac-
tion costs of negotiating, monitoring, and enforcing political agreements (Brown &
Potoski, 2003; Shrestha & Feiock, 2011).
In the context of climate change, most analysts recognize that climate mitigation—­
reducing carbon emissions—­entails a multilevel, global cooperation problem
(Ostrom, 2010). Less recognized are the collective-­action problems associated with
climate adaptation (Adger, 2003), which involves four important types of interde-
pendencies relevant to understanding the evolution of polycentric systems. First,
“shared experience interdependencies” emerge when stakeholders face the same
types of vulnerabilities and related climate adaptation circumstances (Hummel et
al., 2018). Stakeholders facing similar climate adaptation circumstances may be able
to learn from each other or share innovative strategies that have been analyzed or
experimented with by some particular actor. For example, many cities in SF Bay are
facing a similar level of expected sea-­level rise, which may affect different economic
sectors and populations in similar ways.
Second, “vulnerability interdependencies” arise when there are potential spill-
overs in risk from one actor to another. In the context of sea-­level rise and flooding,
this usually occurs geographically when a lack of action by one local jurisdiction
creates a “weak link” where flood waters can spread to other jurisdictions, even if
those other jurisdictions have invested in adaptation strategies. Another example is
when coastal flooding in one area negatively affects infrastructure in other areas, for
example, via increased traffic congestion.
Third, “adaptation interdependencies” occur when adaptation actions taken
by one actor increase or decrease the vulnerabilities of other actors, usually via

Fourth, “policy interdependencies” occur when implementing adaptation actions
requires authoritative decisions from multiple actors. Policy interdependencies are
especially apparent in the context of permitting and funding, where implementing a
specific adaptation project requires permits from multiple agencies and assembling a
funding portfolio from a combination of local, state, federal, and private sources. For
example, the Oro Loma Horizontal Levee project, an important experiment in the

Centralization Versus Decentralization in Polycentric Governance Systems
Like many environmental problems, sea-­level rise and climate adaptation in-
volves analyzing the trade-­offs between the goals of individual local actors and col-
lective, regional costs and benefits. Polycentric systems are multilevel systems (Cash
et al., 2006; Young, 2002) where individuals are nested in neighborhoods, which in
turn are nested in special districts, then cities, counties, regional government, state
government, and ultimately national and global levels. At the local level, horizontal
cooperation is needed within cities and counties where multiple special districts,
nongovernmental organizations, and individual citizens must engage in climate ad-
aptation decisions and behaviors. Cooperation among local jurisdictions is required
to manage interdependences that cross local boundaries. Horizontal cooperation is
also needed the regional level, among governance actors who have authority over
different aspects of the problem (e.g., water quality, wetlands, and species protec-
tion) but must coordinate the application of policy tools at the local level such as
regulatory requirements, funding incentives, and information. Cross-­level relation-
ships facilitate vertical cooperation between different geographic levels, for exam-
ple, by delivering financial and political resources from higher to lower levels of
government or communicating local information and preferences to higher levels.
We argue that centralization and decentralization are two possible processes
for achieving cooperation in polycentric systems. Each of these processes seeks to
resolve the tension between local autonomy and regional priorities. Centralization
involves regional actors creating central policy forums where they invite many
local actors to share information, access financial resources, and make collective

decisions about the best way to address regional problems. Centralization assumes
that regional actors have access to political authority and resources that can strongly
shape the behavior of local actors, via regulatory enforcement or making access to
resources conditional on cooperation (e.g., funding incentives). Regional actors thus
span geographic boundaries and form cross-­scale relationships to help steer the
overall multilevel system (Edelenbos, van Buuren, & van Schie, 2011; Klijn, Steijn, &
Edelenbos, 2010; Lubell, Robins, & Wang, 2014).
Decentralization involves the creation of many local policy forums, where
regional actors are invited to link their resources and regional perspectives to more
local decision making processes. Decentralization recognizes that local actors vary
substantially in their policy capacity and that unique local circumstances, such as
varying levels of vulnerability to sea-­level rise and the presence of disadvantaged
communities, shape the motivation of local actors to contribute to regional goals
(Krause, 2011). Hence, local involvement in regional issues is driven by a few highly
active and innovative actors, while many local actors remain uninvolved especially
at the initial stages of institutional evolution. Decentralization seeks to increase the
overall capacity of local actors, while also respecting local autonomy and recogniz-
ing that regional actors do not have enough political authority or financial incen-
tives to fully control local decisions. Decentralization may stimulate the creation
of local policy forums that build cooperation among local actors, while utilizing
information and resources from regional forums. Rather than relying on funding
from more central authorities, decentralization emphasizes the development of local
revenue sources (Woodruff, Mullin, & Roy, 2020). Decentralization can still involve
making regional resources available at the local level but usually does not involve
coercive policy tools designed to compel cooperation.
The distinction between centralization and decentralization can be analyzed
from a transaction cost perspective. Transaction cost analysis posits that collective-­
action problems are solved when the benefits of cooperation outweigh the transac-
tion costs of searching for the set of mutually beneficial solutions, bargaining over
costs/benefits, and monitoring and enforcing the resulting agreements. Hamilton
and Lubell (2018) argue that transaction costs of cooperation are greater when
authority is centralized at higher levels of geographic scale. At higher levels of geo-
graphic scale, there is greater heterogeneity in policy preferences, more uncertainty
about how collective decisions translate to the local level and less frequent interac-
tions among involved actors. Regions with strong autonomy at the local level, for
example, US states with a tradition of home rule like California, increase the costs of
centralization. Hence, over time, we expect that decentralization will receive greater
emphasis for encouraging local stakeholders to pursue regional goals in SF Bay.
Network Hypotheses for the Evolution of Regional Governance
Networks are a useful tool for analyzing complex governance systems because
they empirically capture interdependence among system components (Lubell et al.,
2012). This paper examines the sea-­level rise governance network as a two-­mode
network in five different time periods from 1991 to 2016. The first mode represents

policy actors (circles in diagrams), the second mode represents policy forums
(squares in diagrams), and a link represents actors participating in forums. The EGF
assumes that actors participate in policy forums to pursue their preferred solutions
to collective-­action problems (Fischer, Angst, & Maag, 2019; Scharpf, 1997). Actors
provide political support to policy forums that are perceived to provide benefits that
outweigh transaction costs, along with acceptable procedural and distributive fair-
ness from the perspective of the actors. The survival and stability of policy forums
depend on receiving support from enough political actors. Polycentric governance
institutions evolve over time as actors create new, dismantle old, and change policy
forums to better pursue their policy preferences.
Translating the theoretical ideas described above into specific network hypoth-
eses requires specifying the types of network configurations expected to be observ-
able as a product of the underlying social and political processes driving network
formation. The argumentative logic of such hypotheses is that local configurations
of network structures, or specific network motifs (Milo et al., 2002), are an outcome
of actors making decisions about creating and participating in policy forums. For
analogy, imagine the network configurations as structural “fingerprints” of the
underlying processes. As will be explained in more detail in the methods section,
ERGM models treat the frequency of different types of configurations as the “inde-
pendent” variables predicting the observed network.
Figure 2 summarizes that the structural configurations that we hypothesize are
important for polycentric systems, as well as how the prevalence of these configu-
rations might change over time for centralization or decentralization (see Appendix
Table A1 for technical names for each ERGM modeling term). Hypothesis 1 focuses
on the presence of open, star-­like structures that are associated with learning and
coordination, which can be centered on actors or forums. Open network structures
have been a central theme in social network analysis at least since the famous work
of Granovetter (1985) on weak ties and Burt (2005) on structural holes. The essential
idea is that open structures provide opportunities to efficiently connect to diverse,
nonredundant sources of information. Berardo and Scholz (2010) extend this idea to
the context of policy networks, arguing that open structures function as “bridging
social capital” (Berardo, 2014a) that overcome coordination problems where there
is not a risk of free-­riding, but actors need the same information to effectively work
together. Actors or forums at the center of many open network structures also tend
to have more power, resources, and authority in the context of polycentric systems
(Henry, 2011).
If centralization is the dominant process, we expect open structures centered on
regional forums to become more prevalent over time because a few regional forums
will attract many policy actors. These regional forums would become the central
focal point for collective decisions, information sharing, and resource allocation.
The collective decisions made in central forums could result in prescriptive regional
rules, for example, permitting requirements for coastal development that impose
new mandates on local actors. Alternatively, if decentralization is the dominant
process, we expect open structures centered on local forums, where local forums
attract many unique actors concerned with sea level rise adaptation in their specific

Hypothesis 2 focuses on the presence of “closed” network structures that pro-
mote clustering and cooperation. The second row of Figure 1 displays “nonedge-
wise shared partner” configurations based on actors and forums respectively.
Technically, nonedgewise shared partners capture the distribution of the number of
two paths between any pair of unconnected nodes from one mode (e.g., actors), who
share a partner via a two-­path connecting through the other mode (e.g., forums; see
Simpson, Hayasaka, & Laurienti, 2011, for intuitive applied example). Nonedgewise
shared partners of actors represent “joint participation” of the same sets of actors
in multiple policy forums, which is expected to enable reputational processes that
support cooperation (Ulibarri & Scott, 2016). Conversely, nonedgewise shared part-
ners of forums represent two forums developing a “joint audience,” in which two
forums tend to attract the same set of actors. In conjunction, joint participation and
joint audience configurations combine to produce a core-­periphery network struc-
ture with a dense community of cooperation centered around a few focal forums.
We argue that centralization versus decentralization predicts contrasting
hypotheses for the rates of joint participation and joint audience configurations.
Centralization would feature increasing rates of joint participation, as a few high-­
degree actors participate in the same set of forums. Centralization would also fea-
ture an increasing rate of joint audience, as a few central forums become the focus
of participation for the same set of actors. A more common social analogy may be
instructive: A positive tendency for both joint participation and joint audience would
be like small set of pubs in a city being popular for a certain genre of music, while
simultaneously the fans of that genre of music frequent the same pubs.
In contrast, decentralization would feature decreasing rates of joint participa-
tion and joint audience configurations, as the emergence of new forums across the
region segments the network into sets of actors pursuing local concerns or special-
ized aspects of adaptation. In terms of change over time, we expect that early stages
of regional adaptation are centralized around highly active regional actors and pop-
ular regional forums that have recognized the impending collective-­action problem
and have authority and capacity to provide leadership in the development of new
plans and institutions. As the network grows and decentralizes, more specialized
and local forums emerge and segment the network into small groups of forums
attracting different sets of actors.
Hypothesis 3 focuses on the relative level of activity of regional actors and
forums. The simplest way to test this hypothesis is with network statistics akin to
dummy variables in a regression that indicate if regional actors or forums have a
higher average number of connections than other types of actors or forums. For cen-
tralization, we expect regional forums and actors to have a higher average number
of connections, with the opposite expectation for decentralization.
Hypothesis 4 focuses on cross-­level networks that span boundaries across levels
of geographic scale (Brondizio, Ostrom, & Young, 2009; Hamilton & Lubell, 2018).
Cross-­level networks are considered essential components of effective polycentric

Research Methods and Results
This section summarizes the data collection and assembly process for the sea-­
level rise governance network in SF Bay. We then examine hypotheses with three
stages of analysis and results: network visualization, descriptive statistics, and expo-
nential graph models. All analyses were conducted using the R statistical environ-
ment with the statnet suite as well as the dplyr and igraph packages.
Data Collection: Constructing Multilevel, Regional, and Local Governance
Networks
Our analysis relies on an inventory of climate change and sea-­level rise adap-
tation forums that are in some stage of planning and implementation within one
or more of the nine counties in the SF Bay Area. The list was provided by the UC
Berkeley Climate Readiness Institute, current to 2016 at the time we assembled this
data. Ties in the network represent organizations participating in forums and were
established by recording the partners listed on each project’s webpage and online
documents. All ties were assumed to have the same weight; it was not possible to
determine tie strength from the website information. The resulting sea-­level rise
governance network for SF Bay comprises 103 forums and 647 unique actors.
The sea-­level rise adaptation network for the SF Bay consists of actors operat-
ing at geographic scales ranging from the local to the national level. We used each
organization’s website to code “scope for work” as “local” organizations operating
within a single county (e.g., municipal and county governments, special districts,
small NGOs) or “regional” organizations operating in multiple counties.4. This
regional designation also includes state level and national organizations whose work
encompass but extend beyond the Bay Area (e.g., state government organizations).
Organizations with a local scope of operation outside the 9-­county Bay Area (e.g.,
City of Santa Monica) were coded as “outside.” Both actors and forums were coded
by type. Actors were classified based on a 16-­category typology: NGO, Industry,

data limits our capacity to observe the “churn” of polycentric systems where forums
are born, change, and die, and actors enter and exit the system and shift patterns
of participation. The structure of the data also prevents full application of dynamic
network models like the stochastic-­actor oriented model, which estimate parameters
for the rates at which new network ties form and old network ties dissolve over time
(Block, Stadtfeld, & Snijders, 2016; Desmarais & Cranmer, 2012). Future longitudinal
research on polycentric systems should strive to more fully observe and model such
fine-­grained temporal dynamics.

Methods: ERGMs
ERGMs analyze the structure of networks based on a set of hypothesized micro-­
level processes. ERGM coefficients represent the change in the log-­odds of tie for-
mation if forming the tie increases or decreases the frequency of different structural
motifs. Technically, the analogs of independent variables in an ERGM are the counts
of sufficient statistics for the different types of motifs included in the model. The
ERGMs include specific terms that capture the effects of social processes related to
network formation hypothesized in Table 1. Table A1 in the Appendix lists the spe-
cific ERGM terms that are associated with the theoretical concepts in Figure 1. We
report ERGM results for Time 1 (forums created between 1991 and 2007) and Time
5 (all forums existing in 2016 when data collection ended). The first period is before
sea-­level rise became a highly salient issue, and the last period is after the explosive
growth in sea-­level rise adaptation projects.
To test H1, we include parameters that govern the extent to which the network
features open, star-­like structures centered on actors or forums. Geometrically
weighted (GW) degree distribution on the actor mode (gwb1degree) accounts for
actor-­based open structures, and GW degree distribution on the forum (gwb2de-
gree) accounts for forum-­based open network structures. Given how the GW degree
statistic is calculated, negative coefficients indicate a greater prevalence of open
structures, while positive coefficients represent fewer open structures. One inter-
pretation of GW degree is “anticentralization,” since negative coefficients indicate


## Page 18

160
Policy Studies Journal, 50:1
many edges focused on a few nodes while positive coefficients represent a more
even distribution of edges across many nodes.5. Technically, GW degree statistics
are equivalent to alternating k-­stars, which assumes declining marginal predictive
benefits for additional open structures. GW degree also captures the variance of the
degree distribution; negative coefficients represent a high-­variance degree distribu-
tion with a few nodes collecting most of the edges, while positive parameters repre-
sent a low-­variance degree distribution such that edges are more evenly distributed
across nodes. High-­variance degree distributions may result from “preferential
attachment,” where new edges prefer to connect with popular nodes.
To test H2, we used GW nonedgewise shared partners of actors (gwb1nsp)
where a positive coefficient represents the general tendency for clustering in the
network where two actors jointly participate in the same set of forums. Conversely,
GW nonedgewise shared partners of forums (gwb2nsp) represent a tendency for
two forums to share a joint audience of actors.
To test H3, we include node attribute factor terms to capture the baseline rates of
tie formation of policy actors and forums with a regional scope. Positive (negative)
coefficients on these statistics indicate that regional actors or forums have a signifi-
cantly higher (lower) number of connections than local actors or forums.
Finally, to test H4 related to cross-­level linkages, we include dummy variables
for local actors participating in regional forums and regional actors participating in
local forums. A positive value on these coefficients means that cross-­level ties occur
more frequently than average conditional on the other parameters in the model; a
negative coefficient indicates a lower rate of cross-­level ties.
We also include control variables for geographic homophily that indicates
whether actors in the same county, or operating the regional level, tend to partic-
ipate in the same forums. The “two-­star” is a three-­node configuration where two
actors operating in the same county (or regionally) are connected to a forum. The
“three-­star” is a four-­node configuration where three actors operating in the same
county (or regionally) are all connected to the same forum. A positive parameter
on the two-­star statistic and negative parameter on the three-­star statistic indicates
decreasing marginal returns for additional geographic homophily; inclusion of both
statistics also helps control runaway processes that threaten model convergence.
The parameters of ERGM models are estimated via Markov–­Chain Monte Carlo
simulation methods that compare the observed network to a distribution of simu-
lated networks. We use two techniques to manage convergence problems (Duxbury,
2018). First, we “fix” the edges of nodes with greater than 30 connections. Second,
we fix the total number of edges in the simulated networks to be equal the observed
number of edges. All our models converge and produce relatively good fit of between
observed and simulated patterns of ties (see Appendix for model fit figures).

Conclusion: An Abundance of Governance
Sea-­level rise adaptation in SF Bay provides an opportunity to observe a
polycentric governance system responding to the emergence of a new regional
collective-­action problem. The EGF argues that in response to new collective-­action
problems, policy actors will change existing policy forums or create new policy fo-
rums that shape the transaction costs of political contracting. The EGF highlights the


## Page 21

LUBELL and ROBBINS: Adapting to Sea-­Level Rise
163
importance of studying the whole system, which we accomplish here by analyzing
two-­mode networks of actors participating in policy forums.
Our analysis shows what one BCDC official described as “an abundance of gov-
ernance”: large, recent, and rapid growth in the polycentric system governing sea-­
level rise adaptation. The early stage of the network is centralized around a small
number of regional forums that incorporate sea-­level rise into their more general
portfolio of environment issues. The growing recognition about the vulnerability
to sea-­level rise and associated interdependencies stimulates a process of decentral-
ization, with new forums at the local level attracting sets of local actors focused on
adaptation in their communities, supported by regional actors who use their pol-
icy resources to shape coordination across communities. As the number of forums
grows, more of them have a specialized focus on sea-­level rise and flooding instead
of tackling a more general set of issues.
The analysis has several important theoretical implications. First, it highlights
how the choice between centralization and decentralization generates opposing
effects on the transaction costs associated with local autonomy versus fragmenta-
tion. Centralization increases the transaction costs that derive from maintaining
local autonomy because local actors will resist the imposition of authority from
higher levels of government and variance in local knowledge and context will not
be adequately considered. Decentralization and the accompanying abundance of
governance increases the transaction costs associated with fragmentation, as differ-
ent communities emphasize their local policy preferences and may be more likely
to ignore regional interdependencies and goals. The balance between centralization
and decentralization, and associated institutional structures, therefore depends on
the relative magnitude of the transaction costs originating from these opposing
sources. The art of governance thus involves balancing decentralization and central-
ization in a way that minimizes the joint transaction costs associated with preserv-
ing local autonomy and increasing fragmentation.
How the trade-­offs between centralization and decentralization affect transac-
tion costs may depend heavily on political context. The home-­rule state of California
has always prioritized local community autonomy. In addition, climate adaptation
requires developing infrastructure that is well-­suited to local circumstances and
engages local community participation. The emphasis on local autonomy is also
manifest in the multitude of incorporated cities and special districts in SF Bay, which
creates a fragmented institutional baseline that constrains the development of new
institutions for climate adaptation. In other regions of the United States or in other
countries, political cultures and existing institutional arrangements may be friend-
lier to centralized governance arrangements that place more limits on local auton-
omy to pursue collective regional goals. Comparative research designs are necessary
to shed light on these questions.
Second, the network features a combination of open network structures for
learning and information sharing and closed network structures for cooperation.
There are groups of highly active actors and very popular forums that tend to be
hubs for information and coordination. The core of the network was mainly at the
regional level to start, but the process of decentralization has created many more

164

local “centers” to complement regional actors and forums. The open network coor-
dination structures co-­exist with more closed network cooperation structures, which
are mainly centered on forums since the process of decentralization has reduced the
rate of joint participation among actors.
Third, the analysis highlights the important role for regional actors who must
effectively navigate the tensions between decentralization, fragmentation, and
system-­level coordination. In contrast to the simple hypothesis that decentraliza-
tion reduces the role of regional actors, our results suggest that decentralization
increases the responsibilities of regional actors to provide leadership and coordina-
tion to lower levels of the institutional system. Regional actors have higher levels
of participation throughout the evolution of the system, yet regional forums attract
fewer participants and there is a large growth in cross-­scale ties from regional actors
to local forums. These regional actors are therefore delivering information, fund-
ing, and other policy resources to the local level, with the hope of incorporating
regional goals into local decisions and on-­the-­ground adaptation projects. Many of
the regional actors are California state agencies with the expertise, finances, and
political authority to shape local decisions. But other regional actors include non-
governmental organizations, associations of private actors, and scientific organiza-
tions that also have regional resources and incentives. These regional organizations
provide crucial leadership to maintain coordination and cooperation as the polycen-
tric system grows with an upsurge in local responses, including helping local actors
to recognize and account for vulnerability and adaptation interdependencies.
What about effectiveness? Will the polycentric system in SF Bay continue to evolve
to increase resilience and adaptive capacity for sea-­level rise? The rapid growth in the
number of forums and actors is a sign of a high-­capacity policy system in a demo-
cratic political culture with many opportunities for participation and institutional cre-
ation. Lower capacity systems appear to have more ephemeral and symbolic bursts
of institutional change (Berardo, 2014b). Yet while our qualitative research indicates
that learning and cooperation is definitely evolving in the system, sea-­level rise gov-
ernance is still at an early stage, with only a few on-­the-­ground projects being imple-
mented and widespread concerns that infrastructure development is not happening
fast enough especially for more vulnerable disadvantaged communities.
Furthermore, SF Bay stakeholders are in the throes of designing a more perma-
nent set of governance institutions that will formalize the process of regional collab-
oration and strike the right balance between centralization and decentralization. For
example, SF Bay stakeholders cannot agree on whether they should establish a new
“network administrative organization” with additional authority or rely on an exist-
ing “lead agency” to coordinate climate adaptation planning (Provan & Kenis, 2008).
The emergence of new central forums over time is indicative of this core governance
challenge (see Appendix Table A3 for top-­10 most central actors and forums). At
the early stage of the system, the most central forum was the Bay Area Integrated
Water Management Plan, a collaborative partnership that addresses a wide range of
water management and only incorporated sea-­level rise adaptation at a later stage.
As the network grows, new forums specifically devoted to sea-­level rise begin to
emerge as central nodes. For example, the CHARG network was established to

coordinate local governments and special districts and the Bay Area Resilient by
Design Challenge (RBD) sought to identify innovative on-­the-­ground adaptation
projects. Interestingly, both CHARG and RBD have become less active and visible
despite initially attracting a high level of attention and participation. Although out-
side the time frame of our data, the newest “big game in town” is the Bay Adapt
regional planning forum, which continues to search for the elusive more permanent
institutional arrangement. The birth, development, and death of individual forums
are an essential process in the evolution of polycentric systems.
How will SF Bay’s polycentric system change in the future? Ulibarri et al. (2020)
outline potential developmental pathways for collaborative governance, which
may have applications to polycentric systems. They suggest that collaborative gov-
ernance experiences a period of activation and institutionalization, followed by
stabilization, or decline at different rates. The SF Bay system experienced a rapid
period of growth and is currently in the throes of institutionalization as policy
actors seek more permanent coordination mechanisms. But in this case, we do not
expect a decline in activity—­instead, the current process of institutionalization seeks
to stimulate more local adaptation planning and project implementation in ways
that consider regional goals and interdependencies. This suggests that in addition
to overall levels of activity, analyzing future trajectories must consider how rates
of institutional change vary across levels of geographic scale or other functionally
differentiated portions of the polycentric system. Furthermore, theories of the pol-
icy process and cooperation suggest punctuated equilibrium dynamics that cycle
between bursts of cooperation and long periods of incrementalism or noncoopera-
tion. Such nonlinear dynamics may also characterize polycentric systems over long
periods of time, perhaps on the order of decades, and including alternating waves of
decentralization and centralization as policy actors grapple with the countervailing
benefits and transaction costs of both processes.
Speculation about the evolutionary trajectory of polycentric systems under-
scores the crucial need for comparative, long-­term research. Polycentric systems
evolve over many decades and feature complex and co-­evolving causal processes,
most likely with feedbacks and nonlinear dynamics. A more complete understand-
ing of these processes requires repeated observation over time. Linking the social
and political processes to environmental outcomes also requires a long-­term per-
spective, because changes in environmental parameters are usually slow or delayed
and influenced by many factors beyond policy decisions. In addition, how these
systems evolve may vary across context depending on social or ecological factors,
which comparative institutional analysis can help untangle. Sea-­level rise and cli-
mate adaptation provide an excellent opportunity to observe how polycentric sys-
tems respond to new collective-­action problems. Unfortunately, conducting such
comparative, long-­term research is a major challenge given funding availability and
various other incentives that emphasize short-­term scientific results.

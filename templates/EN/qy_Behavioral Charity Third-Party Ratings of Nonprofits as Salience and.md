ABSTRACT
Financial disclosure through tax returns is the primary regulatory mechanism for holding nonprofits accountable to donors
in the USA. The assumption that donors will make rational decisions using disclosed information when giving to nonprofits is
central to this regulation. But what if they rely on mental shortcuts instead? This study examines how donors respond to a third-­
party nonprofit rating that is simple, unverified, and based on self-­reported data. Using a five-­year panel dataset covering over a
million nonprofit-­year records, we find that even these basic ratings have a significant impact on donation behavior. Donors use
ratings via two mental shortcuts. First, ratings increase a nonprofit's salience, boosting donations by 7%. Second, donors process
higher ratings as a mental accounting shortcut, giving up to 34% more to the top-­rated nonprofits. The findings suggest the limits
of information disclosure alone in making nonprofits accountable to donors.
1   |   Introduction
There are approximately 1.8 million tax-­exempt public charities,
commonly referred to as nonprofits, in the United States (Faulk
et al. 2021). Standard economics suggests that the more exten-
sive the choices, the greater the donors' power to hold nonprof-
its accountable. This logic is robust when donors are treated as
rational agents, that is, they can compare relevant nonprofits to
identify those that perform better. In reality, many donors pos-
sess limited cognitive abilities, limited attention, and are subject
to cognitive biases (Tversky and Kahneman 1974; Simon 1947;
Gneezy et al. 2014).
New Institutional Economics models of charitable giving,
rooted in Andreoni's  (1990) theory of impure altruism and
Roberts's (1984) model of pure altruism, assume donors make
utility-­maximizing choices under imperfect information about
nonprofit performance. In both frameworks, rational donors
respond to performance signals. Pure altruists fully crowd out
or in, whereas impure altruists adjust giving more modestly de-
pending on the warm glow they receive. If donors behave this
rationally, they should compare nonprofits to identify higher-­
performing organizations when relevant information is avail-
able. Impure altruists may gain additional warm glow from
rewarding stronger performers, while pure altruists may further
prioritize effective nonprofits receiving less government support
to crowd in private giving. Third-­party ratings reduce informa-
tion search and processing costs, enabling such comparisons
and functioning as rational shortcuts. Behavioral research, how-
ever, shows that donors are often influenced by emotions, fram-
ing, situational cues, and cognitive biases (Ideas42 2023; Chan
et  al.  2025). Consequently, the impact of third-­party ratings
may depend on whether donors use them as rational or mental
shortcuts.
Indeed, the logic of nonprofit organizations originates from
the assumption that donors are rational and capable of under-
standing the “non-­distribution constraint” and the capacity
to serve “heterogeneous demands” that for-­profits and public
agencies cannot (Hansmann  1980; Lecy and van Slyke  2013;
Peng et  al.  2015). To help donors choose better-­performing
nonprofits, different forms of disclosure requirements, such as

Sargeant and Woodliffe's  (2007) review demonstrates that

faster decisions. Third-­party ratings of nonprofits have become
widespread nowadays and could have the potential to serve as a
decision aid for donors. Charity Navigator received 11.3 million
visits in 2022 (Charity Navigator 2022). About 12 million people
visit the GuideStar website every year (Fleming Glennon 2021).
According to a large-­scale survey of donors, Cnaan et al. (2011)
reported that 23% of donors consult a third-­party rating website
before donating.
The current nonprofit literature explains the usefulness of
third-­party ratings predominantly from a rational perspective,
arguing that donors use them to maximize the effectiveness
of their donations by penalizing poorly performing nonprofits
(Barber et al. 2022; Yörük 2016; Harris and Neely 2016; Gordon
et al. 2009). Third-­party ratings in this case serve as a credible
signal of nonprofit performance and thus as a rational decision
aid. In this study, we examine an alternative behavioral the-
ory of charitable giving, informed by third-­party ratings from
a Behavioral Public Administration (BPA) lens. Using Tversky
and Kahneman's (1974) “heuristics” and Bordalo et al.'s (2022)
“salience” theory, we argue that the third-­party rating provides
salience and heuristic devices to boundedly rational donors (be-
havioral donors, henceforth) who have insufficient cognitive
and attentional capacity, as well as are subject to cognitive bi-
ases. Behavioral donors lack the time and attention to review
the disclosed information of competing nonprofits to find the
best match for their donation. The rating provides salience and
thus secures donors' attention to only a smaller subset of rated
nonprofits.
If all available third-­party ratings are highly correlated with
nonprofit performance and hence provide credible signals,
then it does not matter whether donors use such ratings ra-
tionally (i.e., donors know by verification that the rating is a
credible signal of performance and therefore rationally use it
to save time and effort) or as a heuristic device (i.e., donors do
not know or care to know if the rating is credible and blindly
follow it to save time and effort). In both cases, we expect the
rating to increase donations. We cannot differentiate between
its rational and behavioral use. However, if some third-­party
ratings are not correlated with nonprofit performance and
are easy to obtain (henceforth, naïve ratings), rational donors
should not respond to them if the credibility of such ratings
can be verified at a reasonable cost. However, if many donors
are behavioral, they will not invest time and effort in evalu-
ating the credibility of such ratings, even when verification is
easy. Hence, any ratings, regardless of their credibility, could
affect donors' decisions, limiting the utility of ratings in im-
proving nonprofit accountability in general.
We examine the effect of a naïve rating system that issues
simplistic evaluations based on unverified, self-­reported data
submitted by nonprofits to earn a rating. Rational donors
should discount such ratings due to their limited credibility.
Nevertheless, using a five-­year unbalanced panel dataset with
more than a million observations of all U.S. nonprofits that
collected donations and filed IRS Form 990 between 2014 and
2018, along with GuideStar's rating data, we demonstrate that
even this naïve rating exerts a significant influence on do-
nation behavior. Findings indicate that donors employ naïve
ratings as two distinct mental shortcuts. First, as a salience
mechanism, ratings draw attention to a subset of rated non-
profits, thereby increasing their visibility. Second, more so-
phisticated donors may engage in mental accounting, using
the ratings as cognitive shortcuts to distinguish among the
rated nonprofits and allocate larger donations to those with
higher ratings. These findings are consistent across multiple
estimation methods, including two-­way fixed effects (TWFE),
Inverse Propensity Weighted (IPW), and Augmented Inverse
Propensity Weighted (AIPW) fixed-­effects models, as well as
other robustness checks.
Our study makes several contributions to current literature.
First, we presented an alternative behavioral theory of third-­
party ratings, as opposed to the rational theory prevalent in
the current literature (Godecki and Duquette 2019). Second,
we empirically demonstrated two potential behavioral mecha-
nisms, salience and mental accounting, that affect donors' de-
cisions. Third, instead of using a sample of nonprofits, which
is dominant in the current literature on third-­party ratings
(Yörük 2016; Szper and Prakash 2011), we used the universe
of nonprofits that submitted IRS tax returns between 2014 and
2018 to minimize biases that may arise from sampling vari-
ability. Finaly, we used inverse propensity weighting (IPW)
and augmented IPW (AIPW) to improve causal estimation of
fixed-­effect models. These empirical methods are relatively
new in the public and nonprofit management literature and

Our findings have several policy and governance implications.
First, they suggest that the current nonprofit regulation, based
on financial and other forms of disclosure, will have a limited
effect on donors and, consequently, on nonprofit accountability.
Second, if many donors are behavioral, then even naïve ratings
can affect donors. Third, since naïve ratings are easier to obtain
than more complex ones, they may eventually dominate more
credible but complex ratings. This indicates a need to improve
the rating market if we want to use ratings as an accountability
tool. Fourth, instead of blanket disclosure, disclosing perfor-
mance information that is most relevant and presenting it as a
simple yet credible rating could be more effective for improving
nonprofit accountability.
2   |   Third-­Party Ratings of Nonprofits
Lowell et  al.  (2005) remarked that online platforms, such as
news and social media, have become vibrant mechanisms for
nonprofit accountability and transparency, with third-­party
ratings at the forefront of this new mode of accountability.
Third-­party rating agencies have emerged as influential inter-
mediaries in the nonprofit sector, mitigating information asym-
metries between donors and charitable organizations (Zhao and
Dull 2025). These entities compile, analyze, and distill publicly
available data into accessible ratings designed to signal trust-
worthiness and effectiveness to prospective donors (Stork and
Woodilla 2008).
Contemporary charity rating organizations employ diverse
methodologies. Charity Navigator initially focused on financial
efficiency ratios before expanding to the “Encompass Rating
System,” which assesses nonprofits across four domains: account-
ability and finance, impact and results, leadership and adapt-
ability, and culture and community (Charity Navigator 2022).
GuideStar operates differently, providing transparency seals
based on self-­reported information disclosure rather than per-
formance evaluation (Candid  2021). CharityWatch adopts
a narrower evaluative lens, concentrating on financial effi-
ciency. Its highest ratings are reserved for organizations that
allocate at least 75% of expenditures to programmatic activities
(CharityWatch 2022). The BBB Wise Giving Alliance applies a
standards-­based approach, evaluating nonprofits against 20 ac-
countability benchmarks covering governance, financial man-
agement, results reporting, and transparency (Better Business
Bureau 2023). These organizations typically generate revenue
through website advertising, premium services, or consulting,
creating potential incentives that may influence rating criteria
(Lowell et al. 2005).
Collectively, these rating systems provide several services to do-
nors at no cost. First, they reduce search costs by narrowing the
pool of potential recipients from more than 1.8 million registered
charities to more manageable subsets (Szper 2013). Second, they
provide standardized evaluative criteria, allowing for cross-­
organizational comparisons that would otherwise demand sub-
stantial individual research (Stork and Woodilla 2008). Third,
platforms preserve and display Form 990 filings to facilitate
deeper inquiry by interested donors. Finally, several rating sites
integrate a payment system, enabling users to make contribu-
tions directly through their platforms. These services together
reduce search and information processing costs for donors
(LeRoux et al. 2024).
3   |   Literature, Theory, and Hypotheses
Public administration scholars argue that third-­party rat-
ings can enhance accountability by shaping perceptions and
decision-­making in the public and nonprofit sectors (Meng
and Li 2025; Hong 2020; Stork and Woodilla 2008). Because
nonprofits are legally required to disclose financial and
governance-­related information, rating agencies can trans-
late these disclosures into simpler performance ratings for
donors. Following the rational tradition of Roberts (1984) and
Andreoni (1990), early studies on nonprofit disclosure concep-
tualized donors as rational actors who process the disclosed fi-
nancial information to reward and punish nonprofits based on
performance to maximize utility from giving. This line of re-
search suggests that donors penalize nonprofits with high ad-
ministrative and fundraising cost ratios (Charles et al. 2020;
Tinkelman and Mankaney  2007; Trussel and Parsons  2007;
Bekkers and Wiepking  2011). Consequently, first-­generation
rating agencies such as GuideStar and Charity Navigator (CN)
emphasized financial ratios as the centerpiece of their rating
frameworks. Under this rational approach, donors seeking to
sanction high-­overhead organizations can consult these plat-
forms to access financial information.
Most empirical studies that found that ratings increase dona-
tions have explained donors' behavior as a rational response
to the quality signals that ratings convey. Donors must choose
from a multitude of available nonprofits. While nonprofits are
aware of their quality, donors are not, giving rise to Akerlof's
(1978) “lemons market” problem. Ratings aim to minimize
this information asymmetry in the nonprofit lemons market
by providing quality signals. Graddy-­Reed (2019), for instance,
found that a 1% increase in Charity Navigator (CN) rating cor-
responded to a 0.3% increase in donations. She further noted
that rated organizations tended to report lower administrative
cost ratios, consistent with the idea that rational donors reward
low overhead. Similarly, Harris and Neely (2016) observed that
ratings increased donations, positing that they provide both
easier access to information and a credible performance signal.
Related studies conceptualize ratings primarily as performance
information signals that mitigate asymmetries between donors
and nonprofits (Sloan 2009; Gordon et al. 2009). Research has
also documented nonprofits responding to ratings, assuming
donors rationally punish nonprofits with higher overhead rate.
Szper  (2013) found that rated nonprofits underreported their
fundraising ratios, while Zhao and Dull  (2025) showed that
CEOs of rated nonprofits suppressed salary disclosures. These
findings are consistent with strategic manipulation of ratings to
satisfy watchful and rational donor expectations.
If donors use ratings as rational shortcuts to performance sig-
nals, two conditions must hold: ratings must credibly reflect
nonprofit performance, and donors must be able and willing to
verify that credibility at reasonable cost (Spence 1973). Charity

Navigator (CN) ratings, the primary ratings used in prior stud-
ies, largely satisfy the first condition, as their financial and
accountability components correlate with nonprofit financial
performance (Lamothe and Shen 2023; LeRoux et al. 2024). If
donors value financially strong nonprofits, relying on CN rat-
ings to guide donations is rational.
We identify three problems with the second condition. First, a
theory–empirical mismatch arises because nonprofit perfor-
mance is multidimensional, encompassing mission fulfillment,
accountability, financial health, and stakeholder satisfaction,
whereas most ratings emphasize financial metrics (LeRoux
et al. 2024; Lowell et al. 2005; Herman and Renz 2004). If ra-
tional donors equally value these dimensions and verify rating
credibility, ratings should have limited effects on giving. Yet em-
pirical studies consistently find that ratings increase donations,
implying either that rational donors prioritize financial perfor-
mance or, more plausibly, that behavioral donors misinterpret
financially driven ratings as reflecting whichever dimension
they value.
Second, rating agencies often produce inconsistent evalua-
tions for the same nonprofit (Stork and Woodilla 2008; Lowell
et  al.  2005). Rational donors should discount such inconsis-
tency, for example, by giving less to organizations with mixed
or poor ratings. However, nonprofits with mixed or even weak
ratings frequently receive more donations than unrated organi-
zations, indicating that donors ignore inconsistency (Harris and
Neely 2016). Similarly, donors respond to CN's star ratings but
not to the underlying numerical scores that convey finer perfor-
mance differences (Yörük 2016).
Third, credible ratings make it difficult to distinguish rational
from behavioral responses. Identifying behavioral use requires
a naïve rating that is uncorrelated with nonprofit performance
and easily obtained even by low-­quality organizations. Rational
donors should reject such a signal, producing no donation in-
crease, whereas behavioral donors, who do not verify credibility,
would still follow it, increasing donations.
From this behavioral perspective, ratings function as salient
cues and cognitive heuristics rather than verified performance
signals. Donors need not assess credibility or consciously prior-
itize specific objectives such as efficiency, accountability, trans-
parency, or mission effectiveness. Instead, they map ratings onto
whichever goals they value. This interpretation resolves the re-
strictive assumptions and empirical inconsistencies of the ratio-
nal account.
Consistent with this behavioral explanation, prior research
shows that donors often neglect or fail to process available in-
formation. Experimental and survey evidence indicate that the
provision of financial or production data does not affect giving
behavior (Coupet and Schehl 2022; Calabrese 2011). Similarly,
Jilke et al. (2019) found that explicitly informing donors of gov-
ernment funding did not affect donations, undermining the
rational “crowding out” prediction. Donors may also misallo-
cate attention. de Vita and Twombly (2005) showed that under
financial constraint, donors favored larger but lower-­performing
nonprofits over smaller, more effective ones. In other words,
donors use “size” as a heuristic to predict performance instead
of processing actual performance information. Samahita and
Lades  (2023) documented that donors punished overhead in-
discriminately, failing to distinguish necessary accountability
costs from excessive indirect costs.
If most donors are behavioral, how is their limited attention allo-
cated? Rather than deliberately directing attention, individuals
respond to cues that become salient through contrast, promi-
nence, or surprise (Loewenstein et al. 2014; Bordalo et al. 2022).
Third-­party ratings meet these conditions. Because relatively
few nonprofits are rated, ratings stand out by contrast, while
symbolic labels such as platinum, gold, silver, and bronze draw
on familiar hierarchies from sports and the financial market
that enhance prominence. As a result, ratings function as sa-
lient cues that capture donor attention. For example, a GuideStar
search in Boise, Idaho shows that only a minority of nonprofits
display seals, making rated organizations visually distinctive
(See Appendix 1.1).
Hypothesis 1.  Salience Hypothesis: Any GuideStar seal rat-
ing, regardless of level, will increase donations relative to having
no rating.
Salience alone may prompt giving, but when multiple rated
options become visible, donors may experience choice over-
load (Iyengar and Lepper 2000) or motivated reasoning when
ratings conflict with prior beliefs (Jilke and Baekgaard  2020;
de Menezes and Peci  2024). Because comparing nonprofits is
cognitively taxing, donors often rely on heuristics, a mental
shortcuts that substitute simpler judgments for complex evalu-
ation (Tversky and Kahneman 1974; Thaler 2015). One plausi-
ble shortcut is mental accounting, which groups nonprofits by
rating level and treats organizations within each level as equiv-
alent. Donors may therefore view all silver-­rated nonprofits as
similar, all gold-­rated nonprofits as similar, and infer a hierar-
chy of quality, that is, platinum > gold > silver > bronze > no
rating, using representativeness heuristics. If so, higher ratings
should attract more donations than no rating.
Hypothesis 2.  Mental Accounting Hypothesis: Each higher
rating will generate more donations than no rating.
Heuristic decision-­making also leads individuals to neglect
readily available information (Thaler  2015; Tversky and
Kahneman  1974). Just as behavioral consumers may ignore
adverse disclosures by firms (Hirshleifer et  al.  2004; Brown
et al. 2012), behavioral donors may overlook poor financial per-
formance shown on rating websites. Consequently, even low-­
performing nonprofits may benefit from ratings.
Hypothesis 3.  Negligence Hypothesis: If donors use ratings
primarily as salience and mental accounting, they will not punish
low-­performing nonprofits using ratings.

We use 5 years of unbalanced panel data (2014–2018) obtained
from GuideStar through a scholarly data-­sharing agreement.
This is the only period the organization releases data for re-
search. A key limitation is that nonprofits already rated in 2014

GuideStar compiles nonprofit data from the IRS Business
Master File (BMF) and Form 990. The BMF provides identi-
fying information (e.g., EIN, name, location), while Form 990
supplies financial and workforce data. GuideStar also assigns
transparency seals, bronze through platinum, to nonprofits that
voluntarily create profiles and self-­report required information.
All variables in our study, including ratings, originate from
GuideStar.
The rating data track each nonprofit's seal status from 2014
to 2018. To obtain a seal, nonprofits must create a GuideStar
profile and disclose specified information. Seals remain valid
for 2 years unless updated to a higher level. Failure to update
results in revocation. During the panel period, nonprofits
may transition from no seal to a seal, from seal to no seal,
or from lower to higher seals, while downward movement is
rare. GuideStar also displays Form 990–based financial infor-
mation for unrated nonprofits and archives filings for donor
review.
Whereas most prior studies rely on Charity Navigator (CN)
ratings, we use GuideStar because it better enables distinction
between rational and behavioral responses. CN ratings are in-
tentionally correlated with financial performance and empiri-
cally linked to user satisfaction (LeRoux et al. 2024), making
it difficult to separate informational signaling from behavioral
salience or mental accounting. GuideStar ratings instead reflect
self-­reported disclosures tied to transparency levels. Although
self-­reporting may correlate with underlying performance, we
address this concern theoretically and empirically within the
limits of observational data.
Spence's (1973) theory of signaling shows that credible signals
must exhibit asymmetric costs, meaning they are significantly
more expensive for low performers than for high performers
to produce. For GuideStar ratings to credibly signal nonprofit
performance, obtaining a seal would therefore need to be more
costly for low-­performing nonprofits. However, the GuideStar
process suggests minimal and largely uniform costs across per-
formance levels. Bronze seals require only mission, contact, and
leadership information, details that any registered nonprofit al-
ready possesses. Silver seals add program descriptions, a web-
site, and social media accounts, which most nonprofits similarly
maintain. Gold seals further require board leadership, demo-
graphics, and financial information, all typically reported in
IRS Form 990 filings and thus not disproportionately costly for
low performers.
Theoretically, only the platinum seal could be more expensive
for low performers, as it requires information on the strategic
plan and metrics on the program's growth and success. If a non-
profit does not track its program activities over time (e.g., the
number of beneficiaries over time), it may not produce infor-
mation on the program's growth and success (see Appendix 1.2
for GuideStar seal process). In practice, most nonprofits with
a platinum seal typically provide a few statements outlining
future targets as part of their strategic plan, along with two to
three bar charts illustrating progress over time. The GuideStar
rating, theoretically, therefore, does not possess the asymmet-
ric cost property, which is essential for signals to be credible.
In interviews with the author, GuideStar leadership confirmed
that the GuideStar rating was not designed to evaluate per-
formance. Their goal is rather to encourage greater voluntary
disclosure (Interview with a senior member of the Candid Seal
Methodology Department on August 7, 2024).
Nonetheless, ratings could still correlate with performance
through managerial behavior. Managers of low-­performing
nonprofits may underestimate the value of obtaining seals,
while rational opportunity-­cost considerations may also make
disclosure relatively more burdensome for them. Either mech-
anism would reduce seal acquisition among weaker nonprof-
its. We test this possibility empirically by examining whether
higher performance predicts a greater likelihood of obtain-
ing GuideStar ratings. Results are presented in the findings
section.

prior research (Gordon et  al.  2009; Harris and Neely  2016;
Szper and Prakash 2011; Okten and Weisbrod 2000), we ex-

as well as nonprofits reporting zero donations. Following

one-­year-­lagged covariates, consistent with prior literature.
As an alternative specification, we also include contempora-
neous covariate values, reflecting the expectation that behav-

as ongoing fundraising campaigns.

To test Hypothesis  3 (the negligence hypothesis), we created a
dummy variable, Low Performers, that equals 1 if a nonprofit in a
given year performs poorly on at least two of three performance in-
dicators. These three indicators are revenue growth, the adminis-
trative cost ratio, and the operating margin. These are the standard
performance measures used in the nonprofit literature (Bradshaw
et al. 1992; Hodge and Piccolo 2011). We included an interaction
between the seal levels and a one-­year lag of this performance
dummy variable in Equation (2) above. We included the lagged
performance dummy as the donors have access to the previous
year's performance information on the website in the current year.
We defined low performance as a nonprofit in a given year
that performs below the median on revenue growth and oper-
ating margins and has an administrative cost ratio above the
median.
Our primary specification uses a two-­way fixed-­effects (TWFE)
model controlling for nonprofit and year fixed effects and ob-
servable time-­varying covariates. TWFE assumes exogenous
selection into treatment, that is, that rated and unrated non-
profits would follow parallel outcome trends absent treatment,
an assumption potentially violated if unobserved time-­varying
factors affect both rating acquisition and donations. TWFE
may also be biased under staggered treatment timing, where
nonprofits receive ratings in different years, because late adopt-
ers are implicitly compared with early adopters when treat-
ment effects are heterogeneous over time (Baker et  al.  2025;
Goodman-­Bacon 2021).
To address selection bias and treatment-­effect heterogene-
ity, we implement two complementary estimators: Inverse
Propensity Weighted Fixed Effects (IPWFE) and Augmented
Inverse Propensity Weighted Fixed Effects (AIPWFE) (Baker
et  al.  2025; Sant'Anna and Zhao  2020). IPWFE reduces se-
lection bias conditional on observables when parallel trends
are violated or difficult to verify (Abadie 2005), and IPW-­
based approaches can outperform standard TWFE when the
propensity-­score model achieves adequate covariate balance
and common support between treated and comparison groups
(Baker et al. 2025).
The IPW estimator reweights observations by their propensity
to receive treatment, assigning higher weights to treated non-
profits that resemble untreated nonprofits, and vice versa. We
estimated the propensity score P(Xit) using the same covariates

identified this set of variables as confounders that influence both
ratings and donations through unobserved nonprofit perfor-
mance (Yörük 2016; Graddy-­Reed 2019; Harris and Neely 2016).

tor, we aimed for the propensity score specification to balance
treated and untreated nonprofits on observable confounders

5.2   |   Is the GuideStar Rating a Naïve Rating?
We measure nonprofit performance using revenue growth, op-
erating margin (total revenue minus total expenses divided by
total revenue), and overhead rate, consistent with prior literature
(Bradshaw et al. 1992; Hodge and Piccolo 2011; Callen et al. 2010).
Although financial performance captures only one dimension of
overall effectiveness (Herman and Renz 2000; Brown 2005), evi-
dence shows that indicators such as revenue growth and operating
margin correlate strongly with board effectiveness (Jackson and
Holland  1998), program outputs (Mirae Kim  2017; Hodge and
Piccolo 2011; Nobbie and Brudney 2003), organizational sustain-
ability (Kim and Mason 2020; Thornton and Lecy 2023), and cli-
ent satisfaction (LeRoux et al. 2024). Financial performance is also
the strongest predictor of perceived overall nonprofit effectiveness
(An 2021; Carman 2011). By contrast, the overhead rate remains
controversial: although it imperfectly measures efficiency because
it reflects inputs without outputs, it is weakly negatively correlated
with efficiency (Coupet and Berrett 2019).

5.3   |   Testing Hypothesis 1 (Test of Salience)
Hypothesis 1 tests whether any seal, regardless of level (bronze,
silver, gold, or platinum), attracts more donations than no seal. If
seals receive greater attention from donors when they search the
GuideStar website, donors with limited attention and effort will
be more likely to donate to nonprofits with seals. Models 1, 2,
and 3 in Table 4 present estimates from TWFE, IPW, and AIPW,
respectively, with covariates lagged by 1 year. Models 4, 5, and
6 also present TWFE, IPW, and AIPW, respectively, with both
lagged and contemporaneous variables as controls. Table A2.3
provides the complete regression output of Table 4. The TWFE
estimate indicates that having any rating increases donations
by 13% to 16% relative to having no rating. The estimate was re-
duced to 10% in IPW and 7% in AIPW estimations. All estimates
are statistically significant at p < 0.001, supporting our claim that
an apparently naïve rating can be used to increase donations.

5.5   |   Testing Hypothesis 3 (Negligence
of Important Information)

punish nonprofits for higher overhead rates, higher fundraising
expenditures, and similar performance indicators (Tinkelman

increase their sales by disclosing more when buyers ignore such
adverse information. Similarly, behavioral donors may overlook
such adverse information and fail to penalize low-­performing

6   |   Discussion
Drawing on behavioral theories of salience and heuristics, this
study shows that a seemingly naïve third-­party rating can still
increase donations by shaping behavioral donor responses. Prior
research often assumes donors are rational and able to review
disclosures or interpret ratings to reward or punish nonprofits.
While some donors may process information carefully, many
face time, attention, and cognitive constraints and remain sus-
ceptible to bias. We find that even bronze and silver ratings,
which require minimal disclosure and effort from nonprofits,
increase donations by approximately 7% and 15%, respectively,
an economically meaningful return. Rating salience helps do-
nors focus limited attention on a small subset of organizations,
reducing cognitive burden, while mental accounting encourages
associating higher ratings with higher quality and giving more
to higher-­rated nonprofits.
Our findings reinforce prior behavioral evidence in charitable
giving. Donors often ignore overhead costs unless those costs
become salient (Gneezy et al. 2014) and show reduced sensitiv-
ity when the term “overhead” is omitted (Qu and Daniel 2021).
We extend this literature by identifying salience and heuristic
processing as key mechanisms through which third-­party rat-
ings influence donations. The results also align with broader
consumer-­behavior research. Salience shapes product choice
under numerous alternatives (Gobrecht and Marchand  2023),
directs attention to specific attributes (Hyeong Min Kim 2006),
and enables firms to boost sales through decoy strategies
(Herweg et  al.  2018). Similarly, mental accounting influences
willingness to pay, purchasing decisions, borrowing and saving
behavior, and neglect of fungibility (Skwara  2023; Gupta and
Kim 2010; Silva et al. 2023).
Behavioral biases in nonprofit giving show comparable patterns.
Salient reference donations increase crowdfunding contribu-
tions (van Teunenbroek and Bekkers  2020), identifiable vic-
tims attract more support (Bhati and Hansen 2020), perceived
proximity raises giving via heuristic impact judgments (Touré-­
Tillery and Fishbach 2017), salient but less relevant information
distorts evaluation (Bartels and Burnett 2011), and donors prefer
simpler, comparable information over more valid but complex
data (Baron and Szymanska 2010).

Together, these findings indicate that many donors rely on men-
tal shortcuts rather than fully rational evaluation. Behavioral
use of ratings is not inherently harmful when ratings reflect
objective performance. However, heterogeneous rating meth-
odologies may weaken ratings' role in nonprofit accountability,
motivating calls for more comprehensive and standardized sys-
tems incorporating financial trends, expert and user feedback,
and reputation measures (Lowell et al. 2005).
Our study offers three policy contributions. First, limited donor
rationality reduces the effectiveness of extensive disclosure,
suggesting regulators should emphasize clear, concise, and
selective information. Second, the strong donation effects of
even naïve ratings place responsibility on rating agencies to en-
sure reliability while highlighting a potential simplicity–rigor
trade-­off that future research should examine. If simple but un-
reliable ratings influence donors more than rigorous ones, low-­
quality ratings may dominate the market, shaping nonprofit
strategy (Suykens et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2025; AbouAssi and
Tschirhart  2018; AbouAssi  2013). Third, rating salience may
erode as more nonprofits become rated, raising the possibility
of quota-­based or performance-­based rating allocation for sus-
tained impact.
Although our design cannot fully separate heuristic use from
credible disclosure signaling, several findings support the heu-
ristic interpretation. Bronze and silver seals, whose information
is already publicly available, still generate sizable donation in-
creases. Donors also fail to penalize financially low-­performing
nonprofits that obtain ratings, even when additional financial
disclosure distinguishes seal levels, particularly gold versus sil-
ver. We, however, do not claim that all donors use ratings as a
mental shortcut or that ratings lack informational value. Rather,
a substantial share appears to use GuideStar ratings as salient
categorization heuristics that substitute for detailed evaluation.
Future research should causally disentangle heuristic versus
disclosure effects of nonprofit ratings.
7   |   Conclusion
Financial disclosure and third-­party ratings are the primary
means by which donors hold nonprofits accountable. The effec-
tiveness of these measures depends on the assumption that do-
nors are rational. Using a panel dataset with more than a million
nonprofit-­year observations, we demonstrated that many donors
are behavioral and therefore fail to consider important disclosed
information when making donations. In this context, even a
naïve rating can significantly influence their decisions through
salience and heuristics. Our findings indicate the limited effec-
tiveness of the current measures of nonprofit disclosures and
third-­party ratings in making nonprofits accountable.

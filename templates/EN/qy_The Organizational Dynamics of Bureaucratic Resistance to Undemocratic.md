ABSTRACT
Democratic backsliding raises new challenges for bureaucracies as politicians undermine democratic institutions and the rule of
law. Although bureaucracies can play a central safeguarding role, little is known about the organizational conditions that foster
resistance to undemocratic pressure. This study tests whether organizational networks (peers and professional associations) and
resources (expertise and voice mechanisms) influence bureaucrats' willingness to oppose undemocratic demands from political
superiors. Drawing on a preregistered conjoint survey experiment with Brazilian bureaucrats (N = 2481; 14,886 evaluations),
we find that support from peers, professional associations, and credible voice channels increases open resistance, whereas peer
disagreement reduces silent resistance. This study is among the first large-­scale survey experiments to manipulate organiza-
tional attributes in democratic backsliding. We advance scholarship by developing a meso-­level organizational framework that
connects networks and resources to micro-­level resistance, bridging research on democratic backsliding and behavioral public
administration, and providing practical guidance for strengthening democratic guardianship in organizations.
1   |   Introduction
In recent years, the rise of democratic backsliding across di-
verse contexts has raised new questions about how bureaucra-
cies respond to populist challenges to the administrative state
(Bauer and Becker  2020; Bauer  2025; Moynihan  2025; Peters
and Pierre 2019; Yesilkagit et al. 2024). Bureaucrats can safe-
guard democratic institutions (Gay and Lopdrup-­Hjorth 2024;
Suzuki  2025; Yesilkagit et  al.  2024) by adhering to constitu-
tional rules and organizational mandates, even when these
conflict with the preferences of illiberal superiors (Bersch and
Lotta 2024; Gofen 2024; Kucinskas 2025; Lotta, de Alves Lima,
et al. 2024). In many settings, they defend institutional missions
despite threats from political leaders, including dismissal, re-
moval, lawsuits, harassment, and persecution (Bauer et al. 2021;
Bersch and Lotta 2024; Gofen 2024; Koga et al. 2023; Lotta, de
Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Trajber Waisbich 2024).
Although democratic backsliding has been widely stud-
ied (Bauer  2025; Foa and Mounk  2016, 2017; Howe  2017;
Johnson 2004; Lotta, de Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Peters and
Pierre  2019, 2022, 2025; Yesilkagit et  al.  2024), we know
comparatively little about the organizational conditions that
impact bureaucrats' willingness to resist undemocratic polit-
ical demands, particularly based on experimental evidence.
Existing scholarship has largely focused primarily on indi-
vidual dispositions or macro-­institutional dynamics, rarely
manipulating organizational conditions to assess their causal
effects. Experimental evidence on meso-­level drivers of bu-
reaucratic resistance during democratic backsliding remains

stantial risks for bureaucrats, including political retaliation
and the exploitation of internal power asymmetries (Lotta,

we investigate the research question: Which organizational
conditions influence bureaucrats' willingness to counter un-
democratic demands from their political superiors? We define
undemocratic demands as political directives that contradict
the rule of law and the institutional mission of public agen-
cies (Gofen 2024; Lotta, de Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Yesilkagit
et al. 2024).
To answer this question, we conducted a preregistered con-
joint survey experiment (N = 2481) with Brazilian federal bu-
reaucrats across various policy domains. We implemented the
study following the Bolsonaro administration, which is seen as
a key example of democratic backsliding (Bauer 2025; Boese,
Alizada, et al. 2022; Koga et al. 2023; Milhorance 2022; Peters
and Pierre 2019). The involvement of Brazilian bureaucrats in
political processes is well-­documented, ranging from active
support for presidential agendas to different forms of resis-
tance (Bunker 1983; Haddad 1981; Schuster et al. 2022; Lotta,
de Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024). In
the survey, we randomized organizational characteristics to
assess the impact of supportive internal and external net-
works, organizational expertise, and reliable voicing channels
such as internal ombuds offices. We then examined whether
bureaucrats embedded in supportive organizational environ-
ments were more likely to express intentions to resist undem-
ocratic demands, either openly or silently. By open means,
we refer to transparent actions taken with the awareness of
coworkers and supervisors, such as registering complaints
through government systems or raising issues upward (Lotta,
de Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024). In
contrast, silent means involve covert resistance, such as sabo-
tage or guerrilla government practices, conducted without the
knowledge of colleagues or superiors (Cox 2025; O'Leary 2017;
Olsson 2016).
The findings show that bureaucrats are more likely to resist
openly when they receive peer support or backing from pro-
fessional associations. Access to credible voicing channels,
such as internal ombuds offices, also increases the likelihood
of open resistance, while unreliable channels discourage it.
With respect to silent resistance, peer disagreement reduces
bureaucrats' willingness to push back against undemocratic
demands. Taken together, these results highlight how organi-
zational dynamics shape bureaucrats' assessments of undem-
ocratic pressure. The perceived risks of resistance depend on
the organizational context. Open resistance may seem more
costly initially, but these costs are reduced with coworker
support, potentially making it less burdensome than silent
resistance. In contrast, silent resistance may lead to harsher
consequences if discovered by peers, as it can be seen as a lack
of solidarity or misalignment with group norms (Bergemann
and Aven 2023; Sufi et al. 2024). Overall, the findings indicate
that organizational environments play a central role in shap-
ing how bureaucrats evaluate the risks and rewards of resist-
ing undemocratic demands.
This study contributes to the growing literature on public ad-
ministration under democratic backsliding (Bauer 2025; Cohen
and Duhl  2025; Guedes-­Neto and Peters  2025; Gofen  2024;
Lotta, Piotrowska, and Raaphorst  2024; Kucinskas  2025;
Peters and Pierre 2019; Yesilkagit et al. 2024) by advancing
a theoretical account of the organizational determinants
of bureaucratic resistance. By integrating insights from be-
havioral public administration with research on democratic
backsliding, we develop a framework that highlights how
meso-­level organizational conditions influence bureaucrats'
willingness to resist undemocratic demands. In doing so, the
study demonstrates how internal features of bureaucracies
can mitigate undemocratic pressures and help safeguard dem-
ocratic institutions (Bauer 2025; Yesilkagit et al. 2024). More
broadly, it also contributes to debates on politician–bureau-
crat relations and the role of bureaucrats as internal checks
on executive authority during episodes of executive overreach
and ethical violations (Brierley 2020; Cox 2025; Ingber 2018;
Kucinskas 2025; Kucinskas and Silveira 2025; Meyer-­Sahling
et al. 2019).

et  al.  2024). Democratic backsliding is commonly defined
as the gradual weakening of democratic institutions by

and Ziblatt  2019; Peters and Pierre  2019; Riedl et  al.  2024,

This process typically involves the concentration of execu-
tive power, the erosion of checks and balances, and attacks on
oversight institutions such as the media, judiciary, and legisla-
ture, alongside the weakening of accountability mechanisms
and civic participation (Bauer and Becker 2020; Bermeo 2016;
Coppedge 2017; Johnson 2004). Democratic backsliding is fur-
ther associated with the rise of antisystem parties and popu-
list leaders, growing public tolerance for abuses of power and
violations of civil liberties, and declining commitment to the
core values of liberal democracy (Foa and Mounk 2016, 2017;
Howe 2017; Johnson 2004). Scholarship also shows that dem-
ocratic backsliding systematically undermines the adminis-
trative state, as illiberal leaders target bureaucracies that may
obstruct their agendas (Moynihan  2025). These strategies in-
clude delegitimizing bureaucratic expertise, politicizing the
civil service, and dismantling institutional safeguards designed
to protect the public interest and preserve administrative integ-
rity (Bauer 2025; Cohen and Duhl 2025; Koga et al. 2023; Lotta,
Tavares, and Story 2024; Moynihan 2025; Silva 2022).
In the face of pressures from illiberal political superiors, bu-
reaucrats can respond in different ways. Some comply and
implement undemocratic directives from their principals
(González-­Vázquez et  al.  2024; Kucinskas  2025; Peci  2021;
Peters and Pierre 2019; Piotrowska 2024), while others engage
in practices aimed at countering or avoiding such orders (Alon-­
Barkat et al. 2025; Bersch and Lotta 2024; Gofen 2024; Bauer
et al. 2021; Lobo et al. 2025; Lotta, de Alves Lima, et al. 2024;
O'Leary  2017). Resistance strategies include voicing con-
cerns, shirking, sabotage, and other forms of guerrilla govern-
ment, as well as exiting the organization altogether (Cox 2025;
Kucinskas and Silveira 2025; Lotta, de Alves Lima, et al. 2024;
Piotrowska  2024; Schuster et  al.  2022; Silva  2022; Peters and
Pierre 2019).
A useful way to classify bureaucrats' responses is to distin-
guish between open and silent forms of resistance (Brehm and
Gates 1999; Cox 2024; Hirschman 1970; Hollibaugh et al. 2020).
Open resistance is visible to superiors and colleagues and may
involve voicing complaints, providing feedback, or issuing
public letters and reports (Alon-­Barkat et al. 2025; Bersch and
Lotta 2024; Kucinskas and Silveira 2025; Lotta, de Alves Lima,
et al. 2024; Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024). Silent resistance, by
contrast, is concealed from both principals and peers and in-
cludes practices such as whistleblowing, deliberate noncom-
pliance, clandestine meetings, informal partnerships, secret
coalition building with external allies, and other forms of co-
vert sabotage (Brehm and Gates  1999; Cox  2025; Hollibaugh
et  al.  2020; Krone  1992; Lotta, Fernandez, et  al.  2024;
O'Leary 2017; Silveira 2024).
Open forms of resistance may expose bureaucrats to higher
perceived risks of retaliation (Cox  2024; Lotta, Fernandez,
et al. 2024), while silent forms, though offering protection, may
involve unaccountable activities (O'Leary  2017). While open
resistance might initially seem more costly, these costs can be
mitigated when supported by coworkers, potentially making it
less burdensome than silent resistance. However, if silent resis-
tance is uncovered, it could lead to harsher repercussions, as it
may be perceived by peers as a lack of solidarity or a failure to
align with group norms (Bergemann and Aven 2023). Therefore,
the cost of silent or open resistance depends not only on the ac-
tion itself but also on group affiliation dynamics and how it is
perceived by colleagues (Bergemann and Aven  2023; Tavares
et al. 2025). Furthermore, open forms of resistance tend to im-
pose higher costs on the government, whereas silent forms are
often more discreet and may cause less immediate damage to
political leadership (Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024). These differ-
ences raise a central question: what organizational factors shape
bureaucrats' choices between open and silent resistance when
confronting undemocratic demands?
Studies emphasize that, even under authoritarian rule, bu-
reaucratic behavior varies systematically with institutional
design, legitimacy, and organizational constraints, underscor-
ing the importance of meso-­level conditions for understanding
when and how resistance emerges (Bunker 1983; Epstein 1984;
Geddes and Zaller 1989; Remmer and Merkx 1982; Cingolani
and Lotta 2026). Beyond macro-­institutional pressures, behav-
ioral public administration highlights how motivation, per-
ceived risks and cost-­benefit evaluations shape decision-­making,
and ethical behavior (Fischer and Weißmüller  2024; Heine
et al. 2022; Meyer-­Sahling et al. 2019; Hollibaugh et al. 2020).
Experimental evidence shows that public service motivation
(PSM) increases willingness to engage in self-­sacrificing be-
havior (Heine et  al.  2022). Relatedly, activating PSM can in-
crease ethical reporting among civil servants (Meyer-­Sahling
et al. 2019). These findings suggest that stated willingness and
ethical action are linked but also context dependent—particu-
larly on whether organizational conditions reduce the perceived
costs of acting (Kang 2023). Building on this logic, we theorize
how meso-­level organizational factors, such as networks and
resources (Cingolani and Lotta 2026), lower perceived risk and
enable bureaucrats to express intentions to engage in specific
forms of ethical dissent or resistance, whether open or silent.
Despite bureaucrats' crucial role in defending democratic in-
stitutions, existing research has given limited attention to the
organizational factors shaping their willingness to resist un-
democratic directives. To address this gap, this study focuses
on two dimensions: (i) internal and external organizational net-
works and (ii) organizational resources, including agency exper-
tise and voicing channels (Cingolani and Lotta 2026).
2.1   |   Organizational Networks
Networks within and beyond organizations provide bureaucrats
with information, guidance, and reference points for action, par-
ticularly during periods of uncertainty (Carothers 2025; Trajber
Waisbich  2024; Riedl et  al.  2025; Cingolani and Lotta  2026).
These relational resources are especially important when bu-
reaucrats confront undemocratic demands from superiors, as
they enable advice-­seeking, collective deliberation, opportuni-
ties for practice-­sharing, and a sense of belonging to a broader
professional community (Kucinskas and Silveira 2025; Stauffer
et al. 2024). Because networks also mediate access to professional

In contexts of democratic backsliding, such networks can acti-

resistance as consistent with professional norms and organiza-
tional missions, strengthening bureaucrats' willingness to op-
pose undemocratic pressures (Lotta, de Alves Lima, et al. 2024).
2.1.1   |   External Networks
Ties to external networks expand bureaucrats' access to strate-
gic knowledge and protection beyond organizational boundaries
(Abers 2020; Carpenter 2002; Evans 1995; Carothers 2025; Lotta,
de Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Silveira 2024; Trajber Waisbich 2024).
Cross-­sector ties—including international organizations, pro-
fessional associations, unions, and NGOs—function as reputa-
tional assets and external safeguards against political interference
(Abers 2020; Bauer and Ege 2016; Gazley 2014; Howell-­Moroney
and Ertas  2025; Kucinskas and Silveira  2025; Olsson  2016;
Vilaça 2020). Because these actors lie outside political hierarchies,
they are less susceptible to political interference and can enable
both overt and covert resistance (Bauer and Ege 2016; Gazley 2014;
Trajber Waisbich 2024; Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024). Evidence
from Brazil, the United States, and the European Union shows that
professional associations and international organizations mobilize
ethical, reputational, and informational resources that support
collective dissent or reinforce public service values (Gazley 2014;
Bauer and Ege 2016; Silveira 2024).
These external entities enhance bureaucrats' ability to respond,
whether silently or openly, to political overreach (Lotta, Fernandez,
et al. 2024). The rationale for this assumption is that external net-
works provide professional standards and strategic guidance for
addressing ethical dilemmas, offer protection and advice against
political retaliation, and support the coordination of both open and
silent responses to illiberal threats, ranging from public tactics to
more covert forms of opposition, such as sabotage or informal co-
alitions with NGOs and legislators (Abers 2020; Lotta, Fernandez,
et  al.  2024; Silveira  2024; Trajber Waisbich  2024). These sup-
ports reduce the perceived costs and risks of resistance, influenc-
ing individual decision-­making toward a greater willingness to
counter undemocratic demands through open or silent means.
Accordingly, we hypothesize that external networks increase the
likelihood of both silent and open resistance:
Hypothesis 1.  Bureaucrats will be more likely to engage in
(H1a) silent and (H1b) overt reactions when they have the support
of external networks compared to when they do not.
2.1.2   |   Internal Networks
Internal ties, such as coworkers' networks, provide bureaucrats
with guidance and reference points for navigating uncertainty, es-
pecially under undemocratic pressure (Cingolani and Lotta 2026).
Shared understandings among coworkers shape how political-­
administrative conflicts are perceived and managed, standard-
izing behavior through peer norms and routines (Borry  2017;
Fleming  2020; Gofen  2014; Silveira  2024; Taylor  2018). High
internal connectedness reduces organizational fragmentation and
strengthens resilience against political interference, reflected in
strong cultures, belonging, and prosocial behavior (Cingolani and
Lotta 2026; Fleming 2020; Foldy and Buckley 2010; Gofen 2014;
Hallett and Ventresca 2006; Keiser 2010).
Evidence further shows that peer deliberation and mutual sup-
port function as coping strategies under illiberal pressures,
enabling joint evaluation of directives, emotional support, and
coordinated dissent, often through silent resistance (Cox 2025;
Gofen 2014; Kucinskas 2025; Kucinskas and Silveira 2025; Lotta,
Fernandez, et al. 2024; Piotrowska 2024; Stauffer et al. 2024).
Comparative studies from the United States and Brazil link peer
support to covert action, viewed as a lower-­risk response under
democratic backsliding (Kucinskas and Silveira 2025).
Because bottom-­up approaches driven by internal networks oper-
ate within hierarchical power asymmetries, bureaucracies that rely
heavily on such networks often turn to silent resistance, including
sabotage, to avoid retaliation from political superiors (Kucinskas
and Silveira 2025; Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024). As open resis-
tance entails higher risks of political reprisal (Lotta, Tavares, and
Story 2024; Story et al. 2023), support from lower organizational
levels is more commonly associated with covert actions—such as
sabotage and other forms of guerrilla government—as strategies of
self-­protection (Hollibaugh et al. 2020; Kucinskas and Zylan 2023;
Lobo et  al.  2025; O'Leary  2017; Schuster et  al.  2022; Story
et al. 2023). Based on these assumptions, we hypothesize that:
Hypothesis 2.  The higher the levels of supportive internal
networks, the more likely bureaucrats will be to engage in silent
reactions.
2.2   |   Organizational Resources
Alongside networks, organizational resources constitute a sec-
ond pillar supporting bureaucratic resistance to undemocratic
pressure (Cingolani and Lotta  2026). These resources include
budgetary stability (Moynihan  2022), access to strategic in-
frastructure such as technology (Cingolani and Lotta  2026),
organizational knowledge and collective expertise (Koga
et al. 2023), voicing channels such as ombuds offices (Cox 2025;
Miceli et  al.  2008), and agencies' legitimacy and reputation
(Carpenter  2002; Carpenter and Krause  2015; Rimkutė and
Mazepus 2025). In this study, we focus on two particular organi-
zational resources: (i) expertise and (ii) voicing channels.
2.2.1   |   Organizational Expertise
This refers to the strategic knowledge and skills that enable
agencies to deliver services and influence decision-­making
in the policy process (Cingolani and Lotta 2026). Such exper-
tise can become monopolistic when agencies develop unique
problem-­solving capacities and exclusive powers critical to
the functioning of the administrative state (Carpenter 2002;
Cingolani and Lotta  2026; Koga et  al.  2023). Comparative
research links technical expertise, merit-­based personnel
systems, and lower politicization to stronger organizational
performance and state capacity, which enhances resistance

and Cheema 2025; Lapuente and Suzuki 2020; Lotta, de Alves

Expertise is closely tied to agency legitimacy and support within
broader policy communities (Carpenter  2002; Carpenter and
Krause 2015; Koga et al. 2023). When strategically deployed, ex-

attract support from external policy communities, enabling bu-

both silent and open resistance (Anteby et al. 2016; Cingolani
and Lotta 2026; Yesilkagit et al. 2024). Therefore, we hypothe-
size that:
Hypothesis 3.  The higher the levels of organizational exper-
tise, the more likely bureaucrats will be to engage in (H3a) silent
and (H3b) open reactions.
2.2.2   |   Voicing Channels
Another important organizational resource enabling bureau-
crats to resist undemocratic interference consists of estab-
lished dissent and voice structures (Cingolani and Lotta 2026;
Kang 2023; O'Leary 2017), such as internal ombuds offices and
whistleblower protection systems (Cox 2025; Kang 2023; Kwon
et al. 2016; Noort et al. 2019). Because bureaucrats hold less
hierarchical power than their political superiors, institution-
alized channels that allow them to voice concerns safely and
effectively are critical organizational resources within agen-
cies (Cox 2024, 2025; Kang 2023; Kwon et al. 2016; Wilkinson
et al. 2020). In contexts of democratic backsliding, bureaucrats
exposed to abusive supervision by political appointees are
more likely to disengage or remain silent to protect themselves
from retaliation (Kucinskas 2025; Kucinskas and Zylan 2023;
Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024; Story et al. 2023). The absence
of credible channels for raising complaints, ethical concerns,
or whistleblowing can therefore weaken agencies' capacity
to detect, document, and respond to undemocratic political
pressures.
Research on prosocial whistleblowing, employee voice, organi-
zational learning, ethical behavior, and guerrilla government
highlights the importance of effective, accountable, and safe
institutional mechanisms that allow bureaucrats to express dis-
sent and be legitimately heard, with options for anonymity and
procedural safeguards (Caillier 2017; Ellmer and Reichel 2021;
Kassing 2002; Kang 2023; Kwon et al. 2016; Miceli et al. 2008;
Noort et al. 2019; O'Leary 2017; Wilkinson et al. 2020). Such
channels reduce the personal risks of dissent by providing confi-
dentiality, due process protections, and insulation from retalia-
tion. As a result, voicing resources lower the costs of expressing
opposition within organizations (Cingolani and Lotta  2026;
Kang 2023; O'Leary 2017). We, therefore, expect that access to
voicing resources will encourage civil servants to prefer silent
over open forms of resistance, as these channels allow dissent
to be expressed while minimizing exposure to political and or-
ganizational sanctions (Story et al. 2023). Accordingly, we hy-
pothesize that:
Hypothesis 4.  The higher the levels of organizational protec-
tion stemming from voicing channels, the more likely bureaucrats
will be to engage in silent reactions.

work. We expect that greater access to relational and organi-
zational resources increases bureaucrats' willingness to resist
undemocratic demands and shapes the form that resistance
takes, influencing the likelihood of silent versus open responses.
To evaluate these expectations, we examine the Brazilian fed-
eral bureaucracy under the Bolsonaro administration, a para-
digmatic case of democratic backsliding.

federal administration largely follows a Weberian model: it
is career-­based, professionalized, and guided by principles
of neutrality, integrity, and constitutional loyalty. Civil ser-
vants are recruited through competitive examinations, pro-
tected by tenure, and enjoy relatively high autonomy (Berman
et  al.  2025; Schuster et  al.  2022). Prior research highlights
their strong public service ethos and dense ties to external
networks—such as international organizations, NGOs, pro-
fessional associations, and unions—which reinforce collec-
tive action and policy engagement (Lotta, de Alves Lima,
et al. 2024; Schuster et al. 2022; Silveira et al. 2024). Unions
and professional associations, in particular, play a central
role in defending labor rights, coordinating collective action,
and shaping policy debates (Kucinskas and Silveira  2025;
Silveira  2024). Historically, Brazilian bureaucrats have re-
sponded variably to presidential authority, ranging from
support for insulated technocracy to more explicit resistance
(Haddad  1981; Remmer and Merkx  1982; Schneider  1992;
Bunker 1983; Schuster et al. 2022).
Bolsonaro's presidency (2019–2022) constitutes a paradigmatic
case of democratic backsliding (Bauer 2025; Boese, Lundstedt,
et al. 2022; Peters and Pierre 2022). His government pursued po-
liticization and control of the bureaucracy, while civil servants
developed multiple resistance strategies (Bersch and Lotta 2024;
Koga et  al.  2023; Lotta, de Alves Lima, et  al.  2024; Lotta,
Fernandez, et al. 2024; Milhorance 2022; Peci 2021; Silva 2022).
Career managers were frequently replaced by politically loyal
appointees, and in sectors such as environmental policy, agen-
cies became sites of militarization, harassment, and abusive su-
pervision (Bersch and Lotta 2024; Kucinskas and Silveira 2025;
Story et  al.  2023). Political appointees employed formal and
informal tactics to silence dissent, including moral harass-
ment, administrative proceedings, and intimidation (Lotta,
Fernandez, et al. 2024; Silva 2022; Story et al. 2023). In response,
bureaucrats engaged in both silent and open resistance, individ-
ually and collectively, with unions and professional associations
playing a central organizing role (Kucinskas and Silveira 2025;
Koga et al. 2023; Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024; Silveira 2024).
Given the Weberian features of Brazil's federal administration
and the intensity of pressures it faced under Bolsonaro, this case
offers a critical setting for analyzing the organizational condi-
tions of bureaucratic resistance. Following Bolsonaro's electoral
defeat and the return of Lula da Silva (2023–2026), our study
was conducted at a moment when bureaucrats could reflect on
these experiences and assess resistance strategies more openly
within a hypothetical backsliding scenario.
3.2   |   Data Collection
We collected data in partnership with Brazil's National School
of Public Administration (ENAP). The study received approval
from the FGV Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 284/2023)
and was preregistered on the OSF platform prior to data col-
lection (preregistration documents available at: https://​osf.​
io/​e3vja​). ENAP administered the online survey via Qualtrics
between November 11, 2023, and January 8, 2024. Conducted
during the first year of President Lula da Silva's third term, the
survey offered a comparatively safer context for reflecting on
experiences under the Bolsonaro administration. Participation
was fully voluntary and anonymous.
We randomly selected email addresses from 50% of Brazilian
federal public servants across 37 federal organizations, includ-
ing ministries and agencies, resulting in 296,048 invitations. Of
these, 5241 respondents accessed the survey (1.8%), and 2481
(47%) completed the full questionnaire. Although the response
rate is low, this is common in surveys of bureaucrats, a popu-
lation widely regarded as hard to reach (Bauer et al. 2021; Lobo
et al. 2025; Schuster et al. 2022; Story et al. 2023). Comparative
evidence from the Global Survey of Public Servants shows that
Brazil has the lowest response rates among 23 countries—11%,
compared to an average of 63% (Schuster et al. 2023). Despite
this limitation, our sample closely mirrors the broader pop-
ulation of Brazilian federal civil servants in age, education,
tenure, and race/color, with a slightly higher share of manag-
ers (see Appendix A in Supporting Information). Nonresponse
bias, therefore, remains a concern, but the experimental design
strengthens internal validity and mitigates this limitation.
3.3   |   The Conjoint Design
We evaluate our hypotheses through a conjoint survey experi-
ment (Hainmueller et al. 2014; Liu and Shiraito 2023). In our ex-
periment, each participant evaluates three pairs of hypothetical
scenarios of bureaucratic resistance. Respondents answer three
questions regarding these different forms of resistance, which
serve as our outcome variables. By randomly varying the attri-
butes of each hypothetical scenario, we causally estimate the im-
pact of each attribute's values on respondents' preferences. With
three repeated tasks, this results in six observations per respon-
dent. This means a total of 14,886 observations. Previous studies
indicate that conjoint experiments with up to 30 tasks maintain
high levels of reliability in response quality (Bansak et al. 2018).
Each pair of scenarios is introduced as follows: “Imagine a situ-
ation in which the federal government insisted that you imple-
ment a decision that you were convinced was against democratic
principles.” This introduction is designed to broadly present the
scenario, thereby preventing respondents from being biased by
specific contexts (Hainmueller et al. 2014). It reflects real-­world
situations in which public employees are often confronted with
undemocratic demands issued by their superiors. Prior research
shows that political-­administrative conflicts over undemocratic
policymaking peaked under Jair Bolsonaro's administration
(Lotta, de Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Lotta, Tavares, and Story 2024;
Peci 2021; Silveira 2024; Story et al. 2023). Civil servants faced
pressure and illegal demands as political appointees sought to
violate legal norms to advance their agendas (Lotta, de Alves
Lima, et al. 2024; Lotta, Fernandez, et al. 2024; Silva 2022).
We constructed the hypothetical scenarios by randomizing four
attributes: “colleagues” (peer agreement as a proxy for internal
networks), “external support” (capturing external networks),
“expertise” (the organization's expertise relative to other agen-
cies), and “protections” (safeguards provided by an internal
ombuds office, capturing voicing channels). These attributes
reflect realistic organizational conditions and are grounded in
prior empirical research on the Brazilian bureaucracy under the

principles, resistance is most likely to emerge when colleagues
converge on the assessment that such demands contradict the
agency's mission (Lotta, de Alves Lima, et al. 2024; Peci 2021;

Based on the number of attribute levels, our conjoint design in-
cludes 3 × 4 × 3 × 3 = 108 possible scenarios. To avoid contamina-
tion effects, both the order of dimensions and the attribute values
were randomized using uniform distributions (Hainmueller
et al. 2014). Additionally, the conjoint design presents two com-
parative situations (a pair of scenarios) with differing attributes
using concise statements. This paired format supports compar-
ative assessment and reduces cognitive burden, requiring sim-
ple attributes for clear table presentation (Bansak et  al.  2019;
Hainmueller et al. 2014; Hainmueller and Hopkins 2015).
After reviewing each pair of scenarios, respondents answer
three questions. The first question involves a binary choice
(Context 1 or Context 2), focusing on the highest likelihood of
open resistance. The remaining questions use a Likert-­like scale
ranging from 1 (“I'd certainly not resist”) to 5 (“I'd certainly re-
sist”) for each context. One question assesses open resistance,
while the other evaluates silent resistance. The questions are as
follows:
1. In which of the two contexts would you be more likely to resist
openly (e.g., acting with the knowledge of colleagues and super-
visors) by deciding not to implement the federal government's
decision?
2. Considering the characteristics of the two contexts presented
earlier, what is the likelihood that you would resist openly (e.g.,
acting with the knowledge of colleagues and supervisors) in im-
plementing this decision, in each of the contexts?
3. Considering the characteristics of the two contexts presented
earlier, what is the likelihood that you would resist silently (e.g.,
acting without the knowledge of colleagues and supervisors) in
implementing this decision, in each of the contexts?
Our survey underwent multiple rounds of refinement, incorpo-
rating content and wording feedback from experienced research-
ers across participating institutions. It was then pretested twice
with Brazilian federal civil servants, whose feedback informed
revisions to improve clarity, relevance, and alignment with their
work realities. In the final version, participants confirmed that
the statements reflected their working conditions and political-­
administrative challenges under the Bolsonaro administration,
particularly undemocratic demands. These pretests support the
face validity of the survey items.
3.4   |   Models
Our analysis centers on the average marginal component effect
(AMCE), which captures how the probability of selecting a sce-
nario changes when an attribute shifts levels (Bansak et al. 2023;
Hainmueller et al. 2014). We estimate AMCEs using ordinary
least squares (OLS) regression, treating attributes as categorical
independent variables.
To assess robustness, we conducted three additional sets of anal-
yses: (a) models including individual-­level controls and agency
fixed effects (Appendix E in Supporting Information); (b) mod-
els estimated on a filtered sample excluding respondents flagged
as speeders (bottom 25% of completion times) or straight-­
liners (invariant responses across conjoint tasks) (Appendix F
in Supporting Information); and (c) models incorporating
the Adaptive Shrinkage (ASH) correction (Stephens  2017)
(Appendix G in Supporting Information).

4   |   Results
The first model examines open resistance as a binary outcome
using a forced-­choice design. Respondents indicated which of
two randomized contexts would make them more likely to resist
openly, defined as refusing to implement a governmental deci-
sion with the knowledge of colleagues and superiors. Figure 1
reports the average marginal component effects (AMCEs).
Results show that colleagues' support strongly increases the
likelihood of open resistance, while its absence substantially re-
duces it. Perceived agency expertise also matters: higher exper-
tise increases resistance to undemocratic orders whereas, lower
expertise dampens it. Voicing channels (protections) further
shape behavior. When an ombudsman's office is seen as pro-
viding credible protection, open resistance becomes the dom-
inant strategy; when it is perceived as consistently ineffective,
resistance is strongly deterred. Compared to internal support,
professional associations increase open resistance, NGO sup-
port decreases it, and international organizations have no effect.
We also measured preferences for open resistance using a 5-­
point Likert-­type scale (Figure  2). Rather than choosing be-
tween contexts, respondents reported how likely they were to
openly resist in each scenario. The results largely mirror those
of the binary measure, with only small differences. Although
“high agency expertise” is no longer statistically significant at
conventional levels, its coefficient remains positive, while “low
expertise” continues to have a negative and statistically signif-
icant effect, indicating the weakest propensity for open resis-
tance. Regarding external networks, NGO support is positive
but not statistically significant, whereas support from interna-
tional organizations reaches statistical significance, and profes-
sional associations remain the strongest external driver of open
resistance.
Our final model examines silent resistance (Figure  3).
Respondents reported the likelihood that they would silently re-
sist—defined as acting without the knowledge of colleagues and
superiors—in each context. Nearly all attributes show null ef-
fects. The only significant factor is coworkers' disagreement: fre-
quent disagreement among colleagues is associated with a lower
likelihood of silent resistance, highlighting the importance of
peer alignment in shaping resistance strategies.

4.1   |   Robustness Checks
Because conjoint experiments test multiple hypotheses and are
susceptible to false positives, we apply the ASH correction to en-
sure robustness (Liu and Shiraito 2023; Stephens 2017). Results
are reported in Tables G1 (forced-­choice open resistance) and
G2 (continuous open resistance) in Appendix G in Supporting
Information. Because the “silent resistance” model did not yield
statistically significant coefficients for most variables, we did
not further analyze this model. “Original Estimates” report raw
effect sizes, “Posterior Means” present ASH-­adjusted estimates,
and “LFSR” indicates the probability that an effect's sign is in-
correct, with lower values indicating greater confidence.
For the binary outcome (Table G1 in Supporting Information),
most LFSR values are extremely low (0.0000–0.0002), indi-
cating a low probability that the estimated effect signs are
incorrect and suggesting that these estimates are robust. By con-
trast, High Agency Expertise and International Organizations
Support show higher LFSR values (0.36 and 0.75), indicating
substantial uncertainty and warranting cautious interpretation.
For the continuous measure of open resistance (Table  G2 in
Supporting Information), LFSR values vary more widely. High
Agency Expertise and International Organizations Support
again display high LFSRs, as do Colleagues Disagreement and
NGO Support, suggesting that these estimates should also be in-
terpreted with caution.
Beyond the ASH correction, we conducted two additional ro-
bustness checks. First, we estimated extended AMCE mod-
els that included individual-­level controls and agency fixed
effects (Appendix  E in Supporting Information). Second, we
re-­estimated AMCEs using both the full sample and a filtered
sample excluding speeders (the bottom 25% of completion time)
and straight-­liners (invariant responses across conjoint tasks)
(Appendix F in Supporting Information). The estimates are gen-
erally consistent across models. The main difference arises in
the silent resistance model: while the coefficient for colleagues'
disagreement maintains its direction, it no longer achieves sta-
tistical significance.
Overall, the robustness checks consistently indicate that support
from colleagues (internal networks) and professional associa-
tions (external networks), as well as credible voicing channels
(organizational resources), are robust predictors of bureau-
crats' resistance intentions, particularly through open forms of
resistance.

reduce the willingness to resist, while higher perceived exper-

Conceptually, this study extends public administration
­scholarship on bureaucratic resistance by advancing a
­meso-­organizational framework for bureaucratic responses
to  democratic backsliding. The framework links two
­organizational pillars—networks (internal and external) and
resources (voicing channels)—to individual-­level capacity to
resist undemocratic pressures. In doing so, it builds on re-
search on the guardian state and administrative resilience by
specifying the organizational mechanisms through which bu-
reaucrats confront illiberal pressures (Bauer and Becker 2020;
Bauer  2025; Cingolani and Lotta  2026; Gofen  2024; Lotta,
Piotrowska, and Raaphorst  2024; Podger and Kettl  2024;
Yesilkagit et  al.  2024). These findings yield four theoretical
implications.
First, it provides new evidence on the organizational condi-
tions shaping bureaucrats' willingness to resist undemocratic
demands. Prior research shows how civil servants protect con-
stitutional norms and agency missions (Lotta, de Alves Lima,
et al. 2024; O'Leary 2017; Silva 2022; Stauffer et al. 2024), but
the organizational determinants of resistance remain less
clearly specified. Our findings demonstrate that organiza-
tional networks and resources, rather than individual traits
alone, play a central role. In doing so, we also contribute to
research on bureaucratic and ethical responses to executive
misconduct, including corruption and violations of the rule
of law (Brierley  2020; Cox  2025; Ingber  2018; Kang  2023;
Kucinskas  2025; Meyer-­Sahling et  al.  2019; Shinar  2013;
Zhang et al. 2025).
Second, we highlight coworkers' influence on bureaucratic at-
titudes, consistent with research on organizational dissent and
prosocial rule-­breaking (Fleming 2020; Gofen 2014; Kucinskas
and Silveira 2025; Silitonga et al. 2019). High peer agreement
increases bureaucrats' willingness to resist, whereas peer
disagreement reduces it for both open and silent resistance.
Contrary to prior research on sabotage (Hollibaugh et al. 2020;
O'Leary 2017), coworker support in our study tends to encourage
open rather than silent resistance. Further research is needed to
identify the mechanisms underlying these patterns (Bergemann
and Aven 2023; Sufi et al. 2024).

Third, consistent with research on bureaucratic autonomy and
relational capacities (Carpenter 2002; Cingolani and Lotta 2026;
Evans  1995; Trajber Waisbich  2024), we show that external
networks shape open resistance. Professional associations and
unions, in particular, enable bureaucrats to respond openly to
undemocratic demands, consistent with prior qualitative work
(Kucinskas and Silveira  2025; Lotta, Fernandez, et  al.  2024).
Operating outside agency hierarchies and facing lower risks
of retaliation (Gazley  2014; Kucinskas and Silveira  2025;
Silveira 2024), these networks provide reputational and norma-
tive backing and facilitate coordination, allowing bureaucrats
to align interpretations and craft resistance grounded in profes-
sional ethics (Koga et al. 2023; Stauffer et al. 2024).
Fourth, contrary to our theoretical expectations, credible voic-
ing channels are associated with open rather than silent resis-
tance. We expected institutional voice to primarily enable covert
action as a self-­protection mechanism in hierarchical settings
(Hollibaugh et al. 2020; O'Leary 2017; Story et al. 2023). Two
factors help explain this pattern. First, when voice mecha-
nisms are perceived as fair, consistent, and effective, they lower
expected sanctions and shift dissent from private to institu-
tionalized forms (Ellmer and Reichel 2021; Kang 2023; Kwon
et al. 2016; Noort et al. 2019; Wilkinson et al. 2020). Second,
visible voice infrastructures legitimate constructive dissent and
normalize openness by embedding due process and linking re-
porting to organizational learning (Gao et al. 2015; Kang 2023;
Noort et al. 2019). Together, these findings suggest that robust
voice systems function as infrastructures for openness: where
protections are credible, bureaucrats engage in visible, account-
able voice; where they are not, employees are more likely to self-­
censor or remain silent (Story et al. 2023).
Finally, with respect to external validity, comparative research
on administrative responses to democratic backsliding suggests
that our findings are relevant to other merit-­based, profession-
alized bureaucracies, including those in most OECD countries
(Bauer 2025; OECD 2025; Suzuki 2025). At the same time, the
extent to which these results travel may be nuanced by specific
patterns of administrative transformation and the predominant
threats observed under backsliding (Bauer  2025). Supportive
external networks may be particularly important in contexts
characterized by politicization and the delegitimization of bu-
reaucracies (e.g., the United States under the Trump adminis-
tration), but less influential where backsliding takes the form of
centralized legal capture and eroded horizontal accountability,
such as in Hungary under Orbán, Israel under Netanyahu, and
Turkey under Erdoğan (Bauer 2025). By contrast, internal or-
ganizational conditions, especially credible voice mechanisms
and peer-­supported ethical deliberation, appear to function as
more consistent safeguards across diverse backsliding contexts
(Bauer 2025; Cox 2025; Lotta, Piotrowska, and Raaphorst 2024;
Yesilkagit et al. 2024).
Our findings indicate actionable steps agencies can take to
help civil servants protect democratic institutions (Podger and
Kettl 2024; Yesilkagit et al. 2024). First, agencies should estab-
lish safe and credible channels for employee voice that protect
against retaliation and ensure confidentiality and due pro-
cess. Mechanisms such as independent ombudsman offices re-
duce the costs of speaking up and help document and address
undemocratic pressure. Second, agencies can build resilience
by strengthening internal and external support networks: cul-
tivating peer-­support environments (e.g., ethics forums and
communities of practice) and partnering with professional asso-
ciations for independent backing and broader community sup-
port. Third, agencies should invest in training and structured
peer discussions to clarify institutional options for addressing
undemocratic pressures. Since peers are the immediate refer-
ence point for action, providing clear information about risks,
procedures, and lawful pathways facilitates collaboration and
the development of legal-­constitutional responses.
6   |   Conclusion
The rise of democratic backsliding governments in recent years
has been accompanied by sustained attacks on institutions
and bureaucracies (Foa and Mounk  2016, 2017; Moloney and
Krislov  2016; Moynihan  2025; Peters and Pierre  2019; Story
et al. 2023). In this context, scholars underscore the crucial role
bureaucracies play in safeguarding democratic institutions and
constitutional principles (Bauer 2025; Yesilkagit et al. 2024). To
examine the organizational conditions that strengthen bureau-
cracies' capacity to withstand undemocratic pressure, this arti-
cle presents evidence from a conjoint survey experiment with
Brazilian federal bureaucrats. We find that organizational net-
works and resources—such as reliable ombuds offices, coworker
support, and professional associations—significantly shape civil
servants' willingness to openly resist undemocratic demands
from political superiors.
These findings have theoretical and practical implications for
debates on bureaucratic responses to illiberal attacks on the ad-
ministrative state (Bauer and Becker 2020; Lotta, de Alves Lima,
et al. 2024; Moloney and Krislov 2016; Moynihan 2025; Peters
and Pierre 2019; Yesilkagit et al. 2024). Practically, they point
to concrete steps agencies can take to support civil servants.
Agencies should establish credible voice mechanisms that pro-
tect employees from retaliation, consistent with research on eth-
ical whistleblowing and organizational learning (Cox 2025; Gao
et al. 2015; Hollibaugh et al. 2020; Kang 2023; O'Leary 2017),
and cultivate peer-­supportive environments while strengthen-
ing external ties, particularly through professional associations
that provide independent backing, reputational protection, and
reinforcement of professional values.
Theoretically, we contribute to the growing literature on bu-
reaucracies under democratic backsliding by extending the un-
derstanding of the mechanisms that shape civil servants' intent
to resist undemocratic pressure (Bauer  2025; Cingolani and
Lotta 2026; Peters and Pierre 2019; Yesilkagit et al. 2024). By
foregrounding organizational networks and resources, we pro-
pose and test an analytical framework that identifies the con-
ditions driving bureaucrats' willingness to resist undemocratic
demands. In doing so, we advance debates on bureaucratic resil-
ience and the guardian state, showing how meso-­level organiza-
tional conditions impact micro-­level resistance strategies.
At the same time, this study has limitations. First, our out-
comes measure stated intentions rather than observed behavior.
Although experimental evidence on intent can offer valuable

insights into real-­world practices (Hainmueller et  al.  2014;
Meyer-­Sahling et al. 2019; Schuster et al. 2024), such findings
should be interpreted cautiously and with attention to context
(Harrits and Møller 2020). Second, the sector-­neutral conjoint
vignette enhances comparability across 37 federal agencies
but may obscure sector-­specific dynamics; future research
could use tailored vignettes to capture variation across policy
domains. Third, despite 2481 completed surveys, the response
rate was low. As civil servants are a hard-­to-­reach population,
future studies could improve participation through repeated in-
vitations or partnerships with human resources units. Fourth,
social desirability bias may arise because open and silent resis-
tance were measured on the same screen, potentially inflating
reports of open resistance. Separating these question blocks
could mitigate this concern.
Building on these considerations, several avenues for future re-
search emerge. First, given that open resistance was the domi-
nant pattern in our findings, further work should examine how
bureaucrats weigh opportunities and constraints when choos-
ing between open and silent strategies, as well as the rationales
underlying these choices. Mixed-­methods approaches could be
especially valuable for investigating how civil servants perceive
and navigate the risks associated with alternative behavioral
choices. Second, future studies should investigate how group
dynamics, especially peer agreement or disagreement, influence
intentions to resist. Finally, extending this conjoint design to
other national and institutional contexts would allow for richer
comparative analyses of bureaucratic resistance under demo-
cratic backsliding.

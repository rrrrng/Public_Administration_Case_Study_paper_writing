ABSTRACT
State and federal governments use governance platforms to achieve central policy goals through distributed action at the local level. For ex­
ample, California’s 2014 Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) mandates local policy actors to work together to create new
groundwater management institutions and plans. We argue that governance platforms entail a principal-agent problem where local decisions
may deviate from central goals. We apply this argument to SGMA implementation, where local plans may respond more to local political eco­
nomic conditions rather than address the groundwater problems prioritized by the state. Using a Structured Topic Model (STM) to analyze the
content of 117 basin management plans, we regress each plan’s focus on core management reform priorities on local socio-economic and social-
ecological indicators expected to shape how different communities respond to state requirements. Our results suggest that the focus of local
plans diverges from problem conditions on issues like environmental justice and drinking water quality. This highlights how principal-agent logics
of divergent preferences and information asymmetry can affect the design and implementation of governance platforms.
KEYWORDS: Collaborative governance; governance platforms; franchising; agency theory; content analysis.
1. INTRODUCTION
California’s Sustainable Groundwater Management Act
(SGMA) of 2014 mandates formation of local agencies to
draft management plans to govern overdrafted groundwater
basins. This paper studies a challenge that is exemplified by
SGMA, but is present in all multi-level governance contexts:
how to implement a centralized mandate in geographically
dispersed settings. A common institutional design approach
to this problem is to create a governance platform model in
which the governing body establishes a collaborative frame­
work that is then replicated in local areas to achieve collab­
oration at scale (Ansell and Gash 2018). Ansell and Gash
(2018) label this type of governance platform as a “franchise
platform” but do not delve into the principal-agent problems
inherent to such arrangements. Derived from the concept of
franchising as a business arrangement, franchise theory is ap­
plicable to “dilemmas inherent in organizational forms that
combine… a central delegator trying to harness the power
of delegation to multiple, structurally separate delegatees”
(Whitford 2010, 37). Although many top-down, geograph­
ically distributed collaborative governance arrangements fit
this definition, the delegation problems of this approach have
been overlooked in the collaborative governance literature.
We argue that viewing these types of arrangements through
the lens of a franchising problem is useful for understanding
the performance of local platforms.
SGMA creates a set of principal-agent problems around
local groundwater pumping in the state. Principal-agent
problems exist where there is: (1) a divergence between the
preferences of the principal and agent; and (2) an information
asymmetry where it is costly for the principal to monitor pri­
vate information controlled by the agent that is relevant to the
agent’s performance (Zeckhauser and Pratt 1985; Waterman
and Meier 1998). Prior to SGMA’s adoption, the state had
abdicated, rather than delegated, management authority, and
pumping was unregulated at the state level. This led to delete­
rious effects such as subsidence, dry wells, saltwater intrusion,
and contamination. To address these issues, SGMA delegated
to local water users the responsibility to create Groundwater
Sustainability Agencies (GSAs), which then write and im­
plement Groundwater Sustainability Plans (GSPs). SGMA
designers supported this delegation with a combination of ex­
plicit and implicit contractual obligations meant to balance
the incentives of the delegator (State of California) and the
delegatees (local water users). For instance, as we describe in
more detail below, local areas that fail to adopt a plan deemed
acceptable by state regulators are potentially subject to state
takeover of local groundwater administration.
What we analyze in this paper is not the choice to adopt a
franchise-style delegation arrangement (versus other methods
the state could have used) but rather the delegation problems
that persist within this contractual model. In particular, a core

sustainability goals are not always shared by powerful local

Milman 2023; Dobbin 2020; Bostic et al. 2023).
Based on the dynamics of the delegation problem created
by SGMA’s franchise platform model, we empirically analyze
whether local outputs are shaped more by the preferences
of the state principal or the local agent. More specifically,
do local plans evolve in ways that reflect attention to state
priorities, or more of the local political economy and distri­
bution of power that characterizes the status quo? The extent
to which local plans reflect state priorities is an indicator of
how much effort the agent is exerting to satisfy the principals’
goals. If local plans reflect state priorities, there is evidence
that the accountability mechanisms created by the state have
ameliorated the principal-agent problem.
The next section briefly describes the SGMA case. We then
summarize the governance platform concept and franchising
approaches wherein a platform model is operated across geo­
graphically distributed local areas. We argue that governance
platform franchising introduces principal-agent problems that
may stymie their capacity to generate collaborative solutions
to local collective-action problems. We apply this argument
to SGMA to motivate contrasting hypotheses about whether
local plans are more likely to reflect local problems, versus
political economic variables. Using a Structured Topic Model
(STM) to analyze the content of 117 basin management
plans, we regress each plan’s focus on core management re­
form priorities on local socio-economic and social-ecological
indicators expected to shape how different communities re­
spond to state requirements.
2. CASE BACKGROUND
California’s Sustainable Groundwater Management Act
requires local water authorities to take management authority
over local groundwater resources by forming Groundwater
Sustainability Agencies (GSAs). The jurisdiction of a GSA
may be an entire basin or only a portion of a basin, or it may
span areas in multiple basins.1 Any GSAs operating within a
basin must collaborate on either the development of a single
Groundwater Sustainability Plan (GSP) for the entire basin or
a set of compatible GSPs that collectively span the entire basin
and are linked via a coordination agreement (see An and Tang
2022 for an explanation). Given these options, there are mul­
tiple levels of variance in collaboration structure: some basins
have multiple GSPs while others have only one; some GSPs
have multiple GSA authors while others have only one; and
finally, some GSAs are composed of multiple collaborating
local agencies while others are composed of only one.
SGMA is managed by the California Department of Water
Resources (DWR) with a backstop from the California State
Water Resources Control Board (SWRCB). DWR provides
a variety of resources to enable local collaboration in­
cluding technical modeling support, a network of facilitation
professionals, and a large-scale grant program. DWR also
designates basins as “high” or “medium” priority based on
eight criteria outlined in California Water Code § 10933(b).
These high- and medium-priority basins were given until June
30, 2017 to form GSAs. Basins that became high- or medium-
priority in the 2019 reassessment of basin prioritization were
given two years from the reprioritization date to establish a
GSA or submit an alternative plan. GSPs for basins which
DWR determined to be “critically overdrafted” were due for
state review in 2020, and GSPs for other high- and medium-
priority basins were due in 2022.
SGMA is designed to recognize local autonomy and allow
groundwater management to reflect local heterogeneity in
social-ecological parameters, but maintain state-level ac­
countability to best practices and goals for sustainable
groundwater management. The state issues guidelines about
the expected content of GSPs, but it is up to the local GSAs to
define their own operationalized criteria by which to measure
plan success or lack thereof. If DWR determines a GSP to be
“incomplete” (e.g., for lacking clear definitions of sustaina­
bility goals) or “inadequate” (e.g., for insufficiently addressing
limitations underscored under public comment review), the
involved GSAs must revise their plan. Every five years fol­
lowing GSP submission, an update on implementation and
sustainability goal progress must be submitted to DWR. The
final deadline by which basins are expected to reach their sus­
tainability goals is 2040.
There are three events along this timeline that could trigger
a state intervention: if the basin (1) fails to form GSAs by the
GSA formation deadline, or (2) if a revised GSP is deemed
“inadequate” by DWR, or (3) if the implementation of the
GSP is deemed inappropriate by DWR, the State Water Board
has the authority to intervene to impose its own groundwater
use rules. At time of data collection, September 22, 2023, 46
plans had been approved, 4 had been found “incomplete” and
returned for revisions, 23 had been found inadequate after
revisions, and 46 were under review. In our dataset, we use
the initial draft originally submitted by each basin area. We
removed one duplicate plan and one improperly formatted
plan from our dataset, to total 117.
3. FRANCHISE THEORY FOR DELEGATED
GOVERNANCE
A governance platform is defined as “an institution that stra­
tegically deploys its architecture to leverage, catalyze, and
harness distributed social action for the purpose of achieving
certain governance objectives.” (Ansell and Miura 2020, 264).
Platforms contribute to governance by connecting actors,

scaling (or downscaling) actions, and sharing or reusing re­
sources. In a platformed approach, governing bodies create
platforms using a combination of coercion (e.g., imposing a
requirement to form a GSA), incentives (e.g., access to funds),
and subsidies (e.g., facilitation resources, technical advice).
Ansell and Gash identify a typology of nine types of gov­
ernance platforms, including franchise platforms which
“create a basic framework… for collaborative governance
and then replicate this in different places in a manner sim­
ilar to commercial franchises” (2018, 28).2 Ansell and Gash
identify wide-ranging examples of franchise platforms such as
local anti-obesity campaigns, marine reserves, and dairy hubs.
There are numerous examples of geographically distributed
delegation in the collaborative environmental governance lit­
erature (e.g., Lubell 2004; Ulibarri 2015; Mehdi and Nabatchi
2023; Bell and Scott 2020). While the design features of these
cases differ, they fit within Ansell and Gash’s definition as
“franchises that establish or promote certain basic design
principles while allowing for local customization via collab­
orative governance” (2018, 29). As explained above, the case
of SGMA in California fits this definition: local groundwater
users are required to collaborate on local planning by creating
a “Groundwater Sustainability Agency” and “Groundwater
Sustainability Plan,” which the state promotes with resource
support (e.g., for data analysis and modeling, plan writing,
etc.) and sanctions for non-compliance.
While Ansell and Gash focus on franchise platforms as
a way to “facilitate the scaling up of collaboration” (2018,
29), organizational economists view franchising as a way to
organize and manage delegated responsibilities. Firms and
governments delegate because monolithic implementation
makes it hard to respond to emerging problems, reflect local
heterogeneity, and account for dynamic preferences (Whitford
2010). Franchising commonly refers to a contractual business
arrangement. Under a franchising contract, the franchisor
imposes a set of conditions (operating rules, profit sharing,
etc.) and provides branding, advertising, and operational sup­
port to independent franchisees who operate local instances
of the business (Mathewson and Winter 1985). Firms choose
to franchise as a way to grow a business while offsetting risk,
overcoming scale and resource constraints, and benefiting
from local operational knowledge. Cities and counties com­
monly use franchising contracts for public service delivery,
contracting with another organization (public or private) to
provide water or other utility services and granting them the
right to use public rights-of-way (“Franchising - An Essential
Tool for Right-of-Way Management” n.d.). Although
franchising theory is most commonly applied in a business
context, governments’ choice to delegate and distribute a
policy via a common organizational form can be understood
on similar grounds (Whitford 2010, 2020). Whether in a busi­
ness or regulatory context, “the theory of franchising is really
a theory of how to delegate to multiple independent subunits”
(Whitford 2010, 35).
Thus, we can think about the design of SGMA like a fran­
chise contract that tries to solve two governance problems.
First, the state of California wants to alleviate deleterious
conditions and provide sustainable groundwater resources,
but is constrained in its ability to act unilaterally and achieve
this statewide. Local groundwater users have the knowl­
edge and means to achieve groundwater sustainability, but
empowering these local actors requires the state to give up
some control. Second, local users need some level of guar­
antee that other users, and basins, will also comply with
the same regulatory standards. If not, free riding (ongoing
overextraction, not bearing costs of more sustainable oper­
ations, etc.) places a compliant basin at a comparative dis­
advantage. The SGMA “contract” that addresses these two
problems works as follows: The state sets forth reform
priorities for sustainability, equity, and health, provides plan­
ning resources, and requires local users to form GSAs and
write plans. The state implements a review process to monitor
whether GSAs adequately comply with SGMA requirements,
paired with a communication strategy that explains state ex­
pectations to local users. If GSAs do not adequately comply
with SGMA requirements (e.g., do not develop an approved
plan), the state will intervene. Local water users do not want
such intervention, because it means a loss of control and im­
position of unwanted costs. The threat of low quality state
intervention (from the GSA’s perspective) aligns the cen­
tral government’s aim (for locals to implement the policy)
with locals’ interests (wanting to preserve local autonomy)
(Whitford 2010).
While franchise contracts address the issues of preference
divergence and information asymmetry that arise in delega­
tion to multiple entities, principal-agent problems remain a
risk in business franchising (Mathewson and Winter 1985).
Likewise, principal-agent problems in delegated govern­
ance may undermine the rationale for delegation when the
decisions (both substance of decisions and amount of effort)
made by the agent do not meet the goals of the principal
(Whitford 2010). For SGMA, state priorities are not nec­
essarily shared (or at least prioritized) by local agricultural
interests and irrigation districts who are involved in GSA
governance and GSP development. Many local water users
do not want to regulate groundwater use or fund planning
and monitoring (Barringer 2022; Tavlian 2020). These users
want to preserve the status quo and not reduce groundwater
pumping. For example, many plans emphasize supply-side
groundwater recharge strategies rather than demand-side
pumping curtailments. Conflict owing to disagreement with
state objectives is reflected by evidence that disadvantaged
communities (DACs) are not well represented in local plan­
ning processes (Dobbin and Lubell 2019), involvement of key
groundwater users (farmers) exhibits a strong socio-economic
gradient (Méndez-Barrientos et al. 2020), and plans adopt
standards that threaten to dry out a considerable percentage
of shallow public and domestic supply wells in plan areas
(Bostic et al. 2023).
4. BALANCING PRINCIPAL AND AGENT
PREFERENCES IN FRANCHISE PLATFORMS
Given that the state reviews and approves local plans, why
are local preferences exerted in SGMA implementation?

Franchise operators charge more, advertise less, and have
lower quality (Combs, Steven, and Gary 2004; Lafontaine
and Shaw 1999; Michael 1999). In theory, the delegator
can reduce the residual principal-agent problem with more
detailed, explicit rules and additional monitoring and over­
sight. But incurring these additional costs (including po­
litical costs) runs counter to the rationale for delegating
in the first place. Because the delegating government must
balance the marginal benefits of ensuring compliance with
the costs, it is not viable to eliminate the principal-agent
problem.
For SGMA, this means that even though the state has re­
view authority over local plans, we expect local plans to
reflect a combination of state and local priorities. Local re­
sponsiveness to state priorities is our version of franchisee ef­
fort in a franchise model. Regulators will use oversight power
to force local plans to address state priorities, but will not
push too hard lest they undermine the rationale for delegating
in the first place. Local groundwater agencies will draft plans
to get state approval, but try to incorporate local preferences
to the extent possible knowing that the state cannot enforce
perfect compliance. Viewing SGMA through the lens of fran­
chise theory, the key questions then become: to what extent
do the decisions of the individual local groundwater “agents”
align with the goals of the state regulatory “principal” and in
what conditions do they deviate?
4.1. Preferences of the governance platform
franchisor
For SGMA, the answer to the first question is whether the
local plans effectively address the core groundwater man­
agement problems the state identified as important for sus­
tainable groundwater management. SGMA requires GSAs
and GSPs to consider “the interests of all beneficial uses
and users of groundwater,” which include “[d]isadvantaged
communities, including, but not limited to, those served by
private domestic wells or small community water systems,”
(California Water Code § 10723.2 (2022)). Also listed are
drinking water sources and providers such as “[d]omestic
well owners,” “[m]unicipal water operators,” and “[p]ublic
water systems” (California Water Code § 10723.2 (2022)).
“Environmental users of groundwater” and “[s]urface water
users, if there is a hydrologic connection between surface and
groundwater bodies,” are included as well (California Water
Code § 10723.2 (2022)). SGMA also requires every plan to
“rely on the best available information and best available sci­
ence to quantify the water budget for the basin in order to pro­
vide an understanding of historical and projected hydrology,
water demand, water supply, land use, population, climate
change, sea level rise, groundwater and surface water inter­
action, and subsurface groundwater flow.” (Cal. Code Regs.
Tit. 23, § 354.18). The state supplied all planning areas with
a common collection of processed climate change datasets,
including climatological projections (California Department
of Water Resources 2018), hydrological point data, and water
operations time series data, through a central hub (“SGMA
Climate Change Resources” n.d.), with a guidance document
explaining how to incorporate the data into a local plan
(California Department of Water Resources 2018).
Drinking water quality and access, protection of disad­
vantaged communities, adapting groundwater use to the
changing climate, and preserving groundwater-dependent
ecosystems were all policy priorities sought by the
California state legislature and DWR—in other words,
were DWR developing each plan, the priorities of the plan
would reflect the relative severity of those local problems.
Demonstrating sufficient attention to these issues is how
local GSAs get plan approval and avoid intervention. That
said, even if the principal and agents were aligned in their
overarching interests, we would still expect to see local
variation depending on the salience of each policy priority
in each area. This leads to our first hypothesis, what one
might think of as the “rational-ideal model” of franchising
a governance platform: that local actors will take advan­
tage of the flexible structure of SGMA to devote more or
less attention to these policy goals in accordance with the
severity of local problems.
Hypothesis 1 (Preferences of Principal): Proportion of
topical focus on different reform priorities will be positively
correlated with the relative degree of problem severity in each
basin.
4.2. Preferences of the governance platform
franchisee
Just as profit sharing does not fully align business franchisor
and franchisee incentives, state review and the threat of in­
tervention does not completely resolve the agency problem
embedded in dispersed delegation. For geographically distrib­
uted governance platform franchises, we argue that local po­
litical ideology and concentrated economic interests are core
determinants of whether local agents align with the aims of
the state principals. In the case of SGMA, we focus on po­
litical conservatives and large agricultural business as two
groups least aligned with groundwater sustainability reform
interests. Political conservatives are more likely to object to
any top-down government policy, while large agricultural
businesses resist policies that potentially restrict water use.
This leads to our second hypothesis, based on divergent po­
litical and economic preferences of state-level regulators and
local managers:
Hypothesis 2 (Preferences of Agent): Proportion of topical
focus on different reform priorities will be lower in basins
that are more politically conservative and where agricultural
operations are a greater share of the local economy.
4.3. Shared versus opposing franchisor-franchisee
preferences
While state regulators are certainly aware of different local
groundwater management preferences held across the state,
they do not have full information about these preferences
and corresponding local conditions.3 Just as local business
franchise operators have better information about local
conditions and actions (Mathewson and Winter 1985), local
groundwater agencies have better knowledge about local ac­
tors and problems. This private local information is part of
the motivation for delegation in the first place. But private
information also means the franchisor is not fully aware of
what local governance platform franchises are (or are not)
doing. For this reason, DWR staff have made a practice of
attending local GSA meetings to understand the dynamics of
local actors and to inform them about state requirements and

be a superfluous investment of time and effort in the absence
of a principal-agent problem created by information asym­

and oversight necessary to produce complete information
regarding the agent’s adherence to the principal’s goals—in
this case, the adequacy of the plans in reliably outlining and
achieving sustainability criteria—is administratively and po­

Accordingly, while the state reviewed, and in some cases
rejected, local plans, approved plans do not necessarily fully
reflect state priorities.
If the principals’ and agents’ interests are aligned, then local
information asymmetry is less problematic since the agent is
working towards a goal the principal shares. Where interests
diverge, governance platform franchises can leverage private
information for their interests. In the case of SGMA plans, we
expect this results in downplaying problem severity. We fit a
set of interaction terms evaluating whether political ideology
and concentrated economic interests moderate the relation­
ship between problem severity and plan focus. The interac­
tion terms capture whether the correlation between problem
severity and plan focus is conditional on the presence of ac­
tors with divergent preferences.
Hypothesis 3 (Agents’ Preferences × Problem Severity):
Local political and economic interests will moderate the rela­
tionship between problem severity and proportion of topical
focus on reform priorities.
Because GSAs submit plans for state approval, it is rea­
sonable to wonder why an information asymmetry problem
between state regulators and local users would not instead re­
sult in plans that appear highly compliant with state aims and
then actions that diverge widely from plans. In other words,
why would GSAs not just capitulate to state preferences in
plans and then do something else? It would be unrealistic to
expect perfect correspondence between plans and implemen­
tation. But setting out clear steps in plans and then markedly
departing from these steps is likely to backfire by highlighting
the defection and providing the impetus for state intervention
that local GSAs (and the state) want to avoid. In other words,
local GSAs should be expected to seek approval for plans
that embed local interests as much as possible. Furthermore,
the causal link between the specific local groundwater man­
agement strategies and sustainability goals is heterogeneous
across basins in ways the state is unable to monitor. Hence,
the state is likely to be somewhat tolerant of this leeway
for the same reason that franchise businesses persist despite
quality control problems (Combs, Steven, and Gary 2004):
the end result is preferable to more costly monitoring or
monolithic implementation. Thus, we expect to observe the
agents’ preferences moderating the role of problem severity in
plans submitted for review.
These originally submitted drafts represent the agents’ initial
solution that they supposed would be acceptable to the principal
while maximizing their own priorities and goals. Although this
is merely a snapshot of the back-and-forth relationship between
the principal and agents in the plan development processes, these
initial submissions are particularly apt because they represent a
time when information is partially shared, but the principal has
not yet revealed a determination of whether they find the plan
to be acceptable, meaning there is still information asymmetry
regarding the credibility of the principal.
5. METHODS AND MATERIALS
5.1. Document collection and processing
Web scraping software was used to collect GSA and GSP
metadata from the SGMA data portal (https://sgma.water.
ca.gov/portal/gsp/all/). Each plan was downloaded as a PDF
document, along with the associated spreadsheet of meta­
data, called a GSP Elements Guide, describing which pages
of the document are connected to each required element of
the plan. Documents were converted into machine readable,
full-text format; a series of customized preprocessing steps
were performed to remove page numbers, maps, figures, and
conversion errors. All pages representing public comments or
reference material were removed,4 since both contain content
produced by others and not the GSA.
5.2. GSP Metadata Processing and Aggregation
For each GSP, we web-scraped metadata regarding collaborating
GSAs. We also collected basin prioritization data from the
California Natural Resources Agency. Next, we performed a
series of spatial overlays and joins to match social and envi­
ronmental indicators measured at the census block group or
census tract level to the spatial extent of each GSP.
DAC boundary shapefiles from the U.S. Census and GSP
shapefiles from the CA DWR were used to determine the per­
centage of the population within the GSP area that lives in a
block group designated as a DAC. There are three geographies
the state of California uses to designate DACs: through census
tracts, block groups, or “places,” which include cities and
census-designated places. In this study, block groups were
used to define DACs, as they are the finest resolution of pop­
ulation estimates that cover the entire state.5
5.3. Text Cleaning
Prior to analysis, we performed a series of preprocessing
steps, such as removing punctuation and standalone numbers,
to clean the raw input and preserve as much meaningful in­
formation as possible for analysis. Each page was tokenized
into individual words and compound words. Next, we added
custom n-grams (word groups) to the corpus so that place
names, agency names, water-related terms, and climate-
related terms would be treated as single phrases rather than a
collection of separate words traveling together (e.g., “climate
change” versus “climate” and “change”).6
Topic modeling uses a “bag of words” approach, meaning
that a document is represented as a collection of words
without respect to word order. Units of text (pages from GSPs
in our case) are represented as a document-term matrix, with
each row representing a document, each column representing
a word or word phrase, and matrix values recording how
many times a given token is observed in a given document.

5.4. Content Analysis
The core idea of topic modeling is to represent the content
of documents as a probability distribution of topics; within
the model, each topic is itself represented by a probability
distribution of words. Arguably, the canonical topic model
method is Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), which uses
a three-level, hierarchical Bayesian model framework to
represent documents as a mixture of latent topics, and la­
tent topics in turn as a mixture of words and phrases (Blei,
Ng, and Jordan 2003). As with an LDA, a Structural Topic
Model (STM) (Roberts et al. 2013) represents documents
as a mixture of topics and topics as a mixture of words.
However, a STM allows us to fit an unrestricted specifica­
tion that models document and topic content to vary by rel­
evant document-level predictors. Specifically, in a STM the
prior distribution of topics for a given document is based
on its set of covariates (document attributes included in
the model), and the distribution of words within topics can
also vary by document attribute (Roberts et al. 2014). This
allows for direct estimation of topic predictors, described in
more detail below. To fit our model, we use the stm package
in R (Roberts, Stewart, and Tingley 2019). Roberts, Stewart,
and Tingley (2019) contains the specifications for each of
the three model levels.7
Because GSP documents are long (and highly variable in
length), we model individual pages as the core unit of anal­
ysis. Pages are then grouped by document-level predictors
within the model. This allows us to account for plan-level
content correlation while also enabling us to more accurately
model varied within-plan content. We also incorporate page-
level attributes such as the document section a page falls
within. All predictors (described in Section 5.6) act on the
model “prevalence” function—i.e., the proportion of a page
devoted to a topic8. This is our dependent variable, which we
refer to hereafter as “topic prevalence.”
In a topic model, the number of topics is set a priori.
Appendix B describes the sensitivity analysis and statistical
criteria we use to select an appropriate number of topics,
according to the approach of Silge (2018). Ultimately, we
chose a model with 30 topics to achieve the best balance of
high semantic coherence9 and relatively high exclusivity10,
without sacrificing much in terms of residuals or held-out
likelihood.
Topics themselves are modeled inductively, meaning that
the words assigned to a given topic are based purely on sta­
tistical associations. The model is unsupervised, so the user
does not seed the model with specific word collections or con­
tent areas around which to build a topic. Rather, the model
calculates the topics and it is up to the analysts to infer what
they are about and give them a meaningful label. Thus, to be
able to say what the meaning of a topic is, we need to evaluate
the words most strongly associated with a given topic. We
identified the most representative words for each topic using
their FREX score (Airoldi and Bischof 2014), the weighted
harmonic mean of each word’s ranked exclusivity to the topic
and overall frequency.
We used a multi-step process to identify topics of interest.
First, we identified candidate topics by searching within
the top 50 representative words in each topic for keywords
related to drinking water or water quality (DW), climate
change (CC), environmental justice (EJ), or groundwater-
dependent ecosystems (GDE). We examined the top FREX
words for each candidate topic to evaluate cohesion, then
verified pages that had been tagged with candidate topics to
ensure that they resembled the content we intended to cap­
ture. Detailed topic results are provided in Appendix C. Of
the 30 topics generated by the model, we identified 10 topics
of interest using this process: Topics 7 and 24 discuss CC;
topics 14 and 29 discuss GDE; topics 10, 16, 25, 26, and 30
discuss DW, and topics 2 and 25 discuss tribal and Spanish-
speaking communities and DACs, which we have tagged
as EJ-related topics (Table 1). Tagged topics are not solely
about these issues—for instance, community engagement is
not strictly about frontline communities—but tagged topics
represent areas of the plan related to issues of interest for
this analysis. We then developed informal descriptions of
the topics of interest by hand after running the model, by
observing patterns in the most representative words for each
topic and observing pages that contain a high proportion
of that topic (Table 1). Topic descriptions are not meant to
be exhaustive of all the dimensions of the concept(s) with
which the topics are tagged.

5.5. Topic Aggregation
If several topics are related to a topic indicator category,
we combine all topics tagged with that category together
into a single large group, then estimate the effects on topic
prevalence of this aggregated topic (see Section 5.7). This
procedure simplifies the interpretation of the correla­
tion coefficients describing the effect of various metadata
predictors on topic prevalence. Rather than calculating
the correlation coefficients of each predictor of interest on
every individual topic tagged within the theme, this method
generates a single correlation summary. This also makes
results more comparable between commonly discussed
themes and rarely discussed themes. Drinking water is
discussed more commonly than groundwater dependent
ecosystems, so it takes a model with a high number of topics
to generate a topic primarily about groundwater dependent
ecosystems. Meanwhile, a model with a high number of
topics generates multiple similar topics about drinking
water, which we have no theoretical reason in this paper to
analyze separately. The choice to fit a model on many topics
and then aggregate related topics together ensures that there
is enough topic granularity to capture at least one topic
on less common themes, while preventing overfitting of
common themes by aggregating similar topics into a single
response variable. Topics that we tag with the same tag
are all positively correlated with each other (Appendix D),
indicating that by combining topics with the same tag, we
are capturing the total prevalence of related discussion areas
within the plans, rather than combining unrelated content in
an artificial category. Figure 1 summarizes the distribution
of aggregate topic proportions for each of the four topics of
interest.

5.6.1. Design controls
As described in Section 2, there are multiple levels of var­
iance in collaboration structure at the plan level for which
we want to account. GSPs may be developed by one or more
GSAs, and furthermore, GSAs themselves may be composed
of one or more local agencies. We separate these two char­
acteristics as separate design controls, since they represent
collaboration at different phases in the planning process. If
a GSA itself is composed of multiple agencies (“Has Multi-
Agency GSA” in our model if this applies to any of a plan’s
authors), this reflects prior planning to organize themselves
through a legal agreement, such as a Joint Powers Agreement
or Memorandum of Understanding, before jointly submitting
their request for governing authority under SGMA. Plans of
this type have joint governance by multiple local agencies
over part or all of the plan area.
Conversely, if a plan has multiple GSA authors, this
indicates that multiple GSAs separately applied for gov­

were required by the state to work together to define non-
overlapping jurisdictions and create a plan. We term this
“Split Jurisdiction.” Arrangements like this split the plan area
into multiple regions, each governed by its own singular GSA.

5.7.Topic prevalence predictor estimation
The stm package (Roberts, Stewart, and Tingley 2019)
contains a function that performs post hoc estimation of
parameters predicting topic prevalence using the method
of composition (Tanner 1993). This is done by repeatedly
drawing a sample of topic proportions from estimated topic
model posteriors, optimizing parameter estimates for each
draw, and then averaging across all results, in a process
known as variational inference (Roberts, Stewart, and Tingley
2019). The end result is essentially a regression wherein the
prevalence of topic t in document d is estimated as a function
of page-level and plan-level predictors. In our model, the unit
of analysis d is a page of a plan. Our dependent variable is
the proportion of a page that is devoted to topic t, or here­
inafter, “topic prevalence.” Estimates incorporate uncertainty
about the topic prevalence as well (see Roberts, Stewart, and
Tingley 2019, 16). Since we are interested in four focal topics,
we estimate a separate set of coefficients for each topic. All
topic prevalence estimates come from a single STM model,
in which prevalence predictors are fit across all topics. Post
hoc variational inference on predictor estimates is performed
separately because each topic has a separate set of relevant
predictors. The base specification for each estimate includes
design controls, political economy variables, and a problem
severity measure that matches the topic (e.g., drinking water
quality violations for the drinking water topic). We then es­
timate two additional terms for each focal topic, interacting
problem severity and either Republican vote share or agricul­
tural share of local GDP.

groundwater dependent ecosystems (Figure 7). Plans generally

this trend holds true across local communities. However, con­
sistent with H3, areas with strong agricultural interests are
less responsive to the presence of GDEs. We see no differ­
ence, however, in GDE topic prevalence between politically
left-leaning and right-leaning communities.
Overall, we find mixed but generally positive evidence

topic priority areas. This manifested in areas with high levels

7. DISCUSSION
This paper’s theoretical contribution centers on how principal-
agent problems may emerge in the context of a governance
platform like SGMA. Roberts and Milman highlight limi­
tations the principal faces in the SGMA case, specifically by
describing the need to mandate the adoption of certain collab­
orative institutional arrangements—while acknowledging that
such requirements “[do not] change the underlying contextual
conditions that influence agencies a priori relationships” (2023,
12). In this paper, we demonstrate how underlying contextual
conditions likewise shape plan focus.
In terms of the principal’s goals, for three of the four state-
defined priorities (H1), salience of the issue in the basin was
strongly and positively associated with greater topic prevalence.
The interaction effects (H3) are also largely consistent with our
theoretical hypotheses. Groundwater basins with political and
economic agent priorities that diverge from those of the state
government are less responsive (and in some cases, respond
negatively) to principal-defined problem severity, compared
to areas whose priorities align with those of the state. We ob­
serve reduced or diminished responsiveness in six of the eight
interactions. For the interaction between GDE focus and polit­
ical preferences, and for the interaction between drinking water
focus and agricultural interests, there is no meaningful effect.
For H2, the relationship between agent priorities and
plan content, we observed two counterintuitive, unpredicted
results that provide additional clues to the political economy
of the groundwater governance platform. First, as the agricul­
tural share of GDP increases, the groundwater sustainability
plans pay more attention to drinking water. This is surprising
because improving groundwater sources of drinking water
entails costs for agriculture. Our conjecture is that this finding
is a consequence of the political salience of drinking water is­
sues in rural California. The capacity of SGMA plans to pro­
tect drinking water has garnered political and media attention
when wells go dry or become too polluted with nitrates to be
safe for human consumption (James 2023; Taxin 2023). From
the principal-agent perspective, these types of outcomes are
less costly to monitor (in part because of extensive monitoring
and reporting efforts already in place under the U.S. Safe
Drinking Water Act and state regulations) and California state
agencies are concerned about the political ramifications of
this conflict. Hence, agricultural areas with well-known local
water quality issues are under greater pressure to demonstrate
to the principal how their plan can guarantee drinking water
quality and choose to emphasize the adequacy of their plan
even more to avoid state intervention.
Second is the disjuncture between climate change indicators
and the extent to which the plans describe climate change.
We think there are two possible contrasting explanations
for the observed disjuncture between climate change vul­
nerability and plan focus: a “head in the sand” effect or a
“capacity deficit.” A “head-in-the-sand” effect would occur
when high vulnerability basins ignore recent climate events
and evidence from climate models, which would force polit­
ically and economically costly water management changes.
The “head-in-the-sand” would be stronger in conservative
and agricultural basins, which potentially have the most to
lose from grappling with climate change. In contrast, low
vulnerability basins may use evidence from climate mod­
eling to justify “business as usual” and signal compliance
with the principal’s goals.
A “capacity deficit” explanation is that climate-vulnerable
basins may also feature rural and low-capacity political ac­
tors (including DACs), who face difficulty in analyzing cli­
mate change when the state provides common data and
models. These same basins may not be able to afford
consultants to support effective climate planning. Which of
these explanations is most plausible warrants further qualita­
tive and quantitative research.
All of these results concern divergence in the content of
local plans. Why does plan divergence matter? Plans make
statements about management priorities, present a commit­
ment to a particular course of action, and outline trade-offs
associated with management tasks and projects. Plans are
also political statements of value and intent. As such, we
should expect other actors in the policy subsystem to respond
to a GSP and judge future actions against the plan. Plan im­
plementation varies greatly depending on political support,
funding, and organizational capacity (Laurian et al. 2004).
A GSA could write eloquently about ecosystem projection or
environmental justice but ultimately choose to limit their con­
sideration in the definition of actual plan metrics and pay little
concern to these priorities in plan implementation processes.
However, plan quality does shape implementation (Laurian
et al. 2004), and plans influence subsequent decision-making
even if not directly followed (Knaap, Ding, and Hopkins
2001; Lyles, Berke, and Smith 2016). There is also an applied
case for using plans as evidence of what different governance
platforms achieve. The state of California, for instance, will
ultimately be able to assess groundwater reserves in the long
term, but in the short term, plans are how regulators monitor
the behavior of local GSAs (e.g., with regulatory milestones
and deadlines tied to plan review and approval).
8. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we argued that delegation strategies relying on
governance platforms have principal-agent dynamics similar to
a business franchising agreement, where the decisions of indi­
vidual agents may diverge from the preferences of the principal
and other agents. The details of our analysis are specific to the
California groundwater context, wherein the level of political
alignment is relatively low between the left-leaning state political
leadership and comparatively conservative basin populations.
Moreover, groundwater management in California occurs with
the backdrop of more extensive environmental regulation and
political commitment to climate change policy. But the under­
lying vertical and horizontal coordination dilemmas posed by
SGMA generalize broadly to geographically dispersed policy
implementation in federalist systems. Delegation occurs because
“a ‘Central Coordinator’ can improve social outcomes by job­
bing decision making and implementation to autonomous local
agents rather than trying to control everything” (Whitford 2010,
50). This delegation creates a vertical coordination dilemma for
the central delegator concerned with the overall outcome and
horizontal coordination dilemmas amongst the delegates. As
with franchise contracts, the results of such policies depend on
how well the institutional arrangements resolve these dilemmas.

Relevant factors in this regard include incentive (mis)alignment
between delegator and delegates, the observability of delegate
efforts, and the monitoring and enforcement capacity of the
delegator. State-local political disagreements and inter-local
free-riding incentives loom large in our particular case. But any
franchised governance platform model that imposes costs of
one form or another on local entities and which poses a col­
lective action dilemma amongst local entities is subject to the
same dynamics. Our case suggests that franchised governance
platforms are likely to work best to the extent that the franchisor
and franchisees have stronger goal alignment, and where the
franchisor can more easily monitor platform behavior and
outputs.
By using franchise theory to highlight how principal-agent
dynamics must be considered in the design and evaluation of
governance platforms, our analysis presents a new dimension
for understanding when such an approach works well. Existing
theories of collaborative effectiveness focus on two levels of
member-defined success: whether the platform is able to produce
outputs and achieve desired outcomes, and likewise whether
participants get what they want out of the platform (Emerson
and Nabatchi 2015). But the franchise platform model presents
a third level of performance: whether platforms—individually
or collectively—achieve the aims of the platform sponsor, i.e.,
the franchisor. Individual platform success and overall policy
success are not necessarily correlated.
How should we evaluate the overall effectiveness of a fran­
chise platform model such as SGMA when we know it will
suffer from some level of principal-agent problems? For in­
stance, if SGMA designers require planning in 117 basins, and
93 ultimately achieve groundwater sustainability (as defined by
state regulators), is this an acceptable rate of franchise failure?
Likewise, is the SGMA platform successful when many local
drinking water wells are still expected to go dry? In other words,
how much divergence between the preferences of the principals
and agents can be tolerated? Understanding how the design
and implementation of collaborative franchising programs
shapes aggregate, program-level outcomes is a critical need
given the large and growing use of collaborative franchising
as a governance approach. Doing so will also require thinking
about collaborative success and failure in new ways.
Evaluating this third level of performance will also re­
quire new research designs that compare franchise platform
models. Contract design for business franchises is evaluated
by comparing franchise business arrangements as units. As
in this study, most collaborative governance research studies
platform-level performance, akin to analyzing the performance
of individual franchise operators within a franchise business.
While performance is not so neatly boiled down to profits in
the case of collaborative governance, what is needed are mul­
tilevel research frameworks that measure the performance of
individual platforms operating under different institutional
designs to facilitate model-level design feature comparisons.

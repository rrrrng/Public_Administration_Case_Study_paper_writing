ABSTRACT
Values are ideals of what is desirable for the individual, profession, organization, or institution. Institutional values attach to
organizations because of their place in a political order, capturing broad governance concerns that extend beyond the details
of management and technical administration. What institutional values do public administration scholars study, how do they
study them, and how do those values relate to democracy? Our comprehensive and systematic review of the contents of public
administration journals reveals ambiguity about the nature of values and the level of social structure at which they operate. As a
constructive step forward, we offer an analytic framework of what we call democratic anchors, conceptions of values that convey
essential democratic content, that can improve the empirical study of how democracy is operationalized into norms of practice
in public administration.
The social structures studied by scholars of public administra-
tion shape values at different levels of human interactions and
social organization. They do so by combining ideals of what is
desirable for an individual, a profession, an organization, or in-
stitutions, thereby operating at increasingly broader scales of
governance. Individual values give people direction and crite-
ria for behaviors and choices (e.g., Schwartz 1992). Professional
values work similarly to direct decision-­making in professional
settings, as do organizational values, shaping organizational
culture, and guiding day-­to-­day operations. We are presently
concerned with institutional values, which operate at a distinct
level of social organization and attach to organizations, or sets of
organizations like government departments, that have acquired
what Selznick (1992, 271) calls “special character” within the
system of authority in a polity. Such organizations are recognized
(formally or informally) to have a distinct identity, values, and a
broader role in the polity.1 The values of institutionalized orga-
nizations broaden to capture broader governance, rather than
simply organizational management, concerns (Selznick 1992).2
Institutional values emerge from an organization's position
within broader governance structures, the hierarchical relation-
ships linking citizen preferences to legislative choices, the au-
thority that enables the management of organizations, and their
primary work to policy outcomes (Lynn Jr. et al. 2000). They do
not arise from an organization's own internal dynamics.
Reference to “public” values by public administration
scholars has become ubiquitous in recent decades (e.g.,
Kernaghan 2003; Jørgensen and Bozeman 2007; Rutgers 2008).
The values of public and private organizations are also placed
in contrast (e.g., van der Wal and Huberts 2008). Values have
further been related directly to specific governance structures
(e.g., Bertelli 2021; Andersen et al. 2012; Nabatchi 2012). Yet
it is worth taking a step back to recognize that values are in-
herently abstract concepts (Schwartz 1992). How have demo-
cratically relevant values been operationalized into the norms

But to address it, we must make some preliminary empirical

associate with the concept?
We begin with a systematic review of the public administration
literature on institutional values. Ours is not the first attempt
to review and to systematize a literature on values (see e.g.,
Van Der Wal et al. 2015). An ongoing effort to disentangle and
refine the conceptualization of values has been proceeding for
some time (cf. Rutgers 2008; Fukumoto and Bozeman 2019)
and an interest in linking these values explicitly to democ-
racy is robust (Kernaghan 2000, 2003; Box et al. 2001; Bryson
et al. 2014; Dahl and Soss 2014; Ventriss et al. 2019). Yet to
our knowledge, a comprehensive and systematic account of
institutional values we present is unavailable, and a broad
empirical foundation is essential for providing the construc-
tive critique we offer later in this essay. Following PRISMA
guidelines, we identified a total of 9289 records published
since 1980, of which 8999 were excluded after screening
at the abstract level and a further 198 after a more detailed
full-­text review. Ninety-­two records met our inclusion crite-
ria and were included for the final analysis. Engaging these
papers identified 19 frequently mentioned value categories,
including accountability, transparency, efficiency, and equity.
Democratic values were discussed in 47% of studies, and their
meanings varied widely. Values related to transparency and
participation were most identified as democratic, although the
way in which authors made connections with core democratic
principles varied substantially.
Our review reveals substantial ambiguity about two essential
aspects of the study of values: the nature of values and the
level at which they operate: individual, professional, organi-
zational, institutional. This ambiguity about definition and
level, we argue, makes it difficult to engage the values of de-
mocracy in a productively analytic way, particularly for em-
pirical work. This critique is constructive because we offer a
clear research agenda in the penultimate part of this essay. We
differentiate institutional from professional, organizational,
and individual values and map the institutional values com-
monly studied in public administration into an institution-­
free definition of democracy. We do this to avoid conflating
the values of institutionalized organizations with democratic
principles and norms, such as free and equal participation in
public affairs or majoritarian decision-­making. The result is
an analytic framework of what we call democratic anchors, or
mechanisms through which values come to convey essential
democratic content. Studying how anchors ground the norms
and behaviors of public officials in democratic ideals can, we
think, reinvigorate the study of democratic values in the field
and stimulate important conceptual and, especially, empiri-
cal work.
1   |   Systematic Literature Review
Our literature review follows the Preferred Reporting Items
for Systematic Reviews and Meta-­Analysis (PRISMA) (Liberati
et al. 2009). We provide a detailed Supporting Information doc-
ument (Supplement) where the PRISMA checklist can be found
in Table  1. To identify eligible records, we first searched two
major online databases, Scopus and Clarivate Web of Science,
cataloging 229 and 90 journals in the field of public administra-
tion, respectively. Second, we searched 15 leading journals in
the scholarly field of public administration to capture relevant
articles that may have been missed in the database searches.
Third, we searched relevant books on the Google Books data-
base. All searches were conducted between February 23 and
March 1, 2023.
We employed a combination of search terms aimed to iden-
tify the broadest set of potentially relevant items. Search terms
included “values”, “public administration” and related terms
such as “principles”, “public management,” “public sector,”
“policy implementation,” and “bureaucracy.” While concep-
tually different, the terms values and principles are closely
related. Values are the antecedents from which principles are
derived. Representing the “building blocks” for the function-
ing of a legal system (Harlow  2006, 190), principles ensure
that specific values infuse decisions and choices among al-
ternatives (Lucarelli 2006). At different levels of abstraction,
both values and principles represent, for instance, “criteria
for action and accountability” (Jørgensen and Bozeman 2007,
377). Scholars in public administration tend to use these terms
interchangeably, so we reasoned that excluding “principles”
from our search would have omitted potentially relevant
literature.
We adopted White's (1926, 2) enduring definition of public ad-
ministration as “the management of men and materials in the
accomplishment of the purpose of the state.” This broad under-
standing of public administration guided our selection of re-
lated search terms. “Public management” has become as public
administration's synonym par excellence (Frederickson  2007;
Lynn 2007), and because public administration concerns “how
the public sector serves the public good” (De Vries 2016, 6), “pub-
lic sector” was also included to capture various tasks of public
governance (Lane 2006). Public administration addresses both

Books and articles were considered eligible if (a) they were
written in English; (b) their content was theoretical or em-
pirical (qualitative or quantitative), with quantitative studies
included regardless of whether they treated administrative
values as dependent or independent variables; (c) their content
helped to conceptualize or to empirically study institutional
values; (c) they were published in a peer-­reviewed journal or
in a book on a prominent public administration or political
science list which included publishers such as Palgrave and
Cambridge University Press.5 Thus, we excluded studies of
values (a) at the individual level; (b) specific to a single or-
ganization; (c) as control variables or as one of several fac-
tors without further detail or exploration. We also excluded
(d) studies adopting the “public value” perspective (see e.g.,
Moore 2013), in which a value is viewed as a product of pub-
lic administration, rather than a criterion for action.6 In some
studies, it was difficult to understand (e) the specific value(s)
to which author(s) were referring; while others (f) focused on
a single value or (g) were too context-­specific,7 prompting our
exclusion. Finally, we excluded (h) records without abstracts.
After removing duplicates, 9289 records were available for
screening based on inclusion criteria.
Using the Covidence management tool for systematic reviews,
we proceeded with inclusion screening in two stages. In the
first, two team members screened titles and abstracts. Where
it was not possible to determine from the title and the abstract
whether the record met our inclusion criteria, such records
were passed on to full screening to avoid excluding relevant
records (Table  S6 summarizes the screening algorithm).
Conflicts were resolved by a discussion among the authors of
this manuscript. The first screening retained 290 records, the
full text of which two team members independently screened.
In cases of disagreement, a third member read the full text
and resolved the conflict. This second screening process
yielded 92 records. The entire screening process is summa-
rized in Figure 1.
Data analysis followed a mixed-­methods approach (Cepiku
and Mastrodascio  2021). On the one hand, we qualitatively
analyzed the data extracted from the selected records with
the aim of identifying value classifications and patterns. We
used an ad hoc data extraction form (Cucciniello et al. 2017).
We also conducted some descriptive and time-­trend analyses
on the extracted data. Our aim was to collect information
that could be subsequently aggregated and systematized: (a)
the main topic of each record; (b) the values included in each
study; and (c) whether some of the included values were ex-
plicitly identified as democratic or linked with the concept of
democracy.
2   |   Results
Exposure to existing scholarship immediately revealed confu-
sion about how values are conceptualized, operationalized, and
even more importantly, how they are distinguished from adja-
cent concepts, such as principles and norms.8 Moreover, frequent
unclear distinctions between what is valued in public adminis-
tration and what creates value make this literature difficult to
access and systematize.
It is therefore important to clarify what values are (and what
they are not). For Kluckhohn  (1951, 395), values are “con-
ceptions of the desirable” that influence the “selection from
available modes, means, and ends of action.” We adopt this well-­
established definition from social psychology, while recognizing
that other disciplinary perspectives may legitimately emphasize

different aspects of values. They are general, referring neither to
specific situations nor to specific functions. This characteristic
marks the biggest distinction between values and norms: val-
ues provide a referent for action, while norms provide the “do's
and don'ts” of situations (Parsons 1961, 43). Principles are posi-
tioned between values and norms; they are sufficiently concrete
to apply to particular situations (Alpa 1994, 1) but sufficiently
abstract to help to decipher values and to operationalize them
into norms and rules.
Suppose that the constitution of country X establishes that all
citizens are equal before the law.9 A clear underlying value of
this provision is equality. How does the value of equality find its
way into country X's public administration? A principle impos-
ing public administration's impartiality could shape the design
of a public organization and the delivery of its services such that
all individual beneficiaries are treated without bias or favorit-
ism. In practice, this entails the adoption of norms, and there-
fore, rules and standards that enforce impartiality. Now suppose
that X's constitution incorporates pluralistic decision making. A
principle of citizen participation can provide X's officials with
an actionable guide to reinforcing pluralism. To operationalize
citizen participation, norms could establish that for any policy
affecting more than a certain number of citizens, public admin-
istration must hold public consultation sessions. These three
layers of normative structures (value, principle and norm) are
often conceptualized differently by current scholarship, which
presents challenges given how important values are to the study
of public administration. Concepts like collaboration and co-­
production, the diffusion of power, lobbying, referenda, and
nonprofit activism are examples of concepts that should be
thought of as instrumental to values, rather than as values per se.
When scholars discuss institutional values, what values do they
discuss? While scholarly interest has increased over time,10 just
over 40% of the studies in our data focus exclusively on insti-
tutional values. In most cases, individual, professional, orga-
nizational, and institutional values are mixed, and boundaries
between these categories are relatively indistinct at both the
conceptual and empirical levels.11 This conflation obscures the
fact that these values operate in fundamentally different arenas
of social organization, each with distinct implications for dem-
ocratic governance. There has been no shortage of attempts to
distinguish between levels in a value hierarchy from a micro
to macro perspective Kraatz et al. (2020). Hood (1991) distin-
guishes between three families of administrative values that
shape organizational design in public administration: “sigma-­
type” values that emphasize matching resources to defined
tasks through economy and parsimony, “theta-­type” values that
prioritize honesty and fairness in administrative processes, and
“lambda-­type” values that focus on security, resilience, and or-
ganizational robustness. Kernaghan (2000, 2003) contrasts eth-
ical values (e.g., integrity and fairness) with democratic values
(e.g., impartiality and the rule of law) and professional values
(e.g., effectiveness and service). Various studies have extended
those taxonomies (Langford 2004; Rouillard and Giroux 2005;
Molina 2015a, 2015b) integrating values derived from codes of
conduct. Similarly, Van Wart (2013) makes distinctions among
individual, professional, organizational, legal, and public inter-
est values. We clarify these efforts by focusing on institutional
values.
Taxonomies also make distinctions among institutional val-
ues. Notable contributions by Rohr (1978), Overeem (2015) and
Koppell (2001) have stimulated work (Rosenbloom 2017; Baehler
et  al.  2014) that distinguished regime values from mission-­
related values and (extrinsic and intrinsic) non-­mission-­related
values, such as openness and fairness in administrative proce-
dures, privacy, and equal protection. Wiersma Strauss  (2021)
sees regime values as a subcategory of macro-­level public values
and catalogs prescriptive regime values, which serve normative
purposes for a regime rather than being descriptive attributes of
it (Overeem 2015). Other scholars have stressed the distinction
between traditional (institutional) public values and market-­
related values emerging from New Public Management reforms
(Denhardt  1997; van der Wal and Huberts  2008; Andersen
et  al.  2012; De Graaf and Paanakker  2015; Feldman  2015;
Correia et al. 2017; Nabatchi 2018).
Once again, the challenge of thinking systematically about these
two groups of values arises. Not only do the terms used to de-
scribe the various categories differ among authors, but there are
also notable differences in the values themselves. For instance,
Nabatchi  (2018) proposes a framework for public values that
distinguishes between political values (participation, repre-
sentation, political responsiveness, liberty, and equality), legal
values (individual substantive rights, procedural due process,
and equity), organizational values (administrative efficiency,
specialization and expertise, authority of positions, merit, for-
malization, and organizational loyalty, and political neutrality),
and market values (cost-­savings, cost-­efficiency, productivity,
flexibility, innovation, and customer service). In the same vein,
De Graaf and Paanakker (2015) distinguish procedural values
(honesty, integrity, equality, transparency, and lawfulness) and
performance values (efficiency and effectiveness). And these are
just two of the examples we could have provided.12 While we
refrain from judging whether one way of thinking about insti-
tutional values is preferable to another, we do feel that the lack
of uniformity among categorizations, coupled with the assign-
ment of different names for the same institutional values, can
present challenges for conceptual and empirical advancement
across studies.

systematic review, we aggregated each into a category (Table S8)

whether between procedural efficiency and democratic ideals,
traditional and emerging values, or ethical standards in a dy-

arise from administrators' diverse responsibilities and roles,
the introduction of new values through globalization and re-

New Public Management (17 articles) was also a frequent topic.

reforms and other values, questioning whether these reforms
might lead to value clashes, unintended consequences, or an
erosion of democratic values. Yet democratic values are cen-
tral to just seven studies. These explore how democratic and
constitutional values can be maintained while adapting to
modern management practices, and they emphasize the im-
portance of aligning public administration with “regime” val-
ues. Seven more articles addressed value congruence across
sectors, investigating whether public and private actors share
common or maintain distinct values for informing and mak-
ing decisions.
The geographical distribution of records reveals a significant
concentration of research activity,14 with Europe leading the
distribution at 40.2%, followed by the Americas, which account
for 39.3% of the total records. Asia contributes a smaller por-
tion (9.82%), with Oceania (7.14%) and Africa (3.57%) smaller
still. In the Americas, studies have been primarily conducted
in (or conceptually built in the context of) the United States
(n = 34). The Netherlands (n = 11), Canada (n = 8), and the
United Kingdom (n = 10) are common contexts. Publications
to date have been heavily concentrated in a few regions, leav-
ing much of the world, particularly developing countries,
underrepresented in the research activities.15 This trend has
been widely recognized, and calls for its reversal are strong
(e.g., Bertelli et al. 2020). All of the conceptual studies meant
to understand institutional practice were designed with the
United States or with Westminster systems in mind (n = 14).
When considering that continental European administrative
systems, like many others around the world, are undergirded
by civil rather than common law, this is a significant limita-
tion indeed.
4   |   Anchoring Institutional Values to Democracy
What role does democracy play in the literature on institu-
tional values? For the purposes of the analysis that follows,
we define democracy as a system of self-­governance where
individuals, guided by the rule of law, have an equal voice in
public affairs and can pursue their own vision of the good,
provided they do not infringe on the same pursuit by other
individuals (Bertelli and Schwartz 2023, 16). This definition,
which synthesizes insights from liberal democratic theory,
provides analytical precision for our present approach while
recognizing that other theoretical traditions emphasize differ-
ent aspects of democratic governance. It also reveals a range of
democratic values: self-­governance, equal representation, free
and equal participation and voice in the public domain, free-
dom to think and to act, and a reasonable pluralism regarding
values. To identify those values important to scholarly discus-
sions of democracy in public administration, we first collected
information about whether the records recognized discussed
values as democratic. Then, we identified patterns and classi-
fied categories of values. Just under 47% of the studies meeting
our criteria contained discussions about what they considered
to be democratic values.
Table 1 shows the results of our categorization in the right-
most column. Scholars label values belonging to all categories
apart from state stability and rational judgment as democratic
in the contributions we reviewed. Some studies (around 44%)
consider professional and individual values—honesty, integ-
rity, engagement, loyalty, leadership, and self-­motivation—to
be democratic values (see Table S12). While it is legitimate to
expect that public officials driven by these values might con-
tribute to the smooth operation of organizations embedded
in democratic systems, they are not values that work in the
institutionalized organizations of governance, such as those
drawn from our working definition of democracy. Efficiency,
which has been considered by some to produce conflict with
democratic values (e.g., Kernaghan  2000; van der Wal and
Huberts 2008; Bumgarner and Newswander 2009), is consid-
ered by others to be itself democratic (see e.g., Bertelli et al.
2025). And while these conceptions might induce public offi-
cials within institutions to reinforce democratic values, they
do not (and cannot) guide them toward choices that are dem-
ocratically desirable—something inherent in the concept of
values (Kluckhohn 1951, 395).
Our critique thus stems from the confusion in both the defi-
nition of values and the levels at which both values (and non-­
values) are applied (or are claimed to apply) in the literature that
we have systematically reviewed. We state this critique strongly
primarily to permit us to make a constructive suggestion. The
literature shows a high and increasing interest in democratic
values, and through a few simple linkages, public administra-
tion scholars can address questions that have enduringly im-
portant implications.

working definition of democracy.16 The leftmost column pres-
ents eight democratic anchors, that is, claims through which
institutional values can be linked to the foundations of dem-
ocratic public administration as we have conceptualized it.

generate guidance for empirical work as well as for adminis-

tutional value directly to our working definition of democracy.
These anchors recognize that democratic governance involves
both instrumental effectiveness and intrinsic commitments,
acknowledging that means and ends are often intertwined in
public administration.
The second column shows the value categories that we un-
covered from the literature that can straightforwardly raise
democratic research questions should a scholar “drop” the

an institutional value in the second column is considered in
light of its implications for a democratic anchor in the first
column, democratic research questions like those in the third

The research questions and problems in the rightmost col-

framework as one systematic approach to connecting values
and democracy (Bertelli et al. 2025), recognizing that alterna-
tive theoretical perspectives may organize these relationships

The first anchor is a “thick” view of the rule of law. Officials
must apply norms, standards, and rules but also maintain
substantive justice and fortify fundamental rights as they do
so. This embraces a vision of the desirable (i.e., value) that (a)
comports with “social equity” arguments (e.g., Riccucci 2019).
Consider the use of a standardized test as a requirement for
graduation of all students in a public school even though a
minority group of students systematically does worse on the
exam, placing their graduation and future opportunities in
jeopardy. This highlights a tension between enabling the en-
actment of laws produced by political institutions (like the
test-­for-­graduation requirement) and limiting the potential
arbitrariness of democratic power through the protection
of individual rights and liberties (like those of the minority
group).17 Beyond legality, studies of rational judgment and
privacy can be democratically anchored by emphasizing the
problems of arbitrary official power and reinforcing the dem-
ocratic values of governance in research questions. Suppose
that a visa applicant were told in January 2025 that processing
the application was delayed because of the lingering impact
on administrative workflow due to the coronavirus pandemic
that eased years earlier. Examining such situations both con-
ceptually and empirically allows one to consider the practical
risks of power abuse, selective enforcement, and unpredict-
able regulation.
Two anchors consider the impartial treatment of individuals
by officials. Input impartiality takes root in political equality,
claiming that everyone can access a fair and impartial process.
Democratically salient questions may ask how neutrality, equal-
ity, and procedural fairness create the conditions for equal treat-
ment of individuals in administrative procedures.18 This can
shed light on the problems of politicization, privileged access,
and implicit procedural biases. The abovementioned standard-
ized test scenario raises significant questions of input impartial-
ity because the expectations of and preparation for the test may
not be fairly distributed across population groups. By contrast,
output impartiality requires that officials avoid relying on ex-
tralegal and personal considerations when implementing the
law. This can directly connect questions of equity to the demo-
cratic value of equality by focusing on how equity can diminish
decision-­making biases in order to shed light on problems of fa-
voritism and corruption. Output impartiality borne by students
due to the disparate impact of the test required for graduation
might well be seen as qualitatively different from that faced by
the visa applicant in limbo, but both have democratic impor-
tance due to their common anchor.
Another pair of anchors relates to accountability. Political
accountability—of representatives to the people—aligns the
process and actions of government with societal values and
the public good. Administrative accountability—of officials
to the representative government—shapes the norms of offi-
cial action in light of those same social values and common
objectives. Institutional values related to officials' respect for
the collective will of the people raise questions regarding the
alignment of administrative action with public preferences
and interests that can shed light on problems of a truly opaque
administrative state that does not consider the full range of
interests undergirding the laws it implements. Answerability
of officials to representatives and responsiveness of officials to
public wants are democratic considerations when considered
against the parameters of duly enacted enabling laws because
they train attention on legitimation problems arising from
both administrative procedures and their outcomes. As an
example, suppose that a public agency in a midwestern state
entered into a consent decree (i.e., a negotiated agreement that
settles a legal case and is approved by a judge) in 1995, and
the violated law that led the agency to enter the decree was
substantially amended by valid legislative action during the
past 30 years. If an official in the agency now takes action that
violates the consent decree but not the current law, an empir-
ical examination of what happened raises the democratic ten-
sion between administrative accountability (to the courts by
respecting the consent decree) and political accountability (to
the people by respecting current law).19
Free and equal participation in public concerns is itself a demo-
cratic value. Institutional values related to balancing competing
interests, public participation in administrative processes, trans-
parent and responsive administration raise democratic ques-
tions about how informed citizens can be about what is done
by officials in the name of the people (see e.g., Cordelli 2020).
Addressing these questions can enlighten problems of institu-
tional trust and poor adaptation in changing circumstances as
well as institutionalized biases. In the consent decree example,
public reporting on the progress made toward successfully im-
plementing the decree is important to citizens and should be
accessible to them. Assessing the clarity and accuracy of com-
munication from public officials about the measures being
implemented to comply with the decree has democratic impor-
tance. How concerns from affected individuals might reach pub-
lic officials and be given timely responses is a similarly anchored
question.

Efficiency, equity, and equality as institutional value categories
become democratic questions when using public resources is
weighed against meeting social goals for public policy. Such con-
siderations are crucially instrumental to democracy when they
influence procedures and official behavior to appropriately pri-

agency that proposes a highly complex rule that has a signifi-

of public comment to a period that is shorter than usual for such
rules.20 Asking whether such action prioritizes officials' own
interests in designing and maintaining procedures over fairer
alternatives is democratically anchored because it considers the
quality of outcomes.
Structural integrity, the final anchor in Table 2, denotes the
coherence and consistency of institutions in upholding legal
norms and reinforcing democratic values (cites withheld).
Questions of integrity are democratically relevant when they
ask how an institutional value helps to make some or all of the
foregoing anchored institutional values function collectively.
Categories in the literature we reviewed such as system value
reinforcement, state stability, rational judgment, institutional
resilience, and representation can all raise such questions.
The problems they enlighten are particularly salient in the
contemporary context of democratic “backsliding.” They in-
clude the erosion of trust and of checks on and balances of
power, the capture of administrative processes and officials by
elites, and the challenges of adapting administration to public
wants and needs.

5   |   Conclusion
Our review highlights a persistent conceptual ambiguity in
the study of values, particularly regarding their nature and
the level of society at which they operate. This limitation,
we argue, hinders a systematic engagement with democratic
­values in a way that is both rigorous and practical. The pub-
lic administration literature we reviewed does not have a co-
herent structure for analyzing the operation of values within
the institutions of governance—public agencies, third-­sector
­actors, private contractors, and so forth—and these are what
our field studies most. Consequently, a connection between
work on values in our field and those with vibrant values
agendas, such as social psychology, sociology, and political
science, is more difficult to forge than it should be. What is
more, the values orientation of the public administration lit-
erature makes far fewer connections with democracy than are
possible.
Given the lessons of our systematic review, we propose a
framework of democratic anchors—mechanisms through
which values can encapsulate the fundamentals of democracy
in public administration research. By mapping commonly
studied institutional values in public administration onto
an institution-­independent definition of democracy, this ap-
proach offers a structured way to examine how such values
shape the norms and behaviors of public officials. Scholars
have demonstrated how democratic values are not merely ab-
stract ideals but are actively shaped and constrained by admin-
istrative structures. For instance, Bertelli (2021) argues that
public administration is not just an instrument of democratic
governance but a locus of active arbitration among compet-
ing values. Democratic values are institutionalized features of
public administration rather than external principles applied
to decisions made by public officials (cf. Andersen et al. 2012;
Nabatchi 2012; Bertelli 2021). The democratic anchors frame-
work thus provides both analytic tools for scholars and prac-
tical guidance for administrators seeking to act responsibly
within democratic systems.
The contemporary challenge of democratic backsliding under-
scores the urgency of developing a more robust framework for
understanding how public administration shapes democratic
values. Democratic erosion is often driven not only by overt
political actions but also by subtler, institutionalized processes
that undermine democratic norms from within (cf. Waldner
and Lust 2018; Bauer and Becker 2020). As democratic values
become contested in many established democracies, under-
standing how administrative institutions embed, transmit,
and sometimes themselves erode these values is critical. By in-
tegrating multi-­disciplinary insights, our framework of demo-
cratic anchors offers scholars interested in these contemporary
challenges a way to systematically assess the role of institutions
both in stabilizing as well as in challenging democratic gover-
nance. We hope that it helps to encourage both renewed vigor
and clarity in how our field studies democratic values. And we
invite future research from diverse theoretical perspectives to
build upon and to challenge our framework, recognizing that
scholarly progress benefits from multiple viewpoints that en-
gage with such important questions.

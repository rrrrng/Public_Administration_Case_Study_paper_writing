Abstract
This paper explores the speed of rulemaking in American state governments. Drawing on a unique data set of over 250,000 individual rules
issued by states from 1993 through 2009, we introduce new measures of the speed and breadth of rulemaking in American state bureaucracies,
providing a new way of evaluating the incidence of rulemaking delay within and across governments. We focus specifically on how profession-
alism and oversight powers of state legislative and executive branches affect rulemaking speed and find that states with more professionalized
legislatures and governments with extensive legislative/executive oversight powers experience greater delays in rule adoption. These findings
provide important new insights into the politics of regulatory delay and suggest disparate ways in which sub-national governments approach
regulatory policymaking in a federal system.
American state governments structure their rulemaking
process in fundamentally different ways (Schwartz, 2010;
Berry, 2016; Broughel, Baugus and Bose, 2022), affecting
how their administrative agencies engage in regulatory poli-
cymaking in a competitive federal system. Although virtually
all states have adopted modernized Administrative Procedure
Acts (Gellhorn, 1986; Mashaw, 1990; Asimow, Bonfield and
Levin, 1998), there remains considerable variation in how
state governments organize and oversee the rulemaking pro-
cess. In many states, regulatory agencies must provide exten-
sive economic and/or social impact statements prior to rule
adoption, publicizing information on the expected cost and
benefit of major rules, or justifying the impact of regulation
on small businesses. In other states, agencies have no such
obligations and can adopt new rules after providing only lim-
ited notification of proposed rulemaking activity.1 Beyond
such procedural requirements, the constitutions of the states
provide legislative and executive branches with very different
different powers of regulatory oversight. Many state legisla-
tures possess unilateral power to veto or suspend regulations,
while in others the rulemaking process is more insulated from
the direct involvement of elected officials (Berry, 2016).
These different constellations of regulatory review institu-
tions affect how state agencies engage in rulemaking—either
by making the rulemaking process more transparent or by
increasing the abilities, opportunities, and capacities of the
elected principals to engage in oversight. Civil servants oper-
ating in states with few formal rulemaking constraints can
produce regulations relatively quickly, while in states with
more expansive and byzantine regulatory regimes, rulemaking

Surprisingly, despite widespread perceptions that state gov-
ernments foster very different regulatory environments, we
know little about the comparative speed and/or delay of
bureaucratic rulemaking in the American states. The question
of regulatory delay features prominently in studies of federal
rulemaking (Potter, 2017; Yackee and Yackee, 2010; Kerwin
and Furlong, 1992); yet, there is little corresponding empir-
ical work documenting its incidence within and across state
bureaucracies. The omission of the states is consequential to
our understanding of regulatory policymaking in the United
States, as it is not clear that insights regarding federal rule-
making translate neatly to the state level. State bureaucracies
operate in fundamentally different regulatory environments
than their federal counterparts, and must navigate the rule-
making process in partnership with legislative and executive
principals that face vastly different constraints on influencing
agency behavior.
The neglect of the states is especially unfortunate given
the central importance of state agencies in American policy-
making. State agencies issue thousands of regulations a year,
establishing policy in areas as diverse as education, crimi-
nal justice, finance, health care, and parks and recreation.
The economic impact of these decisions can be significant.
Nearly 20 percent of the US economy is directly regulated
by state agencies (Teske, 2004). A comparative study of
state-level rulemaking promises to cast new light on how
legislative professionalism and state oversight regimes shape
rulemaking, contributing new empirical empirical leverage to
nascent theoretical accounts. This approach not only pushes
us towards new theory regarding regulatory policymaking in
the states, but also positions us to answer critical questions

In this paper, we explore the speed of rulemaking in
American state governments. Drawing on a unique data set
of over 250,000 individual rules issued by states from 1993
through 2009, we introduce new measures of the speed and
breadth of rulemaking in American state bureaucracies—
providing a comprehensive new way of evaluating and com-
paring the incidence of rulemaking delay within and across
states. We primarily explore how broad differences in the
regulatory regimes of state governments affect the speed of
rulemaking. Unlike studies of “ossification” of the rulemak-
ing process at the federal level (which typically focus on how
procedural requirements impact rulemaking), our approach
focuses explicitly on how types of fundamental institutional
variation condition the speed of rulemaking by state govern-
ments. The remainder of our paper proceeds as follows. In
the next section we draw upon research on federal and state
rulemaking to advance a series of expectations for how the
professionalism of state legislatures and the strength of formal
ex-post legislative and executive oversight powers systemat-
ically affect the rulemaking process. We then introduce our
measures of regulatory speed, providing a novel overview of
the state rulemaking landscape. We use our measures to esti-
mate a series of models for how differences in the powers
of state governments relate to rule speed and broadly find
support for our expectations connecting legislative profession-
alism and political oversight to rulemaking delay. Ultimately,
our focus on systematic impacts of institutional variation con-
tributes new insights into the politics of regulatory delay and
sheds light on the previously obscure patterns of regulatory
policymaking in a federal system.
Federal Rulemaking Delay
There is a broad literature on the problem of regulatory delay
in federal agency policymaking (Carpenter, 2002; Kerwin and
Furlong, 1992; McGarity, 1991; Yackee and Yackee, 2010,
2011). Although the incidence and severity of such delay is
itself a matter of disagreement among scholars of administra-
tive power (Yackee and Yackee, 2010), much of the empiri-
cal and normative work on administrative rulemaking in the
federal government has examined the causes of regulatory
ossification—a challenge characterized by extreme delays in
the process of notice and comment rulemaking in American
federal agencies.
This attention to ossification in federal rulemaking is under-
standable given the high stakes of regulatory policymaking in
American politics. Federal agencies have assumed a central
role in the policy process. They monitor policy problems,
develop viable policy alternatives, and implement new solu-
tions (Workman, 2015). The speed of administrative rulemak-
ing is therefore important because the ability of agencies to
efficiently develop regulatory policy reflects the government’s
capacity to dynamically respond to a diverse universe of
policy problems. As McGarity (1991, 1391–1395) observes,
regulatory ossification imposes a broad range of economic
and social costs on American taxpayers by limiting federal
agencies’ abilities to enact (or for that matter repeal) policy
solutions. This needlessly heightens the costs of regulatory
policymaking, stifling the incentives of agencies to experiment
with new approaches to managing government programs, and
creates pressures for agencies to bypass traditional and
transparent notice and comment rulemaking procedures
through subaltern means.
In most theoretical accounts, the problem of federal regu-
latory delay is understood to have been exacerbated by the
efforts of Congress and the presidency to control the dis-
cretion of regulatory agencies in policymaking (Yackee and
Yackee, 2010).2 Although Congress and the White House
have benefited from the efficiency of increased bureaucratic
participation in policymaking, they have also struggled to
place boundaries on the ability of bureaucrats to shape pol-
icy against their wishes. Rather than engaging in costly ex
post oversight of agency activity, Congress and the president
have instead favored limiting agency discretion through the
prescription of administrative procedures that constrain the
flexibility of bureaucrats in the notice and comment rulemak-
ing process (McCubbins, Noll and Weingast, 1987, 1989;
McCubbins and Schwartz, 1984b). For example, following
the Reagan administration’s lead, the White House’s Office
of Management and Budget (OMB) has historically reviewed
required regulatory impact analyses of all drafts of major rules
with significant economic impacts. Congress has similarly
passed legislation compelling agencies to generate information
on the expected impact of proposed rules on small business
(Yackee and Yackee, 2009). These procedural reforms were
intended to increase the accountability of agencies to the pub-
lic, industry and elected officials. However they have come at
the cost of increasing the rigidity and burden of bureaucratic
policymaking (McGarity, 1991).
Perhaps because of the perceived importance of procedu-
ral policy on federal regulatory delay, most studies of federal
rulemaking have focused on the historical impact of various
administrative procedures on the pace of federal rulemaking—
tracing how changes in congressional or presidential efforts
to manage the notice and comment rulemaking process have
contributed to ossification. Similarly, research on regulatory
deossification reforms has focused in large part on procedural
politics, focusing on how the creation of strict deadlines on the
rulemaking process have influenced the pace of federal poli-
cymaking (Potter, 2017; Kerwin and Furlong, 2011; Gersen
and O’Connell, 2008; Lavertu and Yackee, 2014).
Important as this work is, it leaves aside important ques-
tions raised by observers of state politics, who typically view
regulatory delay as in part the product of a broader variation
in state regulatory review regimes and institutional powers.
State governments provide a fundamentally different way to
explore causes of regulatory delay in the United States. A study
of the states permits a comparative approach that allows us to
asses how different configurations of legislative and executive
oversight powers condition rulemaking delay.

The Institutional Determinants Of Regulatory Delay
In the States
While the debates over the severity and causes of federal reg-
ulatory delay provide a compelling framework for thinking
about the causes and impact of regulatory speed in general,
students of state politics have cautioned that the standard
models of bureaucratic oversight in the federal government
may not map neatly onto the dynamics of sub-national poli-
cymaking (Krause and Woods, 2014). Among many relevant
differences across levels of American government, policy in
the federal government is developed by high capacity politi-
cal institutions that face far fewer resource constraints than
their counterparts in the states. Furthermore, there is remark-
able parity across the executive and legislative branches in the
federal government, where a highly professionalized Congress
works alongside a highly professionalized White House that
manages the delegation of policy development and implemen-
tation to a fully professionalized set of administrative agen-
cies. Such parity across legislative and executive institutions
is atypical in the state capitals, where post-war expansions
of executive power have not been matched by commensurate
investments in state legislatures. This imbalance of power has
been exacerbated by legislative deprofessionalization reforms
adopted over the last three decades (term limits, limits on
session length, reductions in resources and staff allocated
to members and legislative committees) which have further
constrained state legislatures (Boushey and McGrath, 2017).3
Krause and Woods (2014) argue that such variation in the
professionalism and capacity of state political institutions may
create unique incentives for delegating policy authority to the
bureaucracy. Research on state politics has therefore explored
whether state bureaucratic policymaking is conditioned by
the professionalism of state governments (Huber and Shipan,
2002; Volden, 2002; Woods and Baranowski, 2006). This
work has focused on two distinct themes—exploring on the
one hand how shifts in legislative professionalism influence
the legislature’s incentives and abilities to delegate discre-
tion to the bureaucracy (Huber and Shipan, 2002; Boushey
and McGrath, 2017), and on the other how differences in
the capabilities of legislatures to monitor the bureaucracy
shape the behaviors and attitudes of bureaucrats (concerning
generic bureaucratic behavior, see Woods (2004), Woods and
Baranowski (2006), and Palus and Yackee (2016); and con-
cerning more specific regulatory and public service outcomes,
see Boehmke and Shipan (2015), Lillvis and McGrath (2017),
Drolc and Keiser (2020), and Cook and Fortunato (2023)).
We develop expectations for how differences across each of
these institutional characteristics affect state-level regulatory
speed and delay below.
Legislative Professionalism
The low level of professionalism in many state legisla-
tures contributes to important differences in the speed of
bureaucratic rulemaking in two distinct ways (one “ex
ante”, the other “ex post”). First and foremost, the bene-
fits of delegating rulemaking authority to bureaucracy differ
across professionalized and citizen legislatures (Huber and
Shipan, 2002). States with fully professionalized legislatures
(like California or Pennsylvania) resemble Congress in their

administrative agencies through ex ante use of statutory or

of discretion involve an explicit trade-off between the effi-

control (Huber, Shipan and Pfahler, 2001). On the other
hand, policymakers in part-time “citizen legislatures” lack the
time, expertise, and resources to write detailed legislation, and
therefore depend heavily—and almost unconditionally—on
the expertise of more professionalized administrative agencies
to guide lawmaking (Boushey and McGrath, 2017).
Bureaucrats in professionalized states are directed to
develop and implement broad and complex regulatory frame-
works, and must work carefully in consultation with industry
and elected representatives in developing draft regulations.
This means holding extensive notice and comment hearings,
and issuing appropriate regulations only after extensive con-
sultation. These procedures and the consultation that they
require should have the effect of slowing the rulemaking pro-
cess in states with professionalized legislatures, as compared
to more amateur legislative bodies.4
In contrast, bureaucrats in citizen legislatures work under
very different pressures from the legislature in the rulemaking
process. Here, elected officials often delegate discretion out
of necessity rather than for strategic reasons, as policymak-
ers in these states simply do not possess the time, resources,
or expertise needed to write carefully detailed and nuanced
legislation (Boushey and McGrath, 2017). For example, law-
makers in less professionalized state governments are more
likely to engage in “copy and paste” lawmaking, borrow-
ing the text of laws from their neighbors without spending
additional time to amend and tailor the laws for local needs
(Jansa, Hansen and Gray, 2018). Amateur legislators are also
more likely to adopt “model bills” sponsored by interest
groups (Hertel-Fernandez, 2014) and to rely directly on the
bureaucracy to write legislation for them (Kroeger, 2022).
Such delegation creates downstream demand for more
responsive bureaucratic policymaking, as state civil servants
must pick up the slack and fill in the details through regula-
tion. There is some limited support for this idea that citizen
legislatures depend on a more active bureaucracy. Boushey
and McGrath (2017) find that the volume of regulations
increases as the relative balance of expertise between the
lawmaking branches tilts in favor of the executive. This same
pressure may lead to a more responsive bureaucracy—one
that not only enacts more regulation, but does so more rapidly
in order to adapt vague legislation to local needs.

Legislative and Executive Oversight Powers
Perhaps because of concern over dependence on the bureau-
cracy in lawmaking, state governments have experimented
with a number of ex post oversight reforms intended to
increase the ability of elected representatives in the legisla-
tive or executive branches to check bureaucratic discretion.
The constitutions and statutory codes of state governments
provide for a wide range of formal powers related to exec-
utive and legislative rule review. In many states these pow-
ers are very limited—and legislatures can do little more than
issue a joint resolution declaring opposition to administrative
regulations. In other states the legislature has the author-
ity to suspend or veto administrative rules without guberna-
torial approval (Renfrow, West and Houston, 1986; Grady
and Simon, 2002; Schwartz, 2010; Berry, 2016; Broughel,
Baugus and Bose, 2022). Similarly, although governors are
typically expected to hold broad influence over agency rule-
making, there are considerable differences in how state gov-
ernments have organized the role of the executive in rule
review. In many states the executive branch alone possesses
the power to compel agencies to scrap or revisit draft regu-
lations or pull back controversial new rules. In other states
this power is given to an independent review authority,
or is distributed across both the executive and legislative
branches.

Data, Variables, and Methods
Rulemaking Speed and Delay In the States
Rulemaking is the primary way that American bureaucracies
shape public policy, and represents an integral activity for
agencies of all types. Rulemaking procedure is determined
by jurisdictional administrative procedure acts, and most
states follow the 1946 federal APA in distinguishing between
formal and informal rulemaking (Gellhorn, 1986; Mashaw,
1990; Asimow, Bonfield and Levin, 1998). Though there are
nuances by state and policy jurisdiction, the shorthand under-
standing is that “formal” rulemaking (covered by §556–557
in the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946) is the less com-
mon variety, occurring explicitly at the behest of legislation
and proceeding with relatively little agency discretion. On the
other hand, “informal” rulemaking gains its legitimacy from
public notice and comment requirements (stipulated in §553
in the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946). These proceed-
ings are far more common expressions of agency policymak-
ing and are, with some exceptions (Hamilton and Schroeder,
1994; Nielson, 2014), the focus of academic research on
agency policymaking (Kerwin and Furlong, 2011).8 To assess
whether and how forms of political oversight affect the speed
of the rulemaking process, we turn to a comprehensive dataset
of agency “notice and comment” rulemakings in the American
states (Boushey and McGrath, 2017, 2020, as in).

As discussed above, state legislatures vary considerably in
their levels of Legislative Professionalism. The canonical indi-

of measures of staff size, days in session, and compensation

contexts where this pattern is systematic, it could bias our results, leading us
to incorrectly conclude that state governments, agencies, or issue areas pro-

this important first stage of the rulemaking process, and so focus on the sec-
ond estimate of time from proposal to adoption. Future research is needed to
observe and capture the politics of the pre-proposal stage across the states.

States’ formal powers of legislative oversight of agency rule-
making vary greatly. While some states provide the legisla-
ture with only weak powers to register symbolic objections
to administrative rulemaking, other legislatures have provi-
sions allowing them (with agreement of both chambers) to
suspend, sunset, or unilaterally veto proposed and/or adopted
regulations without a signature from the executive branch
(Schwartz, 2010; Berry, 2016; Broughel, Baugus and Bose,
2022). As we argue above, the specter of the legislative veto
may affect the speed with which agencies make policy through
rulemaking. 19 In particular, we expect that when states have
weak oversight powers, agencies can afford to make rules as
quickly as they would like, sometimes using their informa-
tional and resource advantages to subvert legislative policy
preferences. On the other hand, when legislatures have the
power to monitor and veto rules, agencies should take their
time to build stronger political coalitions around their pro-
posals to ensure that their work does not get obviated at the
last moment. In these circumstances, legislative review pow-
ers serve as a constraining handbrake on agency policymaking
prerogatives.
To assess how ex post legislative veto oversight powers
affect rulemaking, we formally categorize each state’s Leg-
islative Review powers regime. This measure is constructed
from two relevant surveys of state legislative oversight powers
(Berry, 2016; Schwartz, 2010), which together provide infor-
mation on both the scope and important historical changes
to state legislative oversight authority during our period of
observation. We assign an indicator to distinguish among
those legislatures that possess no expanded powers of legisla-
tive oversight, those with powers to suspend regulations pend-
ing executive action, and those that are able to unilaterally
veto or sunset regulations without gubernatorial consent. In
addition, we denote the five states (IA, GA, IL, NV, OH) that
allow both suspension and nullification vetoes to be hybrid
systems.

sired rulemaking activity and we expect that agencies need
to work longer to satisfy state legislatures when they possess

Agency-Level Covariates
While the executive salary measure captures state-level varia-
tion, it does not address agency-level factors that can crucially
drive rulemaking speed. Our data include indicators for the
agency responsible for developing each individual rule, but we
lack crucial information regarding agency budgets, staff, and
material resources. Yet, we do know the general type of agency
involved with each rule in the data. The data include an indica-
tor for whether the responsible administrative unit is consid-
ered a department, agency, division, board, or commission.
Some of these distinctions are likely just cosmetic, but there
is a substantive difference between boards/commissions and
departments/agencies. The former are usually led by a mix-
ture of political appointees and invited “outside” members.
These groups tend to focus on distinct licensing and regula-
tory issues and their membership may not be very experienced
with the rulemaking process. In addition, boards/commissions
are likely to have smaller staff sizes dedicated to analyzing and
pursuing policy solutions and thus may develop policy more
slowly than full-time agencies with more resources and policy-
making experience. To control for the potential that different
organizational structures engender different baseline rulemak-
ing speeds, we include an indicator for whether a rule has been
developed by an agency, department, or division.

Having described our data, we can now briefly examine
raw patterns existing between the speed of rule completion
(the dependent variable) and oversight regimes. Overall, the
expected relationship between political oversight regimes and
rule duration can be found in Figure 1, which shows that “no
legislative oversight” states in particular complete rules the
fastest. This pattern is even more apparent in Figure 2. Here,
we control for policy mix and complexity by grouping each
topic’s time to adoption distribution by legislative oversight
regime across states. For each of the 20 most prevalent sub-
stantive topics, Figure 2 demonstrates that agencies in states
with no legislative review powers complete rules more quickly
than in any other oversight regime. Nullification and sus-
pension oversight systems are correlated with longer median
delays and wider inter-quartile ranges, indicating that agen-
cies in theses states are significantly constrained from making
policy quickly across all major policy areas.

models.25

Overall then, it appears that legislative professional-
ism (from Hypothesis 1) and oversight regimes (from
Hypothesis 2) in the states do have predictable and sub-
stantively interesting effects on rulemaking speed. This fact
alone supplements what we know from the federal level and
should stimulate further research. In addition, the main results
regarding Hypotheses 1 and 2 are robust to the estimation of
parsimonious models (Appendix B), estimating Cox propor-
tional hazards models (Appendix D), and estimating within-
state fixed effect models to flexibly control for cross-sectional
confounders (Appendix G).
The patterns are clear, robust, and not particularly sen-
sitive to unmeasured confounders. Appendix H, for exam-
ple, provides a quantitative sensitivity analysis (Cinelli and
Hazlett, 2020). This process gives us a sense of how corre-
lated hypothetical confounders would have to be with rule-
making speed and our main treatment variables to negate the
effects that we report. With respect to the legislative oversight
covariate, a hypothetical confounder would have to explain
incredible variance in the outcome and in the treatment (more
than 5 times the variance as the powerful Total Cumulative
Number of Adopted Rules baseline regressor does) to obviate
reported effects. On the other hand, the legislative profession-
alism effects are more susceptible to a plausible unmeasured
confounder according to the analysis in Appendix H. Here,
a hypothetical confounder similarly correlated with profes-
sionalism and rulemaking speed as Total Cumulative Num-
ber of Adopted Rules would negate the reported effects. It
is unclear what that unmeasured confounder would be in
this case given our efforts to control for various determi-
nants of rulemaking speed and given that the effects of pro-
fessionalism maintain when we estimate within-state fixed
effects models. It is of course possible that some time vari-
ant within-state omitted factor exists that would negate the
legislative professionalism effects, so we have less confidence
in this result as compared to that regarding legislative veto
oversight.
The more theoretically and substantively interesting fea-
ture (to the authors, at least) of the legislative profession-
alism variable can be gleaned from Appendix C, where
we control for explicit across-state rulemaking procedures
(Renfrow, West and Houston, 1986) (Appendix C). These
are the types of procedures that scholars of federal “ossi-
fication” tend to focus on at the expense of the (largely
invariant at the federal level) more constitutional and insti-
tutional variation that we explore. In particular, the “Rule-
making Restrictiveness Index” (calculated using all available
categories from Renfrow, West and Houston (1986) using
data from Broughel, Baugus and Bose (2022)) is a simple
summed index of 10 dichotomous variables (Public Comment,
Petition, Statutory Authority, Register, Leg-Exec Review,
Leg-Exec Veto, Economic Analysis, Rules Review, Substan-
tial Evidence, and Cross-Examination). When included in the
models, this index is found to have no discernible effect on
rule speed, while the estimated effects of legislative profes-
sionalism remain unchanged from those reported in Table 2.
This fact seems to point to the “ex post”, as opposed to the
“ex ante” theoretical mechanism linking professionalism with
rulemaking speed. That is, it seems that professional legisla-
tures slow rulemaking largely due to their ability to moni-
tor and micromanage agency rulemaking actions once they
begin, rather than the ex ante story about professional leg-
islatures being able to establish procedures that effectively
protract agency policymaking. This is consistent with recent
research demonstrating that legislative resources determine
legislative influence over agency policy decisions in a wide
range of substantive areas (Boehmke and Shipan, 2015; Lil-
lvis and McGrath, 2017; Drolc and Keiser, 2020; Cook and
Fortunato, 2023). This ex post/resource mechanism is also
bolstered by Appendix E which shows that the most impor-
tant component of the legislative professionalism index is the
legislative expenditures/staff resources component.
Conclusion
Rulemaking is and has been an incredibly salient topic to
policymakers and regulated entities, as well as legal schol-
ars. It is finally becoming more so to political scientists and
public policy scholars, especially at the state level. Inspired by
a line of important research at the federal level (O’Connell,
2008, 2011; Yackee and Yackee, 2010; Potter and Shipan,
2019; Potter, 2017), scholars have recently worked to charac-
terize the overall scope of rulemaking in the states (Boushey
and McGrath, 2017, 2020). These papers demonstrate varia-
tion across states in rulemaking volume and provide limited
evidence of an increasing role for state bureaucracies in the
policy process over time. According to this research, agencies
are strategic producers of policy and their output is strongly
and consistently determined by political factors. When state
legislatures become deprofessionalized, agencies pick up the
slack (Boushey and McGrath, 2017). When elected branches
quarrel, agencies use gridlock to their advantage and expand
their policymaking activities (Boushey and McGrath, 2020).
While informative, this work has largely neglected issues of
rulemaking efficiency and timing and ignored how tailored
oversight tools of elected branches can constrain rulemaking
behavior of bureaucrats.

can come at high cost. Yet, there is also a cost to state agencies
speedily approving regulations at the expense of democratic
deliberation. While our research cannot yet speak to these nor-
mative issues, what we present in this paper is an important
starting point. One important extension will evaluate how
the speed of state rulemaking relates to the quality of regula-
tions proposed and adopted by state governments—exploring
whether the time invested in rulemaking produces regulations
that are technically more precise, more easily implemented, or
more broadly accepted by the regulated communities.
In sum, our findings illustrate how legislative review pow-
ers, in particular (we found no discernible effects of executive
review powers in our data), can help categorize and elucidate
the considerable variation in rulemaking efficiency that exists
across the states. In particular, agencies that operate in an
environment of minimal formal oversight are most likely to
complete regulations quickly. On the other hand, when state
legislatures have the resources that come from legislative pro-
fessionalism and the formal authority to veto rules ex post,
state agencies become much more deliberative and the average
speed of rulemaking is noticeably slower.
This is an important first step in describing and analyzing
the variation in rule speed across the states, but it should not
be the last. In fact, there is quite a lot of work to do to more
firmly establish the patterns that we initially identify here. In
particular, institutional review powers are not exogenous to
state rulemaking contexts. In fact, it is quite likely that leg-
islative and executive veto powers are themselves a function
of an underlying constellation of separation of powers issues
that differentiate the states cross-sectionally. Future research
should both assess veto power rules as an outcome of inter-
est and utilize tools, such as synthetic case control techniques
(Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller, 2010), to zero in on the
causal effects of rare recent institutional change.28 Similarly,
future research on the separation of powers will profit from
developing a theory of judicial oversight on regulatory policy-
making in the states, allowing us to account for how variation
in the power, capacity, organization, tenure, selection, and
intention of state judiciaries affects the speed of regulatory
policymaking.
Future research should also explore the ripe topic of how
state regulation responds to regulated entities and interest
groups. Despite demonstrating robust regional and inter-state
“diffusion effects”, we do not develop a comprehensive polit-
ical economy model of regulatory speed in this paper, and
future research should build the microfoundations of how
state-by-state economic competition should be expected to
condition regulatory politics in a competitive federalist sys-
tem. Along these lines, we conclude our empirical analysis in
Appendix I where we measure the density of interest group
communities likely to be activated by each proposed rule
(Gray and Lowery, 1988; Lowery and Gray, 1993; Lowery,
Gray and Cluverius, 2015) and assess whether this affects

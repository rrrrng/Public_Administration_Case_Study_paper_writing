ABSTRACT
The personal background and traits of top organizational leaders matter for organizational strategies, policymaking, and out-
comes. Drawing on upper echelons theory, imprinting theory, and scholarship on managerial decision-­making and the transfer-
ability of private-­sector management approaches to the public sector, this study examines the relationship between US governors'
top business experience and budgetary outcomes. Competing hypotheses are proposed and tested to assess whether governors
with significant business experience enhance or hinder budgetary outcomes. Using a panel dataset of 48 states spanning 1960–
2010 and a regression discontinuity design, the analysis finds that electing governors with high-­level business experience leads to
improved budget equilibrium during their terms. These findings suggest that governors with business backgrounds are likely to
be better able to align revenues with expenditures, thus reducing deviations from the budget. The results are robust across alter-
native model specifications and offer critical theoretical and practical insights into leadership dynamics and fiscal governance.
1   |   Introduction
I was in the world of business for 25 years. If you didn't
balance your budget, you went out of business
—Mitt Romney, Former Governor of Massachusetts
(2011).
In recent years, the United States has witnessed a growing trend
of business leaders running for and winning high-­level politi-
cal office. Beyond the high-­profile example of Donald J. Trump,
numerous prominent business figures have successfully se-
cured state governorships. For instance, Rick Scott, founder of
the Hospital Corporation of America, served as Florida's 45th
governor (2011–2019). Doug Ducey, former CEO of Cold Stone
Creamery, held office as governor of Arizona (2015–2023). Kevin
Stitt, founder and CEO of Gateway Mortgage Group, became the
28th governor of Oklahoma in 2019.
Governors wield substantial influence over state budgeting
processes, including drafting executive budget proposals, ad-
vancing fiscal policies, and exercising line-­item veto powers
(Thurmaier and Willoughby 2001; Willoughby 2014; Yackee
and Yackee  2021). They assume office through diverse pro-
fessional trajectories—as career politicians, public adminis-
trators, attorneys, legislators, educators, entrepreneurs, and
corporate executives. Among these backgrounds, experience
as a chief executive officer (CEO) is qualitatively distinct.
Unlike other political or professional roles, the CEO position
entails occupying the highest organizational authority and
exercising ultimate decision-­making power over strategy, per-
sonnel, and resources. This distinctive imprint of top-­level
executive leadership may shape gubernatorial approaches
to budgeting, fostering habits of centralized control, fiscal

nomic development (Neumeier 2018; Szakonyi 2018), and pub-

to the link between high-­level business experience and fiscal
governance, leaving a significant gap in public management
research.
This study addresses this gap by examining the relationship
between US state governors' top business experience and state
government budgetary outcomes. Specifically, we focus on
CEO governors—those who served as major corporate execu-
tives or business owners prior to assuming office (Beach and
Jones 2016; Neumeier 2018). Drawing on upper echelons theory
and imprinting theory, we posit that leaders' professional back-
grounds shape their strategic orientations, cognitive frames,
and decision-­making styles, which in turn influence organiza-
tional outcomes (Hambrick 2007; Hambrick and Mason 1984;
Marquis and Tilcsik  2013). Extending this logic to the public
sector, we integrate insights from scholarship on managerial
decision-­making in public organizations and the transferability
of private-­sector management approaches to the public sector
to develop two competing hypotheses regarding the budget-
ary consequences of CEO leadership (Perry and Rainey 1988;
Petrovsky et al. 2014; Rainey and Chun 2009). On one hand,
their expertise in financial management and emphasis on fis-
cal discipline may enhance budgetary balance and long-­term
fiscal sustainability. On the other hand, the misalignment be-
tween private-­sector management logics and the political and
institutional complexities of public governance could generate
adverse budgetary consequences.
To empirically test these hypotheses, we employ a regression
discontinuity (RD) design using electoral and fiscal data from
48 US states between 1960 and 2010. The RD framework allows
us to estimate the causal effect of electing a CEO governor by
comparing narrowly winning CEO candidates to their closest
electoral rivals—non-­CEO candidates. Our results show that
the election of CEO governors, relative to non-­CEO governors, is
associated with improved budget equilibrium during their ten-
ures. Importantly, this RD design does not assess whether CEO
governors outperform all other types of governors. Rather, it iso-
lates the causal effect of private-­sector CEO experience relative
to a heterogeneous set of alternative leadership backgrounds.
This study makes three significant contributions to the pub-
lic management literature. First, this study extends Upper
Echelons theory and imprinting theory to the domain of fiscal
governance in US state governments by explicitly conceptu-
alizing governors' prior CEO experience as a proxy for dura-
ble cognitive frames and decision heuristics shaped through
top-­level private-­sector leadership. Second, it addresses calls
for further research on the influence of diverse leader charac-
teristics on government performance (C. N. Avellaneda 2008;
Opstrup and Villadsen 2015). By examining the effects of top
leaders' business backgrounds, this study expands the scope
of inquiry into how leadership experiences shape fiscal gov-
ernance. Third, the study advances understanding of how
leaders' personal backgrounds affect organizational outcomes
by using a RD design with five decades of longitudinal data,
reducing endogeneity concerns and strengthening causal
inference.
The remainder of this article is organized as follows. We begin
with a review of the literature and a theoretical foundation for
the relationship between CEO governors and state government
budgetary outcomes. Next, we describe our methodology and
data. Following this, we report the results of our analyses and
discuss their implications in the context of existing research and
policy relevance.

business experience improve state economic performance by in-

Russia's subnational governments enhance the revenues and net
profits of connected firms.

ganizations differ, executive leaders who transition from the
private sector often carry with them a managerial orientation
that emphasizes financial discipline, cost control, and per-

budgeting practices, particularly in efforts to maintain fiscal
balance and promote long-­term fiscal sustainability (Bozeman
and Rainey 1998; Rainey and Chun 2009). This study addresses
this gap by theorizing the relationship between CEO governors
and government budgetary outcomes and by systematically test-
ing this relationship using a comprehensive dataset of US state
governors.
3   |   Theoretical Analysis and Hypotheses
3.1   |   Why Leaders' Business Backgrounds Matter:
Upper Echelons and Imprinting Theories
Two theoretical perspectives—Upper Echelons theory (UET)
and imprinting theory—offer useful explanations for how the
individual backgrounds of public leaders shape organizational
outcomes. UET is fundamentally a behavioral and cognitive the-
ory of executive decision-­making (Hambrick and Mason 1984;
Hambrick 2007; Esteve et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2022). Its central
premise is that organizational outcomes reflect how top exec-
utives interpret their environments and make strategic choices
under conditions of bounded rationality, rather than the direct
effects of observable personal characteristics. Observable ex-
ecutive attributes—such as career experience—are therefore
treated as theoretically meaningful but imperfect proxies for
deeper, unobservable cognitive frames, values, and decision
heuristics.
From a UET perspective, prior CEO experience represents a par-
ticularly strong form of executive cognitive imprinting. Serving
as a top private-­sector executive repeatedly exposes individuals
to high-­stakes budget constraints, organization-­wide financial
tradeoffs, and survival-­oriented accountability for fiscal bal-
ance. These formative experiences cultivate durable cognitive
frames emphasizing revenue–expenditure alignment, cost con-
tainment, and tolerance for politically costly fiscal adjustments.
When individuals with such imprinted cognitive orientations
assume gubernatorial office, these frames shape how they inter-
pret fiscal signals, evaluate budgetary tradeoffs, and prioritize
competing objectives within the institutional constraints of state
government.
Imprinting theory further suggests that pivotal early-­career ex-
periences can leave lasting marks on leaders' cognitive frame-
works and decision-­making logics (Marquis and Tilcsik 2013).
These imprints shape future strategic orientations, adap-
tive learning, and capacity development (Simsek et  al.  2015).
Imprinting theory echoes with the literature on the comparison
between the working experience of public and private sectors,
which argues that the managerial style, cognitive patterns,
motivations, and decision-­making processes have distinctive
contrasts (Andersen 2010; Nutt 2006). Related empirical work
suggests that private-­sector experience may be transferred to the
public sector by shaping managers' cognitive frameworks, inter-
pretive schemas, and decision heuristics, which in turn influ-
ence organizational behavior (Andrews et al. 2021; Boardman
et  al.  2010; Chen  2012; Wiersma et  al.  2024). Consequently,
governors with CEO backgrounds are likely to carry cognitively
imprinted interpretive frames—formed through sustained ex-
posure to private-­sector executive environments—into their
public roles.
3.2   |   How Top Business Leadership Backgrounds
Influence Budgetary Outcomes
Building on these perspectives, research in public administra-
tion suggests that managerial backgrounds shape executives'
cognitive orientations toward fiscal preferences and decision-­
making (Boardman et  al.  2010; Rainey and Chun  2009). Yet
the transferability of business practices to the public sector
remains contested. While private firms emphasize efficiency,
profitability, and shareholder value, public organizations are
accountable to multiple constituencies and must balance effi-
ciency with equity, transparency, and service provision (Perry
and Rainey  1988; Petrovsky et  al.  2014). These institutional
differences suggest that top business experience may operate
as both an asset and a liability in public budgeting, depending
on how executive cognitive imprints interact with public-­sector

3.2.1   |   The Case for Positive Budgetary Impacts
of CEO Governors
Several mechanisms suggest that governors with CEO back-
grounds may improve budgetary outcomes. First, CEOs pos-
sess substantial financial management expertise. Unlike most
other career trajectories, CEOs have been directly responsible
for overseeing the financial health of entire organizations.
From a behavioral perspective, repeated exposure to such
responsibility fosters durable cognitive frames emphasizing
revenue–expenditure alignment, sensitivity to fiscal risk, and
prioritization of long-­term solvency over short-­term political
gains. These orientations shape how executives attend to fis-
cal information and evaluate budgetary tradeoffs. In addition,
CEOs typically develop expertise in corporate finance, stra-
tegic planning, and analytical tools such as forecasting and
cost–benefit analysis. When carried into public office, these
cognitive orientations and skills can support performance
budgeting and targeted spending reviews, thereby enhanc-
ing budgetary balance (Nutt 2006; Sun 2022; Whalley 2013;
Wiersma et al. 2024).
Second, CEOs demonstrate an ingrained commitment to fiscal
discipline. In the private sector, prolonged deficits threaten or-
ganizational survival, while excessive surpluses may signal inef-
ficient resource allocation or missed investment opportunities.
As ultimate decision-­makers, CEOs internalize habits of cost
control, efficiency, and accountability. These experiences fos-
ter a cognitive orientation in which deviations from budgetary
balance—whether deficits or large surpluses—are perceived
not merely as technical outcomes but as indicators of subopti-
mal financial management. As a result, executives with CEO
backgrounds may approach public budgeting with heightened
aversion to fiscal imbalance and stronger preferences for main-
taining budgetary equilibrium. These orientations may persist
in public office, leading to greater emphasis on deficit reduc-
tion, waste elimination, and balanced budgets (Jochimsen and
Thomasius 2014; Rainey and Bozeman 2000).
Third, CEO governors often display a distinctive capacity for
reform. Having risen to the top of private organizations, they
are accustomed to strategic change and decision-­making under
uncertainty. From a cognitive standpoint, these experiences
can increase tolerance for risk and willingness to pursue politi-
cally costly adjustments during fiscal stress. In addition, CEOs'
relative outsider status and weaker ties to entrenched political
networks may provide greater flexibility to pursue efficiency-­
oriented initiatives such as spending cuts, balanced budget re-
quirements, and management reforms. This reform capacity
reflects both private-­sector organizational imprinting and rela-
tive autonomy from traditional political obligations (Witko and
Friedman 2008).
Taken together, financial management experience, fiscal disci-
pline, and reform capacity should be understood not simply as
skills, but as manifestations of deeper cognitive and behavioral
imprints shaped by prior CEO experience. Consistent with UET,
these imprints shape how governors interpret fiscal constraints
and respond to budgetary challenges, suggesting CEO experi-
ence may improve budgetary outcomes (Hypothesis 1).
Hypothesis 1.  All else being equal, governors with prior CEO
experience have a positive impact on the budgetary outcomes of
state governments.
3.2.2   |   The Case for Negative Budgetary Impacts
of CEO Governors
Conversely, several mechanisms suggest that governors with
CEO experience may worsen budgetary outcomes. First, CEO
governors may exhibit growth-­oriented fiscal preferences.
Private-­sector environments often define success in terms of
expansion, investment, and market positioning. Prolonged ex-
posure to these settings can cultivate cognitive frames that em-
phasize growth over fiscal restraint. As a result, executives may
view tax cuts or large capital projects as engines of long-­term
growth. When applied in the public sector, such priorities can
lead to aggressive tax reductions or large-­scale development ini-
tiatives that impose substantial fiscal costs without generating
commensurate revenue, thereby widening deficits and straining
state budgets (Engen and Skinner 1996; Liu and Mikesell 2014;
Romer and Romer 2009; Szakonyi 2018).
Second, CEO governors may experience an organizational
mismatch when transitioning from business to government.
Corporate executives typically operate in hierarchical settings
characterized by centralized authority and rapid decision-­
making. These environments shape cognitive expectations
about control and speed. By contrast, public budgeting is gov-
erned by checks and balances and requires negotiation among
multiple actors. Decision-­making styles rooted in hierarchy and
unilateral authority may translate poorly into political contexts
that rely on bargaining and coalition-­building. This mismatch
can complicate fiscal negotiations and undermine effective fis-
cal governance (Boyne 2002; Bozeman and Bretschneider 1994;
Emmert and Crow  1988; Petrovsky et  al.  2014; Scott and
Falcone 1998).
Third, CEO governors display leadership styles that may mis-
align with the norms of democratic governance. Corporate envi-
ronments reward rapid decision-­making, hierarchical authority,
and results-­based accountability. From a behavioral standpoint,
repeated exposure to these settings can foster decision heuristics
that prioritize speed and output over deliberation and process
legitimacy. When applied in political settings, such heuristics
may increase conflict with legislatures and stakeholders, reduce
cooperation across institutions, and weaken fiscal policy imple-
mentation (Karaevli 2007).
Taken together, these mechanisms suggest that the cogni-
tive and behavioral imprints associated with CEO experi-
ence—while advantageous in private-­sector settings—may, in
public-­sector contexts, bias executives toward growth-­oriented
strategies, misinterpret institutional constraints, and rely on de-
cision heuristics poorly suited to democratic fiscal governance.
Under these conditions, CEO experience may worsen budgetary
outcomes (Hypothesis 2).

4   |   Methodology
4.1   |   Estimation Strategy
This study employs a regression discontinuity (RD) design, a
quasi-­experimental approach that estimates causal effects by
assigning treatment based on whether an observed variable
exceeds a predefined threshold (Lee and Lemieux 2010). This
design leverages discontinuities in treatment assignment—here,
the transition between business and non-­business candidates
in close elections—to estimate causal effects. The RD approach
addresses potential endogeneity biases, such as voters in poorly
performing states electing business candidates in hopes of im-
proving fiscal conditions, a limitation observed in prior research
(Claudia N. Avellaneda 2012).
Our analysis focuses on state-­level general elections where either
the winner is a businessperson (CEO) and the runner-­up is not, or
vice versa. Treatment is assigned at the candidate level, while the
unit of analysis is the state. In cases with multiple candidates in
the final election stage, only the first-­ and second-­place candidates
are considered. The treatment variable, electoral victory, is coded
as 1 if the winner is a businessperson and 0 otherwise. The forcing
variable is the vote margin, with a cutoff at zero. For CEO winners
(treated states), the vote margin is the positive difference in vote
share from the runner-­up. For CEO losers (control states), it is the
negative difference in vote share from the winner. This variable
ranges from −1 to 1. In close elections, the winner's identity can
be considered quasi-­random, enabling an effective random assign-
ment of businessperson candidates as governors.

(overspending) or surpluses (underutilization of resources). From
this perspective, budget equilibrium captures the extent to which
actual revenues and expenditures match, with smaller deviations
reflecting stronger fiscal discipline. Drawing on prior literature

better budget equilibrium, reflecting a government's ability to
effectively align revenues with expenditures. In this framework,
achieving high budget equilibrium represents a positive budget-
ary outcome, signaling prudent fiscal management and high ca-
pacity to sustain stable public finances.
Budget equilibrium is selected as the primary measure of state
governments' budgetary outcomes for several reasons. First,
a balanced budget serves as an intuitive and interpretable
benchmark. It reflects a government's commitment to fiscal
discipline and effective financial management by aligning
revenues and expenditures without relying on persistent defi-
cits or accumulating excessive surpluses. Second, large budget
deficits or surpluses are broadly unpopular with the public.
Persistent deficits may indicate overspending, leading to bor-
rowing and potential tax hikes, while excessive surpluses
may suggest over-­taxation or underinvestment in public ser-
vices, sparking negative reactions from constituents (Ball and
Mankiw  1995; Emrich  2023). Third, extreme budget imbal-
ances can have detrimental economic consequences. Large
deficits can harm a state's creditworthiness, increase borrow-
ing costs, and limit fiscal flexibility for future policymaking
(Gale and Orszag 2003). Similarly, large surpluses represent
inefficient use of public resources, reflecting missed opportu-
nities for public investment or tax reductions that could stimu-
late economic activity (Gore 2009). Finally, significant budget
imbalances—whether deficits or surpluses—are clear indi-
cators of fiscal inefficiency and mismanagement. By empha-
sizing budget equilibrium, this study captures two essential
dimensions of prudent fiscal governance: aligning revenues
with expenditures and maximizing the efficient utilization of
public resources.

out volatility and provides a clearer picture of the overall fiscal
governance of state executives. Second, the study prioritizes the
use of general deficits or surpluses, calculated as the difference
between general revenues and general expenditures. These gen-
eral budget metrics focus on tax and intergovernmental revenues

4.4   |   Treatment Variable—Governors With
Business CEO Experience
The treatment variable is coded as 1 if the elected governor in
a given year meets the criteria for CEO experience and 0 oth-
erwise. To meet the requirements of the RD design, the same
criteria are applied to the gubernatorial election opponent. If the
runner-­up possesses CEO experience, the variable Opponents'
CEO Experience is coded as 1 and 0 otherwise.
Drawing on the definitions established by Beach and Jones (2016)
and Neumeier (2018), a CEO governor is defined as a state governor
who meets one of two specific criteria based on their professional
experience before taking office. The first criterion includes indi-
viduals who held senior executive roles (president, vice president,
member of an executive board, other high-­ranking managerial
roles) in the private sector, such as Ed Schafer, former president of
Gold Seal Company and Governor of North Dakota (1992–2000);
Deval Patrick, executive vice president at Coca-­Cola (2001–2004)
and Governor of Massachusetts (2007–2015); Jon Corzine, CFO of
Goldman Sachs in the 1990s and Governor of New Jersey (2006–
2010). The second criterion includes those who founded or owned
private businesses, such as Asa Hutchinson, founder of a consult-
ing firm and Governor of Arkansas (2015–present), and Sonny
Perdue, founder of an agricultural trading company and Governor
of Georgia (2003–2010). It should be noted that self-­employed pri-
vate jobs, like private attorneys, pharmacists, physicians, as well as
pure farmers or farm owners without any adjacent business, are
excluded from our study sample.
4.5   |   Control Variables
Drawing on the existing literature, this study incorporates con-
trol variables related to governors' characteristics, state economic
conditions, and the political context (C. N. Avellaneda  2008;
Claudia N. Avellaneda  2012; Jochimsen and Thomasius  2014;
Neumeier  2018; Szakonyi  2018). Governor characteristics in-
clude gender, age, education, tenure, political experience, and
party affiliation. State economic conditions are measured by
real income per capita growth, income inequality (Gini Index),
population growth, and population density. The political context
is captured by divided government (whether legislative houses
are controlled by different parties), the political competition
index, citizen liberal ideology, and the percentage of state legis-
lators aligned to governors' party. Finally, we control for the pre-­
election budget equilibrium index and the pre-­election level of
total outstanding debt (real per capita). These controls account
for the fiscal baseline inherited by each governor, allowing us to
isolate the effects of gubernatorial characteristics from preexist-
ing budgetary conditions.
4.6   |   Data Collection
This study uses state-­year observations as the unit of analysis.
Data on state budgetary outcomes and socioeconomic conditions
were obtained from the US Census Bureau's Annual Survey of
State Government Finance, the US Bureau of Economic Analysis,
and the US Bureau of Labor Statistics. Governors' personal back-

5   |   Empirical Findings
5.1   |   An Overview of CEO Governors in the US
Between 1960 and 2010, 479 governors served in 48 states.
Of these, 50 governors (10%) had prior CEO experience. New
Hampshire had the most CEO governors and highest percent-
age of CEO governors (5% and 45%). Table 2 shows the num-
ber of CEO governors by state. Figure 1 visualizes these totals.
Appendix Table A.1 lists the names of all CEO governors. The
frequency of CEO governors varied widely across states.
5.2   |   Assessing the Validity of the RD Design
The validity of the regression discontinuity (RD) design relies on
two key assumptions (Lee and Lemieux 2010). The first assump-
tion is that the treatment assignment, or forcing variable, cannot
be manipulated. In the context of gubernatorial elections, this is
highly unlikely due to systemic safeguards preventing candidates
from directly influencing voter choices. To evaluate this assump-
tion, we use graphical analysis and the McCrary test. Figure 2
displays a histogram of victory margins. It shows a concentration
of elections near the cutoff point (0), with close wins and losses
for businessperson candidates occurring in roughly equal num-
bers. Figure  3 illustrates a discontinuous density function, re-
vealing no statistically significant difference in density on either
side of the threshold. A formal McCrary (2008) test supports this

The second key assumption of the RD design is the local balance
of baseline covariates on either side of the assignment threshold.
If the RD design is valid, there should be no discontinuities in co-

Table 3, indicate a high degree of local balance around the assign-

deficit relative to total expenditures, with smaller imbalances in-
dicating stronger alignment between revenues and expenditures.
While this measure captures fiscal discipline and stability, it does
not directly assess broader efficiency in terms of service quality
or cost-­effectiveness. Our findings therefore highlight the fiscal
effects of electing CEO governors on budgetary balance, while
underscoring the need for future research to examine whether
such improvements in fiscal balance come at the expense of non-­
fiscal performance outcomes.
Achieving values close to 1 on the general budget equilibrium
index—scaled by expenditures—signifies effective fiscal man-
agement, characterized by prudent governance that minimizes
deficits without producing excessive surpluses. A value near
1 under this formulation indicates a close alignment between
revenues and expenditures, reflecting both fiscal discipline and
efficient use of public resources. The graphical evidence reveals
a significant association between CEO governors and higher
expenditure-­scaled budget equilibrium during their terms. This
provides preliminary causal evidence that top business-­oriented
leadership enhances state budgetary outcomes.
To quantify the size of this jump more precisely, we turn to re-
gression analysis. Table  4 summarizes the regression results,
testing whether CEO governors influence state general budget
equilibrium. The table presents results from both simple OLS
models and causal RDD specifications, providing a comprehen-
sive view of the relationship. Models 1 and 2 present the simple
OLS results for the full sample, serving as a benchmark for the
RDD estimates. In Model 1, the results indicate that state gen-
eral budget equilibrium improves when a CEO governor takes
office. Model 2 adds control variables, including state-­level and
candidate-­level predictors, pre-­election budget equilibrium and
outstanding debt, as well as year and state-­fixed effects. While
the inclusion of these controls reduces the effect size, the result
remains statistically significant, affirming the positive relation-
ship between CEO governors and general budget equilibrium.
Model 3, limited to elections decided within ±5%, shows a posi-
tive coefficient for CEO governors, though reduced sample size
weakens significance beyond the 10% level. While these OLS
models do not provide causal evidence, they clearly establish a
strong correlation between CEO governors and improvements
in general budget equilibrium.
Models 4 to 6 employ RDD specifications to provide causal es-
timates. Model 4 narrows the bandwidth to 2.5% using a local
linear control function. Model 5 narrows the bandwidth to 5%
using a local linear control function, while Model 6 widens
the bandwidth to 9.6% using an optimal bandwidth selector
(Calonico et al. 2020). Across both RDD specifications, the re-
sults consistently show a positive and statistically significant
effect of CEO governors on general budget equilibrium index.
These results confirm that states led by CEO governors experi-
ence significant increases in budget equilibrium during their
terms. The causal evidence strongly supports the hypothesis
that electing governors with high-­level business experience
leads to measurable improvements in state budget equilib-
rium. To assess the magnitude of this effect, the estimated
treatment effect of 0.026 in Model 6 (optimal bandwidth)
corresponds to approximately 0.89 standard deviations of the
General Budget Equilibrium Index. Values closer to 1 indicate
stronger budget equilibrium, reflecting a government's abil-
ity to align revenues with expenditures and avoid large fis-
cal deviations. Substantively, this represents a reduction in
the average budget gap by about 58%, moving a state from the
sample mean (0.954) toward the upper end of the distribution
(0.980). In practical terms, this shift reflects a meaningful im-
provement in fiscal balance when a CEO governor is elected,
underscoring that the impact of CEO experience is not only
statistically significant but also substantively consequential
for state budgetary outcomes.

5.4.4   |   Alternative Model Specifications
We re-­estimated the RD models using narrower bandwidths
(±2% to ±7.5%), local-­quadratic and cubic polynomials, alterna-
tive kernel functions (Epanechnikov, uniform), and coverage-­
error–optimal bandwidth selectors. Across all variations, results
remain positive and statistically significant, though precision
naturally declines under the narrowest bandwidths. We also
estimated yearly dynamic effects to capture fiscal trajectories
across gubernatorial terms. Results show consistent positive
effects that peak 2–3 years after a CEO governor's election, sug-
gesting that improvements in budget equilibrium are not only
robust but also persistent over time (Appendix Tables F.1–F.5).
5.4.5   |   Sample Restrictions
We restricted the sample to four-­year gubernatorial term states
(excluding Vermont and New Hampshire). The estimated CEO
governor effect remains positive and statistically significant.
Furthermore, we conducted a split-­sample analysis by time
period. Results indicate weaker and statistically insignificant
effects during 1960–1980 but robust and significant effects
during 1981–2010, suggesting that the influence of CEO ex-
perience on fiscal outcomes has grown more pronounced over
time as private-­sector management practices became more in-
stitutionalized in public budgeting (Appendix Tables G.1–G.2).
5.4.6   |   Alternative Leadership Backgrounds
We examine whether governors with alternative leadership
backgrounds—specifically, top-­level public manager experience
or career politician experience—exhibit similar budget equi-
librium outcomes using panel two-­way fixed effects models.
Separate RD designs for these alternative leadership pathways
are not feasible with the available data because disaggregating
close elections by leadership background yields too few observa-
tions within each subgroup to support credible causal inference.
The results indicate that neither top-­level public manager back-
grounds nor career politician backgrounds are systematically
associated with improvements in general or total budget equi-
librium indices once state and year fixed effects (and covariates)
are included (Appendix Tables H.1–H.2).

6   |   Discussions and Conclusion
Drawing on Upper Echelons theory (UET), imprinting theory,
and scholarship on managerial decision-­making in public orga-
nizations and the transferability of private-­sector management
approaches to the public sector, this study investigates the causal
relationship between US governors' high-­level business experi-
ence and state budgetary outcomes. Using a quasi-­experimental
RD design and analyzing electoral and fiscal data from 48 states
over a 50-­year period, the findings demonstrate that electing
governors with significant business experience is associated
with improved budget equilibrium. Specifically, these governors
enhance the alignment between revenues and expenditures, re-
ducing both budget deficits and excessive surpluses. Importantly,
our empirical design estimates the effect of electing a governor
with prior CEO experience relative to a heterogeneous set of
non-­CEO candidates, rather than establishing that CEO gov-
ernors outperform all other leadership types. While the results
highlight CEO experience as one empirically identifiable and
theoretically meaningful pathway to budgetary equilibrium, they
do not preclude the possibility that other professional trajectories
may generate similar fiscal outcomes through different cognitive
or institutional mechanisms.
This research offers several important theoretical contributions.
First, this study extends UET within public administration by
moving beyond organizational collaboration to the domain of
fiscal governance. Building on Esteve et  al.  (2013)—the foun-
dational study that introduced UET to public administration by
demonstrating that observable executive background character-
istics serve as proxies for deeper cognitive and behavioral differ-
ences shaping collaboration—we advance this line of research to
a substantively different strategic domain. Budgetary decision-­
making involves distinct cognitive challenges, including reve-
nue–expenditure tradeoffs, intertemporal considerations, and
politically salient fiscal constraints. Our findings suggest that
executive cognitive imprinting continues to shape organizational
fiscal outcomes.
Second, this study contributes to imprinting theory and scholar-
ship on managerial decision-­making in public organizations by
refining debates on the transferability of private-­sector manage-
ment to government. Rather than assuming the direct transfer
of private-­sector practices, our findings show that what persists
across sectors are executive cognitive orientations—such as
heightened attention to fiscal balance, cost containment, and
revenue–expenditure alignment. This distinction helps reconcile
mixed findings in prior research on private-­sector experience
in government and clarifies how executive cognition operates
under public-­sector institutional constraints.
Third, this study advances scholarship on public management
and fiscal governance by clarifying how leadership traits shape
fiscal outcomes through executive cognition rather than demo-
graphic effects per se. Consistent with UET, we interpret prior
CEO experience not as a direct causal attribute, but as a theo-
retically grounded proxy for unobservable cognitive frames and
decision heuristics that guide executive fiscal decisions. Because
direct measures of governors' attitudes or cognitive orientations
are unavailable, the results should be interpreted as consistent
with theorized behavioral mechanisms rather than as definitive
tests of specific psychological traits. In this way, the study re-
sponds to calls to open the “black box” of executive decision-­
making in public administration.
In addition to its theoretical contributions, this study offers
practical insights for policymakers, voters, and public admin-
istrators. The findings highlight the potential benefits of leader-
ship diversity and suggest that professional experience from the
private sector can add value to public leadership roles. Business
leaders with strong financial management skills and the capac-
ity to adapt to political and institutional constraints may be espe-
cially well-­suited to navigating fiscal challenges in government.
Yet such adaptability is essential: training and support in areas
such as equity, transparency, stakeholder engagement, and long-­
term service delivery are critical to ensuring that private-­sector
approaches are effectively integrated into the public sector. At
the same time, although CEO governors are associated with im-
proved budget equilibrium, fiscal balance represents only one
dimension of government performance. Policymakers and citi-
zens must weigh fiscal discipline alongside other objectives, in-
cluding service quality, equity, and institutional resilience.
This study also makes a methodological contribution by employ-
ing a quasi-­experimental RD design to identify the causal effects
of electing governors with prior CEO experience. Unlike much of
the existing literature on public officials' characteristics, which re-
lies primarily on correlational or descriptive analyses (e.g., Esteve
et al. 2013), this approach strengthens internal validity by exploit-
ing close gubernatorial elections as a plausibly exogenous source
of variation. The RD design therefore provides credible causal
evidence linking CEOs' professional backgrounds to state budget
equilibrium.
Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that
open promising avenues for future research. First, the RD de-
sign restricts external validity because it applies primarily to
close elections. While this approach strengthens causal identi-
fication, it limits generalizability to broader political contexts.
Future studies could examine whether the positive effects of
CEO backgrounds persist under different electoral margins, po-
litical institutions, or partisan environments.
Second, this study focuses on budget equilibrium as the primary
fiscal outcome. While equilibrium is an important measure
of fiscal balance, it does not fully capture other critical finan-
cial dimensions such as cash solvency, debt sustainability, or
long-­term liabilities. Nor does it reflect non-­fiscal dimensions
of governance—such as education, public safety, or human re-
sources—that are equally central to government performance.
Future research could build on our framework by incorporating
a wider set of fiscal and non-­fiscal indicators to provide a more
comprehensive picture of gubernatorial performance.
Third, data limitations prevent us from developing a fully de-
tailed typology of business experience. Our binary measure can-
not capture variation in the duration of business careers or the
sectoral specificity of prior executive roles. It is plausible that
both the length of private-­sector leadership and the industry
context (e.g., finance, healthcare, manufacturing) shape how
skills transfer to public governance. Incorporating more gran-
ular measures of the scope and nature of business backgrounds

would clarify whether some forms of executive experience are
more relevant to fiscal governance than others.
Fourth, this study does not provide head-­to-­head causal com-
parisons between CEO governors and other leadership trajec-
tories, such as career politicians, top-­level public managers, or
political appointees. Future research could examine whether
alternative executive backgrounds generate comparable fis-
cal outcomes through distinct cognitive frames or decision-­
making logics. Such extensions would help clarify whether
CEO experience represents a unique imprinting pathway or
one of several routes through which executive cognition shapes
fiscal governance.
Finally, this study does not capture the potential influence of
state budget directors, who are often appointed by governors
and play central roles in state budgetary processes (Lee and
Burns 2003). The expertise, preferences, and leadership styles of
budget directors may mediate or amplify the effects of guberna-
torial backgrounds on fiscal outcomes. Future research drawing
on archival records and biographical sources could illuminate
the governor–budget director relationship as a key mechanism
shaping state fiscal performance.
In conclusion, this study contributes to the literature on public
sector executive leadership by providing robust evidence that
CEO governors improve budget equilibrium. By linking leader-
ship traits to fiscal governance outcomes, it bridges the divide
between private-­sector and public-­sector research, demonstrat-
ing how business principles can be adapted to public adminis-
tration. These findings lay the foundation for future inquiry into
the role of professional diversity in government leadership and
offer practical insights for voters, policymakers, and public ad-
ministrators seeking to enhance government performance.

ABSTRACT
Local government capacity gaps are primary barriers to implementing climate adaptation and disaster resilience projects. While
over 85% of US municipalities have fewer than 10,000 people, the extent to which capacity restricts adaptation in rural govern-
ments is not understood. This research addresses that gap by developing a national Rural Capacity Index using indicators that
highlight challenges disproportionately faced by rural places. When results are mapped, the Midwest stands out for lacking
capacity at the county, county subdivision, and community scales. The index is then shown to have a positive relationship with
success in securing competitive federal grants. There is an estimated 4% increase in the amount of funding awarded for every 1%
increase in the index's percentile rank. The Rural Capacity Index provides scholars, practitioners, and policymakers a practical
tool to identify and respond to disparities in local government capacity that constrain rural disaster resilience efforts.
1   |   Introduction
Rural local governments are at a critical juncture. The rising
frequency and intensity of disasters—such as wildfires and
floods—are generating new fiscal pressures and underscoring
the need for greater investment in mitigation and adaptation
(e.g., Liao and Kousky 2022; Miao et al. 2023). Yet, a growing
body of scholarship highlights that many rural governments lack
the capacity to meet the demands of an uncertain future shaped
by climate change and economic volatility (Deslatte et al. 2023;
Helpap 2023; Fila et al. 2023). Compounding these challenges,
many federal programs have historically underserved rural re-
gions (Eisenberg 2020; Gansauer 2024; Muro 2021; Sitaraman
et  al.  2021), raising concerns that these communities may be
overlooked in future investments in climate resilience, disaster
preparedness, and infrastructure. Local government capacity
may ultimately determine whether rural regions are meaning-
fully included in nationwide resilience efforts or are left behind.
In response, this research poses two overarching questions:
Where in the United States are rural government capacity gaps
most acute? And what is the relationship between local gov-
ernment capacity and the ability to access competitive federal
funding? To answer these questions, the researchers developed
a nationwide Rural Capacity Index (RCI) to identify communi-
ties in the United States that will most likely need additional
support, technical assistance, and resources to address climate
risks. As noted by Hall (2008b, 110), capacity is a vague concept
and should be based on the ability “to do something specific.”
For this research, local government capacity is defined as the
ability of local governments to plan, fund, implement, and main-
tain the infrastructure and projects needed to reduce risks from
climate-­related hazards.
This research makes three key contributions. First, it provides
a novel way to measure government capacity nationwide and
highlights constraints to capacity that have disproportionate

2   |   Theory
2.1   |   Government Capacity and Climate Resilience
Government capacity gaps are primary barriers to implement-
ing effective climate adaptation and mitigation projects, as
well as preparing for the overall unpredictability associated
with climate change (Arkin 2023; Williams et al. 2020; Pörtner
et al. 2022). In the United States, local governments are “the first
line of defense” in disasters (FEMA 2003, 34), bearing primary
responsibility for response, recovery, and mitigation. Yet, their
ability to do so varies widely and is often inadequate (Deslatte
et al. 2023; Martin et al. 2023). As climate hazards become more
frequent and severe, local governments will require greater flex-
ibility and capacity to respond and adapt.
2.2   |   Measuring Capacity
Capacity is an elusive, multi-­faceted concept. Decades of re-
search, particularly within the public administration field,
have examined its relationship to government performance,
ranging from the ability to achieve federal policy goals and/
or operate efficiently to managing crises (e.g., Bowman
and Kearney 1988; Carley et al. 2015; Hall 2008a; Rice and
Sumberg 1997; Smith et al. 2019). For example, research has
demonstrated that higher-­capacity governments are more
likely to initiate and sustain economic development activ-
ity (Alexander et  al.  2024; Hall  2008a), sustainability proj-
ects (Hawkins et  al.  2023), and infrastructure (Freemark
et al. 2023). Yet, the methodologies and definitions of capacity
vary across the literature. Quantitative studies often rely on
staffing or fiscal indicators, normalized by population (e.g.,
Collins and Gerber  2006; Terman and Feiock  2014, 2015;
Shybalkina  2023), while other scholars use administrative
proxies, such as the presence of sustainability offices or
adoption of fiscal best practices (Carley et al. 2015; Hawkins
et al. 2023). Some researchers emphasize the role of geographic
context, with rural governments generally having lower capac-
ity than urban counterparts (e.g., Smith and Hernandez 2023;
Pohl et al. 2023; Junod 2024). In climate governance, adaptive
capacity frameworks often take a broader, multi-­dimensional
conception of capacity, frequently extending beyond local
governments to include a wider range of decision-­makers and
often incorporating participatory methods to develop context-­
specific models (e.g., Williams et al. 2020; Máñez et al. 2014;
Jones et al. 2010). These varying approaches underscore both
the centrality of capacity to climate resilience and the per-
sistent challenge of developing consistent, scalable measures.
2.3   |   Capacity as a Missing Dimension of Equity
Capacity plays a critical but often overlooked role in equitable
climate resilience, particularly for its role in resource allocation.
Capacity affects the ability of local governments to secure ex-
ternal funding, including federal grants that are key funding
for disaster preparedness and mitigation (FEMA 2024). Studies
repeatedly show that governments with more staff and stron-
ger finances receive more external funding (e.g., Hall  2008a;
Collins and Gerber 2006; Shybalkina 2023), and limited capacity
can prevent under-­resourced communities from accessing crit-
ical funds.
Further, local governments play a central role in addressing
(or exacerbating) social vulnerabilities and exposure to cli-
mate risks at the community level. Yet, within the climate
adaptation scholarship, social vulnerability and adaptive ca-
pacity are not synonymous, and indicators included in their
measurement are often different (e.g., Cutter  2024; Gupta
et  al.  2010; Siders  2019). As a result, policies and funding
aimed at addressing social vulnerability may not address
government capacity gaps, and vice versa. For example, fed-
eral equity tools like CDC's Social Vulnerability Index, EPA's
EJ Screen, FEMA's National Risk Index, and CEQ's Climate
and Economic Justice Screening Tool do not account for ca-
pacity, despite its crucial role in addressing vulnerabilities
and advancing equitable outcomes. Recent research (Karanja
et al. 2025) underscores this gap: high-­capacity communities
are 14 times more likely to be designated as FEMA Community
Disaster Resilience Zones than low-­capacity communities
and seven times more likely to be identified by the Climate
and Economic Justice Screening Tool. These findings suggest
that national vulnerability tools underrepresent need in low-­
capacity places.

(Helpap 2023), despite rural communities playing critical roles
in the US economy, social fabric, land management, and cli-
mate resilience efforts. More than 85% of incorporated US mu-
nicipalities have populations under 10,000 people (US Census
Bureau 2022), and rural areas account for 97% of the coun-
try's land base (USDA ERS 2022). When rural communities

Small municipalities and rural governments have unique con-

limited institutional capacity and financial resources (Hales

specialized economies, making their revenue streams more
volatile and susceptible to disruptions (Haggerty 2020). They
also often lack the financial resources and technical expertise
required to pursue competitive grants, meet program criteria,
or provide matching dollars for external funding (Gansauer
et  al.  2023; Smith and Hernandez  2023). A national survey
by Lobao and Kelly (2019) found that rural governments are
less likely to have economic development professionals or
grant writers on staff—roles essential for accessing external
funding. The shift toward a more competitive, project-­based
funding model at the federal level has exacerbated these dis-
parities, potentially reinforcing regional inequalities (Stern
and Hall 2015).
Further, rural communities face disproportionate climate
risks and impacts (e.g., Jurjonas and Seekamp 2018), with di-
sasters often leading to prolonged power and communication
outages and repairs taking longer than in urban areas (Hales
et al. 2014). As climate events increase in frequency and severity
(Jay et al. 2023), rural governments need new strategies to adapt
(Siders 2019; Jones et al. 2010). State and federal policymakers
must also ensure that rural communities have the resources
and capacity needed to prepare and respond to climate change.
However, as discussed below, long-­standing gaps and quality
issues with rural data prevent practitioners and policymakers
from fully identifying and addressing capacity barriers in rural
regions.
3.1   |   Challenges in Measuring Rural Government
Capacity
Rural datasets are limited and incomplete, complicat-
ing efforts to assess rural needs and inform policy (Scally
et al. 2020). At the federal scale, agencies define “rural” differ-
ently (Rembert and Blatchly 2024), complicating cross-­agency
data comparisons and coordination. Moreover, research gen-
erated in rural communities lags behind other geographies.
For instance, in 2019, only 25% of rural health departments
participated in a research study, compared to 83% of urban
departments (NACCHO 2019). Given data constraints, many
national studies of government capacity exclude rural govern-
ments altogether.
Even when rural communities are included, common capacity
measures often misrepresent their capacity. For instance, staff-­
per-­capita—a standard proxy for capacity—fails in rural contexts
where governments may have more staff per capita for basic ser-
vices, such as road maintenance, but lack specialized positions
like planners or grant writers (e.g., Lobao and Kelly  2019).
Other indicators, such as the number of nonprofits, are meant
to indicate higher capacity but in rural geographies may signal
government deficiencies or be capacity-­depleting. For example,
Winchester (2022) found that 1 in 11 people in Montana's most
rural communities must volunteer to fulfill nonprofit board de-
mands, compared to 1 in 18 for the state's more urban areas,
raising the risk of rural leadership burnout. These rural-­specific
dynamics are often recognized by practitioners but rarely ad-
dressed in academic literature.
Due to poor data quality, most research on rural local gov-
ernments relies on localized case surveys and/or state-­specific
analyses (e.g., Hall 2008a; Larson et al. 2017). These studies
have resulted in rich, geographically specific insights but
fail to demonstrate national trends, gaps, and needs. In na-
tional studies that do include rural places (e.g., Lobao and
Kelly 2019), data availability constrains the ability to research
rural governments at scales smaller than the county level.
Consequently, little is understood about the capacity barri-
ers and needs of rural cities, townships, and unincorporated
places. In sum, long-­standing gaps in rural data and research
challenge our ability to assess the needs of rural communi-
ties in the face of climate change. The RCI seeks to address
this gap.
4   |   Materials and Methods
4.1   |   Part 1: Creating the Rural Capacity Index
(RCI)
The RCI quantifies local government capacity across US counties,
county subdivisions, and census-­designated places. Its frame-
work draws on a literature review, a practitioner survey, and ex-
pert input from local officials and scholars. The researchers also
reviewed existing place-­based vulnerability indices, including the
CDC's Social Vulnerability Index, CEQ's Climate and Economic
Justice Tool, and FEMA's National Risk Index and Resilience
Analysis and Planning Tool. Together, these processes identified
48 potential indicators of rural capacity and need.
Following best practices for index design (Mazziotta and
Pareto 2017; OECD 2008; Oliveira et al. 2022), the RCI's de-
velopment prioritized simplicity, interpretability, replicability,
and transparency. The following criteria narrowed the list to
12 indicators:
•	 Data are nationally available.
•	 Datasets that are public and consistently updated were
prioritized.
•	 Data available at the county, county subdivision, and
census-­designated place scales were prioritized.
•	 Highly correlated variables were removed (e.g., median in-
come was dropped due to overlap with broadband access).
The final 12 indicators span four categories: (1) local govern-
ment staff and expertise, (2) institutional capacity, (3) economic
opportunity, and (4) education and engagement. One proprietary

4.2   |   Local Government Staff and Expertise
This category includes changes in government staff over time, the
presence of a planner, and metropolitan status, which can influ-
ence access to expertise, resources, and networks. Government
jobs help stabilize many rural economies, and employment
losses can undermine service delivery, infrastructure mainte-
nance, and disaster preparedness (Hall  2008a; Rodden  2024;
Shybalkina 2023). Rather than staff per capita—a poor metric
for rural places—this study instead uses the presence of a land-­
use planner to capture administrative capacity. Planners support
innovation, complex governance, and multi-­level coordination,
especially during times of crisis (Anthony Jr 2024; Göçmen and
LaGro 2016; Tewdwr-­Jones 2002; UN-­Habitat. 2022).
4.3   |   Institutional Capacity
This category includes isolation and access to institutional part-
ners, such as colleges or universities that anchor economies and
support collaborative governance. Remote regions are institu-
tionally thin (Kraenzel 1955; Amin and Thrift 1994; Fikri 2024),
limiting opportunities for regional collaboration. These areas
also often face higher infrastructure costs per capita due to dis-
tance and low population density—the “social cost of space”
(Kraenzel  1955). In contrast, anchor institutions like colleges
and universities can enhance capacity by attracting newcomers,
spurring innovation, and stabilizing local economies (Harker
et al. 2022). Drive time to a city of 50,000+ residents serves as a
proxy for isolation.
4.4   |   Economic Opportunity
This category includes population change, income stability,
poverty levels, and broadband access. These indicators reflect
socioeconomic conditions that affect economic possibilities
and public revenue (Fikri 2021, 2024). Because reliable national
data on local government finances are lacking, proxies are used
to signal fiscal health and revenue predictability—both critical
for delivering services and making long-­term decisions (Riddle
et al. 2021; Alexander et al. 2024). The share of families above
the poverty level reflects both community need and tax base
(Fikri 2024), while broadband access is a known driver of rural
economic growth (Weinstein et al. 2024). Strong public finances
enable governments to invest in staff, partnerships, and technol-
ogy that build capacity.
4.5   |   Education and Engagement
Since government capacity depends on both resources and local
needs (Hall  2008b), this category captures social and struc-
tural characteristics that shape service delivery and citizen
engagement. Educational attainment and health insurance
coverage are proxies for social vulnerability (Cutter  2024),
both of which are more acute in rural geographies (e.g., Byun
et al. 2012; Nielsen et al. 2017). Local governments serving more
vulnerable populations tend to focus on basic needs, potentially
limiting proactive planning for climate resilience. Voter turnout
reflects political capital and broader civic involvement (Flora
et al. 2016), which can improve government accountability and
ensure more equitable decision-­making (Holland 2017). Where
engagement is low, governments may be less responsive and/or
fail to implement effective solutions.
4.6   |   Quantifying and Weighting the Index
Each indicator was oriented to positively relate to capacity—
higher values reflect greater capacity. All indicators were normal-
ized on a 0–1 scale. Data preparation involved addressing missing
values, which mostly stemmed from new or changing geographies
(e.g., counties splitting or merging). Missing values were rare and
replaced with the national median to minimize bias.
To weight the index, expert input was collected via a sur-
vey emailed to 7,000+ members of the Association of State
Floodplain Managers (ASFPM), a national organization focused
on disaster resilience. Respondents rated how strongly each RCI
category influences a community's ability to plan, fund, imple-
ment, and maintain infrastructure that reduces climate-­related
risks. The survey received 250 responses, with more than 63% of
respondents working in government roles (Table 1).
Category weights were based on the survey's mean rating for
each category, relative to the lowest-­rated category. For instance,
“local government staff and expertise” scored 3.73, compared to
3.34 for “education and engagement,” resulting in a weight of
1.12. Table 2 details the 12 indicators, their sources, and weights.

townships), and communities (including incorporated places

4.8   |   Part 2: Validating the Rural Capacity Index
Composite indices help quantify complex, abstract concepts like
capacity. Given capacity's multidimensional nature and the ab-
sence of a definitive measure, a multi-­faceted and iterative val-
idation approach is essential. This study follows best practices
from the composite index literature (e.g., OECD  2008; Greco
et al. 2019), ensuring the RCI is both conceptually sound and
practically useful.
As detailed above, the RCI is grounded in a theoretical frame-
work developed through an extensive literature review and
expert input. Statistical tests confirmed internal reliability,
with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.74 calculated from 12 items. An
uncertainty analysis compared the final weighting scheme to
an equal-­weighted version, yielding a median score difference
of 3.0%, which indicates robustness to alternative assumptions.
For external validation, the RCI's predictive ability was tested
by assessing its relationship to federal grant success—the meth-
odology of which is outlined below. To evaluate model robust-
ness, the comparative analysis was repeated using different
sets of control variables as random effects (Appendix A). While
securing federal funding reflects only one dimension of capac-
ity and the RCI conceptualizes capacity more broadly, this ap-
proach to validation assesses the index's predictive ability and
relevance to grant success, a measurable outcome of capacity.
Validation is an ongoing process that benefits from continued em-
pirical testing and stakeholder engagement (OECD 2008; Burgass
et al. 2017). By publishing the RCI online and including a visual-
ization of the results, this study invites the broader scientific com-
munity to collaborate in refining and improving the index.
4.9   |   Evaluating the Predictive Capacity of the RCI
After developing the RCI, the researchers investigated the rela-
tionship between local government capacity and the ability to
secure competitive federal funding. The researchers designed
a binary logistic regression model to examine the relationship
between the county RCI percentile rank and success in secur-
ing competitive federal grant awards by all local governments
within the county between fiscal years 2020–2023 (n = 3143).
Data on competitive federal grant awards were obtained from
USASp​ending.​gov and adjusted for inflation to 2023 dollars.
Awards were filtered to include only competitive grants and
loans with positive “federal action obligation” or “face value of
loan” amounts and with “award base action dates” from fiscal
years 2020 to 2023. Totals were calculated by summing all rele-
vant awards to local governments within each county over the 4-­
year period. County codes were then used to link the data to the
Rural Capacity Index (RCI). Data prior to 2020 were excluded
due to data gaps in USASp​ending.​gov.
The total award amounts were used rather than a per cap-
ita measurement because of the focus on rural communities.
Projects can be more expensive per capita in rural regions due
to large geographic areas and low population densities, and thus
using per capita measurements could skew results. Additional
analyses, which are described in the following paragraphs, were
conducted to test the degree to which the county RCI explained
variation in access to competitive grant funding when compared
to population size alone.

6   |   Discussion
Government capacity is a key driver of successful climate ad-
aptation and resilience efforts, but rural geographies are often
overlooked in the scholarship. This research addresses rural
data gaps by developing a national Rural Capacity Index (RCI)
using indicators that highlight challenges and capacity barri-
ers disproportionately faced by rural places. The RCI provides
a novel framework for assessing rural governments' ability to
adapt to and/or mitigate climate risks across the United States.
6.1   |   A Capacity Index That Portrays Rural
Government Needs
The RCI builds on established capacity and vulnerability in-
dicators, such as poverty rates, local government staffing,
and institutional partnerships, but adapts them to better
­reflect rural challenges. For instance, instead of measuring
capacity as government staff per capita, which can be higher
in rural places, it measures local changes in government
jobs over time and whether the local government has a plan-
ner, an ­indicator of having more specialized administrative
capacity. Additionally, the RCI incorporates often-­overlooked
factors like economic volatility and geographic remoteness,
which are significant constraints on rural capacity. As a result,
the RCI identifies rural governments most in need of technical
assistance and resources to mitigate or adapt to climate risks.
The research findings demonstrate that large portions of the
rural United States have limited capacity. Local government
capacity gaps are most acute in the Midwest followed by the
Interior West, Gulf Coast, and Southeast. Capacity is high-
est in the Northeast followed by the Pacific Coast and Great
Lakes regions. These results align with prior research on in-
stitutional thinness and regional disinvestment, especially in
remote areas (e.g., Gansauer and Haggerty 2021; Smith 2020;
Jacquet et al. 2017). The RCI contributes to ongoing discussions
on geographic inequality by demonstrating that local govern-
ment capacity varies regionally and remains an underexplored
dimension in the scholarship.
Importantly, the RCI captures a dimension of social vulnerabil-
ity—local government capacity—that is often not included in
research, policy discussions, and national screening tools such
as the CDC's Social Vulnerability Index or FEMA's National
Risk Index. While social vulnerability screening tools measure
the social and demographic factors that influence vulnerabil-
ity, they often do not account for the administrative and insti-
tutional capacity required to implement solutions to address
uneven exposure to disaster risks. The RCI thus adds value by
complementing, rather than duplicating, existing vulnerability
indices by identifying where limited capacity may inhibit access
to funding, programs, and technical assistance, particularly in
rural areas. The research further highlights the need for more
capacity research to investigate rural barriers and to identify
solutions that directly strengthen rural capacity for equitable
disaster resilience.

6.2   |   Higher Capacities Are Associated With More
Federal Competitive Funding
As part of the RCI's validation, this research finds a positive re-
lationship between county RCI percentile values and success in
securing federal competitive grants, reinforcing the link between
local government capacity and resource access (e.g., Freemark
et al. 2023; Collins and Gerber 2006). Counties with higher RCI
percentile values were more likely to secure a federal grant and
more likely to have higher total amounts of grant funding. Low-­
capacity counties (1st percentile) had only a 68% chance of receiv-
ing a competitive grant, compared to 94% for mid-­capacity counties
(50th percentile) and 99% for high-­capacity counties (100th per-
centile). Further, the model estimated a 4% increase in awarded
funding for every 1% increase in a county's RCI percentile rank.
Notably, the RCI outperformed population size alone in explaining
the variation in federal grant awards, underscoring its value for
understanding capacity-­driven disparities in funding outcomes.
These findings have key policy implications for improving
access to federal funding for smaller and rural governments
with limited staff and resources. Policymakers can use the
RCI to identify areas most in need of technical assistance,
resources, and outreach, ensuring rural and lower-­capacity
communities can access state and federal funding. The RCI
also demonstrates the need to streamline grant programs to
improve access—for example, by waiving local matches, of-
fering technical assistance, fostering partnerships, or making
direct investments.
6.3   |   A Methodological Contribution
The RCI offers multiple methodological contributions. First,
it integrates quantitative data with practitioner insights gath-
ered through a national survey, ensuring the index's weight-
ing reflects real-­world priorities and frontline experience.
Second, the RCI's predictive validity is supported by its strong
relationship to actual federal grant outcomes, based on USAsp​
ending.​gov data. Third, the RCI is designed for use at multiple
scales, providing data results at the county, county subdivi-
sion, and community levels—all jurisdictions that (depending
on the state) are responsible for planning and implementing
climate and disaster resilience projects. This multi-­scale ap-
proach enhances the index's practical relevance and flexibil-
ity for practitioners, policymakers, and scholars. RCI data are
publicly available online and downloadable in accessible for-
mats to support transparency, replicability, and widespread
use
(https://​headw​aters​econo​mics.​org/​equity/​rural​-­​capac​
ity-­​map).
7   |   Limitations, Future Research, and Uses
Rural communities—including their assets, politics, tax
structures, and needs—are diverse (Scally et  al.  2021;
Freshwater 2021). While the RCI offers a way to compare local
government capacity across the country, the indicators selected
had to be nationally available and thus do not account for nu-
ances that may be captured in state-­ or region-­specific data-
sets. The RCI does not reflect regional governance, such as the
presence of a federal regional commission, regional planning
organization, council of government, or special district, though
these institutions contribute capacity to rural communities
(Hall 2008b; Goodman 2019). Future research into state capac-
ity and regional institutions is needed to strengthen estimators
of capacity such as the RCI.
Further, Tribal government capacities have their own complex-
ities stemming from settler colonialism and histories of dispos-
session that are not taken into account in the index. Differing
land ownership regimes, as well as complex relationships and
bureaucracies among Tribal, local, state, and federal govern-
ments, have a significant impact on capacity, economic oppor-
tunity, and issues of sovereignty. Assessing Tribal capacity was
outside the scope of this study, but it is a significant research gap
that warrants more attention.
This research used a multi-­faceted approach to validation to en-
sure rigor, including conducting internal consistency analyses,
an uncertainty analysis, and a comparative analysis using federal
grant data from USAsp​ending.​gov to assess the RCI's predictive
analysis. While the quantitative comparison between the RCI
and federal grant awards illustrates that the RCI, as a predictive
metric, explains variation beyond population size, the analysis is
not intended to serve as a complete validation of the RCI since
success with competitive federal grants is not a perfect or com-
plete measure of capacity, only one outcome of capacity. Further
research is needed to continue to validate and improve the RCI.
8   |   Conclusions
The rising costs and severity of US disasters have renewed at-
tention from scholars, policymakers, and practitioners on the
importance of local government capacity in the ability to plan,
fund, build, and maintain climate resilience projects while
meeting the diverse needs of residents. Rural communities
have received less attention within these debates despite having
unique challenges such as limited local government staff, eco-
nomic specialization, geographic isolation, and higher per cap-
ita costs of services and infrastructure. These challenges often
limit the ability of rural communities to initiate infrastructure
and resilience projects that reduce risks from climate-­related
disasters.
The RCI was developed to shed light on variations in local ca-
pacity across the rural–urban spectrum and can be used to
prioritize locations that will likely need additional resources,
technical assistance, and attention to ensure they can ad-
equately prepare for disasters and climate change. Data are
available to download, free of cost, online. The RCI findings
contribute to a growing body of research on the role of local
government capacity in supporting (or inhibiting) adapta-
tion and mitigation efforts, both locally and regionally. The
research also found that capacity plays a critical role in the
ability of local governments to secure competitive federal
funding, suggesting that new rounds of federal funding risk
reflecting and compounding geographic inequalities without
strategic interventions. Identifying and addressing local gov-
ernment capacity constraints are essential for promoting equi-
table climate resilience across the United States.

Robustness Checks on the USAsp​ending.​gov Comparative
Analysis.
To assess the Rural Capacity Index's (RCI) predictive validity, the re-
searchers conducted a comparative analysis examining the relationship
between RCI percentile rank and the ability of local governments to se-
cure competitive federal grants. Robustness checks were implemented
to ensure model reliability. First, “state” was incorporated as a random
effects term in the binary logistic regression model and log-­transformed
linear regression model to account for unobserved heterogeneity across
states. Next, the researchers conducted a sensitivity analysis focus-
ing exclusively on rural counties to ensure that the results of the log-­
transformed linear regression model were not disproportionately driven
by larger, urban counties. This subset analysis assessed whether the ob-
served relationship remains consistent in less densely populated areas.
The mixed effects binary logistic regression model revealed that, con-
trolling for state-­level variation, RCI percentile rank significantly
predicted whether a county received competitive federal funding (p
value = 0.00). Counties in the lowest RCI percentile had a 79.2% proba-
bility of receiving funding, compared to 96.4% at the median and 99.5%
at the highest percentile. Confidence intervals for these predictions
were [72.3%, 84.7%], [95.0%, 97.4%], and [99.1%, 99.7%], respectively. The
log-­transformed mixed-­effects linear regression model demonstrated a
significant fit, with pseudo-­R2 values of 0.25 for fixed effects and 0.34 for
the total model, indicating that the model explains 34% of the variance
in the funding amount received. Model selection criteria supported the
inclusion of random effects (AIC = 11,297.74, BIC = 11,321.56). The re-
sults showed that for every 1 percentile increase in RCI rank, there was
a 4% increase in the amount of funding awarded (β = 0.04, SE = 0.00,
p < 0.001). The inclusion of state-­level random intercepts accounted for
variation across states, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)
of 0.13, indicating that 13% of the total variance in funding is attribut-
able to differences between states. Because the inclusion of state-­level

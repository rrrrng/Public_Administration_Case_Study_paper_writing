Gendered administrative burden: regulating gendered
bodies, labor, and identity
Pamela Herd* and Donald Moynihan

Abstract
Gendered burdens are experiences of coercive and controlling state actions that directly regulate gendered bodies, labor, and identity. Burdens
are not simply about preventing access to rights and benefits, they're about control and coercian. Gendered burdens generate gender inequality
through four mechanisms. First, administrative burdens regulate reproductive bodies, legitimating the state’s direct control over reproductive
health care, including abortions, with consequent implications for peoples’ health. Second, burdens require reproductive labor, shifting unpaid
and underpaid reproductive labor onto women as the policies that support such labor tend to have high administrative burdens that impede
access. Third, gendered burdens restrict reproductive labor, impeding the right to provide such care labor with dignity, by exerting control over
how, and sometimes whether, care is performed, including in rights-granting venues, like redistributive benefits, and rights-depriving venues,
like the supervision of families by child protective services. Fourth, burdens regulate gendered identities, reinforcing heteronormative and cis-
normative constructions of gender, including by directly controlling gender identification. While gendered burdens are not only experienced by
women, they are most strongly applied to poor and racially marginalized groups of women. These claims provide a basis for public administra­
tion scholarship to connect with feminist theory by illustrating the centrality of administrative processes and related experiences to structural
patterns of inequality.
Key words: administrative burden; gender; inequality.
Introduction
Administrative burdens, or peoples’ onerous encounters with
public services, restrict the public’s access to resources, and
rights (Barnes 2021; Herd and Moynihan 2018; Nisar 2018a;
Peeters 2020). Accumulating research shows that burdens
fall unequally across groups, reinforcing, and amplifying ine­
quality (Bell, Wright II, and Oh 2024; Herd et al. 2023; Ray,
Herd, and Moynihan 2022). The relationship between gender
and burdens, however, remains especially under-theorized
relative to the magnitude of its potential effects in shaping
broader patterns of inequality. Consequently, we theorize
how the distribution of administrative burden is gendered.
Burdens are a means by which the state regulates gender
and shapes gender relations. Women are targeted more for
burdens in policy administrative spaces—for example, repro­
ductive rights—that limit their ability to control their bodies,
their health, and their economic independence. They also
shoulder more burdens because of administrative obligations
that come with women’s disproportionate reproductive
labor—the work required to “raise future generations and
sustain life” (Briggs 2018), including all forms of care labor.1
States have also increasingly employed burdens to directly
regulate peoples’ gender identity altogether.
We draw on feminist theories of the state, organizations, and
citizen-state interactions to develop the concept of gendered ad­
ministrative burden. As Acker (1990:146) notes, “to say that…
any analytic unit’s gendered means that advantage and disad­
vantage, exploitation and control, action and emotion, meaning
and identity, are patterned through and in terms of a distinc­
tion between male and female.” Gendered burdens emerge from
gendered states (e.g., policies), organizations, and citizen-state
interactions,2 but are the hands-on tools that structure gender
relations, and maintain gender inequality, via their regulation
of bodies, reproductive labor, and identity. To be clear, gender
is not another word for woman. Gender relations constitute
how resources, power, and labor are distributed across gender
groups, as defined by masculine and feminine identities.
Drawing on feminist theories of the state, organizations,
and citizen-state interactions, we advance four central claims:
(1) gendered burdens regulate reproductive bodies, (2) re­
quire reproductive labor in some cases, (3) restrict reproduc­
tive labor in other cases, and (4) reinforce heteronormative
and cis-normative constructions of gender. Our overarching
argument is that administrative burdens are a means of con­
trol and coercion that regulate gendered bodies, labor, and
identities. Critically, these tools of coercion and control are
applied most aggressively to poor and racially marginalized

What are administrative burdens?
Administrative burdens are the frictions that we experience
when we interact with public services (Herd and Moynihan
2018). Such hassles might be broadly categorized as irri­
tating, but it’s easy to overlook their scale and invasiveness,
and the way they affect marginalized groups (Herd et al.
2023). Burdens include learning costs, such as figuring out
how to apply for and stay on public programs, compliance
costs, such as filling out forms, documenting one’s gender, or
waiting periods for abortion care; and psychological costs,
such as stress, frustration, fear, stigma, or loss of autonomy,
which arise from interacting with government. They can also
include redemptions costs of negative experiences when actu­
ally trying to use public services (Barnes 2021).
While we don’t review this entire literature (for this, see
Halling and Bækgaard 2023), a central theme is how seem­
ingly minor frictions can lead to large distributive effects,
more apt to fall on groups with less power and resources
(Christensen et al. 2020; Heinrich 2018; Nisar 2018a,
2018b), including women, LGBTQ groups, those with health,
or cognitive challenges, and racial minority groups (Barnes
and Henly 2018; Bell et al. 2022; Herd and Moynihan 2020;
Ray, Herd, and Moynihan 2023). They can be a useful tool
for states to reinforce inequalities, in part because they can
conceal politically or culturally unpopular motivations. For
example, ID requirements that make it harder for minority
groups to vote are justified as fraud prevention (Ray, Herd,
and Moynihan 2023).
What about gender and burdens?
With a few key exceptions, the existing literature on burdens
is relatively quiet about gender. Nisar (2018b) shows the
consequences of not conforming to traditional gender
identities when engaging with the state, including establishing
formal identity. Other work shows how burdens reify ex­
pectations about women’s care responsibilities. Cook (2021)
notes how Australia’s child support system can burden mostly
women caretakers by allowing former partners to weaponize
administrative processes to avoid paying childcare costs.
Masood and Nisar (2020) show the burdens that Pakistani
women doctors face applying for maternity leave, which are
informed by broader cultural expectations about motherhood
and professions.
There’s also evidence that women shoulder more burdens.
Martin and colleagues (2022) provide UK survey evidence
that women spend more time managing administrative tasks
related to health, reproductive or care labor, and routine
household management, while men direct more time towards
financial tasks. In short, women are more likely to serve as the
family administrator, thereby being more exposed to burdens,
including the redemption costs of actually using public serv­
ices (Barnes 2021).
While there is some attention to gender differences in the
impacts of burdens, gender has not been theorized as central
to administrative burden, beyond the examples cited above.
Seeing gender in these interactions requires something more
than measuring distributive effects by gender or using it as
a control variable. It requires a rich theoretical exploration
of how burdens systematically influence and shape broader
gender relations, including gender inequality, which we turn
to next.
What feminist theory can contribute to
understanding administrative burden
We draw on existing feminist theory of the state, organiza­
tions, and state-citizen interactions to develop the concept of
gendered burdens. We start with the basic assumption that
“gender” is not synonymous with “women” nor is it syn­
onymous with a binary construct of woman and man (Elias
and Colvin 2020); gender is produced through social rela­
tions (Acker 1990; Bishu and Headley 2020). This contrasts
to gender socialization theories that see individual choices
as the source of inequality. Gender relations, instead, detail
how structures produce inequality, or the “processes through
which inequalities are institutionalized” (Ferree and Hall
2000: 476). Moreover, gender must be viewed through an in­
tersectional lens that incorporates race, class, and sexuality
(Bearfield 2009; Shields and Elias 2022).
Attention to gendered burdens can clarify how the state
shapes gender inequality. The themes that emerge from femi­
nist theory are how the state, organizations, and citizen-state
interactions shape gender relations in ways that shift unpaid
reproductive and labor onto women, limit their control over
that labor, and further structure more rigid hetero and cis-
normative gender identities. We consider each in turn.
Feminist state theory
Feminist state theories show how the state shapes gender re­
lations and inequality via policy design, with attention to race
and class differences (Haney 1996). While the state is not uni­
laterally viewed as patriarchal, most theory points to how the
state reinforces and shifts power and resources towards men,
and more broadly assumes rigid gender and sexual identities
(Haney 2000). Overlapping feminist theories of the welfare
state and reproductive justice show how the state shapes
gender relations via the gendered distribution of reproductive
labor, as well as the right to control that labor, in racialized
ways (Fraser 1989; Orloff 1993; Roberts 2009, 2014). There
is no consensus feminist view on the state’s role in shaping
gender inequality, but we draw on key shared themes around

Feminist scholarship and activism has long attended to the
state’s regulation of reproductive bodies and reproductive
labor, which is the work required to “raise future generations
and sustain life” (Briggs 2018). Central to this is reproduc­
tive rights, often framed as human rights, especially in law
scholarship (Siegel 2006). For example, Bunch (1990, 489)
notes, “The denial of women’s rights to control their bodies
in reproduction threatens women’s lives, especially where this
is combined with poverty and poor health services.” There’s
no shortage of examples regarding how the state limits indi­
vidual control over reproductive bodies, ranging from access
to safe and effective birth control to the right to terminate
pregnancies. State policies related to gender violence, in­
cluding rape and domestic abuse, are also relevant (Yilo and
Torres 2016). For example, policies that consider gender vi­
olence within marriages as legal are another form of limiting
women’s control over their own bodies.
More recently, however, Black feminist scholars have argued
instead for a reproductive justice framework (Roberts 2014).
As Luna and Luker (2013:333) note, “affluent White activists
pressed the cause for increased access to both birth control
and abortion—that is, for reproductive rights that would
enable women to not have children—many poor women,
women with disabilities, and women of color struggled for
the rights to have children and to parent the children they
have.” In short, not only do states regulate the choice to not
have children, they also regulate the right to have children
and to parent them “with dignity” (Luna and Luker 2013).
Most feminist analysis shows that states continue to reg­
ulate that reproductive labor in ways that shift care labor
towards women, undervalue it, and exacerbate inequalities
among women (Pailhe 2021). Some states tightly enforce a
breadwinner family structure where reproductive labor, such
as care for children or the elderly, is performed by hetero­
sexual married women in male-headed households. For ex­
ample, some countries in the Middle East directly hinge
women’s citizenship rights, even the right to travel and work,
on their male spouse’s permission. Different roles and expec­
tations from men and women can even be written into the
constitution (Grünenfelder 2013), often in a way that centers
heteronormative assumptions around women’s employment
and reproductive labor.
Wealthy democracies are typically less directly coercive, but
some, especially the United States, still foster a breadwinner
model (Harrington Meyer and Herd 2007). For example, the
U.S. government has devoted resources to marriage coun­
seling classes for poor individuals that promote “marital
masculinity,” the idea that being good husbands and fathers
is tied to breadwinner status (Randles 2013). U.S. policies
that directly support reproductive labor outside of marriage
tend to be miserly, restrictive, and contingent on adminis­
trative requirements (Abramovitz 2017; Fraser and Gordon
1994; Gordon 1991; Herd and Moynihan 2023; Mink
1990). Indeed, the only cash assistance program that directly
supports reproductive labor for children, without requiring
paid employment, benefits only 22 out of every 100 families
with children living below the poverty line, and comprises
less than.1% of the federal budget (CBPP 2020). By contrast,
the most generous policies maximally benefit married couple
households with single earners (Herd 2005). For example,
Social Security, which provides disability, retirement, and sur­
vivor benefits, comprises 21% of the total federal budget and
provides the largest benefits to married couples with single
earnings.
While the breadwinner model remains relevant, welfare state
reforms in many wealthy democracies have pushed mothers
into paid labor, and reproductive labor onto lower paid racially
marginalized groups (Duffy 2007; Nazareno et al. 2022). Most
countries have eroded benefits that allowed for parents, typi­
cally mothers, to stay out of the labor force, such as children
allowances. States vary, however, in the degree to which they
provide supportive benefits like subsidized child care and paid
leaves to facilitate parents’ paid employment. More generally,
increased labor forced participation among mothers in many
wealthy countries has effectively been subsidized by low wage
care labor provided by racially marginalized women, especially
immigrant women (Nazareno et al. 2022).
Attention to how state policy regulates sexuality and
cis-normative gender identity is relatively new and rapidly
evolving. There’s growing evidence that state policies, particu­
larly around health policy and legal definitions of discrimina­
tion, exclude transgender individuals (Flores and Jody 2015;
Goldenberg et al. 2020). More attention has also been paid to
explicitly examining how the state has reinforced heteronor­
mative sexuality. For example, Canaday (2009) detailed how
the American administrative state of the early 20th century
systematically excluded and penalized gay men, effectively
making them second class citizens. Morrison and colleagues
(2023) showed how Chilean programs focused on supporting
childrearing assume a heterosexual couple where the mother
is the primary caregiver, thereby disadvantaging gay parents.
In short, state policies typically assumed heterosexuality and
binary gender identity, and this has implications for basic
rights, including access to basic rights, including adequate
health care and protection from discrimination in institutions
ranging from education to the workplace.
Gendered organizations
Gendered organization theory, like feminist state theories de­
tailed above, assumes that gender is a set of relations rather
than an individual identity, a set of norms, or a process of
socialization; thereby, gender inequality is a function of struc­
ture rather than individual choice (Acker 1990). The key
claim is that organizations are designed around gendered
bodies and gendered labor. As Acker (1990: 450) notes, “the
abstract worker is actually a man, and it’s the man’s body,
its sexuality, minimal responsibility in procreation, and con­
ventional control of emotions that pervades…organizational
processes.” Here, we focus especially on public organizations.
Women are excluded from work spaces or segregated
within occupations that are devalued, viewed as feminized,
and are consequently lower paid. This holds true in public
organizations, where women tend to be concentrated in jobs
that focused on care labor, such as social work and teaching.
Stivers (2000) mapped out the processes Acker describes in
American public administration’s history. During the progres­
sive era, men took control of public organizations and the
field of public administration, while women were directed
to charitable endeavors and the emergent field of social
work, where caregiving tasks dominated. As occupations be­
come feminized, they become devalued, with evidence that

Gendered practices are embedded; an organization is not
simply the sum of individual bureaucrats and their preferences.
This distinguishes gendered organizations from representa­
tive bureaucracy theories. For example, Lee and colleagues
(2008) show how gay men working in public health organi­
zations disconnect from their sexual identity when discussing
sexual health with their gay clients; organizational culture,
practices, and policies constrain the influence and expression
of individual identity.
While bureaucracies are not immutably patriarchal (Martin
2013), they strongly influence gender relations in ways that
reinforce gendered divisions of reproductive labor. For ex­
ample, even as organizational policies around family leave
have changed, in part due to broader social change, they
continue to pattern gender, race, and class inequality. Paid
leave policies in the U.S. are largely confined to White and
white-collar workers. Men continue to be far less likely to use
these policies, in part because organizational cultures remain
built around male workers, effectively discouraging men from
taking leaves (Choroszewicz and Tremblay 2018).
Gendered citizen-state interactions
Citizen-state interactions are also gendered. Haney (1996:759)
argues that the advantage to conceptualizing the state at the
“institutional level” rather than at the “macro-level” is that
it “widens the scope of feminist theory to include the micro
apparatuses of state power.” In particular, “programs are
constructed and reconstructed by state actors and female
clients who regulate and resist each other….the state is not a
uniform structure that acts to impose a singular set of gender
expectations on women” (Haney 1996: 760).
That state influence is not uniform over time and place is
critical to understanding how it shapes inequality. Attending
to citizen-state interactions makes clear that beneficiaries
are not automatons; they have agency. They can negotiate
some terms, such as engaging in activism or advocating for
themselves in the face of biased treatment (Barnes and Henly
2018; Cox 2015; Massod and Nisar 2020; Michener 2018).
Such agency, however, depends upon individual resources,
such as administrative capital (Masood and Nisar 2020). For
example, women have less money, due to discrimination at
work and unpaid reproductive labor at home, to help navi­
gate bureaucracies, such as paying for an advocate to access
state services for a disabled child. Masood and Nisar (2020)
detail how social, culture, and economic capital influence
physicians’ administrative capital needed to access mater­
nity leave in Pakistan. Because Pakistani men handle “mas­
culine bureaucratic spaces,” reflecting familial and religious
structures, women accrue less administrative capital to navi­
gate the process (Masood and Nisar 2020). Even with equiv­
alent implementation, these differential resources influences
citizen-state interactions.
Street-level bureaucrats influence how gender identities are
formed and reinforced (Dolan 2002). For example, the public
sees women street-level bureaucrats as warmer than men (de
Boer et al. 2020), and street-level bureaucrats react more
harshly to women who don’t behave “like women” (Bisgard
and Pedersen 2022). Further, in “masculine” occupations, like
policing, officers engage in hypermasculine “performative
gender” ways that reinforce negative gendered interactions
(Durose and Lowndes 2024). This can harm men too. For
example, in domestic violence incidents in Finland, when po­
lice officers and the victim are men, there is less likely to be
an intervention or arrest (Fagerlund 2021). Research has also
attended to binary gender constructions. Front-line workers
reinforce gender binary categories with emotions like disgust
(Nisar and Masood 2020).

interactions.

Gendered burdens
Gendered burdens emerge from gendered states, organiza­
tions, and state-citizen interactions, but are the hands-on
tools that structure gender relations, and maintain gender
inequality, via their regulation of bodies, reproductive labor,
and identity. As table 1 details, we make four claims, rooted
in feminist theories detailed in the previous section, as to how
burdens shape gender relations and inequality; they regulate
reproductive bodies and reproductive labor, the gendered dis­
tribution of reproductive labor, and gendered heteronorma­
tive and cis-normative constructions of gender.
The remainder of the paper is then organized around these
claims. For each, we focus on policies in their relevance and mag­
nitude, which best illustrate that claim, broadly representing
reproductive health policy, social welfare policy, and identity
related policies. Specifically, we focus on: (1) burdens that most
directly either prevent or require women to reproduce; (2)
burdens linked to reproductive or care labor policies, which
comprise 40 percent of all U.S. federal spending; and (3)
burdens related to obtaining state identification, which all sub­
sequent benefits and obligations of residency and citizenship
are linked. The examples are illustrative rather than original
research, reflecting that the core contribution of the paper is
establishing the theoretical claims rather than empirical anal­
ysis. The cases are also largely U.S. specific. While we believe
the claims broadly hold across settings, they may operate in
different ways in different contexts because gender is a social
construct, and related gender inequalities vary substantially
across countries. Moreover, because we attend to race, the
cases also reflect patterns of racial inequality particular to the
U.S. Further, while the cases could illustrate multiple claims, we
focus on a specific case for the claim it best illustrates.

The right to have children.
While debates around reproductive rights today center on ac­
cess to abortion and contraception, sterilization campaigns
were common throughout the 20th century. Such campaigns
emerged from the eugenics movement, embedded in white su­
premacist ideologies, which claimed major social problems,
like poverty, and crime, were rooted in genetic difference or
more specifically genetic inferiority (Herd et al. 2019; Roberts
2014). The solution, then, was to encourage the “fit” to have
more children and prevent the ‘unfit’ from having children,
with racist, gendered, and ableist ideologies defining who fit
in these categories (Hansen and King 2013; Roberts 2014).
The practice, the underlying rationalization, and how burdens
were used to legitimize it, changed over the 20th century.
By the early 20th century, thirty-one states allowed the
“unfit,” usually those diagnosed (often erroneously) with
cognitive disabilities, to be surgically sterilized (Stern 2011).
These typically involuntary procedures were nearly always
performed on the institutionalized. Though both men and
women were sterilized, the underlying rationale differed. Men
were sterilized due “to reduce male sex drive and alleviate
their excitability and aggressiveness,” which was theorized
to improve everything from their academic outcomes to their
sleep (Carey 1998:78). Women were sterilized to control their
consensual sexual behavior, in short for the sin of promis­
cuity, as well as the belief that their childbearing was a pri­
mary cause of poverty; racialized minorities were especially
targeted as being hypersexual (Lira and Stern 2014; Carey
1998). Moreover, by the 1930s women were disproportion­
ately more likely to be sterilized (Carey 1998).
Burdens shaped this early period of mostly institutional
sterilizations by providing cover for ‘due process.’ Prior to the
late 1920s, new sterilization laws were repeatedly challenged
in the Courts, in part on the grounds that patients lacked due
process. The 1927 Supreme Court decision, Buck v. Bell, pro­
vided a template for a constitutionally sound forced steriliza­
tion law, and led to a significant increase in both sterilization
laws and the numbers of sterilizations actually performed
(Reilly 1987). Though institutional superintendents largely
drove the process, including proposing sterilization in the
first place, the Virginia law, for example, required a board
to oversee the process, for the individual and their guardian
to be notified, as well as the right to appeal. But, as Kaelber
(2012) notes, “appeals were heard, but rarely considered.” In
practice, however, the most legally sound sterilizations, that is
those least likely to be targeted for lawsuits, had patient con­
sent (Carey 1998; Wellerstein 2011). There is evidence, how­
ever, that social workers and medical staff coerced women
into consenting, including compliance costs like a common
requirement for sterilization to be discharged (Braslow 1996;
Carey 1998; Wellerstein 2011). In California, where Mexican
women were disproportionately targeted, there is evidence
that family members actively fought sterilization of their
family members, but generally failed to overcome costs to
learn about and navigate the processes of an adversarial bu­
reaucracy (Lira and Stern 2014). In short, while in theory,
individuals or family members could prevent sterilization, in
practice, they rarely overcame the burdens to actually access
due process rights.
Though the fall of Nazi Germany discredited the broader
eugenics movement, the ideology regarding reproduction
remained (Kluchin 2011; Roberts 2014). By the 1960s, how­
ever, the focus was largely on women’s economic depend­
ence on the state and promiscuity, rather than mental acuity.
Racialized tropes of Black women’s sexuality fed this shift.
Racially marginalized women were targeted, including those
deemed “immoral, loose, or unfit for motherhood” (Hansen
and King 2013:218). As one South Carolina physician noted
to a Black woman, “Listen here, young lady, this is my tax
money paying for this baby, and I am tired of paying for ille­
gitimate children” (Hansen and King 2013:253). While ster­
ilization campaigns targeting racially marginalized groups
was not new, they expanded significantly, especially in the
South. For example, whereas Black people made up 23% of
sterilizations in North Carolina in the mid-1930s, they made
up 65% by the 1960s (Hansen and King 2013).
Administrative burdens became more visible as “voluntary”
sterilizations became common. Sterilization was no longer
confined to those institutionalized, but rather practiced more
broadly in the community. Administrative burdens helped
legitimate the practice by undermining consent processes.

psychological costs that effectively negated consent. Women
consented without realizing they would be sterilized. The

The right to not have children.
Over the last 30 years, anti-abortion proponents in America
have aggressively used administrative burden to limit women’s
right to not have children, while generating a much broader
impact on women’s overall reproductive health care. To sim­
plify somewhat, the Roe vs. Wade (1973) Supreme Court deci­
sion made abortion bans unconstitutional across the country.
The Roe era ended with the Supreme Court’s 2022 Dobbs
decision. The court decided to, contrary to Roe, remove a
national standard around abortion, and to delegate authority
to state governments to regulate access to abortions.
During the Roe era, anti-abortion policymakers imposed
burdensome requirements on clinics and individual women
instead of outright bans, which were illegal. Their explicit
aim was to limit abortions (Herd and Moynihan 2018).
Dobbs might have been assumed to make such strategic use
of burdens irrelevant since state legislatures could now di­
rectly ban abortion access. However, administrative burdens
continue to limit women’s reproductive rights. Indeed, their
reach is even broader now, with significant and negative
implications for women’s health and well-being, especially for
poor and racially marginalized groups.
States without new abortion bans still employ burdens
enacted prior to the 2022 Dobbs ruling. Here, burdens in­
clude both compliance and psychological costs. For example,
compliance costs include requiring women to wait after
their initial clinic visit before they can have the abortion or
requiring abortion providers to meet regulatory requirements
that are not medically necessary for out-patient procedures,
such as building modifications, which make it too expensive
for clinics to operate. The result is few abortion providers,
requiring women to travel long distances to access care, ex­
acting compliance costs in both additional time and money. In
terms of psychological costs, some states require mandatory
vaginal ultrasounds and viewing fetal images before the abor­
tion procedure, neither of which are medically necessary (see
Herd and Moynihan 2018 for full details).
In states with new limits on abortion enabled by Dobbs,
the resulting burdens affect nearly everyone who is pregnant
or can become pregnant, not only those seeking abortions.
The key issue is that abortion procedures are, in fact, a rou­
tine form of health care, one of the most commonly employed
surgical procedures in the U.S (Cooper and Menefee 2021).
They’re used to treat miscarriages, ectopic pregnancies,
uterine bleeding, and for diagnostic procedures. Further, the
drugs used for medical abortions, particularly mifepristone,
are also used to treat illnesses, particularly Cushing’s disease
and fibroids (Allen and O’Brien 2009).
The post-Dobbs laws enact a new and broad set of burdens
that influence people’s ability to use basic healthcare, enacting
high and deeply consequential redemption costs. There are
burdens linked to miscarriages being criminalized, or that “an­
yone who becomes pregnant…will become newly vulnerable
to legal surveillance, civil detentions, forced interventions,
and criminal prosecution” (Paltrow, Harris, and Marshall
2022). Nearly 20% of pregnancies end in miscarriage (Dugas
and Slane 2022). Moreover, criminalization increases risks for
racially marginalized groups (Paltrow, Harris, and Marshall
2022). For example, among those charged under existing
fetal homicide laws, present in 38 states, over half were Black
women and 71% were low-income (Paltrow and Flavin
2013). Moreover, two-thirds of women charged were due to
“risk” of harm rather than actual harm, an even more ambig­
uous claim to disprove (Paltrow and Flavin 2013).
New burdens involve proving one is not guilty of an abor­
tion, or in claiming an exempted category that allows abor­
tion. “Proving” that one is not at fault for a miscarriage, or
risk of harm, or has suffered rape or incest, or is in mortal
danger, entails high learning, compliance, and psychological
costs. A key source of burden is the ambiguous standard of
proof applied; state actors may or may not accept evidence
presented to them. For example, Michelle Greenup, arrested
in Louisiana after going to an ER with bleeding and stomach
pain, was charged with second degree murder and imprisoned
after hospital staff suspected she had given birth (Paltrow and
Flavin 2013). Her medical records ultimately showed that she
was not more than 15 weeks pregnant (so it was a miscar­
riage), and that she had received an injection that likely led to
that miscarriage. The psychological costs are obvious, and the
compliance costs are enormous, specifically the costs (time, fi­
nancial, and loss of liberty) borne in the process of “proving”
that one did not cause a miscarriage.
Other burdens are related to exceptions to protect the
mother’s health. While there’s not systematic data given the
recency of these laws, emerging evidence is worrying. For
example, while thousands of women should be eligible for
exceptions, the recorded number of abortions in states that
have them has been nearly zero in the aftermath of Dobbs
(Walker 2023). And there are numerous stories of women
being forced to carry pregnancies, that can, and do, harm
their health.
Here, burdens restrict access by targeting third parties, in
this case hospitals and health care providers, ultimately af­
fecting most pregnant women in these states. After Dobbs,
states enacted new aggressive laws directed at health care
providers, including large fines, loss of a medical license,
or even imprisonment if they’re judged to have provided,
or aided, access to an abortion procedure (Walker 2023).
Medical providers face new learning, compliance, and psy­
chological costs. They’re unsure how to interpret vague laws
on what constitutes a threat to the life of a mother. Their
medical judgment may be second-guessed by hospital lawyers
seeking to avoid risk, or by judges. In addition to this learning
cost, there are compliance costs. Physicians must provide de­
tailed documentation to prove they didn’t violate the laws. In
some states, the burden of proof is on medical providers, and
third parties can sue them.
Psychological costs follow. Medical providers fear lawsuits,
experience a reduced sense of personal autonomy, and shame
that they can’t provide appropriate care. One physician pro­
viding reproductive care said the “laws were designed to
sow confusion and fear, and they’re working. People want to
stay out of trouble, and physicians are no exception to that”

Claim 2: requiring reproductive labor: shifts unpaid and
underpaid care labor onto women.
Our second claim is that burdens shift unpaid care work
onto women, ultimately devaluing it. The case we detail here
is co-production in LTC provision built into LTC policy.
Co-production occurs when both citizens and the state en­
gage together to develop and deliver services. As we detail
below, burdens are deployed in ways that ensure that women
end up disproportionately responsible for unpaid care labor,
both in navigating burdensome LTC policies, but also in the
unpaid provision of LTC when those systems are too onerous
to navigate. LTC is not a small policy domain. It’s associated
with $475 billion in annual U.S. spending, and is a growing
concern in any country with aging populations. Most LTC
is provided by women and that labor is uncompensated or
undercompensated. Nearly half of those over age 65 receive
LTC supports, and approximately 75% of that care is pro­
vided informally, mostly by women (Spillman et al. 2021). As
we detail, the U.S.’s LTC system is also highly burdensome,
for both care providers and recipients.
Before detailing the burdens in LTC, we describe the basic
policy. The Medicaid program devotes nearly 60% of its
budget to LTC. It’s a poverty-based program, individuals need
sufficiently low incomes, and assets to qualify. LTC is very
expensive, so many middle-class individuals “spend down”
their assets to qualify (Grabowski and Gruber 2007). Because
Medicaid is funded by both federal and state governments,
eligibility rules, including income, asset, and functional needs
criteria, and the administrative processes required to docu­
ment them, vary widely across states, and sometimes even
within states (Chidambaram and Burns 2022). Importantly,
Medicaid LTC services are co-produced by formal and in­
formal carers. Eligibility processes are designed to ensure
informal unpaid care isn’t “crowded out” (Grabowski et al.
2010). States employ assessment tools for Medicaid Home
and Community-based services that calculate informal
supports to determine the number of services that Medicaid
should provide (Machledt 2015).
How do burdens shift unpaid care to women? The elderly
and disabled, and those providing care for them, face high
learning and compliance costs to access LTC services. The eli­
gibility process is so complex that few eligible for services can
navigate this process independently given their physical and
cognitive health. Hence, informal caregivers, typically women,
provide unpaid care labor navigating burdens, as well as pro­
viding care when they fail to navigate those burdens success­
fully (Herd and Johnson 2024). Learning costs derive from
high variance in eligibility procedures, and covered services,
across and within states. Processes regarding how to apply
and what will be covered are highly opaque, with many states
even lacking basic websites that clearly describe programs,
eligibility guidelines, and clear application procedures (Herd
and Johnson 2024).
Compliance costs are also high. In addition to the time costs
resulting from sorting out where and how to apply, one must:
(1) gather documents such as financial statements, income
verification letters, tax documents, estate documents, and life
insurance policies; (2) complete the application, waiting up
to 90 days for the application to be reviewed; (3) review the
determination letter because up to 35% contain errors made
by Medicaid staff. If there was an error, they must then then
manage the appeal for denials (American Council on Aging
2022). Critically, each step above varies depending on the
state and county in which one lives.
Accessing Medicaid home and community-based services is
especially complicated because they are largely funded under
“waiver” rules that don’t require states to provide services
even if individuals are eligible (Chidambaran and Burns 2022;
O’Malley Watts and Musumeci 2020). The result is waitlists.
In 2017, there were over 700,000 eligible people on waitlists
waiting to access services (Musumeci and Chidambaram
2019). Compliance costs include the time spent waiting and
efforts to stay on the waitlist, when wait times can last for
years (Schonfeld et al. 2022). Because agencies may lose
track of those on the waitlists, simply maintaining one’s place
requires diligence and effort (Bouek 2023; Moore 2016).
The burdens embedded into Medicaid LTC policies
leave many informal care providers with little option other
than to provide intensive unpaid care because they cannot
make it through these processes (Herd and Johnson 2024).
Racialized minorities also appear less likely to access these
services, even though they have a greater risk for disa­
bility (Fabius, Wolff, and Kasper 2020). A study examining
Medicaid home and community-based services found that
White beneficiaries tended to get more services than did
Black beneficiaries (Gorges, Prachi, and Tamara Konetzka
2019). Relatedly, Black beneficiaries were more likely to end

Claim 3: restricting reproductive labor: gendered burdens
impede the right to provide care with dignity.
The U.S.’s social welfare system is embedded with gendered
burdens. Collectively, they impede the right to provide care
with dignity, in gendered and racially patterned ways. The
scale of their impact is large; the welfare programs we discuss,
including the Temporary Aid to Needy Families Program,
which provides cash assistance, the Supplemental Nutrition
Assistance Program (SNAP), which provides vouchers for
food, Medicaid, which provides health insurance, and Social
Security, which offers retirement and disability benefits, con­
stitute 40% of the federal government budget. Such programs
center around caregiving, for both children and other family
members. Overall, these burdens exert control over how, and
whether, care is performed, especially for women from ra­
cially marginalized groups. This is accomplished, in part by
regulating a particular family form, specifically breadwinner
families, or married heterosexual one earner couples. These
burdens are in rights-granting venues, impeding access to so­
cial welfare benefits, and rights-depriving venues, including
supervision of families by child protective services.
Since its inception, the U.S. welfare state has provided the
most generous and least burdensome supports to middle or
upper middle-class White heterosexual families (Harrington
Meyer and Herd 2007). Single mothers, disproportionately
Black, immigrant, and poor, are funneled into means-tested
programs that have higher levels of administrative burdens, as
well as enhanced front-line official discretion that exacerbate
inequalities. White heterosexual and married mothers, are dis­
proportionately served in social insurance programs that have
fewer burdens (Mettler 1998; Soss et al. 2001). The former
programs explicitly link eligibility to caregiving, with the frac­
tion of eligible receiving benefits between 40% and 80%,
whereas the latter are linked to marriage or paid employment,
with the fraction of eligible receiving benefits being 100%.
The means-tested programs that support single parents’
(mostly mothers) reproductive labor, tend to have high
learning, compliance, and psychological costs, making it dif­
ficult to access and maintain support so they can provide
care for their children. Poor mothers, who are disproportion­
ately Black and unmarried, face high learning costs, such as
figuring out how to apply and maintain benefits, as well as
compliance costs like lengthy eligibility forms, substantial
documentation of income, interviews, and recertification as
frequently as every 6–12 months (see Herd and Moynihan
2018 for more detail).
Burdens in these programs penalize non-breadwinner
families in highly racialized ways by targeting non-custodial
fathers for child support and increasing related burdens for
custodial mothers (Cook 2021). In the policy and media dis­
course, it is particularly poor Black fathers, who are framed as
absent and failing to fulfill their breadwinner role (Rambert
2021). To receive cash welfare, custodial parents must help
the state access child support from non-custodial parents
(CBPP 2020). This generates compliance costs, including time
and paperwork to meet or be exempt from the requirement,
and psychological costs, including forcing women to engage
with former partners who may have been abusive. In contrast,
states leverage low burden systems to maximize the collec­
tion of these payments, such as wage garnishment, from typi­
cally low-income non-custodial parents (Curran and Abrams
2000). A few states do this for SNAP recipients, with evidence
that the resulting burdens decrease SNAP take-up, but doesn’t
increase child support payments (CBPP 2020).
Moreover, because these programs are administered by
states and localities, both organizations that administer
the programs and front-line workers managing eligibility
processes have significant discretion as to how all the burdens,
detailed above, are imposed. Indeed, they vary substantially
within states, with additional evidence that case workers
differentially apply burdens on racialized minorities, mostly
women (Ray, Herd, and Moynihan 2023). For example, Black
and Hispanic mothers are more likely to be sanctioned than
White mothers (Monnat and Bunyan 2008; Soss, Fording,
and Schram 2011).
In contrast to means-tested benefits that disproportionately
target Black women, White and well-off women more com­
monly access benefits via social insurance programs that have
few burdens, but link benefits to marriage, not reproductive
labor. Eligibility for Social Security retirement and survivor
benefits is tied to either employment or marital status. Over
half of women receive benefits based on their marital status
eligibility (SSA 2023). These benefits were designed to keep
women out of the labor market and ensure they married
(Herd 2005). To be eligible, one need never to have been em­
ployed, one needed only to have been married. While over
90% of white women qualify for these benefits, just under
half of Black women qualify (Harrington Meyer, Wolf, and
Himes 2006). Burdens are very limited, requiring completion
only of a short form. The government and employers keep
track of marital status and lifetime earnings to determine eli­
gibility and benefits. Moreover, there are no redemption costs,
which are the difficulties people experience using benefits
(Barnes 2021). Unlike the Women, Infants, and Children pro­
gram, which has numerous rules about what food people can
buy with their benefits, down to the brand types and con­
tainer sizes, Social Security is a cash benefit with no rules on
how it can be spent.
Burdens serve coercive state power that not only limit ac­
cess to support for reproductive and care labor, they can more
directly constrain caregiving rights, best highlighted by child
protective services (Edwards et al. 2023). These services are
a sprawling network of state surveillance and control, fo­
cused on disproportionately poor and racially marginalized
women. “In many Black and Indigenous communities, being
investigated by a child protection agency has become a routine
part of childhood” (Edwards et al. 2023, 218). Social workers
are not the only direct face of child protective services. There’s
a vast army of mandated reporters, such as teachers or med­
ical providers, who provide “eyes in the house” by surveilling
children and caretakers for evidence of failure (Edwards et
al. 2023). To provide a sense of scale and impact, 37% of all
children experience a child protective services investigation
with more than half of Black children versus 28% of White
children investigated (Kim et al. 2017). It’s easy to fall under

Caregivers face a distinct array of burdens to extract them­

Claim 4: regulating gendered identities: gendered burdens
reinforce heteronormative and cis-normative constructions
of gender.
Formalizing identity is a foundational state process that
impacts everything from the ability to drive, travel, and ob­
tain employment to the ability to participate in sports (Maier
2020). It is a useful prism to see how the state uses burdens
to control the assertion of gendered identities, both for cis
gender straight individuals, and especially for those that don’t
fit within hetero and cis-normative categories, such as trans,
nonbinary, and gay and lesbian individuals (Nisar 2018b;
Sederbaum Forthcoming).
Burdens typically serve to reinforce heternormative
identities. One example is the adoption of last names. Roughly
80% of married women take their husband’s last name in
the U.S., with even more children taking their father’s last
name (Pilcher 2017; Scheuble et al. 2012). A woman changing
her name faces numerous bureaucratic tasks, from obtaining
a new Social Security card (the American equivalent to a
national identity card) and driver’s license, to updating health
insurance, billing, and banking accounts. While the overall
process is complicated and time consuming, the basic name
change document is simple: a woman places her male spouse’s
name on the marriage certificate for her last name. The mar­
riage certificate then serves as documentation for other legal
documentation changes (Hurst 2021). If a male spouse wants
to take his wife’s name, with the exception of ten states, men
are required to do a statutory name change, which requires a
hearing and judicial consent. Some states require advertising
the name change, for example, in a newspaper, entailing addi­
tional cost and effort (Hurst 2021).
Those who don’t fit heteronormative gender identity
categories are more likely to experience burdens. For gay and
lesbian marriages, most states require more complicated docu­
mentation for name changes, though it’s gendered. For women
married to each other, only one partner has to complete a court
petition. For men, however, both typically have to petition the
court (Hurst 2021). And some states make the process partic­
ularly onerous. For example, Ohio requires men to not only
schedule, attend, and persuade a judge, they must also take out
an ad to notify the public of their name change (Hurst 2021).
For couples who don’t take a partner’s last name, there are
other more consequential burdens. Proving your legal status, ei­
ther as a married individual or parent, is necessary in schools,
when traveling, and in health care settings. For example, a parent
whose last name doesn’t match their child, typically women, they
may need to produce a birth certificate when traveling interna­
tionally (Williams 2018). This is especially challenging for family
structures outside of a heterosexual two-parent marriages. In
one estimate, over 100,000 parents are stopped in airports an­
nually in the United Kingdom due to this issue. A simple change
to passports that placed parent’s names on children’s passports
would eliminate this burden (Flemming 2014).

Identification documents, like a driver’s license or a pass­

how individuals who don’t conform to a specific gender—

individuals also face more employment discrimination, and
trouble accessing state-provided income supports that require
identification, their risk for economic insecurity is higher
(Carpenter, Eppink, and Gonzales 2020; Shannon 2021).

Conclusion
The concept of gendered burdens illustrates how the admin­
istrative burden framework can broaden the relevance of ad­
ministrative studies to broader questions regarding how the
state shapes inequality. Public administration can both draw
from, and contribute to. broader theory, such as gender theory,
and disciplines, such as sociology and political science. Public
administration’s close attention to administrative processes
and administrative actors make it uniquely positioned to con­
ceptualize how, in practice, the state shapes inequality.
Gendered administrative burdens reinforce the state’s control
over gendered bodies, labor, and identity. The claims and related
cases we detail, illustrate different ways that burdens regulate,
coerce, and control. While much of the administrative burden
literature centers on rights-granting situations, such as the pro­
vision of benefits, our cases also illustrate the role of burdens
in rights-depriving venues. Here, individuals may have limited
opportunity to learn about their rights, have to manage unrea­
sonable administrative demands, and face extreme psycholog­
ical costs, such as fear and shame. In the case of reproductive
services, burdens weaken women’s access to reproductive health
care and ultimately their control over their own bodies. In the
case of co-produced services such as LTC, burdens reinforce the
gendered distribution of unpaid reproductive labor. Social wel­
fare polices employ burdens that reinforce “traditional” family
structures as a way to exert control over reproductive and re­
productive labor, which, at the extreme, entail rights denying
processes embedded in s child protective services. Finally, the
burdens linked to gender identification polices foster heteronor­
mative and binary gender identities, once again in ways that can
directly regulate peoples’ bodies.
We further document how these tools of coercion and
control are most strongly applied to poor and racially
marginalized groups of women, and can be constructed and
wielded by women.
Gendered burdens can’t be understood without attention
to intersecting inequalities. In the case of the U.S., race and
class are fundamental to understanding how burdens shape
gender inequality. Poor and racially marginalized women face
far greater risks for the coercive consequences of gendered
burdens. For example, they’re more likely to receive state sup­
port for their reproductive labor in programs that are laden
with administrative burdens and are also more likely to face
burdens in ways that deny basic parental rights in the case
of child protective services. Or as in the case of new abor­
tion laws, racially marginalized women, given both that
they’re more likely to have miscarriages and are more likely
to face risk for criminal charges if they have them, face dis­
proportionate coercive and harmful impacts. More broadly,
well-off women, and White women, are more likely to be in
positions of power, be it in the context of policy design, in
organizational leadership, or on the front lines as street-level
bureaucrats, to shape and impose these burdens.
The claims offered in this paper are not intended to be ex­
haustive or definitive, but instead invite elaboration, nuance,
and original empirical research to establish their boundaries.
There are many paths to better understand how burdens
shape gender relations and inequality. We focused mostly on
burdens that arise from policy design. But these burdens can
also emerge in organizational contexts. Future work, for ex­
ample, might examine how variation in the rigidity of these
burdens, the allowance for discretion, varies across policy
domains and professions. Why do welfare caseworkers have
more discretion over the gendered burdens they employ
than do abortion providers? Our article also describes the
tension between burdens and professional autonomy; some
professionals may exit their job or state instead of imposing
burdens that hurt rather than help the public. This opens con­
sideration of studies at the intersection of professions, public
service motivation, and bureaucratic responses, such as exit,
voice, loyalty, and sabotage (Schuster et al. 2022).
In order to consider the implications for future research, we
can consider how this framework would have altered existing
administrative burden research. First, as with the original ad­
ministrative burden framework, or attention to specific groups
or processes associated with disparate outcomes (Christensen
et al. 2020; Ray, Herd, and Moynihan 2022), the conceptu­
alization of gendered burdens makes it easier for researchers
to “see” gender as a topic, and to connect it to broader state
actions and purposes. For example, while Herd and Moynihan
(2018) identify burdens abortion access as a domain where
gender is central, they do not connect it to a broader program
of control over women’s bodies or to reproductive care that
this paper identifies in accessing of safety net benefits such as
Medicaid. While the original burden framework highlighted
the common patterns in how burdens operate across settings,
gendered burdens does so with explicit attention to how power
relations around gender reoccur across those settings, raising
questions about whether such burdens exist, what purpose
they serve, how explicitly they are experienced by the actors
involved, and whether they can be remedied. In doing so, it
allows the accumulation of a more specific and specialized form
of knowledge about the operation of state power to emerge.
Attending to gender will therefore open an entirely new way of
understanding the impact of burdens.
More research should explore how cultural and political
contexts, particularly across countries, influence gendered
burdens. In advancing our claims, we rely primarily on il­
lustrative examples from the American setting. As with the
broader category of administrative burdens, we expect gen­
dered burdens to be relevant in other settings. But the form,
intensity, and effects of these processes will play out differ­
ently in different cultural, societal, and state contexts. For ex­
ample, our discussion of access to identity for trans individuals
echoes the experience of gender non-conforming individuals in
Pakistan and India. While these groups have been recognized
for centuries, they still face barriers when it comes to accessing
formal identity and essential services, including stigma (Nisar
2018b; Reddy 2006). The ways in which gendered violence
intersects with burdens—when it comes to reporting on do­
mestic abuse, sexual harassment, and sexual assault, for ex­
ample—will also vary by setting and by cultural norms.
Recognizing the importance of context, we have therefore kept
our claims at a general level, with illustrations from a specific
setting, while acknowledging the need to better understand
how gendered burdens function in other settings.
Further work could delve more deeply into the historical
roots of these gendered burdens. How did they evolve? What
political forces were at play? When do individuals see these
burdens and when do they become invisible, the natural order

ABSTRACT
A new set of political corruption concepts is introduced, impervious corruption foremost among them. Impervious corruption
occurs when a corrupt political actor is unchecked by institutional guardians, giving the corrupt individual a sense of invulner-
ability. The resultant relationship between impervious corruption and deformation of democracy is then explained. The focus
turns to President Donald Trump's political corruption and shows how the current United States political ecology has suborned
his corrupt behavior. It is suggested that President Trump is an innovator in political corruption, engaging in acts heretofore not
evident in the political history of the United States. The concluding section provides a set of prescriptions for addressing corrup-
tion and attendant deformation of democracy. These prescriptions do not require direct action from government institutions,
reasoning that some official guardian institutions have been systematically diminished by the Trump administration and some
political leaders have put partisan loyalty over their Constitutional duties.
1   |   Introduction
In the United States, as in even the most vibrant, successful
democracies, political corruption occasionally raises its ugly
head (Heidenheimer et al. 2024). One sign of the strength of a
nation's democracy is the ability to shirk off what are in some
instances anomalous acts of corruption and move ahead with
commitment to repairing the damage (Johnston 1991; Blake and
Martin  2006). But even among strong, resilient democracies,
history shows us that political corruption can reach a tipping
point where damage is not easily repaired (Warren 2004), lead-
ing ultimately to the deformation of democracy (Näsström 2021;
Bozeman et al. 2025).
Studies of the relationships between democracy and corruption
have of late become more common, perhaps due to the rise of au-
thoritarianism in many democratic nations (Kochi 2023; Adler
et al. 2023). This article builds on this burgeoning literature in-
cluding Bozeman et al. (2018) political corruption typology, but
it extends the typology by introducing a new political corruption
concept, “impervious corruption.” Impervious corruption oc-
curs when the individual believes that his or her corrupt acts
will not be met with punishment or resistance by government
officials. Thus, unlike other forms of corruption, impervious
corruption requires complicity; unlike other forms of corruption,
it is usually performed in full public view.
Impervious corruption is an especially powerful instru-
ment for the deformation of democracy (Beetham and
Schwarzmantel 2025). According to Bozeman et al. (2025, 797),
the “deformation of democracy occurs when the very political
officials charged with preserving democracy…are the ones un-
dermining it.” Impervious corruption works hand in glove with
deformation of democracy as each necessitates laxness or com-
plicity on the part of legitimate political authorities (James 2025).
While there are several factors affecting the contemporary
deformation of democracy in the United States1, this article
focuses on one, political corruption, and specifically the corrup-
tion of President Donald Trump and his administration. While

2   |   Objectives
Objectives for this article include: (1) to introduce a new concept
of political corruption, impervious corruption; (2) to show the
reciprocal relations between various forms of political corrup-
tion and the deformation of democracy; (3) to examine instances
of corruption by the Trump administration, including some ac-
tions that are deemed “corruption innovations”; (4) to analyze
the reciprocal relationships between the corrupt acts and the
deformation of US democracy.
This article is not a “wake up call.” No waking up is needed. In
the first half of 2025 we witnessed literally millions of people
marching in the streets protesting policies and actions deemed
injurious, immoral or corrupt. Even in a nation that remains
deeply divided, a great many people feel that the United States
democracy is threatened. A recent poll (Contreras 2025) asked
more than 5000 American voters to respond to the statement
“President Trump is a dangerous dictator whose power should
be limited before he destroys American democracy.” While par-
tisan divisions correlated with these responses, 52% agreed with
the statement.2

4   |   Political Corruption: An Encompassing
Concept
Given the already formidable and still rapidly growing political
corruption literature (e.g., Philp  1997; Kurer  2005; Bozeman
et al. 2025) there seems little reason to once again review this
literature in depth. However, there is good reason to be clear as
to the current article's definition of “political corruption,” espe-
cially since the definition used here differs from many others.
One controversy in the scholarly literature is whether corruption
requires illegal behavior. For example, Glaeser and Saks (2006,
1055) view political corruption as “crimes by public officials
for personal gain.” While many (e.g., Rose-­Ackerman  1999;
Lederman et al. 2005; Da Ros and Gehrke 2024) view corruption
as involving breaking the law, an increasing number of schol-
ars take a more expansive view of corruption, one that does not
necessitate crimes or even civil illegality (e.g., Kaufmann 1997;
Gupta et  al.  2002). The definition political corruption em-
ployed here:
Political corruption occurs when a public official
intentionally eschews society's core public values,
ignores or bends laws, or violates established ethical
codes in pursuit of personal goals unrelated to the
legitimate aims of public office.
5   |   A Typology of Political Corruption
The current article extends previous work (Bozeman et al. 2018;
Weißmüller and Zuber 2023) that contrasts two quite different
forms of political corruption, venal and deep corruption, adding
despotic corruption and introducing a new concept, impervious
corruption. Each type is discussed below and then elaborated in
a political corruption typology.
5.1   |   Venal Corruption
Venal corruption is an everyday sort of corruption, a form that is
in most instances illegal (Bozeman et al. 2018). Typically, venal
corruption involves bribes or kickbacks, nepotism, cheating on
expense accounts, or illegally granting favors in exchange for
political support. Venal corruption varies greatly in its scale.
Thus, a police officer who decides to withhold a traffic ticket in
exchange for a $20 bribe is guilty of venal corruption; but so is
a mayor who receives a huge bribe in exchange for awarding a
public works contract to a corrupt construction company. While
venal corruption is quite common across the world (Tanzi 1998;
Lambsdorff  2006), its pervasiveness should not lull us into

5.2   |   Deep Corruption
In deep corruption public officials manipulate or undermine
legitimate government institutions or laws to gain private
benefit, typically personal enrichment, power, or electoral ad-
vantage. Bozeman et al. (2018: 2) define deep corruption as
“authoritative actions using structures of government, gover-
nance, or public policy to thwart society's core public values.”
While in some cases deep corruption violates existing laws, in
most cases deep corruption is legal, with perpetrators hiding
behind the law or changing or manipulating it to their own
advantage.
5.3   |   Despotic Corruption
Despotic corruption occurs only when there is truly monolithic
rule by an unchallenged despot. Unlike most other forms of
corruption, despotic corruption plays no role in undermining
democracy because there is little or no democracy to under-
mine (though superficial or artificial vestiges may remain).
There are many historical examples of despotic corruption, but
at least for the past decade, Vladimir Putin's regime in Russia
provides a paradigmatic example (Wegren  2022; Zaloznaya
et al. 2022). Levitsky and Way (2025: 231) note that the US basis
in its Constitution and rule of law is sufficient to thwart a truly
despotic regime, though it is in danger of evolving into “com-
petitive authoritarianism, in which he formal architecture of
democracy…remains intact, but the system is not democratic be-
cause incumbents rig the game by deploying the machinery of
government to attack opponents(.) In the United States, despotic
corruption serves as a worst-­case scenario, implausible but not
impossible.
5.4   |   Impervious Corruption
In impervious corruption the public official's corrupt acts
take place in full public view; because the corrupt official has
learned to expect that government anti-­corruption guardians
will pose no threat of prosecution, significant ethical or moral
approbation, reprobation, impeachment, or any of the signif-
icant official responses, other than perhaps supporting the
corrupt act.
The definition of impervious corruption:
Impervious corruption occurs when political
officials engage in political corruption [as defined
above], but do not take care to shield their corrupt
acts because they have reason to believe they
are invulnerable to sanctioned punishment or
approbation from government officials empowered
by law to do so.
Impervious corruption resembles despotic corruption; but,
unlike despotic corruption, authoritarian or corrupt leaders
have not managed to completely subvert democracy or the
rule of legitimate law. If those public officials engaged in
impervious corruption are turned out of office (or age out or
voted out) with minimal damage to democratic institutions,
then its effects, even if massive, may prove temporary. But
impervious corruption also has the potential to permanently
alter government institutions and political culture, depending
on the type and depth of its impacts. What mitigates the long-­
term impacts of impervious corruption is the strength of po-
litical institutions, the quality and significance of the nation's
constitution, and the political expectations and actions of its
citizens.
Impervious corruption has much in common with deep corrup-
tion; the chief difference pertains not to the nature of the corrupt
acts but rather to the distinctive political ecology of impervious
corruption. Part of the political ecology of all democracies is
the institutional guardians charged with protecting the public
against political corruption.
Table 1 below provides a typology of political corruption and dis-
tinguishing features among the various types of corruption. One
point emphasized below is that each of the forms of corruption
can have negative impacts on a nation's democracy. However,
the major concern of this article is the role of impervious corrup-
tion in the deformation of democracy, in part because the very
fact that impervious democracy in high places is even possible
suggests that democracy is threatened.
A major theme of this article is that corruption and deforma-
tion of democracy go hand-­in-­hand. Figure  1 below depicts
components related to both corruption and deformation of
democracy. In the US, each branch of government is charged
with unique responsibilities for guarding democracy and
for guarding against corruption. The executive branch is
examined in three components, including the Office of the
President, which includes not only the President but the vice-­
president and the White House staff, the political bureaucracy,
referring to political officials appointed by the President, and
the professional bureaucracy, those career civil servants who,
until recently, continued to serve regardless of the party or
individual occupying the Presidency.
Having responsibilities for guarding democracy and against cor-
ruption does not ensure the responsibilities will be acted upon.
There are different reasons why guardians choose to ignore
their prescribed duties, including, among others, loyalty to su-
periors, political self-­interest, indolence, personal ambition, or
fear of retaliation.
Another crucial element of the model depicted below is the rec-
iprocity between corruption and deformation of democracy.
Deep corruption has the potential to undermine democracy
because, by definition, deep corruption is performed by public
officials who are bending or breaking laws or thwarting public
values to achieve personal ends. As democracy is undermined,
opportunities for corruption multiply, resulting in still greater
deformation of democracy (Caiden 1997; Warren 2004).

A final element of the model is the potential for the evolution of
corruption. Corruption is not stable, and neither are the types
of corruption (Jancsics 2019). As we have seen in sections on
corruption in the Trump administration, venal corruption can
transform into deep corruption or even impervious corruption

6   |   Impervious Corruption During the Gilded Age:
The Example of Boss Tweed
Donald Trump can be “credited” with being an innovator in
the realm of impervious corruption. But he is not the inventor.
An invention is something new in the world, an entirely new
product, process or design. By contrast an innovation entails
the adaptation of ideas into a new use. Nor is Trump the only
impervious corruption innovator. Before analyzing Trump's
unique contributions to impervious corruption, let us consider
those of another corruption innovator, one operating long ago,
in a different level of government, with a different political
ecology.
When the term political corruption is used, one person who
comes quickly to mind is William Magear Tweed (born in 1823,
died 1878), better known as Boss Tweed. 3 Tweed was the unchal-
lenged ruler of Tammany Hall, another synonym for corruption
but in the 19th Century just the popular name for the executive
committee of New York City's Democratic Party. Tweed was first
elected to public office in 1851 as a city alderman and just a year
later was elected to the US House of Representatives. Neither his
work in Congress nor as an alderman was nearly as notewor-
thy as his subsequent reign as king of Tammany Hall and his
control of the entire netherworld of mid-­19th Century New York
City politics. Tweed and his cronies (the Tweed Ring) enriched
themselves in wide variety of ways, including not only the usual
political kickbacks for patronage and government contracts but
also a number more imaginative enterprises such as with false
vouchers for government expenditures, faked leases on nonexis-
tent properties, payment for votes from nonexistent voters, and
“legal services” to large corporations in need of favor such as
targeted public works or licenses.
Deep corruption (in the sense used here) was at the root of the
Tweed Ring's ability to accumulate millions of dollars (in mid-­
1850 dollars no less) from their corrupt activities. In 1870, a new
city charter was approved that gave primary responsibility for
City audits to political controllers (i.e., the Tweed Ring) and,
with it, unlimited control of the city treasury. The Tweed Ring
illustrates the capacity for legal manipulation of political struc-
tures, laws and norms for private gain, and exemplifies deep cor-
ruption, as defined above.
Tweed's regime exemplified not only venal and deep corruption
but also impervious corruption. During Tweed's tenure, almost
all those elected as governor of New York, speaker of the New
York State assembly and, of course, mayor of New York, were
handpicked by the Tweed Ring. Since so many of the powerful
public officials owed their office to Tweed, and were held ac-
countable by the Tweed Ring, we can cite Boss Tweed as one of
the early US innovators in impervious corruption.

Two important points from the Tweed example require empha-
sis. The first is that Tweed, as powerful as he was, was limited
in the extent to which his corruption could damage American
democracy. First, his damage was confined by his geographic
hold—New York. Second, the Tweed case underscores that im-
pervious corruption does not require that the corrupt individ-
ual's perception of imperviousness is correct. Partly due to the
indefatigable efforts of reformers such as Lincoln Steffens and
Samuel J. Tilden (later New York Governor and Democratic

but was extradited and returned to the City's Ludlow Street Jail,
where he died of pneumonia in 1878.
7   |   Political Corruption and the Trump
Administration4
The remainder to this article focuses squarely on a contempo-
rary political boss, one even more innovative in his corruption
than was Boss Tweed, namely Boss Donald Trump. Trump's
actions are examined in connection with venal, deep, and im-
pervious political corruption. Further, the article considers the
dynamics of corruption, including its evolution.
7.1   |   Venal Corruption: Trump and the Art
of the Deal
One constant is Donald Trump's adult life is an obsession with
salesmanship, whether real estate, memecoins, or himself. A
personal landmark that does much to explain this obsession is
Trump's ghost-­written book The Art of the Deal. The book is
not a source of Trump's own, original ideas, but it has served
him as a guide to action, and the book has even become a sort
of personification. What started as a best-­selling book featur-
ing his brand but not his direct participation5 later resulted
in life-­imitating-­art, first in business and entertainment
and then in politics. When Trump ventured into politics, he
brought his Art of the Deal persona with him, including its
governing principles: (1) every transaction is an opportunity
to negotiate, threaten, or bully; (2) instincts (at least Trump's)
are more important than evidence; (3) people can be divided
into just two categories,”winne” or “loser,” (4) when in doubt
or in the wrong, sue. Trump has had no difficulty transferring
these principles from business to politics, perhaps because he
has developed few, if any, guiding principles since those artic-
ulated in Art of the Deal.
Trump's acts of venal corruption are examined here, much
stemming from his seemingly obsessive need to profit on both
a grand scale (Qatar-­gifted aircraft) and in the smallest ways
(e.g., MAGA key chains, refrigerator magnets, and dog collars).
While the primary focus is on Trump's venal corruption during
his second Presidential administration, one can find many
instances in his first term and well before his political career
began (Ackerman 2023). Many instances of corruption are sim-
ple ones that involve bad business practices. According to one
accounting (Reilly 2025), Trump and his businesses have been
defendants in at least 60 lawsuits and many government filings,
each involving accusations that Trump, either personally or his
businesses, failed to pay people for work they performed (inter-
estingly, some of these suits were filed by attorneys hired by him
to defend him in failure-­to-­pay cases).
Other cases of corruption center not on employees but cus-
tomers. One fine example is Trump University, an unaccred-
ited, chiefly online university that deceived thousands of
students and was closed after three lawsuits for “deceptive
practices.” One of the lawsuits was settled for $25 million

Making judgments about political corruption is not always
easy. Does the sale of the above commercial offerings comprise
venal corruption? Or does one judge this personal enrichment
as simple greed or tackiness, but not corruption? Or perhaps
President Trump is following the advice of a notable predeces-
sor, another famous Tammany Hall boss, George Washington
Plunkitt. Boss Plunkitt ruled New York City politics beginning
in the early 1920's, was charged by local newspapers as being
corrupt. Defending his honesty, Plunkitt famously explained,
“there's an honest graft, and I'm an example of how it works. I
might sum up the whole thing by sayin': I seen opportunities and
I took “em.” (Riordan 1995, 3–16). Perhaps Trump's inveterate
merchandizing is “honest graft.” However, not all his commer-
cial activity could easily be construed as “honest graft.” We find
an excellent example in the President' dealings with Crypto, per-
haps the most egregious of his corrupt activities thus far in his
second term.
8   |   Venal Corruption Begets Deep Corruption
Begets Impervious Corruption: The Case of Trump
and Cryptocurrency
In his personal financial dealings, President Trump has walked
a fine line along legal requirements and public expectations (e.g.,
Rosenthal  2016; Kiel and Buettner  2024; Yourish et  al.  2025;
Petry 2025). For example, he says that he will release his tax
record and then does not; or he says his investments are in a
public trust, but they are controlled by family; or they are in his
own grasp.
One distinctive example of Trump's crossing the line is his
work with cryptocurrency and his memecoins. This case shows
how venal corruption can evolve into deep corruption and then
change again into impervious corruption.

announced that he would be selling a new token, a memecoin,
known as $TRUMP. This item would be launched by World
Liberty Financial, a company developed as a platform for those

veloped working agreements to be paid for helping promote
the company. In November 2025, the company announced
that it had 20,000 or more token holders in what the company
called “A platform inspired by Donald J. Trump” (World Liberty
Financial 2025). If, indeed, its coins were purchased by more

10   |   Discussion
Has the United States body politic, which historically has
had institutional safeguards against the disease of political
corruption, now encountered a disease variety to which it
has little or no immunity? The apparent reluctance of insti-
tutional anti-­corruption guardians effectively to respond to
Trump's corrupt acts have dire implications for the deforma-
tion of American democracy. Historically, the US has relied
on Congress, the federal judiciary, statutory ethics, and anti-­
corruption investigatory agencies to respond to the misdeeds
of public officials. In most cases, especially during the 20th
and the 21st centuries, these institutions have been diligent
and have responded effectively to corruption. Now, much has
changed. Below, some arguments are given as to why we have
not been able to rely on the institutions charged with defend-
ing democracy or serving as anti-­corruption guardians. This
analysis is followed by some modest prescriptions ones—not
relying on increasingly feckless federal government institu-
tional guardians.

14   |   De Facto Corruption: Yet Another Innovation?
Having in an above section explored the destruction of exec-
utive branch anti-­corruption watchdogs, let us briefly turn to
the broader plot of scorched earth—the federal bureaucracy
and its personnel. Consider these questions: does a near-­
endless string of demonstrably harmful decisions and massive
budget cuts add up to a de facto corruption, thwarting public
law and public values? If so, what is the nature of this corrup-
tion and what are the thresholds at which cutbacks bleed over
into corruption?
These are complex issues, ones about which well-­meaning pub-
lic interest-­focused citizens may disagree. Rather than seeking
answers to questions about the ethics and morality of Trump's
various attacks on federal agencies and personnel, let us instead
ponder some further questions, ones that may put a useful frame
on the nature of de facto corruption.
1.	 If the President engages in massive firings, especially in
agencies that do not match his ideological preferences, is
that de facto corruption? What if some of those agencies
(e.g., the FBI) were involved in investigating him in his
first term?
2.	 If the agencies dismantled are ones created by Congress and,
by most interpretations of the law, require Congressional
approval, is the action illegal or de facto corruption or both?
3.	 What if Congress either ignores or fails to press its duties
to oversee entities it has created? Is that de facto corruption
and, if so, on whose part: Congress or the malfeasant polit-
ical official, or both?
Article II of the US Constitution sets duties for the President,
stipulating that the President has primacy in executing pol-
icy, but also stipulating that this duty entails taking care that
the legislation passed by Congress be “faithfully executed.” Is
it de facto corruption or deformation of democracy when the
President decimates agencies charged with frontline execution
of the laws duly passed by Congress? If Congress displays lim-
ited opposition to the large-­scale elimination of these entities
and their missions, is that the President's corruption or defor-
mation of democracy or the Congress' or both? Clearly, this is a
question not anticipated by the founding fathers—the Congress'
willing abrogation of its two most important duties: (1) ensur-
ing the execution of its own laws and related matters, and (2)
faithfully using its power of the purse and controlling the federal
budget and budget policy.

16   |   Conclusions
Despite Trump's rapid pace in undermining democracy through
corruption and consolidation of power, the citizens of the United
States retain many resources for checking authoritarianism and
corruption. If fully deployed, these resources are likely to pre-
vent Trump's deformation of democracy as well as the illegal
third term he has “joked” about.
One major asset the US has in fighting deformation of democ-
racy is its Constitution, which has withstood crises, has evolved
through amendments and judicial review interpretations, and is
today embraced by almost all citizens and policymakers. While
there are alternative interpretations to the Constitution and
some problematic aspects remain, such as the archaic electoral
college which has given the US five Presidents who received a
minority of votes, the US Constitution remains the legitimate

The US judicial system, despite recent Supreme Court rulings

Historically, the career public service has been one of the truly
stalwart institutions guarding the US from both corruption
and the deformation of democracy. President Trump's effort
to vilify the career public service is not any sort of innovation
(Milward and Rainey 1983; Chen and Bozeman 2014; Hendriks

from many past administrations. However, Trump has exceeded
all previous Presidents in his dedication to destroying unfavored
federal agencies and firing public employees en masse in the
name of efficiency (while passing a budget that increases the
deficit by more than $3 trillion over the next decade). Still, one
of the greatest strengths this nation has for guarding democracy
and standing up to corruption is the professional public service.
Wounded though it may be, this institution is not going away,
because it literally cannot, there are not enough Trump ideal-

to protect us in ways small and large. Trump has a penchant

(e.g., Sleepy Joe Biden, Crooked Hillary, Gavin Newscum) but

about as accurate as his others. An alternative meaning for the
so-­called deep state is that it is deeply committed to performing
its statutory duties, carrying implementing laws, and working
to achieve the legitimate missions and objectives their agencies,
with little regard to party or ideological preferences of the oc-
cupant of the White House. As Raadschelders (2025) observes,
“there is ample evidence that government oversight and regula-
tion and bureaucratic expertise are the guardrails of large-­scale
democracy(.)” They will continue to be.
The ultimate guardian against both corruption and the defor-
mation of democracy is not institutional but popular. True,
many American voters did freely choose to elect a President
that had a role in one of the most infamous anti-­democratic
episodes in American history, the January 6, 2021 mob insur-

Congress, killing and injuring capitol police, occupying the
offices of members of Congress, and spontaneously emitting
human feces for the purpose of leaving on the desk in the
office of then-­Speaker of the House Nancy Pelosi. President

the insurrection, a contribution which included a motivational
speech featuring the idea that Vice President Mike Pence “de-

Biden. Even today, Trump frequently rails about the “stolen
election” and Trump has pardoned more than 1500 persons
convicted for crimes related to the January 6 riots.
Regarding Trump and his most ardent acolytes, there is at pres-
ent little reason for optimism. But these most committed loyalists
are a small percentage of the US population, and their numbers
will continue to shrink with the accumulation of bad outcomes
from bad decisions. Political culture and political history suggest
an impending rapprochement. American citizens remain a stub-
bornly independent lot and show no signs of quickly or easily re-
linquishing their right to vote, their civil rights, or their right to
participate in politics. When citizens begin to feel the full effects
of the President's executive orders and witness Trump's contin-
ued efforts to tilt nearly all governance authority in his direction,
then things will change. While some citizens may have little
concern when a major political institution freely relinquishes
its foundational rights, they will respond differently when their
own rights are threatened. “Inalienable rights” motivate, espe-
cially in the full light of political corruption and the deformation
of democracy.

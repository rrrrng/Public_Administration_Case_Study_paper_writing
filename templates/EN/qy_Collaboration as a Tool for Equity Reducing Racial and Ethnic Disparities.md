ABSTRACT
Despite its importance in theory and practice, little is known about whether, how, when, and for whom a collaborative gov-
ernance strategy achieves socially equitable outcomes. Using a staggered difference-­in-­differences design and data from the
Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, we analyze how well Oregon counties that adopted collaborative governance bridge
racial disparities in healthcare access compared to non-­adopting counties. We find that collaborative governance is associated
with reducing racial and ethnic disparities in financial barriers to visiting a doctor after 3 years of implementation and that
such effects grow stronger over time. In addition, the estimated effect of collaborative governance on having a personal doctor
is greatest for Hispanics. This article points to potential benefits of a collaborative governance strategy that designs and scales
collaboration through a collaborative platform and collaborative governance regimes, sustains long-­term efforts to meet health
equity goals, and identifies and serves the most vulnerable populations.
1   |   Introduction
With a paradigm shift from government to governance, collabo-
rative governance has become widely accepted by scholars and
practitioners in public management (e.g., Ansell and Gash 2008;
Bingham  2011; Emerson and Nabatchi  2015; McGuire  2006;
Milward and Provan  2000; Provan and Kenis  2008;
Sirianni 2009). In general, scholars assert that collaborative gov-
ernance can promote the values of diversity, equity, and inclu-
sion (DEI) by engaging a diverse set of stakeholders (e.g., Kim
and Siddiki  2018), encouraging the inclusion of stakeholders
with various viewpoints in the decision-­making process (e.g.,
Kim 2024), and generating more equitable outcomes for disad-
vantaged members in a community (e.g., Dobbin et al. 2023).
However, collaborative governance is not without its own chal-
lenges, such as the time-­intensive nature of deliberative pro-
cesses or the large amount of financial and human resources
required for broad stakeholder engagement (Koontz et al. 2020).
The high costs of initiating and managing collaboration raise
the question of whether collaborative governance is worth the
effort. This question can be answered with normative or instru-
mental arguments.
Normative arguments in favor of collaborative governance begin
from the premise that it is intrinsically good (Freeman 1997).
Champions of collaborative governance argue that collaboration
is (or at least should be) a public value to be pursued in public
administration (e.g., Amsler  2016). For instance, advocates of
collaborative management often justify the need for collabora-
tion based on a value-­laden assumption that collaborations are
desirable when resolving conflicts, overcoming parochialism,
building civic capacity, and increasing democratic accountabil-
ity (Koontz and Thomas 2006). However, normative arguments
can generate and reinforce an orthodoxy that collaborative gov-
ernance is ‘the right way’ to manage public affairs.
In contrast, instrumental arguments seek to gauge whether, how,
when, and for whom collaborations lead to intended outcomes.

Advocates of this approach posit that collaboration is a policy
tool that requires objective evaluations of performance. However,
rigorous assessments of collaborative outcomes are among “the
most outstanding knowledge gap[s] in the study of collabora-
tive governance” (Gerlak et al. 2013, 10). This gap is particularly
wide when it comes to the evaluation of the “problem-­solving
capacity” of collaborative governance in promoting equitable
outcomes for individuals with racial, ethnic, gender, environ-
mental, socioeconomic, political, cultural, legal, lingual, or other
disadvantages (Rogers and Weber 2010).
To bridge this gap, we analyze whether, how, when, and for
whom a collaborative governance strategy consisting of a
collaborative platform and its connected collaborative gover-
nance regimes is an effective tool for advancing social equity
in their target regions. Specifically, we examine the collabora-
tive outcomes of Regional Health Equity Coalitions (RHECs),
externally directed collaborative governance regimes in-
tended to increase health equity in Oregon. Using a staggered
difference-­in-­differences design that compares counties that
adopted collaborative governance and others that did not in
the study sample, we explore whether and how four outcome
measures of health equity vary over time and across different
subgroups of the target population. We first review the key
theoretical lenses used in this study. Next, we provide back-
ground information about the empirical context for the study,
including the RHECs. After explaining the data, measures,
and methods, we present the results and conclude with a dis-
cussion of implications.
2   |   Theoretical Lenses
Two key theoretical lenses are used in this study: health equity
and collaborative governance. We discuss each lens below.
2.1   |   Health Equity
In general, equity can be defined as a fair distribution of re-
sources to members of a society. Equity theory, initially
developed and studied within the field of workplace psychology,
posits that individuals compare the perceived ratio of inputs
they provide to an organization and the outcomes they receive
from it with the perceived ratio for other individuals in the orga-
nization (see Adams 1965). In this respect, the concept of equity
fundamentally entails comparisons with others, and individuals
may have low levels of perceived equity if more resources (or
more opportunities to attain resources) are distributed to those
who bring similar inputs to a society. Therefore, in contrast with
equality, which emphasizes uniformity in distributions, policies
aiming for equity often result in unequal distributions if some
individuals or groups deserve (or need) more resources than
others, whether because of the characteristics of the recipient
(e.g., socio-­demographic factors), of the resource to be distrib-
uted (e.g., differential values to different recipients), or of the
decision-­making process (e.g., equal starting point, voting right)
(Stone 1988). While some scholars criticize government agen-
cies for treating social equity as a “nervous area of government”
(Gooden 2014) and for using a “myth of bureaucratic neutral-
ity” to shun it (Portillo et al. 2022), social equity remains a key
pillar of public administration and many policies and programs
are aimed at addressing systemic inequities in communities
(Frederickson 1990, 2015).
As one type of equity, health equity centers around the concept
of a fair (and often unequal) distribution of resources for under-
served individuals in a society (Sen 2002). One widely known
definition of health equity asserts that “ideally everyone should
have a fair opportunity to attain their full health potential”
(Whitehead 1991, 7). More specifically, health equity can be un-
derstood as the absence of disparities between advantaged and
disadvantaged social groups that differ in various social deter-
minants of health, such as race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status,
gender, and/or geography (Braveman and Gruskin 2003). One
way to achieve health equity is to remove systematic and unjust
differences in healthcare so that those disparities no longer af-
fect the health status of individuals (Whitehead 1991).
Although many factors shape health equity, such as education,
housing, income, safety, food, and environmental conditions
(Braveman et al. 2011; Marmot and Wilkinson 2005), healthcare
access is a major determinant that public health authorities have
the power to (perhaps most immediately) affect through legis-
lation or policy. Healthcare access equity is defined as “equal
access to available care for equal need, equal utilization for
equal need, equal quality of care for all” (Whitehead 1991, 8).
Healthcare access disparities, or the lack of healthcare equity,
can remove opportunities for disadvantaged groups to improve
their health conditions and pose a serious threat to health equity
in a society (Brown et al. 2000). Healthcare access disparities
have been a long-­standing issue in numerous countries and can
be exacerbated by crises like the COVID-­19 pandemic that isolate
vulnerable groups from necessary medical treatments (Shadmi
et al. 2020). Eliminating systematic and unfair obstacles to ac-
cessing healthcare services is crucial for achieving equity in
health status among all members of a society (Andrulis 1998).
Therefore, we focus on healthcare access disparities in this arti-
cle and analyze them as the main outcome variable of interest.
Scholars have suggested multiple public policy solutions to
address disparities in healthcare access and health status. In

may decide on and implement activities in a unilateral man-

surance mandate has been shown to reduce racial and ethnic
disparities in healthcare coverage, especially for Hispanics
and African Americans (Chen et al. 2016; Lillie-­Blanton and

health agencies to provide healthcare services beyond insur-

the quality of healthcare by directly providing immunization,
testing, and treatment services (Derose et al. 2011). Multiple
public health agencies might also work together by forming
a service providing network that aims to reduce dispari-
ties in primary care and preventive screening processes, but
without involving any actors outside of government (Politzer
et al. 2001).
Second, government agencies may choose to engage nongov-
ernmental stakeholders in deciding on and implementing ac-
tivities in a collaborative manner. More specifically, they can
direct the use of cross-­sectoral collaborative public health in-
terventions aimed at bridging healthcare access disparities.
Government-­directed collaboration in marginalized commu-
nities can promote a systems approach that accounts for com-
plex community contexts and uses local knowledge, expertise,
funding, and other resources (Emerson 2018). Including and
empowering underrepresented participants in collaborations
can influence interpersonal relationships and trust among
stakeholders, which in turn may affect their performance pos-
itively (Frankowski 2019; Johnston et al. 2011). For instance,
such collaborative approaches may reduce power imbalances,
build community capacity, contextualize prior policy inter-
ventions, integrate community-­specific values and practices,
and improve the sustainability of policy outcomes (Wallerstein
and Duran 2010).
2.2   |   Collaborative Governance
In examining cross-­sector collaboration, this study relies on
three key theoretical concepts: collaborative governance, collab-
orative governance regimes, and collaborative platforms. First,
collaborative governance is defined as a governing arrangement
in which autonomous actors span boundaries (e.g., organization,
sector, jurisdiction, geographic) to achieve a goal beyond what a
single actor can achieve alone (Ansell and Gash 2008; Bryson
et  al.  2015; Cooper et  al.  2006; Emerson and Nabatchi  2015;
Sirianni 2009).
Second, collaborative governance regimes (CGRs) are bodies
that enact collaborative governance. More specifically, a CGR
is “a particular mode of, or system for, public decision making
in which cross-­boundary collaboration represents the prevailing
pattern of behavior and activity” (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015,
18). Compared to other collaborative efforts, such as public-­
private partnerships, government outsourcing, and ad hoc col-
laborations, CGRs are more strongly oriented toward public
policy and management, representation of diverse interests by
autonomous actors, intentional norms and rules that facilitate
collaboration, and long-­term, repeated interactions among the
participants (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015).
Third, a collaborative platform is “an organization or program
with dedicated competences, institutions and resources for facil-
itating the creation, adaptation and success of multiple or ongo-
ing collaborative projects or networks” (Ansell and Gash 2018,
20). Collaborative platforms may not necessarily be a collabora-
tion of multiple actors but can be a stand-­alone nonprofit orga-
nization or government agency (Ansell and Gash 2018). They
facilitate the efforts of CGRs that work toward similar purposes
or the same policy area, for example, by providing financial, lo-
gistical, and human resources as well as structured guidance,
monitoring, and evaluation (Cochran et al. 2019). Collaborative
platforms enable the “scaling up” of collaborative governance
by initiating, supporting, and connecting similar collaborative
efforts across different locations (Yoon et al. 2022). Therefore,
collaborative platforms provide a viable solution to addressing
an important challenge in collaborative performance manage-
ment: can government agencies move beyond success stories of
a single collaborative arrangement and replicate and broaden a
collaborative franchise in multiple contexts?
We unite these three concepts to address the main research
questions of this study. Specifically, it assesses the instrumental
value of collaborative governance by examining the overall and
dynamic effectiveness of a multi-­layer collaborative governance
strategy with CGRs (Regional Health Equity Coalitions) and a
collaborative platform (Oregon Health Authority) that manages,
funds, and scales up those CGRs.
3   |   Empirical Setting
To investigate the instrumental value of collaborative gover-
nance, this study examines the health equity outcomes gen-
erated by Regional Health Equity Coalitions operating in the
state of Oregon. In the state legislation, Regional Health Equity
Coalitions (RHECs) are defined as “autonomous, community-­
led, cross sector groups focused on addressing health inequities
experienced by priority populations, at the policy, system, and en-
vironmental levels, with the leading priority being communities
of color” (Oregon Administrative Rules 950–020-­010). RHECs
are externally directed collaborative governance regimes estab-
lished and/or funded by the Oregon Health Authority, which
serves as a collaborative platform. They support community-­
driven and culturally specific collaborative activities to address
the social determinants of health disparities and promote health
equity at the county level. They place a priority on serving racial
and ethnic minorities in Oregon while serving other secondary
target populations, including LGBTQ individuals, immigrants,
refugees, and people with disabilities.
The first RHECs were established in Oregon in 2010. Additional
RHECs were created in 2011, 2014, and 2018, resulting in a total
of seven RHECs that serve 13 counties across the state. Twenty-­
three counties in Oregon do not have an RHEC in our study pe-
riod between 2006 and 2020. Because RHECs were created over
time and not all counties are served, we can investigate changes

Although the exact governance structure of each RHEC var-
ies and evolves, it is often composed of a fiscal agent, decision-­
making body, member organizations and individuals, and
subcommittees and work groups.1 Under the guidance of
decision-­making bodies called steering committees, advi-
sory groups, or general leadership teams, RHECs aim to use
consensus-­based and democratic decision-­making processes.
According to government reports published by the Oregon
Health Authority,2 intended outcomes of RHECs include
increased and authentic community engagement, strength-
ened organizational capacity, system change, social norm and
environment change, and policy change.3 A key approach that
RHECs take to reach these goals is lowering cultural and lin-
guistic barriers in deliberative processes that design and im-
plement community-­specific solutions for health equity. At
the initial stage, each of the RHECs conducts a community
needs assessment through in-­depth, authentic conversations
with the residents from communities of color. RHECs actively
engage residents of color in consensus-­based local policy-­
making processes and ensure that their voices are heard
throughout. To provide an illustrative example, one RHEC
held a town hall meeting with immigrants and refugee com-
munity members in Spanish and asked all English speakers,
including a mayor, a chief of police, and city government of-
ficials, to wear translation devices. This effort led to a better
articulation of the viewpoints of Spanish-­speaking residents,
who used to wear translation devices in previous meetings,
even when they outnumbered other English-­speaking partic-
ipants. Through many innovations like this in deliberative
forums, RHECs aim to facilitate more inclusive decision-­
making processes for underrepresented and underheard res-
idents in Oregon communities.
Through community conversations that encourage minority
residents to speak about their lived experiences and share
their thoughts about what needs to be changed and addressed
to achieve health equity, RHECs identify priority issue areas
(e.g., healthcare utilization, public transportation, housing,
food, child and elder care, environment, reproductive health,
mental health) that guide directions for establishing the stra-
tegic plans of RHEC and demand system-­level policy changes
for each community. As of October 2019, there were 24 com-
munity engagement events in 11 counties with more than 400
participants. Of the 24 events, 14 focused on the Latino popu-
lation and 8 used Spanish as the primary language spoken in
meetings.4
One of the major plans that emerged is providing healthcare in-
terpretation services and training healthcare interpreters in mar-
ginalized communities, which can further mitigate cultural and
linguistic obstacles in healthcare access. In collaboration with
the local Coordinated Care Organizations, multiple RHECs help
the healthcare interpreters to better connect and assist individ-
uals who face challenges in understanding medical terminology
in English and need culturally appropriate healthcare services.

4   |   Data and Measures
To measure the healthcare access of Oregon residents, we
use annual survey data from the Behavioral Risk Factor
Surveillance System (BRFSS). We chose the BRFSS dataset be-
cause it is the largest ongoing healthcare survey in the world
and because quantitative studies on minorities tend to be data-­
hungry. Also, to the best of our knowledge, it is the only dataset
that provides data on both the county of residence and health-
care access of Oregonians. County is the most granular geo-
graphic unit available. The scope of the data includes responses
from Oregon adults in the years between 2006 and 2020. The
BRFSS data uses a disproportionate stratified sample design and
provides weights that adjust for different probabilities of selec-
tion across the strata. The strata are determined based on geo-
graphic locations (i.e., area code and prefix combinations) and
local government jurisdictions. In the case of Oregon, the entire
state is one stratum. The final weights assigned to the individ-
ual respondents account for the unequal probabilities of selec-
tion based on their demographic characteristics, including age,
sex, and race/ethnicity, and correct for potential nonresponse
and noncoverage bias. To account for the stratified nature of the
dataset, we use the final sample weights and the “svy, subpop

While most BRFSS data are available to the public, the county
variable is restricted and can only be accessed at Research Data
Centers (RDCs). Since the independent variable of the study is

One point is worth noting regarding the restricted BRFSS
data. To ensure the confidentiality of personally identifiable
information of the respondents, true names of the counties are
masked and replaced with randomly generated numbers in the
final dataset. Also, the BFRSS data owner prohibits the disclo-
sure of any outputs that analyze a subset of counties that ad-
opted an RHEC in each year. Therefore, we are not authorized
to explore if different RHECs achieve different outcomes that
are specific to their target counties. Instead, we are only able
to analyze whether the existence of collaborative governance
makes a difference in health equity outcomes on average. While
we acknowledge this limited data availability, our analysis still
addresses an important gap in the literature, which is assess-
ing whether a macro-­level governance strategy for collaboration
delivers at all and achieves the intended outcomes of a collabo-
rative platform that supports externally directed collaborative
governance regimes in local communities.
We use four survey questions to measure healthcare access, all
of which were framed in terms of the past 12 months: (1) “Do you
have any kind of health care coverage?” (health insurance); (2)
“Did you visit a doctor for a routine checkup?” (annual checkup);
(3) “Do you have one person you think of as your personal doc-
tor?” (personal doctor); and (4) “Was there a time when you
needed to see a doctor but could not because of cost?” (financial
barrier). The health insurance, annual checkup, and personal
doctor variables are coded as 1 if the respondent answered “yes”
to the survey questions and as 0 if “no”. The financial barrier
variable is reverse-­coded: 1 if the respondent answered “no” and
0 if “yes”. Among these four outcome variables, we expect that
the effects of RHECs would be stronger for personal doctor and
financial barrier than for health insurance and annual checkup.
This is because the main activities of RHECs do not directly
provide or expand insurance coverage and medical checkups
but may alter the perception of minority residents about their
personal doctor and financial barrier. Also, given their activities
that center around lowering linguistic and cultural barriers for
non-­English speaking or immigrant residents, we anticipate that
their effects would be generally greater for Hispanic populations
than other non-­Hispanic minorities. Hispanics are the largest
racial or ethnic minority group in the United States and linguis-
tic and cultural barriers have been shown to be major obstacles
to healthcare access for Hispanics (Pearson et al. 2008; Rivers
and Patino 2006; Velasco-­Mondragon et al. 2016). We come back
to these expectations later in the results section.
Using the survey responses, we calculate racial and ethnic dispar-
ities in healthcare access in two ways, first, by looking at overall
racial and ethnic disparity in healthcare access (i.e., comparing
non-­Hispanic Whites to all other minorities combined), and sec-
ond, by looking at subgroup disparity in healthcare access (i.e.,
comparing non-­Hispanic Whites, non-­Hispanic Blacks, non-­
Hispanic Asians, Hispanics, and other groups). Unfortunately,
the number of individual observations is insufficient for pro-
ducing a valid aggregate measure of healthcare access disparity
for each minority subgroup at the county level. Therefore, we
analyze overall healthcare access disparity with a difference-­in-­
differences method at the county level. We then conduct an ex-
ploratory examination of subgroup disparity using a pooled linear
probability regression model at the individual level. We describe
the research design of each model in the following sections.
5   |   Methods
Using the difference-­in-­differences method at the county level, we
compare the changes in the overall racial and ethnic disparity for
adopting counties (the treatment group) and non-­adopting coun-
ties (the control group) before and after the implementation of col-
laborative governance (i.e., the creation of an RHEC). However,
if counties with wider pre-­existing disparities were more likely
to adopt collaborative governance, this could raise endogeneity
concerns in the full sample of 13 ever-­adopting counties and 23
never-­adopting counties. To mitigate those concerns, we create a
subsample where we compare five early-­adopting counties (i.e.,
counties that created an RHEC in 2010 or 2011) and eight late-­
adopting counties (i.e., counties that created an RHEC in 2014 or
2018) and limit the study period to years before 2014. We include
the subsample in the analysis because we expect that prior differ-
ences between treatment and control counties may affect both the
decision to adopt collaborative governance and that overall dis-
parities could be smaller if late adopters, rather than non-­adopters,
are used as a control group (Shybalkina and Bifulco 2019).
All difference-­in-­differences regressions in our study include
county fixed effects that control for unobserved differences
across counties that remain constant over time, as well as year
fixed effects that control for unobserved differences across years
that affect all counties equally. For example, year fixed effects
control for the adoption of Coordinated Care Organizations
across all 36 Oregon counties in 2012. This rules out an alter-
native explanation that the estimated effects from our analysis
reflect the impact of Coordinated Care Organizations on health-
care access equity. However, if any time-­varying factors have
heterogeneous effects on different counties and affect whether
and when counties decide to adopt collaborative governance,
they could lead to a violation of the parallel trends assumption,
which must be met to obtain unbiased estimators of the two-­
way fixed effects. This assumption requires that the trends in
the overall racial and ethnic disparity of treatment and control
counties have a similar slope in the absence of collaborative gov-
ernance. We conduct a series of analyses that indirectly test this
assumption and check whether they suggest any counter evi-
dence that may invalidate the main results.

the greatest increase in the number of total and Spanish-­speaking
healthcare interpreters until 2014 in Figure 2, which could have
reduced costs related to linguistic barriers in doctor visits. Yet,
the estimated coefficients on the personal doctor variable are
positive (i.e., widening the gap between non-­Hispanic Whites
and others) after 1 year of implementation. One possible expla-
nation for this finding is that the establishment of RHECs might
crowd out support for other existing government programs and
policies for health equity and that underdeveloped and young
collaborative arrangements may be ineffective at making up for
such reallocation of support (Mehdi and Nabatchi 2023).
Overall, the second key finding might suggest a ‘tipping point’
exists and that it may take time for collaborative outcomes on
social equity to emerge and gain momentum. This finding is
consistent with the theoretical expectation that a collaborative
process is time-­consuming (Ansell and Gash 2008) and empiri-
cal evidence that collaborative governance working in the public
health policy area evolves and requires a certain amount of time
to achieve its intended policy outcomes (Emerson 2018). Also, in
relation to previous studies that examine temporal variations in
collaborative outcomes, we confirm other findings that collab-
orative governance may produce more equitable outcomes over
time (Ahn and Baldwin 2024; Ulibarri et al. 2020).
Third, of the four variables for healthcare access, personal doctor
is the only one for which collaborative governance generates the
greatest benefits for the least advantaged subgroup (Hispanic).
For other variables, collaborative governance does not necessar-
ily allocate more access to the less advantaged. For the Hispanic
population, healthcare interpretation services evidenced in
Figure 2 may have been major factors that led to stronger effects
on the likelihood of having a personal doctor. This may explain
why activities related to lowering language and cultural barriers
to healthcare tend to distribute more benefits to Hispanics than
other minority subgroups, which is consistent with previous
findings in the literature (Cabral and Cuevas 2020; DuBard and
Gizlice 2008).
A main policy suggestion for the third main result is that collabo-
rative platforms should carefully identify and prioritize those who
have a greater need based on objective and subjective evidence
collected from the target populations (Alegría et al. 2016). While
it could be challenging to fine-­tune collaborative activities to cater
to their specific needs, collaborative platforms should still try to di-
agnose who needs which service more than others and distribute
more benefits to the more disadvantaged. In doing so, they may di-
rect and support the empowering processes of CGRs in a way that
encourages all historically disadvantaged participants to actively
raise their voice at the individual level, provides support and re-
sources to organizations that work to empower these individuals,
and drives holistic changes in the system context of communities
across policy areas and sectors.
8   |   Conclusion
Collaborative governance has the potential to advance social
equity by distributing more benefits to less advantaged popula-
tions. As the first study to empirically assess the distributional
outcomes of multiple externally directed CGRs in the health
policy area, this article links the public management and health
policy literatures. It explores macro-­level governance strategies
beyond lower-­level healthcare programs or government subsi-
dies and assesses their impact on public health outcomes at the
community level. Specifically, it examines whether, how, when,
and for whom collaborative governance achieves socially equi-
table outcomes in healthcare access. It finds that collaborative
governance can reduce racial and ethnic disparities in having
a personal doctor and perceived financial barriers to visiting a
doctor in the long term. The estimated effects become stronger
with more time and for the likelihood of having a personal doc-
tor for the Hispanics.
Our analysis is not without its limitations, which can guide addi-
tional areas of research in future studies. First, it does not parse out
potential heterogeneities in the collaborative outcomes depending
on various structural, procedural, and contextual characteristics
of the CGRs. The BRFSS data owner prohibited us from analyzing
individual effects of a single RHEC or a selected group of RHECs
in selected Oregon counties. Instead, it only allows aggregated
outcomes that include all RHEC adopters in the treatment group.
The lack of panel datasets on RHECs and their participants also
limits us from analyzing more direct outcomes on individual par-
ticipants of RHECs. If data allows, future studies can add more
nuance to the findings of this study by examining CGRs that have
great variations in institutional design with each other (e.g., Fung
and Wright  2001). For example, scholars can examine whether
collaborative outcomes are greater for CGRs that represent a more
diverse set of organizations and sectors in the participant makeup,
have facilitators who shape the collaborative process to be more
inclusive of diverse viewpoints, or work in areas where the issue
of health equity is more urgent or grave than others.
Second, the regression models in this study do not fully rule out
endogeneity issues that may affect both the RHEC adoption and
healthcare access levels. We acknowledge that potential endog-
eneity in treatment can create bias in the estimated effects. To
increase the internal validity of the results, future studies can
search for natural experiment settings where exogenous shocks
or discontinuities generate plausibly random variations in the
decision to adopt collaborative governance, even though such
opportunities are uncommon. If such cases emerge, they will

Nevertheless, this article has several implications for theory and
practice.
First, it indicates the overall effectiveness of a collaborative
governance strategy involving a collaborative platform that im-
plements and supports community-­specific CGRs in different
localities. This finding adds support to previous findings in the
literature on the roles of a collaborative platform in enhancing
and sustaining collaborative outcomes (Ansell and Miura 2020;
Bell and Scott Tyler 2020; Lee 2023; Lee and Liu 2024). Although
the empirical setting of RHECs does not allow us to compare
which specific characteristics of CGRs lead to greater outcomes
than others, the findings still show the joint effectiveness of a
multi-­level collaborative governance strategy. To achieve similar
outcomes in other regions beyond Oregon, practitioners should
consider scaling collaborative activities that reduce linguistic
and cultural barriers to accessing healthcare services and assist
minority residents with receiving eligible benefits from social
welfare and health programs.
Second, it emphasizes the importance of using collaborative pro-
cesses over an extended period of time. The findings indicate that
distributional effects of collaborative governance may snowball
over time for policy problems like financial barriers to visiting a
doctor and the absence of a personal doctor if participants keep
working together. In this respect, we suggest that a collabora-
tive governance regime may be a more appropriate approach for
achieving equitable outcomes than ad-­hoc collaboration because
of its long-­term, repeated interactions among the participants
and its capacity to address complex policy issues through mul-
tiple phases of collaborative actions (Mehdi and Nabatchi 2023).
Third, to make the best use of time and resources over a long
duration of collaboration, leaders and participants of collab-
orative governance should diagnose which subgroups are at
higher risk than others and prioritize serving the more vul-
nerable. In doing so, they may need to continuously monitor
how the healthcare access levels of different subgroups evolve
and adjust their activities and investments according to the
changing conditions. Altogether, these implications provide a
meaningful lesson for the Oregon Health Authority as it plans
to continue launching additional RHECs, as well as for other
collaborative platforms that leverage collaborative governance
strategies to advance social equity.

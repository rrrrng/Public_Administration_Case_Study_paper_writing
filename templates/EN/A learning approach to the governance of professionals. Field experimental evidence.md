Abstract
Performance management is used by governments worldwide to incentivize professionals in schools and other public organizations. Yet, much
research shows that these incentives may generate perverse dysfunctional effects. Based on a Bayesian model of learning, we propose that
simply providing professional teachers with performance information—without changing their extrinsic incentives—may be enough to make
them update their beliefs about their students and act accordingly. However, measuring performance may itself affect professionals’ behavior,
which makes it difficult to isolate the effect of providing performance information. We designed and preregistered a field experiment in which
we can isolate the effect of making performance information available to teachers and study how it affects their posterior beliefs and behavior
towards the students (N = 2028). The results confirm the primary hypothesis and therefore provide indications that the provision of information
itself may be effective in the governance of public organizations.
Key words: Bayesian learning; performance management; field experiment; education; teachers.
Governments often rely on performance management to con-
trol professionals in their administration and service organi-
zations (Moynihan, 2008; Heinrich and Marschke, 2010). On
the one hand, with little or no oversight of the bureaucracy,
there is a risk of low levels of service delivery (Raffler, 2022).
On the other hand, ample evidence shows that these perfor-
mance management systems often generate negative effects
for democracy (Bertelli and John, 2010; Boyne et al., 2009;
Kogan et al., 2016), inducing data manipulations in the police
force (Eckhouse, 2022), teachers cheating with student test
scores (Jacob and Levitt, 2003), more bureaucratization as a
response to poor school results (Meier et al., 2000), or wel-
fare workers increasing sanctions on poor people (Soss et al.,
2011b, 2008). This has led to calls for experimental studies of
alternatives to tight monitoring and accountability (Nathan
and White 2021).
Performance management relies on a cyclic combination
of (1) setting performance goals, (2) measuring performance,
and (3) delivering the performance information to the organi-
zations (Andersen, 2008, 543; Moynihan, 2008, 5–6; Kroll,
2015, 461). These performance management systems vary in
the extent to which incentives are directly or more loosely
tied to the performance output. In this study, we propose
that delivering performance information to professionals—
in a system with low incentives and without increasing the
incentives—may itself make them learn from the informa-
tion and change their behavior accordingly. This would be
an important insight because it may pave the way for achiev-
ing some of the benefits of making performance information
available without generating the negative effects that stem
from high-stakes incentives.
Based on a Bayesian model of learning, we argue that to
understand how professionals react to performance informa-
tion, it is crucial to take into account what their priors were.
If new information is consistent with their priors, they should
not change their behavior. Without knowing their priors, such
nonresponses might be interpreted as “passive” use (Moyni-
han, 2009), while they indeed used the information to update
their priors. Without taking priors into account, we could
even think of situations where a purposeful reaction would
be misinterpreted as a “perverse” (Moynihan, 2009) effect:
Imagine that a student performs poorly on a test, yet the
teacher decides to reduce the level of support for that stu-
dent. Without knowing the teacher’s priors about the student,
that may seem like a perverse effect perhaps driven by cream
skimming. However, it could be that the teacher’s prior about
that student was even lower than the test score, and that the
teacher therefore learns that the student may be better and
therefore needs less support than previously believed by the
teacher.
Testing the proposition that making performance informa-
tion available affects professionals’ beliefs and behavior is
difficult, though (Nathan and White, 2021). The act of mea-
suring performance in an organization may itself affect pro-
fessionals because they may anticipate that the information
will be used against (or in favor of) them at some later point,
or because the act of measuring itself may change their atten-
tion to the outcomes that are being measured. To solve this,

we designed and preregistered a field experiment including 82
public schools and more than 2,000 students.1 We repeatedly
measured the reading skills of students in both treatment and
control group using an IT-based reading test (i.e., treatment
and control group were exposed to the same number of tests
at the same time). However, only the treatment group received
information on the test scores during the intervention period.
The control group received the test results after the interven-
tion. Teachers were blinded to this difference in terms of when
they received the performance information. There was no dif-
ference in their incentives or the accountability pressure from
school principals or parents since they did not receive the test
results. We explicitly told the teachers in both experimental
conditions that we would not share the test results with oth-
ers. Therefore, we can isolate the cause of any observed effects
to the regular provision of information.
To see if treated teachers used the information to change the
level of support to the students, we exploited an online learn-
ing system in which we could monitor the level of support that
teachers provided to each individual student. The results are
consistent with our theorizing about professional learning. In
the treatment group, teachers updated their beliefs according
to the model, such that they placed relatively more weight on
the test scores (which they are informed about) and less on
their priors. Moreover, as specified in our preregistered, pri-
mary hypothesis, the teachers chose actions according to the
new information, such that students in the treatment group
received support that better matched their skill level.
These results demonstrate the potential of using perfor-
mance information—without increasing incentives—to pro-
mote learning in public organizations. In the conclusion, we
discuss how teachers’ detailed knowledge of their students
may mean that the effect of such information is smaller than
it would be in settings where public employees have much less
prior information about their clients.
Our study is related to research on accountability and
performance management systems, which has argued that
depending on the incentives, the uses of performance infor-
mation may be purposeful, passive, political or even perverse
(Moynihan, 2009). The declared intent of performance man-
agement regimes is that organizations use the information to
learn about their performance and use that to improve. On
the one hand, however, with no incentives to improve, the
reaction to performance information may be passive, and the
professionals may ignore the information. On the other hand,
strong political signals about desired levels of performance
seems to reduce managers interest in taking action, when these
political-aspiration levels are achieved (Holm, 2017). And if
incentives become too strong, they may have perverse effects,
as documented by Soss et al. (2011a) showing increased sanc-
tioning of low-income families as a response to performance
ratings and political ideology. Our results show that within
a specific system of incentives, providing relevant informa-
tion does change the behavior of professionals in a purposeful
way. It may be important, though, to know the priors of the
decision makers, in order to validly assess whether responses
are passive, perverse, or purposeful. We also note that our

Our study is also directly relevant to studies in public
administration on Bayesian learning. A few studies have used
Bayesian Learning as a theoretical foundation without empiri-
cally testing the individual components. Andersen and Nielsen
(2020) find that testing students (‘ measuring performance”)
and making the test results available to teachers, parents and
students themselves (“delivering performance information”),
lead to improved student learning (“performance”) two years
later. The study builds upon a Bayesian model of learning,
even though there are no data on prior beliefs. Meier et al.
(2015b) theorize on how past performance of the organiza-
tion as well as the performance of other organizations, may be
used as indications of managers’ priors. The theory thereby is
closely related to March and Simon (1958), who suggest that
managers’ decisions are not only a result of absolute perfor-
mance levels, but the gap between performance and aspiration
levels (see also Holm, 2017). What Meier et al. (2015b) por-
tray as a rational, Bayesian reaction to past performance is
framed as biases in several previous studies (Petersen et al.,
2019; Mikkelsen et al., 2023). For instance, they see it as a
“negativity bias,” if teachers react to performance informa-
tion showing low students performance relative to prior test
score. We contribute to this early literature on Bayesian learn-
ing in public administration by directly measuring teachers’
prior beliefs and their posterior beliefs, and by showing how
the difference between priors and new information is relevant
for understanding their posteriors and reactions.
In a newer study, Damgaard and James (2024) combine the
focus on performance management and Bayesian learning by
arguing that if public performance information should be used
to improve accountability for public service providers, it is
necessary that the users of information—in their case parents
choosing schools—update their prior beliefs about schools in
an (approximate) Bayesian manner. In contrast to the authors’
expectations, parents did not react much to positive informa-
tion showing that their school performed better than parents
expected. They reacted more strongly to negative information.
This provides evidence against the theory of motivated rea-
soning suggesting that people only or predominantly react to
new information, when it supports their existing views. Their
results thereby resonate with Coppock’s (2023) research on
public opinion showing that citizens do tend to change their
posterior attitudes in the direction of new evidence—even if
their posteriors are largely dominated by their priors. The
strong influence of priors explains why, for instance, Repub-
licans and Democrats may continue to differ in their (pos-
terior) opinions, even after presented with the same piece of
persuasive information.
We contribute to these more recent studies by showing in
a field experiment that new information not only influence
attitudes and beliefs, but also have consequences for teach-
ers’ behavior. Effects of new information on attitudes are
small and decay fast. Within ten days they are between one
third and one half of the initial effect Coppock (2023, 14).
Therefore, it is not evident that new information would influ-
ence teachers’ behavior over the course of four months (even
if they receive four reports with new information along the
way). Furthermore, whereas parents may have very uncertain

In sum, our proposed use of performance information may
pave the way for a new approach to performance manage-
ment in public organizations. The measurement and use of
performance information is already widely used in public
organizations (Moynihan, 2008). Incentives as in rewards or
sanctions are sometimes directly coupled to the fulfillment
of performance goals (Andersen et al., 2025), while at other
times there is only a weak connection as when the publi-
cation of performance may give organizations an indirect
incentive to attract customers or clients through better per-
formance. What we show in this study is that performance
information influence the behavior of teachers even when
the information is concealed to anyone else than the teacher.
The information may influence teachers’ feelings of personal
achievement and adherence to collective norms. In this way
the information may influence their intrinsic motivation (and
in some sense their incentives, if these are defined in a very
broad sense). However, in our study, we did not change the
extrinsic incentives, yet we still saw the expected effect on
behavior. In that sense, our study is an indication that some
of the intended benefits of performance management may
come from providing the information to the frontline work-
ers themselves, without increasing the external incentives and
thereby risking some of the unintended, maybe even perverse
effects.
Theoretical model
Main model
Performance management and more broadly New Public
Management build at least to some extent on ideas from
public choice and principal-agent theory (Hood, 1991, 5).
Based on classic assumptions about information asymmetry
(the principal cannot observe the behavior of the agent) and
conflict of interests (the agent is self-interested and may there-
fore not pursue the goals of the principal), the generic solution
is to measure the performance of the professionals (their out-
put or outcome), deliver that information to the organization,
and reward high performance.
Relaxing the assumption of conflict of interests—by allow-
ing that some professionals care about the policy outcomes
(e.g. better health for patients or more learning for students;
Brehm and Gates, 1997; Gailmard and Patty, 2007)2—we
argue that making information about performance available
to the professionals may itself contribute to better perfor-
mance. We study a system in which incentives at baseline are
weak, which means that the risk of dysfunctional reactions is
low, but also that the chances of intended behavior are low. In
contrast to traditional performance management approaches,
which would increase both incentives and use of performance
information, we study what happens if we only increase the
availability of information, without changing the incentives.
In our model, the professionals choose a policy or an action
(a type of treatment or a level of support), at, at time t based
on their prior beliefs, beliefst about a state of nature, 𝜔t, in
our case, the skill level of the student, and other factors, 𝜖t,
including the incentives facing the professional:
at = f(beliefst(𝜔t), 𝜖t)
(1)
At time t + 1, they choose a new action based on their
posterior beliefs, beliefst+1, about 𝜔t+1:
at+1 = f(beliefst+1(𝜔t+1), 𝜖t+1)
(2)
If other factors such as incentives are held constant, 𝜖t = 𝜖t+1,
a difference between posteriors and priors should therefore
result in a change in the action:
Δa = at+1 – at = f(beliefst+1(𝜔t+1), 𝜖t+1) – f(beliefst(𝜔t), 𝜖t)
= f(beliefst+1(𝜔t+1) – beliefst(𝜔t))
(3)
Therefore, an important question is how professionals update
their beliefs about the state of nature. In models of expertise,
the professionals choose whether to invest in expertise. If they
invest, they learn about the state of nature; if not, they retain
their prior beliefs (Bendor et al., 2001; Gailmard and Patty,
2007, 876). As the cost of acquiring expertise approaches
zero—such as when professionals automatically receive sys-
tematic information on their clients—we expect that (on aver-
age) they will use this information to update their beliefs.
In line with Rockoff et al. (2012), we therefore propose a
simple Bayesian model of updating in which professionals
update their posterior beliefs at time t + 1 based on their pri-
ors at t and any new information they acquire either through
their own observations or from information provided—such
as systematic information from tests of students:
beliefst+1(𝜔t+1) = f(beliefst(𝜔t), observationst+1
(𝜔t+1), informationt+1(𝜔t+1))
(4)
All three sources of posterior beliefs are assumed to be imper-
fect signals of 𝜔. Combining these models, we reach the
following result:
Δa = f(f(beliefst(𝜔t), observationst+1(𝜔t+1),
informationt+1(𝜔t+1)) – beliefst(𝜔t))
(5)

Information should be correlated with priors since both are
imperfect signals of the state of nature. (2) Since observations
in period t and information are both signals of the state of

for that student in order to challenge her or him more. Vice
versa, if the test score is low relative to the teacher’s prior, the
level of difficulty should be reduced.3
In many settings, there is a nonlinear relationship between
the chosen action (type of treatment) by the professional and
the outcome (response). Many medications may be ineffective
or even toxic if given to cure the wrong type of disease, so the
better the physician knows the state of the patient, the better
she can prescribe the correct type of treatment. If students are
taught at too high (low) level with too little (much) support
compared to their current level of skills, they learn less than if
teaching is tailored to their skill level (Banerjee et al., 2017).
To be clear, our model does not claim that information
would be effective in the absence of any incentives. However,
within a specific set of incentives, new information would
be used by professionals to get closer to the desired pol-
icy outcome. We argue that by controlling information flows
to professionals, i.e., providing them with relatively precise
information about an outcome the managers care about as
opposed to other outcomes the managers care less about (cf.
Holmstrom and Milgrom, 1991), the managers are reducing
agency costs to themselves by biasing agent behavior toward
that concern. Whether the costs of the information system out-
weigh the agency costs depends, of course, on the size of both.
Our aim is to demonstrate that providing information may
itself affect professionals behavior in a predictable direction
without increasing the incentives.
Boundary conditions
In our preregistration, we had considered two extensions to
the simple Bayesian learning model. However, the study was
not fully powered to test these (which is why we distinguished
between the primary and the secondary hypotheses in the
preregistration). Therefore, we are cautious to conclude on
these results, but we see an avenue for future research study-
ing how these boundary conditions influence the impact of
performance information.
First, we would expect (based on Bayes’ theorem) that the
reactions to new information would depend on (1) the cer-
tainty of the professionals’ priors and (2) the consistency of
the direction of the gap (both pre-registered as secondary
hypothesis). Related, the extent of interaction between the
professional and the citizen might importantly affect the pre-
cision of the professional’s priors and thereby either limit
or increase the potential for learning from the new infor-
mation. Second, the actions needed to target specific citi-
zens or clients may differ in their costliness. Therefore, the

ence what actions are taken towards each individual (Junge,
2022). For instance, spending additional time on low perform-
ing students might be costlier than just providing them with
easier materials. This might of course influence the dynamics
in responses (though the learning process might be the same).
Previous research support that perceptions of the costliness of
helping specific citizens significantly affect their prioritizations
(Baviskar, 2019; Guul et al., 2021; Döring and Jilke, 2023).
Here we also note that several studies show that biases might
enter the prioritization at the front line under high workload
(Andersen and Guul, 2019; Bell and Meyer, 2024), and thus
learning effects might be larger related to minority citizens
(also pre-registered as secondary hypothesis).
A couple of other boundary conditions are beyond the scope
of our empirical test, but may be highly relevant for future
research. So far we have assumed a baseline system in which
incentives are relatively weak and the risk of dysfunctional
responses therefore low. If incentives are strong there is a risk
of dysfunctional behavior, and we could speculate about sit-
uations in which increasing the availability of performance
information would push towards more dysfunctional behav-
ior. For instance, if teachers at baseline are only incentivized to
support a subgroup of students (those around a performance
cutoff), or only some performance dimensions (academic skills
and not socio-emotional skills), then new performance infor-
mation may reinforce dysfunctional responses by making the
professionals optimize along the incentivized dimensions. Pre-
vious studies find strong strategic and dysfunctional responses
in systems with strong incentives (Jacob and Levitt, 2003;
Meier et al., 2000; Soss et al., 2011b; Eckhouse, 2022). In
these situations, the net effect of new information may depend
on the strength of these incentives combined with the pref-
erence distribution within the workforce. If incentives are
strong and many teachers are only self-interested (do only
pursue the incentives and disregard the goals of the organiza-
tion), the net effect may become negative. In contrast, Burgess
et al. (2021) find positive effects of teachers observing, eval-
uating and providing feedback to each other, “even without
attaching (explicit) incentives.”
Finally, previous research shows that the source of infor-
mation importantly affects how professionals respond to per-
formance information at the organizational level (Petersen,
2020). The same might be true at the disaggregated level.
In sum, the effect of new information envisaged by our the-
oretical model, may depend on various contextual factors.
However, in comparison to previous theory on performance
management—which would expect no response in settings
with low incentives, and dysfunctional responses in settings
with strong (and maybe misaligned) incentives—we argue that
at least in some settings, information itself may lead to func-
tional responses, even without changing the incentives for
reacting to the information.

Institutional setting—the Danish school system
We study the isolated effect of information in a Danish school
setting. The Danish school system consists of 10 years of
compulsory schooling from students are around six years
old (Kindergarten class) until they are about 16 years (ninth
grade). Students generally tend to stay in the same class from
Kindergarten class to ninth grade (unless they move to another
school). Teachers in Danish (i.e., reading and writing) typi-
cally stay with the same class from first grade to third or fourth
grade. Therefore, in the middle of second grade, when our
intervention began, teachers have typically known their stu-
dents for one and a half years, and they may have formed an
impression of the students’ reading skills.
About 80 percent of the students attend a public school, and
the remaining students (with a few exceptions) attend private
schools. At the time of our intervention in 2018 it was manda-
tory for all public school students to take a national, stan-
dardized, computerized, adaptive reading test in the spring
in grades 2, 4, 6, and 8. The test draws items from an item
bank, so the students receive different items if they take the
same test twice. The algorithm estimates the skill level of
the students based on the difficulty of the items and the stu-
dents’ responses. Mandatory tests are taken in the spring. On
a voluntary basis, teachers can also test their students in the
fall using the same test system. Comparative research indi-
cates that Danish school teachers are delivering their service
with relatively high levels of discretion compared to US school
teachers (Meier et al., 2015a)
Recruitment and sample selection
We collaborated with a provider of digital learning tools, who
invited schools and individual teachers to sign up second grade
classrooms for participation. They were truthfully told that
our research project was about differentiated instruction. All
participants could use the provider’s digital learning tool free
of charge in the project period. Schools and teachers were
contacted through various information channels (website, e-
mails, newsletter). 114 teachers and their 2,324 students in 82
schools signed up for the trial.
Before starting the recruitment of schools, we made power
analyses. One of them showed that with 114 teachers, which
ended up signing up, intraclass correlation of 0.20, and R2
of 0.50, we had 80% power to detect a standardized effect
size of 0.20. With randomization at the school level, power
is a little smaller. We therefore decided that we only would
preregister one primary hypothesis. If we wanted to test more
hypotheses on equal terms, we would need to adjust for mul-
tiple hypothesis testing, which would mean lower power for
any given effect size. Low power not only means that there is
risk that true effects will not be detected. If a study is under
powered and nevertheless reaches statistical significance, the
effect size is exaggerated (a magnitude error) and may even
be in the wrong direction (a sign error) (Button et al., 2013;
Gelman and Carlin, 2014).
Table 1 compares participants to all other second grade stu-
dents in the country. We see that mothers to the participating
students have a little shorter education, and students are on
average a little older (8.6 years compared to 8.1 in the rest
of the country). Other than that, the sample is similar to the
population on these observed variables.
Randomization procedure
We cluster randomized at the school level to avoid spill-over
effects, and we used stratas to increase the statistical power
of the trial. The 82 schools that signed up for the trial were
placed in 22 strata (16 strata of four schools and six strata
of three schools. We added six ghost school observations
with random values on the strata variables to make it ran-
dom which schools ended up in the three-school strata and
whether one or two schools were assigned to treatment within
these strata. Table 2 shows that the randomization created
two well-balanced groups with only a few significant differ-
ences (specifically maternal education and income) that, how-
ever, still could affect the results. Therefore, as a robustness
check, we conduct analyses both with and without control for
background characteristics. Results are similar in substantial
terms.

Preregistration, ethics, and blinding
Before the beginning of the intervention we preregistered the
study with the following primary hypothesis: “Our primary
hypothesis is that the provision of information makes the
teachers more likely to change the level of help for individual
students in the direction of the gap between the teacher’s prior
beliefs about the students’ skill level and skill level as assessed
by the reading test (i.e., more help for students with lower
reading skills than expected by the teacher prior to the inter-
vention and less help for students with better reading skills
than expected) and that this effect is stronger the larger the
gap.”6
We sent an information letter to the participating teach-
ers and allowed the students (or their parents) to withdraw
their contribution to the data collection. As mentioned, we
explained honestly to the teachers that the project was about
differentiated instruction, and we informed the participants
upfront that they would participate in different versions of
the program, so we used no deception. The treatment con-
sisted only in a time-lag in the provision of information. The
treatment was blinded to the participants in the sense that
we did not inform them that the the other group of teach-
ers received the test results continuously versus at the end
of the intervention. Carrying out the reading tests and using
the digital reading tool are common practice in schools and

Measures
Teacher decisions about level of difficulty. Our preregistered,
primary outcome is the level of difficulty that teachers decided
for each individual student using the digital reading tool. In
the digital tool, students read books at four different levels
of difficulty (coded 0 to 3). At the lowest level, the text was
read aloud to the students, and each word was highlighted. At
the second level, students could access different types of text-
to-speech help depending on word type and difficulty. At the
third level, text-to-speech help was only available for the most
difficult words. At the fourth and highest level, students read
the text without any help. The positive effect of the digital
learning tool compared to the active control group using tra-
ditional, paper-based reading materials has been documented
in a cluster randomized trial (Gissel and Andersen, 2021).
Teachers could assign their individual students to the difficulty
level that they found appropriate. If students receive too much
support, they do not practice reading difficult words. If stu-
dents receive too little support, they are not able to read the
text and may give up. The provider of the digital learning tool
that we used for the intervention generously offered all partic-
ipants free access to the tool. The teachers could differentiate
their teaching according to the needs of the individual students
in other (unmeasured) ways. Thus, the data from the learning
tool constitutes a conservative measure of the possible effects
of providing teachers with information about the reading test
scores of their students in the sense that we only measure one
of potentially multiple actions. So if teachers also respond by
changing other types of support, these effects would come on
top of what we capture. Yet, it is not necessarily a conserva-
tive measure of other actions, since other actions may require
more effort by the teachers. We use the last chosen level of
difficulty (posterior level of action) at the end of the trial as
our primary measure.
Teacher beliefs about student reading skills. To measure the
teachers’ priors at baseline, we asked the teachers to evaluate
the reading abilities of all their students on a scale from 0 to
10. We asked the teachers to state the lower and upper bound
of their assessment of the student skills (using a slider with
two markers). We use the mean of the interval as a measure
of their belief. We intentionally did not specify on what scale
they were supposed to evaluate the students (e.g., compared
to the national average or the class average) since we wanted
to let the teacher’s own perceptions dominate the evaluation.
After the intervention, we asked the teachers to evaluate all
their students similarly to the evaluation before treatment. We
use the posterior belief as a secondary outcome measure. We
standardized both measures within classrooms to take into
account differences in the teachers’ distribution of students
across classrooms.

Students’ reading skills. Students’ reading skills are mea-
sured by (1) a national, standardized, computerized test and
(2) the four brief reading tests that we developed for the study.
Since our treatment relates to the brief reading tests, this is our
main measure of reading skills in our analysis. We created a
simple measure of reading skills combining both the informa-
tion about reading speed and comprehension by taking the
mean reading speed across texts multiplied by the number of
correct answers to the comprehension questions. Thereby, fast
readers who did not grasp the content of the materials were
penalized, and slower readers with a good grasp of the materi-
als were rewarded. (We note that the teachers could weigh this
information differently.) We standardized these results within
classrooms to take into account differences in the distribu-
tion of reading skills across classrooms. In the Results section,
we demonstrate the validity of our simple measure of read-
ing skills by showing its correlation with the comprehensive,
national tests.
Gap between teacher beliefs and test results. We created a
measure of gap between teacher beliefs and test results by cal-
culating the simple difference between the two measures (Test
results - Prior beliefs) meaning that positive values signal that
the student have better reading skills according to the test
result than the teacher initially believed and negative values
signals that the student have worse reading skills according to
the test result than the teacher initially believed (and close to
zero indicating beliefs about the student’s reading abilities that
are close to the test score measure of student reading abilities).
Consistency of the information signal. We measured the
consistency of the information signal by first calculating the
gap between the teacher’s priors and the available test results
from each report, second recoding the direction of the gap
such that a negative gap equals –1 and a positive gap equals
+1 for each report and then third summarizing the direction
of the gap across reports. Thus, the Information signal consis-
tency has a range from –4 (negative gap signal in all 4 reports)
to +4 (positive gap signal in all 4 reports).
Finally, we used unique personal identifiers to link the mea-
sures and to obtain demographic data on all participating
students concerning gender, ethnicity, maternal education,
income, and occupational status.

Conclusion
The use of performance management by governments are
widespread and the negative and perverse consequences of
that are well-known. We proposed—based on a Bayesian
model—that simply providing professionals with perfor-
mance information can be enough to make them update their
beliefs and act accordingly. Our research design allowed us
to isolate the effect of providing student performance infor-
mation to teachers, while holding constant both the act of
measuring performance and the level of financial and other
incentives. The results showed not only that treated teachers
updated their beliefs about the students in accordance with the
information, they also reacted to this information by adjust-
ing the level of support to the students. The results are in
accordance with our simple Bayesian-learning theory and our
preregistered primary hypothesis, and they thereby indicate
that providing performance information to professionals—
without changing their incentives—may be a way forward
for performance management that benefit from the potential
learning effects while avoiding some of the unintended per-
verse effects that have been documented in previous research.
However, it is important to consider the theoretical and
empirical generalizability of these results. The results are
in accordance with what has been called “a simplified
Bayesian-learning framework” (Hjort et al., 2021, 1455–
1456). Our data only partly support a secondary hypothesis
based on a more complex Bayesian model. We did see some
indications that teachers factored in the consistency of the
information signal, but not the uncertainty of their own pri-
ors. These results should be interpreted in the light of limited
statistical power (which is why we preregistered this only as
a secondary hypothesis), as well as other explanations. It may
be the case that our way of measuring teachers’ uncertainty
(using a slider with an upper an a lower bound) does not
capture their uncertainty well enough. It may also be because
teachers’ decision-making processes do not take into account
their uncertainties. At the same time, theories on information
processing and decision making may also be able to explain
the results. In their study of teacher performance informa-
tion, Rockoff et al. (2012) also use a simple Bayesian learning
model and find indications that school principals take into
account the uncertainty in the information.
It is also important to note that the effort involved in our
primary outcome measure—i.e., teachers adapting the level
of support within the digital reading tool—is somewhat lim-
ited. On the one hand, leveraging provision of performance
information without changing the financial incentive may not
be enough to generate behavioral changes that require more
effort. In a field experiment in India, Muralidharan and Sun-
dararaman (2011) find improved student performance when
teachers had stronger private incentives (pay for performance
bonuses). It is not clear if our results generalize to such sit-
uations that aims to create more costly behavioral changes.
On the other hand, professionals always work within some
sets of incentives and types of accountability. Even if their
salary may not be directly tied to concrete measures of perfor-
mance, they typically run the risk of being fired if they do not
perform at all. In that way they always face some types of per-
formance incentives. Our study is conducted in a setting with
some basic levels of incentives, but without strong incentives
tied to the performance of the individual students. Thereby
the results indicate that information provision itself may have
positive effects even without strong financial incentives. It
is also worth noting that while we find positive effects of
making performance information privately available to teach-
ers, Bergman and Hill (2018) find that making information
about individual teachers’ performance public increases sort-
ing of high-performing students into high-performing teach-
ers’ classroom, thereby potentially increasing inequality in the
educational system. Since the information was only made pri-
vately available to teachers we do not expect that they would
have reacted dysfunctionally (as, for instance by cheating on
the tests). We do not observe the process from we deliver per-
formance information until teachers update their posterior
beliefs. We interpret this as learning process, but we cannot
rule out that this process may also involve other psychological
mechanisms (such as anxiety).
Another concern might be that treated teachers reacted
within the digital reading system as a consequence of a
researcher demand effect (similar to Hawthorne effects).
However, we note that teachers were blinded to the treatment
condition (i.e. whether they received the student performance
information during or after the intervention period). There-
fore, teachers in the control group may have had the same
inclination to substitute behavior towards the digital read-
ing system at the cost of other types of support. Therefore,
the additional effect of receiving the information—net of any

experienced—should be an effect of the information and not
an artifact of the experiment.
We also note that enhancing the performance of profession-
als does not necessarily depend on costly efforts such as the
number of working hours, but may (also) rely on adequate
level of treatment for the clients such as the right dosage of
medicine at the hospital, the optimal level of support and
pressure for job-seeking clients etc. These types of treatments
rely on the professionals’ level of expertise (which may be
enhanced by performance information) rather than their level
of costly effort.
These considerations also raise the question about the gen-
eralizability of the results to other types of professionals
and other empirical contexts. Meier et al. (2015a) found
that teachers in Denmark seem to work more independently
from their managers than do teachers in Texas. The impor-
tance of growing professionals’ level of expertise may be
more important for professionals with high level of discretion
and less so for employees that rely more on fixed rules and
managerial oversight. There may also be a difference in the
effect of performance information provision on professionals
working in service organizations (such as education and
health) and those working in regulatory agencies. Especially,
teachers have repeated interactions with their students and
may therefore have more certain priors about them than what
is the case for other public employees that only have single
encounters with citizens. Based on the Bayesian theory, we
would expect that the new information would have stronger
effects in settings where the front-line workers have more
uncertain priors. In our case, the teachers’ prior and poste-
rior beliefs were correlated at 0.88, which makes it a hard
case for new information to have behavioral consequences.
We would expect that in first encounters between frontline-
workers and individuals (as for instance case-workers pro-
cessing applications), priors may be weaker (and more based
on stereotypes). In such situations, the effect of new infor-
mation about each individual is likely to have even stronger
effects on posterior beliefs and behavior than what we
find.
Combinations of theory development and rigorous empiri-
cal studies in different types of public organizations is needed
in order to test the boundary conditions of the theory. We
see great potential in doing so based on an increased level of
digitization of the public sector, which should make it pos-
sible to design experiments that provide updated information
to frontline workers (using smartphone or tablet applications)
and track their subsequent behavior.
In sum, our sample of students resembles the national
cohort of Danish school students, indicating that our con-
clusions could be generalized to this larger population at the
student level. However, the teachers self-selected into our
study, which warrants caution in relation to expecting sim-
ilar results if scaling up our design. In addition, we would
stress the importance of considering other factors influencing
the relationship, such as the incentives facing the profession-
als. Some professionals might be motivated differently and,
thereby, be less likely to choose an action that improves the
policy outcome.
Our results show that in our case sheer provision of
performance information to teachers—without changing the
incentives—was enough to make teachers update their beliefs
about the students and adjust the level of difficulty that they
assign to the students when they practice their reading skills.
The results thereby indicate that teachers—even in a
country such as Denmark without strong accountability
pressures—are motivated to learn about their students’ skills
and use that information to adapt the level of difficulty for the
students. This paves the way for more research on whether the
potential unintended effects of performance management may
be reduced by lowering the incentives at stake and increas-
ing the quality of the performance information provided to
professionals.

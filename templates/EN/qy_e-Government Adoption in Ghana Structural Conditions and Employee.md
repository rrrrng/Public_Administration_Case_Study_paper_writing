ABSTRACT
Globally, technological innovations are driving governments towards e-­government adoption. Digitization efforts have met with
more resistance and challenges in the Global South context due to high levels of financial, logistical, and technical constraints.
The information and communication technology for development literature provides two lenses for examining e-­government
adoption: structural conditions (e.g., infrastructure, financial resources) and the affective orientations (e.g., attitudes, beliefs, val-
ues) of actors towards technology. Connecting these two lenses frames the analysis of a case study on a nascent digital nonprofit
registration process in Ghana built by drawing on historical information, country context, observations, and interviews with
public bureaucrats representing the central government and 17 local government authorities. The findings identify key structural
conditions and affective orientations, including informal bring-­your-­own-­device norms, that have become necessary for leverag-
ing technological capabilities. Insights may be extended to other world regions and contexts that face similar constraints in their
pursuit of digital transformations.
1   |   Introduction
Globally, technological innovations have compelled govern-
ments to move towards e-­government. E-­government involves
the digitization of public services, including the use of infor-
mation and communication technology (ICT) for the storage
and dissemination of government data (Bindu et al. 2019; Osei-­
Kojo  2017). Despite technological advances, e-­government
adoption is uneven across countries (e.g., emerging economies)
and contexts (e.g., resource-­constrained, rural) (Heeks and
Stanforth 2015; LePere-­Schloop et al. 2022; World Bank 2024).
However, recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and open
data are rapidly shifting the nature and promise of technology
for the public sector. This also widens the digital divide globally,
as resource-­rich governments advance technological adoption
and adaptation, and scholars seek to develop updated theoretical
frameworks to account for technological innovation. The World
Bank (2024) documents this widening digital divide, noting that
e-­government adoption is uneven, with many national and sub-­
national governments across countries (e.g., emerging econo-
mies) and contexts (e.g., resource-­constrained, rural) continuing
to rely on paper documents and non-­digital processes for service
delivery and regulatory oversight.
ICT for development (ICT4D) scholars (Adjei-­Bamfo et al. 2020;
Heeks  2022; Parasuraman and Colby  2015) identify two root
causes of e-­government implementation failure. The first uses a
lens of structural conditions that examines the environment (e.g.,
technology infrastructure) and organizational capacity (e.g., pol-
icies, financial resources, technology equipment) to explore the
macro-­ and micro-­level factors impacting e-­government adoption
successes and failures (Heeks 2022). These studies use inventory

the implementation of e-­government initiatives in resource-­

registration process project from 2016 to 2020. The authors

the theoretical framework by examining interdisciplinary re-

key challenges in this context. Next, we discuss our case study
design and data gathering. In the third section, we present our
findings, examining the structural conditions and affective ori-

practice, followed by our concluding comments.
2   |   Theoretical Foundation
2.1   |   Structural Conditions: Technology,
Organization, Environment
Preexisting institutional factors that impact the success or fail-
ure of an adoption are acknowledged across the e-­government
adoption literature but are recognized under different terminol-
ogies and framing. Rogers' (1983) diffusion of innovation the-
ory uses the term ‘prior conditions’ to identify factors such as
previous experience, known challenges, capacity for innovation,
infrastructure, and resources (e.g., financial and human capi-
tal). Within ICT4D, terms such as critical success factors (CSF)
(Apleni and Smuts 2020; Touray et al. 2013) or contextual condi-
tions (Mbatha and Ocholla 2011) are also used.
Many systematic reviews have tried to catalog the range of CSFs
but have failed to establish a unified system for organizing the dif-
ferent factors (Napitupulu and Sensuse 2014; Touray et al. 2013).
Classifications can range from a set of broad categories to over 40
individual CSFs, such that organizational attributes (e.g., policies,
culture, resources) and individual attitudinal or cognitive orien-
tations (e.g., technology literacy, beliefs, attitudes, skills) may be
either missing or not differentiated from ICT infrastructure when
determining the effectiveness of e-­government adoption processes.
The technology, organization, environment (TOE) framework
simplifies the numerous categories and indices used, allowing for
greater precision in the analytical lens because it separates ICT
infrastructure from organizational strategy, while also including
macro-­institutional factors. Technology includes computers, inter-
net access, and other aspects of ICT infrastructure. Organization
includes attributes within the government, such as funding, train-
ing, policies, goals, and internal operational capabilities. Finally,
the environment addresses the broader institutional context in
which organizations are embedded, including issues external to
the actors engaged in the adoption process, such as international
actors, nonprofits, and citizens. When coupled with the technol-
ogy readiness index discussed in the next section, TOE will help
tease out the affective orientations of employees towards various
aspects of identified CSFs.

discomfort, and insecurity. Optimism captures the hopes of
individuals about the utility of technology to improve the cur-
rent state. Innovation examines the individual's orientation to-
wards change and openness to implementing new technologies.
Discomfort accounts for how an individual feels when faced
with a technology-­based challenge, perceived needs for support
or training, and their risk orientation and boundaries for (not)
adopting new technologies. Finally, insecurity is used to identify
technological dependencies, security and accountability issues,
as well as perceived challenges to technology adoption and use.
TRI allows for the capture of cognitive and attitudinal factors, as
well as employee orientation towards change and change man-
agement (Parasuraman and Colby 2015).
Combining the TOE and TRI frames enables an integrative map-
ping of the structural conditions onto the affective orientations
and generates a more robust discussion of the interplay between
the two. The next section provides an overview of the methods.
3   |   Methods
A case study design was employed to understand the relevant
conditions for adopting e-­government in the Global South con-
text (Yin 2015). The case study design is a qualitative research
approach that enables scholars to gain new insights into the
dynamics and complexity of nascent e-­government implemen-
tation projects to better enhance public service delivery (Miles
and Huberman 1994; Yin 2015). This approach is appropriate
for this study because it focuses on an underdeveloped area of
inquiry where available knowledge is post hoc and dominated
by success or failure factors.
3.1   |   Case Selection
The case for this study is the Digital Nonprofit Registration
Process (DNRP) project, a project partly motivated by Ghana's
policy declaration to modernize and transition its public service
to e-­government. The DNRP project was officially introduced
by the Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection (here-
after referred to as ‘the Ministry’). The DNRP involves a multi-­
year e-­government adoption project with three primary goals:
(i) digitize the nonprofit registration paper archives; (ii) create
a website providing a digital repository of registered nonprofits;
and (iii) create a digital nonprofit registration process through
the Ministry's web portal. Under the Ministry, the Nonprofit
Organization Secretariat (also referred to hereafter as ‘the
Agency’) administers the operational aspect of the project. Its
implementation involves various public bureaucrats working at
both the Ministry and all 261 local government authorities.
This case is selected for four key reasons. First, the DNRP proj-
ect is an example of e-­government. Second, the project is being
implemented in an under-­resourced Global South context.
Third, it is at its nascent stages and therefore offers an oppor-
tunity to understand actors' attitudes and structural conditions
earlier in its implementation. Fourth, the DNRP project is being
implemented in a stable democracy, often typologized as a proto-
typical political and economic system for other countries in the
Global South (Zook et al. 2023). Besides its geographic location,
Ghana, like most other Global South countries, is a lower-­middle-­
income economy characterized by high income inequality and
unequal access to digital services, infrastructure challenges,
and struggles to coordinate and integrate e-­government services
across multiple agencies (Adjei-­Bamfo et al. 2020; Heeks 2022).
If the DNRP is successful, Ghana will become one of the few
sub-­Saharan African countries to have an e-­filing system and
a publicly available digital repository of information on its non-
profit sector (LePere-­Schloop et al. 2022).
3.2   |   Data Collection
This case study on the DNRP project draws insights from tri-
angulating data from multiple sources. Our main sample pop-
ulation consists of public bureaucrats working in both central
and local government authorities in the Greater Accra region of
Ghana. There was a need to understand the existing structure
and processes for nonprofit registration and data retention in
Ghana—inventoring the current ICT infrastructure and engag-
ing public bureaucrats in assessing the relevant environment
and organizational needs. Other data sources include historical
information, documentary analysis on country context, unstruc-
tured observations, and 27 in-­depth interviews conducted with
public bureaucrats who have been directly involved in the non-
profit registration process for at least the past 2 years. Seventeen
out of 31 local government authorities participated, includ-
ing Metropolitan, Municipal/District Assemblies (MMDAs),
Regional Coordinating Councils (RCCs), and the Ministry
involved in the nonprofit registration process (i.e., a response
rate of 55%). These institutions represent most of the nonprofit
registration and permit renewal offices in the Greater Accra re-
gion. Greater Accra is the most populous region of Ghana, and
it is also where most nonprofits are headquartered, according to
the Ministry. The unstructured data we used privilege our case
study design to explore social dimensions and triangulate with
historical and emergent documents (Creswell and Poth 2016).
This study employed a grounded theory approach (Dahwa 2024;
Glaser and Strauss 2017). Grounded theory has helped advance a
new theory on e-­government in the Global South context, which
the existing dominant Euro-­ and Global North-­centric literature
cannot explain and continues to struggle with. Grounded the-
ory assumptions elicit insights into interactions and relation-
ships between people and processes, and how they construct
reality (Gioia et al. 2013; Glaser and Strauss 2017). Grounded
theory is not atheoretical; thus, TOE and TRI are used as sen-
sitizing concepts to provide readers with advanced knowledge
of our emergent findings (Glaser and Strauss 2017). Our semi-­
structured interview questions were also designed based on this
study's research question and to highlight structural conditions
and affective orientations in the selected case (see Appendix 1
for interview guide).

(see Table  1). Participants included public bureaucrats desig-
nated as Agency Directors, Agency Administrators, Principal

3.3   |   Data Analysis
Interview data, observation notes, and relevant country and
historical information gathered from secondary sources
were analyzed inductively from an interpretive research
paradigm (Miles and Huberman  1994). We follow the first
and second-­order coding methods as outlined by Miles and
Huberman (1994) to identify overarching themes via thick de-
scriptions and anonymized quotes. First, two research team
members performed the open coding independently and in-
ductively analyzed the textual data using a manual and iter-
ative coding process. The multiple coding process continued
throughout our constant comparative analysis method (Glaser
and Strauss 2017). During the constant comparative analysis,
observation notes (capturing data on non-­human elements)
were compared with interview transcripts. Where distortions
existed, additional data were obtained in a second round of in-
terviews and observations.
Codes were simultaneously developed independently for data
quotes. This process was repeated for two additional rounds to
ensure that the final set of codes was consistent between coders
and comprehensive across all interviews. To ensure inter-­coder
reliability, a third member of the research team then acted as
a coder adjudicator, reviewing the codes and quotes of earlier
coders and settling any disagreements. The open codes were
subsequently categorized into 7 second-­order codes, which were
further categorized into two main aggregate dimensions (Gioia
et al. 2013). Emergent themes from the coding were triangulated
against the literature for construct validity. Figure 1 provides a
data structure of our emerging codes showing the data-­to-­theory
connection, while Appendix 2 presents codes and supporting il-
lustrative quotes for context.
For the qualitative research design, limitations were addressed
using the five-­pronged lens of trustworthiness (Korstjens and
Moser 2018; Ospina et al. 2018). First, we triangulated insights
from interviews with bureaucrats from the three government
levels involved in the nonprofit registration process with an ex-
amination of organizational documents and continued obser-
vations over time, taking a quasi-­ethnographic approach. The

4   |   Structural Conditions and Organizational
Antecedents
In Ghana, the nonprofit sector encompasses a broad spectrum
of organizations operating in several sectors, such as health
services, education, agriculture, advocacy and human rights,
environmental protection and local economic development
(Bawole 2018). Since the establishment of Ghana's 4th Republic
in 1992, efforts have been made to enhance effective regulation
and monitoring of Ghana's nonprofit sector within the Ministry.
Currently, all nonprofit organizations are required to register
with the Ministry to obtain an operating permit, which must be
renewed annually upon submitting the required documents.
The nonprofit registration process in Ghana is highly decen-
tralized, involving four key offices. Figure 2 shows a visual
representation of offices and outlines the process that non-
profits must follow to obtain a required permit, which en-
ables them to conduct business in Ghana. The first step for
any nonprofit or business in Ghana is to obtain a Certificate
of Incorporation from the Registrar General; however, the
Registrar General is not involved in the registration process
beyond this initial step.
Nonprofits are required to submit additional documentation to
register or renew their permit at the Ministry level through their
respective MMDA, where the nonprofit's office is located. The
MMDAs conduct a Social Investigation Report (SIR) to ensure
the nonprofit is adhering to its stated mission and activities. All
documentation is then forwarded in duplicates, with 4–6 copies
to the RCC along with a recommendation letter. The RCCs are
responsible for coordinating, monitoring, and harmonizing the
activities of all MMDAs under their jurisdiction. The RCC then
recommends and forwards the application to the Agency under
the Ministry where the nonprofit permit is granted. Data were
captured primarily through the Ministry's two-­page handwrit-
ten application form completed annually by a representative of
the nonprofit organization.
4.1   |   Paper-­Based Process Era
The registration and monitoring of nonprofits was conducted
in a predominantly non-­digital environment—that is, the pro-
cesses are driven by the collection and transfer of paper doc-
uments and files, with disparate or no ICT infrastructure to
support this process. As such, the storage and retrieval of paper
documents were consistently deemed critical to administrative
operations by the participants. However, officials also noted that
nonprofit files stored in their offices: i) did not protect confiden-
tial data, ii) lacked storage infrastructure leading to document
loss (due to weather/wear and tear), and iii) lacked a standard-
ized filing system (i.e., alpha-­numeric), which limited the effec-
tiveness of data retrieval.
At all the sampled offices, either metal or wooden cabinets and
drawers were used to store nonprofit files. Most of these cabinets
and drawers were poorly managed and maintained (e.g., broken
drawers, broken or no locking mechanism). Excess documents
were routinely observed stacked on desks and throughout the
office due to inadequate storage facilities—this included out-
door spaces such as balconies, where files were exposed to the
weather.
So, when it rains – the space, the files are competing
with the spaces in the office… If somebody registered
[a] long time ago… how [would we] even look for it,
how [would we] even retrieve that file?
Knowledge of file management was inadequate among officials.
Officials from the sampled institutions had no clear standard
filing system or protocol for document storage and retrieval;
they all used different systems based on their infrastructure
and capacity. Officials reportedly lacked the time to organize
and file reports, citing the need to prioritize processing cases
over filing and maintaining processed files. Some officials also
acknowledged a lack of skills and training in installing an ap-
propriate filing management system. This was compounded
by the wide range of case management responsibilities each

4.2   |   The Blended Data Formats
Here, public bureaucrats began to produce and use digital data
formats alongside the paper manual system. Some offices were
equipped with scanners and scanned nonprofit files (especially
certificates) into PDF/image format. Although not a procedural
requirement from the Ministry in the registration process,
scanned documents helped officials avoid relying entirely on
paper copies. Information from scanned documents was used
to monitor or contact the nonprofit to implement sanctions (e.g.,
in instances where there was cause to suspect inappropriate be-
havior by the nonprofit) or if there was a failure to renew their
operating permit.
We scan their certificates…because what happens
sometimes is that when they go, their certificate gets
missing so when they come back to tell us then we
take that one and then we help them go back there to
get a new one.
Very useful… if we are just scanning, digitize it, it
would be very helpful…I could read, for me I'll always
want to go to the field…I'll always want to see the
physical [location] and link it… If we had pictures to
support our reports for the nonprofit, then we'd talk
about the property.
Some offices transferred data from their logbooks to digital
formats using computer application software such as Microsoft
Word and Microsoft Excel. Most of these officials created ta-
bles with word processing software to capture data from log-
books, but two agencies used Microsoft Excel to perform the
same task. While the latter group of officials acknowledged
that Microsoft Excel was preferable, it was rarely used, as most
participants considered it too technical and required special
training.
By the end of 2020, the Ministry had instituted a quarterly
filing system, where all registrations received in that quarter
were bundled with twine and labeled (e.g., Q4 2020) for stor-
age in a designated facility. While individual files within that
quarter were not organized (i.e., alpha-­numerically or by reg-
istration number), this was an improvement for potential file
retrieval. Moreover, by bundling with twine, it overcame chal-
lenges due to a lack of storage infrastructure, such as cabinets.
The Ministry had also started to digitize their logbook, using
Microsoft Excel to store pertinent information about each reg-
istered nonprofit—that is, more than just the registration/re-
newal dates contained in the handwritten logbooks. But this
was not a practice being implemented or disseminated at the
regional or local levels.
4.3   |   Digital Capacity
Officials discussed limited knowledge and experience with tech-
nologies that would otherwise be useful for executing basic tasks
such as preparing SIRs and capturing nonprofit data. This in-
cludes knowledge of hardware (i.e., computers, scanners, and
printers) and software (i.e., Microsoft Excel and Word), email, and
the internet. Most participants acknowledged a need for additional
training, demonstrating a desire to perform tasks more effectively
and move towards more digitized tools and processes:
We would require training on how to track the
nonprofits on their websites and other computer
programs, how to use projectors to do presentations,
cameras, etc.
5   |   Affective Orientations for the DNRP Project
We found that the DNRP project had some positive environ-
mental reinforcements. During interviews, the Ministry iden-
tified several national and international pressures driving the
decision to implement an e-­government platform for nonprofit
registration. The Ministry oversees a variety of registration
and regulatory oversight processes, including investigations of
child welfare and the registration of preschools and nonprofit
organizations. At the time the interviews were collected, the
Ministry had a webpage, but it only included general infor-

though the Ministry is yet to institute a digital nonprofit regis-

In addition to national-­level pressures, the Ministry identified
international pressures. In interviews, Ministry officials re-
ported pressure from macro-­institutions and governments of the
Global North—namely, the European Union (EU) and United
Nations (UN)—to digitize their nonprofit registry and registra-
tion process to strengthen fiscal oversight of the nonprofit sec-
tor. For example, Ghana was temporarily listed as a high-­risk
country by the EU's Financial Action Task Force for deficien-
cies in its Anti-­Money Laundering regime and failure to exercise
fiscal oversight over the sector, with stated goals to root out or
identify nonprofits that might have ties to terrorist actors and/or
activities (Ghana's Financial Action Task Force 2022). In turn,
the Ministry reported that the Ghanaian Parliament cited these
actions and applied pressure on the Ministry. These represent
important broader environmental conditions but also help un-
derstand the environmental motivations of the Ministry in de-
ciding to move forward with implementing the e-­government
initiative.
5.1   |   Informal BYOD Norms
Official ICT resources, such as computers, printers, mobile
phones/telephone lines, scanners, global positioning systems
(GPS) devices needed for monitoring and tracking processes,
and internet connectivity, were missing from the existing
infrastructure setup. Indeed, there was a pervasive lack of
government-­issued computers or phones for office use or field-
work. Officials instead relied on personal devices for digital
document retrieval or communication with nonprofits in their
case load, out of necessity. At the local, regional, and Ministry
levels, employees were observed using a variety of personal de-
vices, including laptops, tablets, and smartphones, to conduct
their day-­to-­day work.
Even in locations with access to devices, office technology was
damaged, inoperable, or contained computer viruses, demon-
strating a lack of resources for maintaining existing infrastruc-
ture. Consequently, the pervasiveness of using personal devices
for official business reinforced BYOD norms among officials—
using their personal devices, including laptops, mobile phones,
and internet data, and printers to improve the efficiency and ef-
fectiveness of their work.

6.1   |   Data Privacy and Security
Under the current system, concerns have been raised about how
data privacy and management are compromised both digitally and
due to the inappropriate filing system and limited storage facilities.
Paper files and documents can be easily damaged, tampered with,
or misplaced due to the absence of an adequate data management
system. Indeed, nonprofit files include employee information (e.g.,
copies of government IDs, passports, right-­to-­work documents), as
well as organizational data (e.g., bank accounts).
BYOD and unreimbursed employee expenses also present a two-­
pronged challenge. First, the individual risk of unreimbursed
expenses incurred by officials in the course of their work is a
financial loss to them. In their effort to recoup this cost, officials
may engage in exploitation and corrupt behaviors, which could
affect the quality of the public service provided. Second, there is
also an organizational risk related to data privacy and security
issues for employees storing sensitive information on personal
devices. These norms, if left unregulated, may thwart efforts
to protect certain confidential data within the Agency and the
MMDA, in general. Additionally, the relevant government in-
stitutions may establish policies on monetary and in-­kind do-
nations to mitigate the risks of conflict of interest, particularly
when nonprofits donate essential resources and infrastructure
to those with regulatory authority.
6.2   |   Reinforcement for an Integrated System
Respondents highlighted the value of an integrated information
system (IIS) to validate the authenticity of nonprofits, adding
credibility to their work and enhancing trustworthiness and co-
ordination across districts.
If we are all hooked on the common network, we'll
be able to know at a particular point in time what
your colleague in [inaudible] assembly is doing, and
probably somebody under the guise of being the same
NGO has been to your district. So [this would] prompt
you to call your colleague and find out more about
that NGO.
Given the decentralized nature of the process, with paperwork
flowing from local to regional to central government, it was ob-
served that access to technology was not evenly distributed. For
offices with greater access to technology infrastructure, partici-
pants responded more favorably to an IIS. Across all offices, this
value was also mitigated by an official's perception of the extent
to which scanned images could support their work in the field,
related to the lack of access to government-­issued devices.
7   |   Discussion and Implications for Theory and
Practice
Our case study findings make several contributions at the in-
tersection of ICT4D and preconditions for e-­government im-
plementation beyond the dominant post hoc success or failure
factors. First, we map the nonprofit registration process across
three levels of government (from Figure 2) and demonstrate the
decentralized, disconnected, and constrained nature of the ICT
infrastructure. This mapping supported our methods, including
designing a sampling frame and developing appropriate proto-
cols to answer the research question—capturing the structural
conditions and affective orientations of government employees
engaged in e-­government adoption for the nonprofit registration
process. Moreover, mapping the network assisted with identi-
fying power distribution and decision-­making authority—ul-
timately found to be held at the Ministry level, but strongly
influenced by international actors pressuring the Ghanaian
government through sanctioning and pressure campaigns.
This section integrates the results by overlaying the TOE and TRI
frameworks to examine the interaction of structural conditions
and affective orientations, as well as the utility of using an inte-
grative framework for e-­government adoption. Aligned with pre-
vious research, most of the challenges identified are logistical and
infrastructural, including inadequate file management and data
storage facilities, and limited ICT infrastructure and resources
for new investment and management of existing infrastructure.
Existing employee norms and attitudes may facilitate the adop-
tion of an e-­government platform, such as employee eagerness to
learn new digital skills, the informal use of technology through
BYOD and document digitization when ICT infrastructure is
available. Indeed, employees demonstrated initiative with infor-
mal adoption and efforts towards digitization, despite the lack of
access to resources and infrastructure (Table 2).

Desktop and laptop computers, internet connectivity, networked
systems, and other ancillary ICT appliances, such as scanners
and printers, and telephones, are largely inadequate in Global
South public sector institutions (MacLean and Titah  2022;
Mustafa et al. 2020). Indeed, this was also observed in this case
study. The nonprofit registration process is heavily reliant on
paper processes, such as physically transporting files across gov-
ernment institutions—MMDAs, RCCs, and the Ministry. This
process can be slow and burdensome and can often cause the
MMDAs to exceed their standard processing time. Our study
findings suggest that the delays in the process discourage some
nonprofits from renewing their permits, which also impacts the
effectiveness of government monitoring and collaborating with
nonprofits in the provision of critical social services.
The value of having a decentralized but integrated system is
significant in facilitating effective service delivery (Dunleavy
et  al.  2006; Osei-­Kojo  2017). A networked ICT infrastructure
could significantly enhance the effectiveness of these public
institutions in performing their nonprofit registration tasks,
such as implementing and maintaining best practices for digital
storage and retrieval. Internet connectivity (either broadband or
mobile data) would enable offices to more effectively communi-
cate with each other and with nonprofits. These initial digitiza-
tion steps are necessary to move Ghana towards an integrated
system for receiving registration documents without requiring
nonprofits to submit printed hard copies (with duplicates) to a
physical location.
In technologically enabled environments, such as those asso-
ciated with the Global North, the use of personal devices for
work—BYOD—is an intentional organizational policy origi-
nating from an organizational goodwill policy that employers
extend to employees (Dhingra 2016; Disterer and Kleiner 2013).
Across a range of systematic reviews on e-­government adop-
tion in Global South and Global North contexts, BYOD was not
identified as a CSF, nor a strategy used by employees or orga-
nizations (MacLean and Titah  2022; Mustafa et  al.  2020; Ni
et al. 2017; Rana et al. 2021). Considering the pervasiveness of
this practice and its implications on organizational processes
and data privacy and security implications, a greater exploration
of the use of this practice in various e-­government, open govern-
ment, AI integrations, and other technological adoptions across
institutional contexts is recommended.
Several norms and attitudes from our case study characterize
hopefulness and a posture that embraces a digitized nonprofit
registration process. For example, quests for digital training
on technologies, efforts to digitize existing paper documents,
BYOD, and support for a decentralized integrated system
align with the optimism and innovative orientation elements
of the TRI. Training and capacity building are critical compo-
nents of the innovation process. Training empowers users to
internalize the meaningfulness of digitally integrated systems
that promote efficiency, accountability, and transparency
(Margetts 2022).
Importantly, the current nonprofit registration process does not
yet enable the use of digital technologies; however, nonprofits
and officials collaborate in various ways using personal devices
to navigate administrative challenges. While these collabo-
rations may cultivate a positive attitude towards the adoption
of digital technologies, Rogers (1983) notes that the views and
opinions of colleagues (in the organization) about digital inno-
vation hold greater weight in influencing the attitude to adopt
digital innovation in the nonprofit registration process. Also, the
use of personal technology devices and voluntary digitization of
the process by public bureaucrats offers positive implications for
introducing a digitized process in granting nonprofit permits
(LePere-­Schloop et  al.  2022). Introducing a digitized system
for the nonprofit registration process in resource-­constrained
environments could also leverage the convenience that ac-
tors associate with existing BYOD behaviors. A digital system
could enhance the work performance of the officials, save office
space, and drastically improve the speed with which services
are delivered (Osei-­Kojo 2017). Ultimately, it would no longer be
necessary (except perhaps for verifying electronic documents)
to physically exchange documents between the various insti-
tutional levels. Instead, this may be done in near real-­time or
within a few days.

8   |   Conclusion
The era of data analytics and data science (including artificial
intelligence and machine learning algorithms) has led schol-
ars to ponder the exponential technological and social implica-
tions of digitization across all segments of the socio-­technical
landscape. This work investigated the structural conditions
and affective orientations influencing e-­government adoption
in a Global South country context. Drawing on TOE and TRI
demonstrated that conditions, including the level of digital in-
frastructure and resources, capacity for implementation, and
existing affective norms, are important to officials engaged in
the adoption, but not necessarily to the decision to implement
e-­government. Indeed, external and international pressure was
the impetus for this e-­government adoption.
That said early e-­government adoption literature identified gov-
ernments moving incrementally and slowly towards integrative
and transformational e-­government models—most stopping at
the implementation of a website (Coursey and Norris 2008). From
this perspective, the current state of the Ministry's e-­government
adoption process is a partial success—achieving two of the three
goals. Moreover, the current retrenchment—removing some web-
site data due to emergent fraudsters—is an appropriate response to
assess and address shifting environmental conditions. As such, ex-
pectations for fully integrative and transformational e-­government
systems may be unreasonable when governments are embarking
on digitization and the initial implementation of e-­government
systems. Instead, allowing for benchmarking as a part of an in-
cremental implementation process aligns with demonstrated best
practice (Coursey and Norris 2008; Salem 2007; Wescott 2001).
The Ministry website provides information on policies, pro-
cesses, documents, and nonprofits operating in Ghana; however,
the registration process is still paper-­based, meaning the adop-
tion is still in progress. As such, this case represents a nascent
innovation adoption, providing an opportunity to explore and
construct narratives around structural conditions and affective
orientations in real time. For international actors, such as those
encountered in this case (e.g., UN, EU), tempering broader in-
ternational goals and standards with the realities of resource-­
constrained contexts may be necessary. Due to international
pressures, in contexts such as the Global South, decisions to im-
plement e-­government initiatives may be top-­down due to pres-
sure from the international community, reducing the agency
and engagement of line-­level employees in the adoption process,
which reduces the success and efficacy of outcomes (Effah and
Nuhu 2017; Prakash 2016; Rana et al. 2021).
Current practices for technology adoption and use in Ghana and
other Global South public institutions and nonprofit registration
processes face many infrastructural and logistical challenges.
Despite the challenges to address structural constraints, offi-
cials in this case seemed eager and optimistic about leveraging
available ICT tools to implement more efficient and effective
processes. In many Global South contexts, such as Ghana, the
government may have policy statements to move towards e-­
government, but in practice, those same governments have
limited technology budgets for developing the public sector's in-
formation systems and adopting technologies that facilitate ser-
vice delivery. Indeed, agencies and departments may be charged
with technology adoption without any infrastructure support or
resources provided by the central government.
This work also broadens our understanding of informal norms
such as BYOD. In the absence of technology infrastructure (e.g.,
computers, cell phones, internet access) provided by an em-
ployer, employees, in this case, used personal devices to bridge
gaps in structural access to technology and leverage technol-
ogy in the workplace. This differs from bring-­your-­own-­device
(BYOD) policies enacted by employers to enhance the flexibility
and productivity of their workforce. BYOD presents challenges,
such as ensuring the security of information accessed and
shared on personal devices, but also presents opportunities, spe-
cifically in terms of affective orientations, such as generating in-
terest and optimism towards the adoption of future technology,
thus reducing resistance to technological changes in the future.
Future research should account for BYOD behaviors in the CSF
inventories.
Finally, this case revealed a dearth of clear document storage
and retrieval practices. Often, e-­government adoption is not sim-
ply moving paper processes online, but the digitization of paper
documents. When e-­government adoptions require digitization
of historical documents, inventories of CSFs for that adoption
should include non-­digital “readiness” metrics, such as the cur-
rent document filing, storage, and retrieval systems in prepara-
tion for digitization efforts.1

ABSTRACT
Administrative restructuring is an organizational phenomenon suggested to improve under-­represented groups' managerial rep-
resentation by disrupting networks and institutions. However, extant tests of a ‘disruption hypothesis’ are collectively inconclu-
sive. We elaborate and test it with a qualitative-­to-­quantitative study of local health agency managers and mergers across the
Italian NHS from 2014 to 2020. Agency leader interviews reveal disruption indicators: number of agencies merging, staff ration-
alization, changes in geographical scale, and agency heterogeneity. Using administrative data, we find disruption measures have
some positive associations with women's share of management, post-­merger retention, and new hires, providing modest support
for the disruption hypothesis. However, there is an unexpected ‘winners-­and-­losers’ dynamic: incumbent women had higher
post-­merger attrition than men, but merged agencies hired more women than non-­merging agencies. We offer three abductively
developed interpretations of this finding, extending the disruption hypothesis' connections with public management theory on
the informal, organizational antecedents of diversity in senior management.
1   |   Introduction
Women's underrepresentation in senior public management has
persisted long after the removal of formal barriers to employ-
ment and advancement (Bowling et al. 2006), despite the pub-
lic sector traditionally being a significant employer of women
(Riccucci  2009). Recognizing informal barriers to women's
progression as an important issue for public administration and
management more generally (Pandey et al. 2022), there has been
increasing attention to the organizational antecedents of women's
managerial representation, including contraction (Groeneveld
et  al.  2020; Yang et  al.  2022), expansion (Krøtel et  al.  2019),
and changing economic conditions (Ashworth et  al.  2023).
Administrative restructuring is a common organizational
phenomenon across sectors (Swianiewicz 2018; Tumanut 2020;
Sześciło 2022; Harford 2024) that has gone relatively unexam-
ined. We contribute a novel perspective to this body of public
management research by elaborating and testing what we term a
‘disruption hypothesis’ of administrative restructuring.
The disruption hypothesis is an expectation (Zhang  2022;
Edwards et al. 1999) that mergers will foster parity in wom-
en's managerial representation by upsetting prior networks
and institutions which may reinforce glass ceilings. However,
its treatment in disparate literatures lacks explicit theoretical
grounding, and empirical results have been collectively in-
conclusive. Our main research question is: Does higher merger
disruption increase women's managerial representation? To

move beyond prior studies which conceptualize and operation-
alize mergers as disruptive per se, we first pose the interme-
diate question: What indicates a more or less disruptive merger
from managerial perspectives? We apply an exploratory, se-
quential mixed-­methods design in the setting of Italian local
health agencies (LHAs), where a wave of mergers among over
60 LHAs took place in different regions between 2014 and 2017.
Italy's National Health System is a politically and economically
important sector and reflects international trends in healthcare
and other public sectors of women's persistent underrepresenta-
tion at senior levels and frequent consolidation reforms.
The qualitative phase uses 20 semi-­structured interviews with
agency managers with collective experience of the 2014–2017
merger wave. We identify the merger-­led degree of staff ratio-
nalization, number of agencies merging, geographical scale in-
crease, and heterogeneity of pre-­merger organizations as more
refined yet measurable indicators of merger disruption levels.
These facilitate our primary aim of testing the disruption hy-
pothesis with national register data on a legally defined, clinical
population of LHA senior managers. Our hypothesis tests exam-
ine the stock and flow of senior management, providing modest
support for the disruption hypothesis overall: different disrup-
tion measures have positive, significant associations with wom-
en's share of management, new hires, and women's retention
post-­merger. However, a story of winners and losers emerged:
the apparent benefits of disruption were driven by more women
being hired in post-­merger agencies, surpassing a higher depar-
ture rate among incumbent women managers.
We abductively develop three interpretations of the unexpected
winners-­and-­losers finding, revisiting broader literature on gen-
der differences in managerial careers. Each interpretation devel-
ops connections to wider theories on organizational antecedents
of women's managerial representation. The first is that under-
represented women managers benefit specifically from disrup-
tion, which increases network and institutional formality. The
second is that incumbent women may be different from those
newly hired, due to skills, education, or factors that helped
their entry to pre-­merger agencies but disadvantaged them in
organizational change contexts. The third is that women man-
agers' lower organizational embeddedness entails a lower buf-
fer for disruption at sub-­organizational levels (like stress) and
supra-­organizational levels (like dynamics concerning external,
political, social ties).
Bringing the disruption hypothesis to a public administration au-
dience, the article makes two key contributions. First, we develop a
more sophisticated approach to testing the hypothesis based on in-
dicators of more or less disruptive mergers. Applied in a healthcare
context, our results provide modest support for the hypothesis's
general idea but also the unexpected finding of winners and losers
among women at different phases of merger disruption. Second,
we elaborate on the theoretical underpinnings of the disruption
hypothesis. We do this by illuminating its foundations in dispa-
rate areas of theory; then, following our empirical inquiry, we re-­
engage with wider literature on women's organizational behaviors
to develop interpretations of the winners and losers finding.
Considering the ambiguous empirical findings in scholarship on
managerial effects of mergers, our findings clarify that proces-
sual phases of disruption—the ‘during’ and ‘after’—may have
different implications for underrepresented groups in manage-
ment. This unifies the literature's conflicting results into a more
coherent processual picture. The interpretations we develop of
our winners-­and-­losers finding elaborate and embed the dis-
ruption hypothesis within wider theory, providing a promising
research agenda and implications for talent and diversity man-
agement for public organizations planning restructuring.
2   |   Merger Disruption and Women in Public
Management
Several scholars have hypothesized organizational changes as
relevant to women's representation in public administration
and management generally (Bishu and Headley 2020; Ely and
Meyerson  2000). Recent public management research has ex-
amined organizational contraction and a glass cliff hypothesis
(Groeneveld et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2022; Sabharwal 2015), ex-
pansion (Krøtel et al. 2019), or effects of different and changing
economic conditions on women's relative outcomes using im-
printing theory (Ashworth et al. 2023).
We explore gendered effects of two or more agencies merging, an
organizational phenomenon that is qualitatively different from
contraction or expansion. As well as having important effects
for internal social networks (Zhang  2022), mergers generally
also lead to radical changes in formal and informal institutions
(Oliver 1992), including culture (Buono and Bowditch 2003) and
day-­to-­day practices (Tarba et al. 2016).
Mergers are widespread in private sectors (Harford 2024), af-
fecting 5 million workers in the United States annually since the
1970s (Zhang 2022), and public sectors (Fleischer et al. 2023) at
both central (Sześciło 2022) and local levels (Swianiewicz 2018).
Notwithstanding their frequency as ‘upscaling’ forms of re-
structuring, there has been little research on public mergers
compared to ‘downscaling’ and other forms of decentralization
(Tumanut 2020), and relatively little is known about their work-
force effects (Zhang 2022).
Evidence for Practice
•	 Mergers pose disruptive risks and opportunities for
senior talent and diversity management, which should
be considered as part of re-­organizational planning
and implementation.
•	 Women managers were more likely than men to leave
their agency after a merger but were a higher share
of external new hires in post-­merger than non-­merged
agencies.
•	 One interpretation of this ‘winners-­and-­losers’ finding
suggests diverse senior talent retention and acquisition
may be promoted by formalizing talent retention and
development initiatives throughout merger processes.
•	 Talent retention strategies should consider gender
differences in network and institutional embedded-
ness and how they may shape attrition, for instance
through higher exposure to stress or burnout.

Several studies in past decades have suggested mergers may
increase women's representation through what we term a ‘dis-
ruption hypothesis,’ or the idea that women stand to gain when
networks and institutions previously ‘stacked against them’
in established organizations are interrupted. For Edwards
et al. (1999, 1), mergers are an opportunity to break male “mo-
nopolies on management”, disrupting “established power struc-
tures […] and methods of working”. Zhang (2022, 1) theorizes
mergers as events that ‘shake up’ organizational inertia and ‘old
hierarchies.’ Such perspectives connect gendered perspectives
with more general theories of organizational change and work-
force management.
On the networks side, social ties are acknowledged as import-
ant for human resource management (Mossholder et al. 2005;
Moynihan and Pandey 2008). Literature on turnover and so-
cial networks has not considered mergers per se, but empiri-
cal findings suggest other sources of network disruption (like
downsizing or outsourcing; ibid) may affect turnover intentions.
The disruption hypothesis expects an uneven effect between
male and female managers, especially the more women are in a
minority. This is based on arguments and findings that under-­
represented women tend to be disadvantaged by entrenched
social networks (Zhang 2022), for instance due to the phenom-
enon of network homophily (McDonald 2011; Lutter 2015), less
support from others in the organization like male line managers
(Woodall et al. 1997), or structural network factors. For instance,
Edwards et al. (1999) theorize mergers as dispersing manage-
rial status through decentralizing organizational structures and
breaking up tall hierarchies which favor male managers.
On the institutional side, integrating cultures (Buono and
Bowditch 2003) and processes (Tarba et  al.  2016) in pursuit
of mergers' instrumental aims is a confirmed part of mergers.
Oliver's  (1992, 565) foundational work of neo-­institutionalism
suggested mergers are an “antecedent of deinstitutionalization”,
introducing a new culture that disrupts “historical continuity”
of organizations' “value systems, [creating] social pressures […]
to revise its established procedures” (ibid, 576). Like social ties,
institutions (including working practices, culture, and policies)
are also relevant for personnel outcomes, for instance, through
‘person-­organization fit’ (Moynihan and Pandey 2008). The dis-
ruption hypothesis is premised on the idea that established in-
stitutions favor men (Acker 1990) or other majority groups and
are costly to change (Zhang 2022). Edwards et al. (1999) suggest
mergers can transform managerial career paths and lead to the
feminization of managerial qualities.
In synthesis, the disruption hypothesis implicitly draws together
different theories of how individuals' relational and institutional
embeddedness matters for turnover and progression. It assumes
that over-­represented male managers in established organi-
zations are typically more embedded along both dimensions
than under-­represented women. Thus, it hypothesizes that the
latter benefit when networks and institutions are disrupted by
mergers.
Collectively, qualitative and quantitative empirical findings
relating to the disruption hypothesis have been ambiguous.
Several authors have found evidence of corporate mergers in-
creasing (Zhang  2022; Dencker  2008) or decreasing (Woodall
et al. 1997; Haveman et al. 2009) women's representation. For
instance, Worts et al. (2007) found male and female employees
had different reactions to restructuring events, with women's
perceptions of the meaningfulness of their roles in the organiza-
tion generally becoming more pessimistic.
Woodall et  al.  (1997) examined two private and one public
restructuring process and found women managers were dis-
advantaged by merger processes when managerial positions
were reduced, which they argue is due in part to reinforcement
of exclusion from “invaluable informal organizational net-
works” which enable access to career opportunities (ibid, 2; see
Haveman et al. 2009 for similar results). Edwards et al. (1999,
62) found that women—who traditionally had opportunities in
more specialist career tracks in their case organizations—gener-
ally appeared to be disadvantaged by changes which saw man-
agerial ‘role broadening’, and career advancement forms which
became “more opaque” in the informal processes by which
they were distributed. Literature on glass walls and escalators
suggest gendered occupational stratification affects women's
chances to progress to managerial levels (Sabharwal 2015), and
this finding of Edwards et al.'s suggests that the relationship be-
tween occupational segregation and women's progression is not
straightforward.
Meanwhile, using a difference-­in-­differences design with
40 years of data on large-­N corporate mergers in the US,
Zhang  (2022) found mergers increased women's managerial
representation in companies, albeit favoring higher-­skilled
workers. Through interviews with corporate managers, Zhang
identified personnel reshuffling and changes to routines and
practices, ostensibly focused on performance or diversity, as
mechanisms through which women may advance following
mergers. ‘Intentional’ use of mergers by organizational lead-
ers as opportunities to diversify management contrasts with
Edwards et  al.'s  (1999, 60) finding that change processes had
pushed equal opportunities “well down the agenda” in their
UK-­based case study (with similar findings summarized by
Dencker 2008).
We consider these collectively ambiguous empirical results to
call for more clearly specified tests of the disruption hypothesis,
which can exploit variation in merger disruption for more con-
clusive findings. This necessitates a mixed-­methods approach.
At the same time, the general puzzle motivating the disruption
hypothesis is the persistence of women's underrepresentation
despite gains at lower ranks. Ruling out simpler explanations of
there being fewer women in the pipeline to senior levels or the
more specific issues senior women may face in organizations
that are male dominated overall (Choi and Ko 2025) sharpens
the focus on informal barriers at the top. We now present our
mixed-­methods approach and empirical setting of Italian local
health agency mergers and clinical senior managers.

4   |   Empirical Setting
Healthcare systems are a significant and growing share of GDP
in developed economies. They reflect the broader puzzle of wom-
en's persistent underrepresentation at senior levels despite gains
and even overrepresentation at lower ranks (Eurofound 2015).
They provide an ideal setting for elaborating and testing the
disruption hypothesis because they are also commonly subject
to restructuring reforms (Postma and Roos  2016). These fea-
tures are reflected in our specific context of Italian local health
agency mergers (LHAs).
LHAs are vertically integrated, public health organizations re-
sponsible for administering, financing, and providing health
services by 21 regional health systems. Their numbers fell
from over 600 in 1992 to about 100 in 2017 following succes-
sive waves of mergers (Romiti et al. 2023), with reorganizations
having similar political drivers and scale of reforms with other
countries with public health insurance models, including the
United Kingdom and some Scandinavian countries (Mariani
et  al.  2022). Stated political drivers, especially from center-­
left coalitions, included the pursuit of economies of scale and
scope, increased resource efficiency, reduction of overcapac-
ity, and clinical quality improvements (Varriale 2023; Di Novi
et  al.  2018). Consistent growth in women's share of the NHS
workforce in recent decades was not accompanied by corre-
sponding increases in women in upper management (Saporito
et al. 2019).
A more distinctive feature of our setting is its less developed
implementation of diversity-­related initiatives. The disruption
hypothesis' largest-­scale test to date (Zhang 2022) is set in the
United States, arguably an extreme case for diversity manage-
ment practices and discourse. Diversity management concerns
arose some decades later in Italy than in other countries, with
significant gaps remaining between equal opportunities legis-
lation, espoused organizational rhetoric, and their enactment in
practice (Murgia and Poggio 2014). This enables testing of the
disruption hypothesis when diversity is less of a salient concern
for organizational leaders.
This context also enables us to leverage sub-­national variation in
merger characteristics and managerial outcomes over time, with
qualitative and quantitative data on 22 agencies resulting from
mergers among 64 pre-­merging agencies, across eight regions,
and corresponding quantitative data from non-­merging agencies
(100) and regions (12). Figure 2 illustrates the focal merger wave
of our analysis.
We test the disruption hypothesis on a national population of se-
nior clinical managers charged with directing LHAs' “complex
operating units”. These senior managers occupy a hierarchical
level immediately beneath LHAs' top managers, as illustrated in
Figure 3. Whereas managerial skill profiles or career paths may

5   |   Phase 1: Data and Methods
We initially embarked on the broader research question ‘how
do managers experience and handle merger-­led disruption?’.
We conducted semi-­structured interviews with 20 purposely
sampled senior and top LHA managers with first-­hand expe-
rience of the 2014–2017 wave of merger processes between
December 2021 and July 2022. Participants covered all re-
gions that saw mergers between 2014 and 2017, with four from
Veneto, three from Lombardy, three from Piedmont, three
from Lazio, two from Tuscany, two from Emilia-­Romagna,
two from Friuli, and one from Sardinia. Eight interviewees
were women.
We recruited informants through professional and research con-
tacts, snowball sampling, and direct invitations to participate
by email. Interviews were held in Italian, lasted approximately
20–40 min, and followed a topic guide of five open questions
(Appendix A) designed to capture participants' perspectives on
the merger processes, their effects on the agency, and on career

We began interpreting interview data inductively. Participant
accounts revealed senior managers as both actors in, and sub-
jects of, merger processes. We inferred notions of disruption
from themes in informant descriptions of what changed as a re-
sult of mergers, what made merger processes more challenging
to implement and what made them harder to adapt to. Open cod-
ing by both researchers with regular confrontation saw themes
emerging early on in disruption types, levels and individual con-
sequences for managers.
With a priority of testing the disruption hypothesis, we judged
that emerging themes in types and individual impacts of dis-
ruption were less amenable to operationalization. We thus con-
strained our interpretive focus to identifying organization-­level
indicators of disruption levels, narrowing our Phase 1 research
question to: ‘What indicates a more or less disruptive merger
from managerial perspectives?’. This emergent design choice
gave way to an abductive interpretive approach (Ashworth
et al. 2019) in our continued collection and analysis of the inter-
views, in which we sought to connect data-­driven themes aris-
ing across informants' accounts with merger literature.
6   |   Phase 1: Results
Four observable aspects of merger processes emerged as indi-
cating higher disruption: (i) the extent of merger-­led workforce
rationalization, (ii) heterogeneity of merging agencies to be rec-
onciled, based on differences in local populations served (iii)
the increase in geographical scale served by the new agency,
and (iv) the number of agencies that are party to a merger.
Correspondences were found for each with merger literature re-
garding factors which are found to complicate merger processes
or raise (economic) risk or failure rates.
Table 1 summarizes the indicators and their correspondences
with the literature. See Table B1 for supplementary interview
quotes. Evidencing our abductive approach, workforce ratio-
nalization and agency heterogeneity found especially strong
correspondences in wider literature –in terms of scholarly at-
tention and more consistent results– whereas geographical scale
increase and number of merger parties emerged more inde-
pendently from the present data, with less attention or conclu-
sive findings in the literature.
7   |   Phase 2: Data and Variables
We use observable measures of the merger disruption indicators
as summarized in Table 1 to test the general idea of the disrup-
tion hypothesis from three vantage points for a fuller perspec-
tive on changing managerial gender composition. These are:
(1) women's share of agency senior management, (2) incumbent
senior managers' retention 2 years after their agency merges and
(3) women's share of new senior managerial appointments in an
agency-­year. The corresponding sub-­hypotheses (taking wom-
en's overall under-­representation as given, which is the case for
all our agency-­year observations) are:
H1.  Higher disruption has a positive relationship with women's
share of senior management.
H2.  Higher disruption has a positive relationship with women's
relative likelihood of retention 2 years after their agency merges.
H3.  Higher disruption has a positive relationship with women's
share of new senior managerial hires.

8   |   Phase 2: Methods and Results
For the proportional, longitudinal dependent variables, we use
fractional response longitudinal regression (Ramalho 2019;
Papke and Wooldridge 2008), while for the binary-­variable reten-

9   |   Discussion
The qualitative phase identified four indicators of merger-­led
disruption that resonate with managers' accounts of restructur-
ing processes and with concepts from the literature. These are
the number of agencies party to a merger, geographical scale
change, staff rationalization, and the degree of heterogeneity
between agencies that go on to merge. These indicators are intu-
itive and appear transferable across country and policy contexts,
providing a more sophisticated way of analyzing disruption ef-
fects across contexts.
Geographical scale change may be less relevant to disruption
for organizations with less direct service provision, and agency
heterogeneity can be measured in multiple ways. We opera-
tionalized it as the relative population density of areas served
by pre-­merger agencies for two reasons. First was an assump-
tion that urban and rural areas are likely to entail differences
in population needs and managerial push and pull factors, with
implications for the similarity of pre-­merger agencies' structure,
workforce, and culture. Second, the measure had limited cor-
relation with other disruption measures compared to alterna-
tives. Further development and alternative operationalizations
could better validate the measure.
Our analysis of the relationship between disruption variables
and women's shares of agency management (Table  2) finds
positive, significant associations for the composite disruption
variable and merger-­led staff rationalization. The composite
measure of disruption appears to weakly moderate women's rel-
atively higher attrition (Table 3), while it is positively and sig-
nificantly (p < 0.05) associated with women's share of new hires
(Table 4). We consider these results overall to lend modest sup-
port to the disruption hypothesis. Given the lower salience of di-
versity management in the Italian context compared to countries
like the United States (Zhang 2022), we might expect disruption
effects to be stronger in contexts with higher diversity-­related
pressures on organizational leaders.
At the same time, our results reveal that there are winners and
losers from merger processes among women, with prospective
managers ‘winning’ and incumbents ‘losing’ in representational
terms. Merging agencies shed incumbent women at a higher rate
than men, but they also hired new women managers at a higher
rate than non-­merged agencies. Overall, women's higher attri-
tion was outweighed by their higher share of new hires in the
post-­merger agencies.
In the abductive spirit of “[working] iteratively from surprises and
tensions toward theoretical insight” (van Hulst and Visser 2024,
568), we return to the broader literature on gendered organiza-
tional behaviors and outcomes to develop three theory-­informed
interpretations of our unexpected ‘winners and losers’ finding.
9.1   |   Benefits to Women From Disruption…
or From a ‘Weberian Reset’?
The first interpretation is that women managers do not bene-
fit from disruption per se, but from disruption which leads to
a more formal reset of organizational practices and networks.
Findings in the literature on women's organizational outcomes
suggest that formalization alleviates inequality (Elvira and
Graham  2002), and that women rely more on formal compe-
tencies (often for a relative lack of social capital, Lutter 2015;
Fernandez-­Mateo  2009), with men relying more on informal
networks and tacit knowledge.
That the Italian LHA mergers had formalizing effects is re-
flected in the literature (Carbone et al. 2015) and in our qualita-
tive analysis. In the words of one of our interviewees, merger-­led
changes to processes meant moving from an ‘artisanal’ profes-
sional level to more ‘structure’ and ‘sophistication’ (Interview 3,
director of management control), indicating that the mergers we
observe had formalizing effects.
In contrast, Edwards et al. (1999) found that the three merger
processes they observed in the UK negatively affected wom-
en's post-­merger progression, which they argue is due to those
merger processes reducing, rather than increasing, formality
and transparency in managerial career paths. We thus consider
it plausible that the more widely studied gendered implications
of organizational formality and transparency may affect behav-
iors and outcomes in navigating the organizational environment
during and after merger implementation. In this interpretation,
it is not simply disrupting the status quo in any way that may
improve women's managerial representation but rather inter-
rupting it such that ambiguity and informality are minimized
during the change period, and ultimately reduced in the agen-
cy's ‘final’ configuration, in a kind of Weberian reset.
9.2   |   Benefits to Different Women: A Story
of Skills-­Based or Situational Fitness?
The second interpretation is that newcomer women who benefit
from merger disruption are different from incumbent women.
This could explain our results if newcomers have skill or expe-
rience profiles that are a better fit for the new organization, for
example, or if incumbents are less well fit to the dynamics of
organizational change periods. The first possibility would align
with Zhang's (Zhang 2022, 422) suggestion that disruption dis-
proportionately favors those with better education and training,
and general findings that women's workplace advances are con-
strained to affluent groups (Ely and Meyerson 2000).
Meanwhile, recent work on organizational imprinting and
women's careers (Ashworth et al. 2023) would suggest that how
women fare in the ‘during’ and ‘after’ stages of restructuring

11

may be affected by their organization's economic conditions at
the time that they join (pre-­ or post-­merger), imprinting ‘skills,
habits and routines’ which are a better or worse fit for subse-
quent resource conditions (Tilcsik 2014, 3). Interpreted through
such a lens, women's greater post-­merger attrition rates in the
present context could suggest that they were likelier to have
been appointed in periods of resource abundance, especially if
they were relatively less experienced (Ashworth et al. 2023). Our
register data do not permit us to determine managers' experi-
ence. Future extensions could seek to examine disruption effects
for women's retention subject to historical budget constraints or
crises, for example.
9.3   |   Disadvantages to Incumbents Arising From
Sub-­ or Supra-­Organizational Disruption?
A central assumption of the disruption hypothesis is that under-
represented women in management are less socially and institu-
tionally embedded than male peers in established organizations,
with lower embeddedness associated with higher turnover in-
tentions (Moynihan and Pandey 2008). While our organizational
disruption measures appear to partly mitigate women's higher
post-­merger attrition, a third interpretation of our winners-­and-­
losers finding is that incumbent women may be less buffered
from disruption dynamics at sub-­ or supra-­organizational levels,
which our measures do not capture.
At the supra-­organizational level, senior managers' embedded-
ness in networks with external and higher-­level actors (including
politicians) could disadvantage incumbent women. Moynihan
and Pandey (2008) found individuals' external social ties were
not very significant to turnover intention, but their study did not
account for gender differences or organizational change contexts
like mergers. The latter, by intensifying competition for a contract-
ing pool of senior positions (Haveman et al. 2009) and associated
bureau-­politics, may make such ties more salient. Public sector
restructuring generally issues from political-­administrative de-
cisions (Fleischer et al. 2023), and Baekgaard and George (2018)
find that politicians' attitudes affect their support for appointing
women to senior administrative roles. Our senior managers are
not appointed by politicians, but Director-­Generals, who have the
final say in their selection, are. The political-­administrative nex-
uses with restructuring and senior managerial careers are likely a
key distinguishing factor between public and private mergers, and
merit exploration in future development of the disruption hypoth-
esis in public management.
At the sub-­organizational level, women's presumed lower em-
beddedness implies they are less buffered from individual-­level
stressors, which are widely documented for merger processes
(Buono and Bowditch 2003; Dencker  2008; Eurofound  2015)
and thus likelier to depart during change processes. This ex-
planation is consistent with findings and arguments in Worts
et al. (2007) and Edwards et al. (1999), for example, and in the
Italian context, a recent survey of healthcare system workers
found women reported suffering from burnout at nearly double
the rate of men (Quotidiano Sanità 2025).
The three interpretations of the winners-­and-­losers finding are not
mutually exclusive. Overall, they suggest there is value in adopting
a more processual understanding of disruption impacts—the
‘during’ and ‘after’—where underrepresented groups in man-
agement may be affected in different ways. The ‘Weberian reset’
interpretation suggests it is not disruption that helps women, but
rather when such disruption leads to greater formality. Future
work should explore and test types of institutional and network
disruption, especially comparing formalizing versus informaliz-
ing merger types, to clarify this. Future inquiry can also look at
whether change process formality or transparency (as opposed
to being emergent and ambiguous) affects gendered outcomes. If
the ‘Weberian reset’ explanation finds support, inquiry may turn
to the empirical prevalence and drivers of formalizing versus in-
formalizing restructuring, which may differ between public and
private sectors (Eurofound 2015, 97). For practitioners, it may spur
attention to the specific dimensions of network and institutional
change that affect gender differences in outcomes, which can
speak to the broader literature in organization studies on work-
place practices and diversity (Amis et al. 2020).
The second two interpretations are especially concerned with
incumbent women managers and encourage further analysis
of the conditions in which they joined pre-­merger agencies and
their experiences once there. This is where the disruption hy-
pothesis may complement or be enriched by existing theories
on organizational antecedents of women's managerial repre-
sentation, like imprinting (Ashworth et al. 2023) and glass cliffs
(Yang et al. 2022). For practitioners implementing restructuring,
synthesis of such theories could help identify and manage risks
to talent and diversity management, for example by profiling ex-
isting managerial talent based on their skills, experience, and
cohort, and developing plans to manage role assignments and
stressors (for example) in a more targeted way.
Our robustness analysis (Appendix  E) does not signal that
women were systematically appointed to ‘lesser’ roles. However,
cost-­conscious LHAs may have offered newly created senior
managerial positions at the lower end of formal pay bands. In
this scenario, women's increased appointments could reflect a
dynamic in which less-­remunerated directors replaced more
expensive incumbents. While lacking pay data to assess this
directly, we consider the possibility that ‘cheaper’ women man-
agers does not conflict with our interpretations of the winners-­
and-­losers puzzle. For example, reduced space for informal
salary negotiations within established pay bands may lower bar-
gaining advantages among majority-­male managers under our
‘formalizing’ interpretation. Lower fixed pay could reflect new-
comers' organizational fit profiles, especially if a larger share of
their compensation package is performance-­based in agencies
becoming more managerially sophisticated and undergoing
complex change management (‘fitness’ interpretation). Finally,
differences in pay between male and female incumbent manag-
ers may reflect gendered differences in embeddedness, in turn
influencing relative attrition likelihood in our last interpreta-
tion. Thus, the possibility of merger-­led pay dynamics does not
contradict our three interpretations but suggests that represen-
tational gains may not fully translate into remunerative ones.
Future research should examine whether merger-­related hiring
intersects with compensation dynamics.
Regarding the further generalizability of our findings, we re-
iterate that our focal population is both highly skilled and

relatively homogenous in terms of formal skill levels, types, and
career paths into management. This contrasts with Edwards
et  al.'s  (1999) study, for example, in which male and female
managers cleaved along generalist versus specialist lines with
implications for their post-­merger outcomes. We have argued
that examining a more homogenous group enabled a cleaner test
of the disruption hypothesis, but at the same time, it may con-
strain the applicability of our findings (for instance, compared
to healthcare systems where managers do not necessarily have
clinical backgrounds and roles). Exploring or controlling for
such factors in future research would help to clarify boundary
conditions.
Reflecting on the generalizability of our findings beyond the
public sector, we have already noted above that the political-­
administrative dimension of public mergers may be an import-
ant difference that bears further investigation. Meanwhile,
whether mergers are ‘formalizing’ or ‘informalizing’ may vary
between public and private sectors (Eurofound 2015, 97).
Finally, we found women's existing share of agency manage-
ment was a strong and consistent predictor of their share of new
hires (Table 4). This not only confirms similar, previous findings
(Cohen et al. 1998; Cohen and Broschak 2013) but also suggests
that efforts by leaders planning and implementing mergers to
prevent women's higher attrition may also pay dividends for
talent acquisition and the promotion of gender-­diverse manage-
ment post-­merger.
10   |   Conclusion
Ensuring women's participation and opportunities for leader-
ship in public life is a goal widely expressed by organizations
as well as a UN Sustainable Development Goal. This study ad-
dresses the need to better understand organizational factors
affecting women's progression and retention in management.
It elaborates and tests a disruption hypothesis of administrative
restructuring, whereby women's presence in management may
increase through resetting networks and institutions that previ-
ously reinforced informal barriers.
We first contribute a more sophisticated way of testing the hy-
pothesis by developing simple, replicable measures of merger
disruptiveness from the case of Italian local health agency
mergers and clinical senior managers. Using these measures
in an analysis of gendered impacts on managers across a wave
of 2014–2017 mergers, we find modest support for the disrup-
tion hypothesis, but also an unexpected finding that incumbent
women senior managers ‘lose’ and prospective female managers
‘win’ from merger processes in representational (but not neces-
sarily remunerative) terms.
Our elaboration of the disruption hypothesis goes beyond mak-
ing its prior foundations in gendered perspectives on social
networks, institutionalist and organizational change theories
more explicit. We further embed it and extend its connections
to wider scholarship in public administration and beyond on
organizational determinants of women's senior representation,
with three abductively developed interpretations of the winners-­
and-­losers finding. These are (i) that women senior managers
may benefit from more formalizing, and formal, change pro-
cesses rather than disruption tout court, (ii) that incumbent and
newcomer women may differ in their experiences and skills
(especially informal or situational, including organizational
imprinting; Ashworth et  al.  2023); and (iii) that underrepre-
sented women's lower organizational embeddedness may leave
them more vulnerable to disruption dynamics below or above
the organizational level, including emotional or stress impacts
and external political network dynamics. These interpretations
present a promising research agenda for further testing and de-
velopment of the disruption hypothesis.
Like all studies, ours has limitations. First, besides seeking to
recruit balanced numbers of female and male informants, our
qualitative phase did not impose a gendered lens ex-­ante, de-
spite the organization literature's well-­established critiques of
‘gender-­neutral’ understandings of organizations and organi-
zational practices (Acker 1990; Ely and Meyerson 2000; Amis
et al. 2020). This was motivated by the limited time senior infor-
mants were able to commit to interviews in the post-­Covid pe-
riod, survivor's bias in perspectives from interviewing managers
who had remained after their agencies' mergers, and the expec-
tation that gendered dynamics could be identified quantitatively
which may not be observable through interviews due to uncon-
scious or indirect effects of gender. Given our aim of identifying
parsimonious disruption indicators, we consider our ‘neutral’
approach to gender in the qualitative phase to be a reasonable
trade-­off. However, there is promise in using a gendered episte-
mological approach to further explore disruption mechanisms.
We intentionally focused on a relatively homogeneous mana-
gerial population in formal terms compared to those examined
in prior studies. It is possible that there is variation in de facto
seniority or status among senior managers at this level, but a
limitation of our register data is that we do not have a robust way
of operationalizing this kind of ‘informal’ variation. However,
population-­level analysis did not indicate a rebalancing of age
or disciplinary profiles of male and female senior managers be-
tween merged and non-­merged agencies (Appendix E).
Our research design does not enable causal claims, in the ab-
sence of suitable quasi-­experimental instruments or a larger
population of organizations. Future research could gather
longitudinal data from pre-­, during, and post-­merger agencies
(with pre-­ and post-­merger surveys, interviews, or fieldwork,
for example), or exploit a larger population for a difference-­in-­
differences design.
We have highlighted a number of promising areas for future
inquiry, particularly in our interpretations of the ‘winners-­
and-­losers’ finding. There is also value in testing the disruption
hypothesis in the context of other levels and types of reorganiza-
tion, like internal restructuring. While mergers between public
agencies are frequent occurrences, administrative restructuring
within agency boundaries, like decentralization, specialization,
or intra-­agency mergers (combining departments and units),
may be more common still. Future qualitative research could
also explore mechanisms of merger disruption in more depth—
for instance, our results suggest that urban mergers in Lazio and
especially Lombardy were more ‘successful’ cases of disruption
for women's representation, possibly making them good case

13
Public Administration Review, 2025
studies for further research. Gendered impacts of reorganization
reforms could also be examined for the general workforce or for
other under-­represented groups.
Our findings overall suggest practitioners may be able to pro-
mote diverse talent retention and acquisition by instating more
formal initiatives (for instance, mentoring, information-­sharing,
upskilling training) in planning and implementing agency reor-
ganizations, considering whether and where the change process
will either formalize or informalize networks and institutions,
and through profiling incumbent senior managers on the basis
of their experience, skills, or satisfaction levels as well as per-
formance, in order to better target retention interventions, for
example. Future research on the disruption hypothesis should
seek to clarify the conditions for using such approaches, in what
combinations, and their effectiveness for diversity and talent
management objectives.

Introductions, opportunity to ask questions.
1.	 What motivated you to become a director in this particular agency?
To probe: How did you find out about the role before you were appointed?

you were a part of?
To probe: Changes to the way the agency works? How did those changes come about? Changes to your role? Did change [X] make your role easier?
More challenging?
3.	 Who would you say it is most important to maintain good working relationships with to carry out your role well?
To probe—actors within the agency? Outside the agency?
4.	 How much did the merger affect key working relationships among the actors or the agencies involved? How did it change them?
To probe: In terms of tasks? Responsibilities? Information flows?
5.	 How, and to what extent, has the restructuring affected hiring and headcount?
To probe: At managerial and non-­managerial levels? Voluntary departures?

Abstract
Following decades of incremental digital development, public agencies today are permeated by a plethora of digital systems and tools.
Transcending the dominant focus on individual technologies in the extant literature, this study introduces the concept of “digital layering” to
capture the characteristics of this setting and develop a unique contextualized understanding of frontline work in the digital age. Drawing on
extensive ethnographic fieldwork in The Danish Agricultural Agency and the Danish Tax Agency, the study shows that digital layering creates a
context for frontline work characterized by the experience of complexity, fragmentation, and instability or fragility of digital technologies, which
deeply affects both backstage tasks and public encounters. In response, frontline workers employ a range of compensatory practices in the
organizational backstage, including workarounds, temporal flexibility, and collaborative IT support, and take on new organizational roles as “dig­
ital janitors” and “digital liaisons” to fix errors and raise awareness of the consequences of higher-level decision-making on the ground. During
public encounters, they avoid or take precautionary measures when using digital tools and engage in digital detective work on behalf of citizens.
Conceptualized as “digital repair work,” these compensatory practices and roles are aimed at protecting professional and bureaucratic values
such as efficiency, transparency, responsiveness, and trustworthiness. The study results in a novel theoretical framework to guide future inquiry
into digital layering and its implications for (frontline) work and organizations, including employee well-being, the continued enactment of profes­
sional and bureaucratic values, and citizens’ trust in government.

Introduction
Digitalization is currently transforming the public sector, as
digital information and communication technologies (ICTs)
enable new ways for governments to provide information,
deliver services, and engage with citizens and stakeholders.
While promising greater efficiency, responsiveness, and trans­
parency, these benefits have proven challenging to fully realize
in practice (Baldwin, Gauld, and Goldfinch 2012; Dunleavy
et al. 2006; Tummers and Rocco 2015; Yildiz 2007).
A growing body of research has examined digitalization spe­
cifically from the perspective of frontline workers, or street-
level bureaucrats (Lipsky 2010), who operate on “the ground
floor of government” (Hupe 2019), where policy meets
the realities of everyday practice and public-bureaucratic
values are enacted on a case-by-case basis (Brodkin 2011;
Zacka 2017). These studies provide insight into how digital
technologies are used in practice, and how they constrain and
enable frontline discretion and shape state-citizen encounters
(Breit et al. 2021; Buffat 2015; Busch and Henriksen 2018;
Lindgren et al. 2019).
Empirical studies so far have mainly focused on specific
technologies. Yet digital technologies are rarely introduced on
a blank slate. After decades of digitalization efforts, street-
level organizations today are permeated by digital systems
and tools whose cumulative impact on frontline work and
public encounters may be of a different kind than that of each
digital system or tool viewed in isolation. This study brings

digital development” denotes the continuous introduction of
various digital systems and tools over a decade or longer. The
aim of the study is to explore how this development impacts

and organizational roles that extend beyond dealing with in­

The study is based on extensive ethnographic fieldwork in
two government agencies: the Danish Agricultural Agency
and the Danish Tax Agency. Denmark is a global frontrunner
in digital government (OECD 2024). Both agencies are highly
digitalized and comprise characteristics of street-, screen-, and
system-level bureaucracies (Bovens and Zouridis 2002). Their
empirical richness regarding the impact of incremental digital
development on frontline work makes them well-suited for
the aim of theory development (Flyvbjerg 2006). In the fol­
lowing, I outline the theoretical background for the study and
introduce the concept of digital layering. Following an inter­
pretive research approach (Schwartz-Shea and Yanow 2012),
this concept was developed during the analytical process to
capture a phenomenon that was ubiquitous in the empirical
observations yet missing from the extant literature. The re­
search approach is unfolded in detail below.
The analysis offers an empirical grounding of the concept
by showing how incremental digital development has resulted
in a layering of digital systems and tools, which is experi­
enced by frontline workers as a work context characterized
by fragmentation, complexity, and instability. The analysis
further reveals that frontline workers’ grievances about “IT
problems” often reflect genuine concerns that challenges as­
sociated with digital layering may compromise core values
and deteriorate citizens’ trust in government. The main
part of the analysis focuses on the compensatory practices
frontline workers employ in response to these challenges
and underscores their crucial role in mitigating these risks.
Compensatory practices in the organizational backstage in­
clude workarounds, temporal flexibility, and collaborative IT
support, while practices employed during public encounters
include avoidance, precautionary measures, and digital detec­
tive work. Furthermore, many frontline workers fulfill indis­
pensable organizational roles as digital janitors and as digital
liaisons who fix errors, mend gaps, and seek to raise aware­
ness of the implications of higher-level decision-making on
the ground.
Taking incremental digital development as its point of de­
parture, this study makes several contributions. First, it offers
a unique contextualized understanding of frontline work in
the digital era and hence an updated and timely “ethnog­
raphy of a modern job” (Orr 1996). Second, with the de­
velopment of the novel concept of digital layering and the
identification and integration of associated compensatory
practices and organizational roles into a coherent frame­
work, the study makes an original theoretical contribution
and provides rich grounds for further inquiry into the nature
and future of frontline work. Building on the notion of policy
repair (Masood and Nisar 2022), I suggest that the identified
practices and roles may be viewed as constituting a form of
digital repair work, the prevalence of which may well ex­
tend to other street-level contexts and beyond. The findings
further highlight interactions between frontline workers, IT
professionals, and system-level bureaucrats as a central av­
enue for future research into the digital state. I return to these
issues in the discussion.
Street-level digitalization
Researchers have long examined the ongoing digitalization of
administrative processes and citizen-oriented services under
headings such as e-government, digital-era governance, and
digital transformation (Dunleavy et al. 2006; Scupola and
Mergel 2022; Yildiz 2007). A subset of studies focuses on
street-level bureaucracy. In their seminal contribution, Bovens
and Zouridis claim that “the implementation of the law has
virtually been perfected” and describe how the introduction of
ICTs and digital case processing entails an evolution toward
“screen-level” and “system-level” bureaucracies that increas­
ingly minimize street-level discretion (Bovens and Zouridis
2002, 174). In the screen-level bureaucracy, frontline workers
rely on ICT for information processing and become “digital
bureaucrats operating computers instead of interacting face-
to-face with clients” (Busch and Henriksen 2018, 3). In the
system-level bureaucracy, decisions are made in digital sys­
tems without human intervention (Busch, Henriksen, and
Sæbø 2018). Bovens and Zouridis argue that, with the trans­
formation toward system-level bureaucracies, discretionary
powers shift from frontline workers to system analysts and
software designers, and new challenges arise to ensure fun­
damental public values such as legality, accountability, and
transparency (Bovens and Zouridis 2002; see also Fest et al.
2023).
Following Bovens and Zouridis’ seminal work, research on
street-level digitalization has been preoccupied with assessing
its implications for frontline workers’ discretion (Busch and
Henriksen 2018). Buffat (2015) argues that most studies
adopt either a “curtailment” thesis (digitalization diminishes
street-level discretion) or an “enablement” thesis (digitaliza­
tion enables street-level bureaucrats to use their discretion
in new and unanticipated ways). In a more recent review,
Marienfeldt (2024) distinguishes between decisive and sup­
portive digital tools and finds that the former tends to cur­
tail discretion in service-oriented organizations, while the
latter enables decision-making in regulation-oriented organ­
izations, and highlights the “continuation” thesis (human
judgment continues to play a crucial role in digitalized policy
implementation).
Recent empirical studies also focus on street-level dis­
cretion when examining the impact of, for example, auto­
mated case processing and algorithmic decision support
on frontline workers’ decision-making and enforcement
styles. Meijer, Lukas, and Wessels (2021) and de Boer and
Raaphorst (2023) both find support for curtailment as well
as enablement, but also call for the literature to move beyond
the dominant focus on discretion. Others examine how ICTs
impact public encounters, showing how ICTs transform the
nature of encounters as face-to-face interactions are replaced
or supplemented with digitalized communications, for ex­
ample, via email or digital self-service systems, and that ICTs
may have significant implications for information processing
(Breit et al. 2021; Busch, Henriksen, and Sæbø 2018; Hansen,
Lundberg, and Syltevik 2018; Lindgren et al. 2019; Løberg
2022; Pors and Schou 2020; Tummers and Rocco 2015).

For example, fragmented information from digital case man­

workers often go beyond their roles to help citizens when
their problems do not fit digitalized service categories, or if
digital tools create obstacles for them (Pors and Schou 2020;

The extant literature has yielded many important insights,
demonstrating that frontline workers play a central role in
the implementation of e-government, that they change their
behavior to fit new situations, and that there is nothing de­
terministic about digital technologies: Their impact depends
on how they are used in practice, and this again is influenced
by rules, resources, norms, values, and interpretative schemes
in specific social and organizational contexts. Consequently,
scholars underscore the need to study digital technologies-in-
use (Buffat 2015; Busch, Henriksen, and Sæbø 2018; Høybye-
Mortensen 2019; Meijer, Lorenz, and Wessels 2021). Yet, one
important limitation stands out, namely, that most studies
focus on the implementation and impact on one specific tech­
nology. Hence, the literature has yet to recognize that, after
decades of incremental digital development, street-level or­
ganizations today are permeated by a plethora of digital sys­
tems and tools, whose cumulative impact on frontline work
may be of a different kind than that of each digital system or
tool viewed in isolation.
Digital layering
Building on this study’s empirical observations, and inspired
by the notion of “layering,” which has long been employed in
institutional theory to describe the processes by which new
elements are added on top of pre-existing structures (Capano
2019), I propose that many frontline workers today operate
in a context characterized by digital layering. The widely used
conceptual distinction between street-level, screen-level, and
system-level bureaucracies indicates a demarcation between
different “digital states,” where digital systems or tools may
support traditional street-level work (e.g., by providing access
to digitized information), change its nature (e.g., by relocating
the encounter from the street to the screen), or replace it
(e.g., by automating decisions and relocating discretion at
the system-level). In contrast, the concept of digital layering
attempts to capture the nonlinear and incremental nature of
digital development in practice as exemplified by the variety
of digital technologies in use in frontline agencies such as the
ones studied here (see also Marienfeldt 2024).
Digital layering denotes a situation in which new digital
solutions are introduced while older systems and tools are
still in use, including so-called “legacy systems” that are often
difficult or impossible to replace (i.e., outdated software or
hardware that meets the needs it was originally designed for
but does not allow for growth). In a context characterized
by digital layering, digital technologies and work characteris­
tics that are typically associated with either street-, screen-, or
system-level bureaucracy will co-exist and may be intertwined
in practice. Accordingly, digital layering is likely to impact
many aspects of frontline work at once, including public
encounters and backstage practices (e.g., case management),
which are closely intertwined (Goldman and Foldy 2015;
Møller 2021), and organizational roles, that is, functions that
do not pertain to individual cases (as exemplified in studies of
street-level policy entrepreneurs (Cohen and Aviram 2021) or
policy repair (Masood and Nisar 2022)).
By asking how the continuous introduction and layering of
digital technologies impacts frontline work, this study takes a
novel approach to street-level digitalization. Taking the per­
spective of frontline workers as a starting point, the aim is to
develop a rich empirically grounded understanding of how
the “cumulative effect” of incremental digital development
is experienced by frontline workers and how it shapes their
daily work. As unfolded below, the notion of digital layering
was developed as part of the analytical process to capture
a prevalent empirical phenomenon. The empirical analysis
substantiates its relevance and provides a basis for theoret­
ical development regarding the impact of digital layering on
frontline work and the identification of issues that warrant
further inquiry.
Research approach
The research approach is interpretive and abductive in the
sense that the study was motivated by a puzzle stemming from
initial fieldwork observations and applied an open-ended
approach but is informed by extant theory (Schwartz-Shea
and Yanow 2012). Guided by the theoretical developments
outlined above, I focus on digital technologies-in-use and un­
derstand these technologies to encompass both material and
social aspects, where the latter denotes the different meanings,
participants attach to digital technologies, and the various
features they emphasize and use (Orlikowski 1992). I specifi­
cally focus on digital office technologies (Høybye-Mortensen
2019) required to accomplish the work, including both
hardware (e.g., computers and tablets) and software (from
digitized case files to advanced forms of digitalization, such
as digital case management and automated case processing).
Inspired by Orr (1996), I also focus on work practices, that
is, what frontline workers do and say to accomplish their
tasks, including their explanations and sensemaking. I hence
follow a practice-based approach in letting the perspective of
research participants decide what is important, why and how,
and use this as a starting point for theorizing (Feldman and
Orlikowski 2011).
Research context
The data are generated through multi-sited organizational
ethnography (Ybema et al. 2009) in the Danish Tax Agency
and the Danish Agricultural Agency. With a total of approx.
5,000 employees, the Tax Agency is responsible for ensuring
correct payments of taxes and debts for the entire popula­
tion of taxpayers. The Agricultural Agency has approx. 1,500
employees and is responsible for the administration of sev­
eral national and European Union support schemes, ensuring
compliance with regulations, and facilitating green transition.
Denmark is a recognized global frontrunner in digital govern­
ment (OECD 2024). Both agencies have introduced numerous
digital systems and tools in the past two decades, including
digitized and automated case processing, self-service systems,
and GPS antennas and tablets to be used during inspections.
Some are provided by external IT vendors; others are devel­
oped in-house by sizable internal IT units.
Both agencies also employ large groups of frontline
workers who perform inspections and/or provide government

future of much frontline work. For these reasons, they are
likely to provide rich empirical grounds for practice-based

Methods and data
Fieldwork encompassed interviews and observations in
four units in one location in the tax agency, and three units
across four locations in the agricultural agency. Observations
were conducted as shadowing (Czarniawska 2007) of four
frontline workers and four frontline managers in each
agency. The sixteen participants were shadowed for 3 days
each. In total, observations amounted to 47 days (327
hours) and 1,942 pages of transcribed fieldnotes. The au­
thor shadowed the frontline workers, and another member
of the research team shadowed the managers. This involved
observing participants at their desks, accompanying them
out on inspections, listening-in on telephone conversations
with citizens, participating in physical and online meetings
with co-workers, higher-level bureaucrats, managers, IT
developers, etc., lunch and informal conversations.
Qualitative interviews were conducted with participants
before and after shadowing. Pre-fieldwork interviews were
approximately 30 minutes long, semi-structured, and fo­
cused on participants’ backgrounds and tasks. Post-fieldwork
interviews lasted approximately 1 hour and focused on
participants’ uses of and reflections on digital technologies
with reference to our observations. Consequently, these
interviews were loosely structured and adapted to each
participant. Group interviews were conducted with four
to five additional frontline workers in each organizational
unit, also focusing on their uses of and reflections on digital
technologies. All lasted 1 hour. We also conducted interviews
with higher-level managers and agency directors focusing
on organizational history, current challenges, and strategic
initiatives in relation to digitalization. While the current
study focuses on the experiences and perspectives of frontline
workers, these multiple and varied data sources provided in­
valuable grounds for interpretation and contextualization of
findings. Table 2 presents an overview of data sources. For
ethical considerations, see Appendix 1.
Analytical procedures
All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim.
Field notes were transcribed with as much detail as possible
and supplemented with analytical and theoretical memos
(Charmaz 2014; Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw 2011). The
analysis was a recursive process of searching for significant
patterns of action and thought in the generated data, criti­
cally reviewing these patterns, and interpreting the findings
in light of previous research on street-level digitalization.
This involved shifting attention between data excerpts and
the larger dataset as well as between data and theory in sev­
eral overlapping phases, as is typical for interpretive research
(Charmaz 2014; Schwartz-Shea and Yanow 2012).

Findings
The findings are presented in four subsections, which together
demonstrate how the “cumulative effects” of longstanding in­
cremental digital development are experienced by frontline
workers and how it affects their work. The first subsection
presents the curious observation that, even though frontline
work in both agencies is highly digitalized and digitalization
has been ongoing for decades, frontline workers feel that
the process is in its infancy. The second subsection details
the challenges they experience in their daily work and the
practices they employ in response, namely, workarounds,
temporal flexibility, and collaborative IT support. The third
subsection demonstrates how frontline workers take on or­
ganizational roles as digital janitors and digital liaisons. The
fourth subsection focuses on the public encounter and shows
how frontline workers perceive technical errors as threats to
their professional authority and trustworthiness in the eyes of
citizens. Consequently, they avoid digital tools or take precau­
tionary measures and engage in digital detective work.

One needs to spend only a few minutes in each agency’s offices

Despite this pervasiveness of digital systems and tools,
frontline workers in both agencies express the sentiment that
digitalization is still in its nascent stages. Some even claim that
“there is NO digitalization in the public sector!” (cf. above).
Yet, most are positive toward digitalization as such. They
willingly give examples of how digital systems and tools have
improved efficiency and communications with citizens and
other stakeholders. When asked in group interviews about
their dream scenarios, most mention more digitalization, not
less. Most importantly, however, they want what they already
have to work (better). In their experience, the many digital
systems and tools that permeate their tasks often do not work
as intended. Suboptimal functionality, non-integrated sys­
tems, and frequent breakdowns often result in cumbersome
operations and long waiting times for both frontline workers
and stakeholders. As their dependence on digital systems and
tools increases, the consequences of technical failure become
more critical, and frustrations seem to grow.
It would be easy (and not entirely incorrect) to categorize
frontline workers’ complaints over “IT problems” as the type
of everyday gripe that is an artifact of social life in most organ­

The frontline manager recognizes that “IT problems” cause
difficulties but argues that many complaints reflect “subjective”
preferences. However, a closer analysis of frontline workers’
experiences and practices reveals more pressing issues that are
not only related to individual technologies but also to the sheer
number (layering) of digital systems and tools on which frontline
workers’ tasks rely and the pervasiveness of “IT problems” in
their daily work. The following sections unfold the challenges
associated with this digital layering as experienced by frontline
workers, outlining the compensatory practices they employ
backstage, the roles they take on to make digitalization work in
practice, and the impact on public encounters.
Compensatory practices: workarounds, temporal
flexibility, collaborative IT support
In both agencies, there is a generalized expectation among
frontline workers that digital systems and tools will fail. Their
slight surprise when things actually work indicate that errors
and breakdowns are common:
Catherine tries to log on and mumbles: “Now I can’t ac­
cess [the system] … oh, there it is! Well, it has been re­
ported as being out of order the past few days.” She tells
me she signed up to receive notifications of errors “back in
the day,” which is why she knows. She continues: “I just
about thought that I would not be able to access it today,
but it turned out it was just slow.”
(Field notes, tax agency, Day 2)
In addition to server breakdowns and technical failure,
frontline workers find many digital operations quite cum­
bersome. In their view, digital systems are often developed
without considering how they are to be used in practice, in­
cluding the fact that one system or tool is often part of a
longer sequence of operations. During a lively discussion,
two frontline workers characterized IT developers as people
with “paper towel rolls in front of their eyes.” Holding up
his hands in front of his eyes to illustrate the apparent tunnel
vision of IT developers, one stated that “they only see their
own little part of the process” and not the complexity of
the combined tasks and processes that must work together
in everyday practice (Field notes, agricultural agency, Day
15). In response to suboptimal tools, ad hoc solutions and
workarounds are an integral part of frontline work:

When systems crash or response times are slow, frontline
workers generally employ a strategy of temporal flexibility,
that is, they postpone or switch tasks. Particularly in the ag­
ricultural agency, where frontline workers divide their time
between the field, the office, and their home office, they often
end up going home to complete the work later, sometimes late
in the evening, or wait until the next day. Following the inter­

she will try to reboot at home, or just wait: “Sometimes, time
is the solution!” This flexibility is generally considered one
of the perks of the job and something that is highly valued
by many. However, the layering of digital tools and systems,
each with their own “temper” (i.e., frequency and severity
of technical issues), clearly necessitates this temporal flexi­
bility. If frontline workers in the agricultural agency insisted
on working “nine to five,” they would spend a lot of time
waiting.
Frontline workers’ frustrations are exacerbated by an ap­
parent lack of adequate IT support. Many find that the IT
support personnel are not sufficiently familiar with the digital
tools on which their work depends. For example, one frontline
worker explains that no one in the IT support unit has exper­
tise in tablets, although tablets have been introduced widely
across the agency in recent years. This problem is exacerbated
when new specialized software is introduced. Frontline
workers complain that they “get parked in the system” or that
the IT support unit will mark a request as completed even if
the issue is not solved. Many feel that precisely their area is
being neglected in terms of support and development, while
other areas have greater priority. In response, they uphold a
practice of collaborative IT support. Field notes include nu­
merous examples of help-seeking, advice-giving, collabora­
tion to identify and fix errors, and sharing of workarounds
and informal knowledge about expert co-workers. Sometimes
workers would join forces and report a pressing error to the
IT support unit all at the same time, to gain visibility, but oth­
erwise, reporting was mainly a way to keep formal track of
the prevalence of technical failures.

janitors and digital liaisons

between them prompts frontline workers to take on organiza­

links between systems, for example, when case processing
procedures span different digital systems that do not com­
municate, or when operating older systems that cannot auto­
matically integrate data from, for example, digital registries.
Consequently, many frontline workers spend their time per­
forming manual operations such as copying and pasting data

the system-level bureaucracy. Errors may arise due to rapid
policy changes, as digital systems may not be adjusted ac­

“backtrack” system operations and manually correct each

not replace frontline work but relies on it, and only partially

Furthermore, frontline workers are frequently involved in
the development of screen- and system-level bureaucracies as
they are required to test adaptations to digital systems and
report errors to higher-ups who then communicate with IT
developers to fix them. For some, this is an integrated part
of their job, on which they spend approximately 3 months
per year. As policymakers typically change rules annually, if
not more often, agencies frequently need to adapt their old
legacy systems, even though they were never designed for
that. Because of this continuous incremental development,
digital systems gradually become more fragile and less stable,
and the risk of technical failure, breakdowns, and errors in
the data on which frontline workers base their inspections
and decisions increases. As one participant explained:
The challenge with [one of the digital case management
systems] is that every year when new rules are made, the
system has to calculate something extra, or in a new way,
and then they [the IT developers] just build that on top
of it. And the system becomes more and more fragile be­
cause new calculations are added. I mean, it really does
need someone to start over and build new tasks from the
ground. But because all the rules are changed every year,
they add new tasks every year, and that, I think, is getting
worse and worse. Because there is not going to be less po­
litical attention to how much financial support farmers will
receive. So, every year, they change [the rules] a little bit,
for the Ministry to make their own mark. It is challenging,
because then every year there is something new to add, and
no one has tested whether it works. It would be nice if they
could add more stability, but I do not really think that it is
possible, in this agency. And we do not make the rules our­
selves, so we cannot simply decide that now we will start
over and then make a set of rules and not change it.

Day 16)

their initiative.
Public encounters: avoidance, precautionary
measures, and digital detective work
The compensatory practices and organizational roles outlined
so far primarily belong to the organizational backstage. The
“frontstage,” that is, public encounters, present different
challenges, as fragmentation, breakdowns and errors also
cause problems for citizens and stakeholders. For example, an
agricultural inspector tells me that farmers are required to reg­
ister information that forms the grounds for inspections in a
self-service system on an annual basis. However, the system—
which is adapted incrementally—always crashes on the day
it opens for new registrations. This results in long waiting
times and frustrations for citizens and stakeholders. During
a regional meeting, a higher-level manager suggests that the
agency’s low scores in a recent user satisfaction survey can be
attributed to farming consultants, who mainly interact with
the agency through the digital systems, as satisfaction with
inspectors is consistently high. He later presents an upcoming
annual task, firmly stating: “We begin 10 September! That
is, if our IT system is ready on the day. Let’s count on that.
Fourth time must be the charm!” (Field notes, agricultural
agency, Day 12).
Although the performance of digital self-service systems
does not necessarily reflect poorly on frontline workers,
some still feel that the imminent risk of technical failure
compromises their professional standards. An inspector in the
agricultural agency explained that some of their tools are less

Arnold explains that he really sees a lot of potential in

less precise than those used by land surveyors, “and still
my measurement and judgment is the authoritative one!”

he wishes their equipment would measure up to that used
by others in the business.

me: “If we cannot provide an answer, then who can? We
are the tax agency! We really should know and be able to
explain it!”
(Field notes, tax agency, Day 5).
In sum, some frontline workers perceive technical errors and
breakdowns as potential threats to their professional au­
thority and trustworthiness as representatives of the state
in the eyes of citizens and stakeholders. To mitigate these
threats, they employ compensatory practices during public
encounters: They avoid using digital tools, take precautionary
measures, or take on digital detective work on behalf of citi­
zens to clear up errors and resolve ambiguities.
Discussion
Frontline work inside the digital state unfolds amidst a con­
tinuous layering of digital systems and tools on which the
work depends, and which impacts this work in ways that
are not only related to the (mal)functioning of individual
technologies but also to the number of digital technologies in


## Page 11

143
use and ensuing pervasiveness of “IT problems.” Digital lay­
ering entails fragmentation, as case processing often requires
a mix of digital and manual operations or relies on several
digital systems and tools that are not integrated. Even au­
tomated processes may require manual backtracking. This
results in complexity and a lack of insight and overview.
The steady introduction of new digital systems and tools
combined with a continued reliance on outdated legacy sys­
tems results in frequent breakdowns and experiences of fra­
gility and instability, accompanied by seemingly inadequate
IT support. Accordingly, many frontline workers view the
digital technologies that permeate their work as suboptimal
at best and undependable at worst. To enhance efficiency and
mitigate what they perceive as threats to their professional
authority and the trustworthiness of government, they em­
ploy a range of compensatory practices and take on new or­
ganizational roles.
Figure 1 presents a theoretical framework delineating the
nature of digital layering and its impact on frontline work as
suggested by these empirical findings.
The findings confirm and extend prior insights on street-
level digitalization, particularly regarding discretion (Buffat
2015; Busch and Henriksen 2018; Marienfeldt 2024). Digital
layering influences frontline workers differently across the
two agencies. In the Agricultural Agency, where tasks com­
bine traditional street-level work with elements of screen- and
system-level bureaucracies, decisions remain largely non-
automated. Here, digital tools enhance professional judgment
by providing access to supplementary information in the field.
Conversely, in the Tax Agency, where screen- and system-level
bureaucracies dominate, automated decision-making signif­
icantly curtails discretion. However, workers still exercise
discretion by offering services beyond formal requirements
(e.g., when engaging in digital detective work on behalf of
citizens). Frontline workers in both agencies also exercise
considerable discretion regarding the backstage practices
they employ to make digital government work, that is,
workarounds, temporal flexibility, and collaborative IT sup­
port. Furthermore, as digital liaisons, they engage and affect
discretionary decision-making at the system level.
In relation to the public encounter, the findings support
previous studies in suggesting that frontline workers, also
in digitalized settings, often “move toward” and provide
help to clients in need (Løberg 2022; Pors and Schou 2020;
Tummers and Rocco 2015). Yet, in the present study, this
rarely happens because citizens’ problems are “too complex
for digital services” (Løberg 2022, 19) but rather because
the digital systems are too complex for citizens. Accordingly,
encounters become increasingly focused on helping citizens
navigate the digitalized bureaucracy, yet frontline workers
may be left unable to understand yet alone explain to citizens
how their case will be processed and what to expect. There
is a real risk that the fragmentation and lack of transparency
caused by digital layering may hinder the enactment of bu­
reaucratic values and compromise administrative law, partic­
ularly when accompanied by increasing automation (Grøn
and Møller 2024; Olsen, Slosser, and Hildebrandt 2021; Røhl
and Hansen 2024).
The analysis highlights how the fragility caused by digital
layering and suboptimal technologies introduces a sense of in­
stability and lack of control into the encounter, that is, a form
of “digital uncertainty” that adds to other forms of uncer­
tainty in frontline work (Raaphorst 2018). Some experience
this as a real predicament in relation to their bureaucratic

ethos (Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022) and role as state
representatives. Viewing technological failures as threats to
their professional authority and trustworthiness, they worry
that citizen perceptions of inefficiency or unresponsiveness
during encounters could undermine broader trust in gov­
ernment. While their compensatory practices (i.e., avoid­
ance, precautionary measures, and digital detective work)
may be construed as “coping,” that is, psychological defense
mechanisms rooted in the need for self-preservation (Breit
et al. 2021; Lipsky 2010), they are better understood as
expressions of “state professionalism” (Busch, Henriksen, and
Sæbø 2018), oriented toward accomplishing the work while
preserving the integrity of the state and upholding values of
responsiveness, transparency, and consistency. As bureau­
cratic encounters may indeed shape citizens’ trust in govern­
ment (Christensen and Lægreid 2005; Eckhard and Friedrich
2022), the pragmatic value of these practices should not be
disregarded.
The findings also speak to the well-established discrepancy
between frontline workers’ responsibilities and their conditions
for fulfilling them (Hupe and Buffat 2014). Digitalization does
not change this fundamental condition, but introduces new
requirements, responses, and consequences. For example,
frontline workers accommodate the frequent breakdowns
and long waiting times associated with digital layering by
employing temporal flexibility. While the freedom to plan their
own work is generally perceived as a benefit, this temporal flex­
ibility is in fact necessitated by the instability of systems and
hence cannot be refused, or agency operations would suffer.
Similarly, frontline workers have always spent time correcting
errors, fixing problems, and testing new procedures, yet the
nature and scale of this work appears to change. With digital
layering, a surprising number of manual operations are needed
to connect fragmented processes and systems. Furthermore,
automated case processing is efficient also when things go
wrong: “Automated errors” may affect thousands of cases be­
fore corrections can be made. The role of digital janitor hence
becomes a core function in the digital state.
Finally, the identified practices cut across the street-, screen-
and system-level bureaucracies as these are intertwined in
daily operations. Even as automation increases, frontline
workers still encounter citizens in the field or on the screen,
and these encounters are affected by the (mal-)functioning
and perceived unreliability of digital systems and the prospec­
tive lack of transparency and predictability regarding screen-
and system-level operations, which stem from the continued
reliance on manual operations as well as automation. The
roles of digital janitor and digital liaison both span the street,
screen, and system levels, as frontline workers call attention
to and seek to alleviate errors in the organizational backstage
and at the same time seek to mitigate or compensate for their
potentially negative impacts during public encounters. The
system-level bureaucracy has not replaced frontline work, as
Bovens and Zouridis (2002) suggested, it depends on it. In
the digital state, the street-, screen-, and system-level bureauc­
racy co-exist, leaving frontline workers to navigate them all
at once.
Limitations
Some important limitations should be noted. First, the
study conveys the perspective of frontline workers. Hence,
the analysis does not provide a systematic account of how
technologies associated with street-, screen, and system-level
bureaucracy interact and overlap but remains focused on how
frontline workers experience and navigate the “cumulative
effects” of incremental digital development (i.e., a work con­
text characterized by digital layering) through the identified
practices and organizational roles. Furthermore, what stands
out in this analysis are the challenges associated with digital
layering, while effective digitalization is largely rendered in­
conspicuous. While there is little doubt that digitalization has
increased efficiency and transparency in many ways compared
to the old days of pen and paper, we mainly notice it when
things do not work as expected.
Second, this study of digital layering and its impact on
frontline work is linked to a particular context and historical
development. Denmark is among the most digitalized coun­
tries in the world, and the studied agencies are frontrunners
who pay the prize of trial and error. Different challenges may
arise in contexts where digitalization efforts are more recent,
based on newer technologies, and informed by the experiences
of early adopters.
Third, the study is focused on specific policy areas and types
of frontline work. The transferability of the identified roles
and practices may hence vary. However, as “[w]ork today al­
most always entails the digital” (Orlikowski and Scott 2016,
88), the need to navigate digital layering may well extend
to other areas of frontline work and beyond. For example,
studies of medical professionals suggest that they may expe­
rience similar challenges and employ similar practices both
backstage and during encounters as their work is increasingly
infused with digital technologies (Stana and Hertzum 2024).
In contrast, practices of avoidance and precautionary meas­
ures may be irrelevant for police officers patrolling the streets
as in the traditional image of street-level bureaucracy; yet po­
lice officers may well need to employ compensatory practices
such as workarounds and temporal flexibility when proc­
essing cases in the organizational backstage, or take on roles
as digital janitors and liaisons to IT professionals in relation
to the introduction of AI and data-driven policing (Fest et al.
2023; Meijer, Lorenz, and Wessels 2021).
Conclusion
Studies of street-level digitalization have long focused on
how specific digital technologies impact frontline discretion
and public encounters. By approaching digitalization as an
incremental development process that involves the contin­
uous introduction of numerous digital systems and tools and
results in digital layering, this study contributes with a rich
contextualized understanding of frontline work in the digital
age and an original theoretical framework regarding the im­
pact of digital layering. While being historically and contex­
tually grounded, the dynamics and relationships articulated
in the theoretical framework may serve to explain and guide
action in other situations (Feldman and Orlikowski 2011,
1249).
Further research is needed to further substantiate the
proposed concepts, relationships, and implications for
theory and practice. For example, the identified compen­
satory practices and organizational roles may be further
conceptualized as a specific type of “policy repair” (Masood
and Nisar 2022), namely, digital repair work (see also Strebel,
Bovet, and Sormani 2019). Whereas participants in this study

text of health care have been associated with technostress and
burnout (Stana and Hertzum 2024). The emotional and psy­
chological consequences of digital repair work warrant fur­
ther inquiry, as does the notion of digital uncertainty.
Another avenue concerns the role of frontline workers in
the continued development of e-government. A key character­
istic of repair work is that it remains “tangential to the formal
policy process and results in local, transient, and context-
specific fixes rather than new policies” (Masood and Nisar
2022, 257). Digital repair work may hence be conspicuous
to frontline workers, yet invisible to those in charge of devel­
oping and implementing new digital solutions (Justesen and
Plesner 2024). Furthermore, given the fragmentation caused
by digital layering and variable public value-sensitivity of
IT professionals (Fest et al. 2023), frontline workers may be
uniquely positioned to detect inconsistencies and call atten­
tion to potential violations of public-bureaucratic values in
daily operations (Grøn and Møller 2024). As digital liaisons,
frontline workers may help clarify how things work, raise
awareness of actual implementation costs, and anticipate un­
intended consequences. By developing digital layering as a
theoretical lens on frontline work in the digital era, this study
encourages scholars and practitioners to consider the forest
as well as the trees, as new technologies are introduced in al­
ready densely digitalized settings.

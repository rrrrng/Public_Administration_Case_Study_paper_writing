Abstract
This study investigates leadership representation and the perceptions of public employees, specifically focusing on a fire department led by a
Black fire chief. Drawing from literature on representative bureaucracy and relational demography, we explore how leadership representation
influences street-level bureaucrats’ views on diversity and representation within the organization. Through in-depth interviews with both Black
and White firefighters, we find distinctions in their perceptions regarding racial diversity and representation. First, Black firefighters viewed the
presence of a Black leader as empowering, leading to opportunities for professional advancement and a commitment to community representation.
In contrast, White firefighters perceived race-based diversity efforts as undermining meritocracy. Second, White firefighters preferred to avoid
having discussions about race and diversity within the workplace, often referring to their familial bond and collective firefighter identity that min­
imized racial differences among their colleagues. Moreover, White firefighters are colorblind until race poses a material threat to them. Black
firefighters reported selectively engaging in discussions depending on the context or cultural environment, or avoiding conversations, especially
when they perceived potential consequences. Our findings suggest that leadership representation serves as a context to further interrogate these
divergences. This research expands our understanding of leadership representation within public agencies and practically highlights the importance
of managing diversity issues to foster inclusivity and mitigate tensions.
Key words: representative bureaucracy; leadership; interviews; firefighters; race.
Introduction
Scholarship examining the importance and consequences of
the demographic makeup of government abounds in the public
administration literature (Frederickson 1990; Meier 1975;
Meier and Nigro 1976; Selden and Selden 2001). This literature
has focused on representation as a crucial feature of a govern­
ment that serves diverse constituents, and the literature has
examined its impact on citizen–state interactions (Carrell and
Mann 1995; Pitts 2005; Riccucci 2021; Rice 2015). Most of
the diversity literature in public administration centers on the
theory of representative bureaucracy (Hindera 1993; Meier
1975; Meier and Capers 2012), which, at its most basic level,
asks, “What happens when government reflects the sociode­
mographic characteristics of the public?” The theory postulates
that when public organizations share similar characteristics
with the community, they are more likely to produce positive
outcomes for the public and enhance their perceived legitimacy
(Meier, Wrinkle, and Polinard 1999).
Representative bureaucracy scholars have identified three dis­
tinct types of representation: passive, active, and symbolic repre­
sentation. When a public sector employee shares a similar
demographic attribute or social identity with a member of the
public, it is identified as passive representation (Bishu and Ken­
nedy 2020; Headley and Wright 2020). Active representation
highlights the shared experiences, values, and understanding
between a bureaucrat and citizen (as a result of shared demo­
graphics), which is assumed to result in enhanced tangible out­
comes for the public (Bishu and Kennedy 2020; Bradbury and
Kellough 2007, 2011; Grissom, Nicholson-Crotty, and
Nicholson-Crotty 2009; Selden 1997). Finally, symbolic repre­
sentation is the effect of having a representative workforce on
public perceptions, irrespective of changes in bureaucrat behavior
or service delivery outcomes (Headley, Wright, and Meier 2021;
Theobald and Haider-Markel 2008; Wright 2022). The represen­
tative bureaucracy literature primarily focuses on the importance
and impact of representation on the public in areas where prior
inequities have existed. While the public is an important focal
point, understanding how employees are represented by lower
and upper management within these organizations is also import­
ant (Grissom and Keiser 2011), particularly if internal organiza­
tional inequities also exist. Management scholarship continues
to wrestle with ways to improve the perceptions and experiences
of employees in the workplace (Schwarz, Eva, and Newman
2020). We assert that one area that impacts employee perceptions
is when frontline employees are in contact with supervisors and
leaders who exhibit their same racial and ethnic characteristics
(and this holds both symbolic and substantive value). Hence, we
seek to understand: “How are bureaucrat perceptions related to

Empirical studies in relational demography have found that
demographic similarity between employees and supervisors,
often but not always, promotes positive workgroup outcomes
(Brower, Schoorman, and Tan 2000; Foley et al. 2006; Joshi
and Knight 2015). Thus, guided by the representative bureau­
cracy literature, focusing explicitly on internal representation
and leveraging insights from relational demography, this study
seeks to understand the role of leadership representation on
employees’ perceptions in a highly bureaucratic and hierarchi­
cal government organization—a fire department. According to
the National Fire Department Registry, approximately 27,000
fire departments exist in the United States (Fent et al. 2022),
employing over 1.2 million fire personnel. Like other emer­
gency management service personnel, fire service personnel
operate within (mostly) public agencies and interact daily with
the public.
This study analyzes in-depth interviews with 47 firefighters
in a medium-sized city in the southeast region of the United
States. This fire department is headed by a Black fire chief,
which represents less than 6% of the total fire chiefs in the
United States and less than 1% worldwide (U.S. Fire Admin­
istration 2024). Through the qualitative analysis of the inter­
view transcripts, this study seeks to understand how employees
view issues of racial diversity and representation under the
leadership of a Black fire chief. To do so, we oversample Black
firefighters in this fire department and compare their responses
to their White peers. In our findings, Black firefighters viewed
enhanced representation in the department as important for
the community, leading to more inspiration and opportunities
for advancement. They reported being inclined to discuss sen­
sitive topics directly related to their lived reality selectively (e.g.,
at opportune times or with select people who could relate), but
not within the organization as a whole. On the contrary, White
firefighters valued meritocracy in personnel processes, admitted
to not seeing racial differences but rather appealing to familial
ties to the organization, and were less prone to discuss sensitive
racial issues. In this case, representation in leadership drew out
the differences in how street-level bureaucrats viewed issues of
representation and diversity in the workplace.
Representative bureaucracy
Most representative bureaucracy literature analyzes the trans­
lation from passive to active representation. Active representa­
tion involves bureaucrats proactively engaging in making
decisions that benefit represented groups or advocating for the
interests of the represented groups (Bradbury and Kellough
2007, 2011; Headley, Wright, and Meier 2021; Wright and
Headley 2020). Empirical evidence on active representation has
shown that it can lead to several positive outcomes. For instance,
racial representation is associated with reductions in police mis­
conduct and crime rates (Hong 2016, 2017), improved educa­
tional outcomes for students of color (Grissom, Nicholson-Crotty,
and Nicholson-Crotty 2009; Nicholson-Crotty et al. 2016), and
more contracts awarded for minority-owned businesses (Fer­
nandez, Malatesta, and Smith 2013). Further, gender represen­
tation is associated with better math outcomes for girls in school
(Keiser et al. 2002) and more successful sexual assault case
resolutions (Meier and Nicholson-Crotty 2006). However,
increased representation may also be correlated with adverse
outcomes, as in the case of racial representation in policing and
increased racial disparities in vehicle stops (Wilkins and Wil­
liams 2008, 2009), or have no impact at all, as in the case of
female representation and government contracts awarded for
women-owned small business (Fernandez, Malatesta, and Smith
2013). While these latter studies highlight that increased active
representation may not always lead to enhanced equity for the
targeted group (Anbarci and Lee 2014; Brown and Frank 2006;
Headley 2022b), there seems to be less evidence supporting the
negative effects of representation. That said, research has argued
that certain conditions are required for passive representation
to lead to active representation, including things like discretion­
ary latitude, identity-relevant policy areas, or limited organiza­
tional socialization (Bishu and Kennedy 2020; Jung and
Ronquillo 2021; Keiser et al. 2002).
Recent studies of representative bureaucracy have begun
focusing more on the symbolic effect of passive representation
(Park 2022; Riccucci, Van Ryzin, and Jackson 2018; Van
Ryzin, Riccucci, and Li 2017). Symbolic representation focuses
on how the public perceives government officials when they
share similar demographics, irrespective of any differences in
bureaucrat behavior or public service outcomes (Headley,
Wright, and Meier 2021; Lee and Nicholson-Crotty 2022). It
is symbolic because perceptual changes may not always require
bureaucrats to actively represent groups sharing similar back­
grounds. Instead, symbolic representation suggests that the
mere presence of a bureaucracy that represents specific groups
can influence the attitudes and behaviors of the public, regard­
less of individual bureaucrat actions or organizational out­
comes (Riccucci and Van Ryzin 2017; Riccucci, Van Ryzin, and
Li 2016; Xu and Meier 2022). Thus, the notion of symbolic
representation was conceptualized innately to relate to the
organization’s perceived similarity and associated legitimacy.
However, Headley, Wright, and Meier (2021) demonstrate that
the symbolic effects of representation are undermined without
some level of tangible active benefits, namely in the form of
fair or equitable treatment of the public.
To date, most of the research in representative bureaucracy
has focused on the impact of passive representation (i.e., the
demographic makeup) on public outcomes, whether it is pub­
lic perceptions (e.g., symbolic representation) or the provision
of public services (e.g., active representation). Whether
through active or symbolic representation, enhanced passive
representation can facilitate a platform for minority groups’
voices and provide opportunities to recognize and redress their
distinct needs and concerns, heightening a sense of belonging
and legitimacy among the represented group (Moggi et al.
2022; Riccucci, Van Ryzin, and Lavena 2014; Schuck, Baldo,
and Powell 2021). In fire services, empirical studies suggest
that passive representation is associated with enhanced public
service quality regarding fire service effectiveness and more
positive perceptions of improvements in organizational per­
formance (Andrews, Ashworth, and Meier 2014). However,
the effectiveness of representation hinges on what occurs
within the internal working environment, including policy
changes or contagion effects within the workplace (Meier and
Xu 2023).
For instance, Roch, Pitts, and Navarro (2010) argue that
sociodemographic representation can influence the selection of
policy tools used to respond to the represented population
within a given organization. If workforces with greater repre­
sentation are more likely to implement equitable policies, this
speaks to a change occurring within the agency’s internal

representation within the organization.
Internal representation
A large body of research on relational demography illustrates
the importance of intragroup diversity on workplace outcomes
(Kaur and Ren 2022; Tsui and O’Reilly 1989), focusing on
employees’ demographic similarity or dissimilarity to peers and
supervisors—in areas such as race and gender (Joshi, Liao, and
Roh 2011; King et al. 2017). The relational demography liter­
ature argues that when supervisors and subordinates share
similar demographic characteristics, employees will have more
positive benefits, including less conflict and more trust (Brower,
Schoorman, and Tan 2000; Foley et al. 2006; Joshi and Knight
2015). Drawing insights from this research, a small body of
representative bureaucracy literature has applied the theory of
representative bureaucracy within the bureaucracy to relation­
ships between supervisors of color and their employees of color.
Scholars argue that supervisors of color may have shared values
and attitudes with same-race employees, implement policies or
practices that benefit those employees, advocate on behalf of
those employees and gain cooperation or trust easier from their
same-race employees (Grissom and Keiser 2011; Grissom,
Nicholson-Crotty, and Keiser 2012; Lee 2022; Marvel 2015;
Rabovsky and Lee 2018). For example, representation in man­
agement may shape how employees are perceived and sup­
ported by both peers and superiors (Schwarz, Eva, and
Newman 2020). As such, recent studies identify how racial
representation at a leadership level is an important factor to
consider in internal organizational management (LeRoux and
Medina 2023), even when the effects of diverse leadership may
be negative.

Both the direct actions and indirect presence of leaders of
color may impact the perceptions and behaviors of White
employees and employees of color in different ways. For
instance, employees of color may perceive organizational
changes as removing prior perceived hurdles or barriers
within the workplace, be motivated or encouraged to perform
differently in the organization, feel more accepted or included
within the organization or from their peers, have more posi­
tive perceptions of the leader (e.g., trust) starting off even
without any organizational changes, and/or experience neg­
ative perceptions if their expectations of a leader of color are
not met. This was shown in the education setting where Gris­
som and Keiser (2011) found that Black teachers with Black
principals report greater levels of satisfaction, support,
encouragement, and recognition, are less likely to turnover,
and receive more supplemental compensation. Alternatively,
research in relational demography has shown that demo­
graphic similarity in hierarchical work relationships can also
lead to poor job satisfaction (see Randolph-Seng et al. 2016;
Turban and Jones 1988; Vecchio and Bullis 2001). Specifi­
cally, adverse outcomes for employees of color may result
when supervisors perceive employees from their same ingroup
as a threat (Duguid, Loyd, and Tolbert 2012), when employee
expectations of their ingroup supervisors are not met (Simons
et al. 2007), or when demographic similarity enhances rela­
tionship conflict and reduces trust (Miller, Richard, and
Ford 2019).
At the same time, enhanced representation at the leadership
level may lead White employees to exhibit more positive per­
ceptions toward issues of equity or their peers due to enhanced
cultural understanding, or negative perceptions of their leader
of color and peers due to perceived threat or unfair treatment.
In studying gender representation between principals and
teachers, Grissom, Nicholson-Crotty, and Keiser (2012) found
that male teachers (compared to their female peers in the same
schools) reported lower satisfaction and a greater likelihood
of turnover when working for female principals. This latter
finding displays a similar pattern to Lee’s (2022) research,
which shows that White federal employees exhibited more
negative attitudes and reported more discrimination when
working for supervisors of color. Similarly, the relational
demography literature also demonstrates that when supervisors
and subordinates have dissimilar demographic characteristics,
there is lower perceived career support for employees (Foley et
al. 2006; Jeanquart-Barone 1996), enhanced role ambiguity
and role conflict (Jeanquart-Barone 1996), heightened
perceptions of racial discrimination (Avery, McKay, and Wil­
son 2008; Schaffer and Riordan 2013), lower mentoring qual­
ity (Richard et al. 2019), and less satisfaction with supervisors
(Vecchio and Bullis 2001).
The ways in which employees of the same race or differing
race perceive and experience leaders of color, whether positively
or negatively, can also impact work outcomes for all employees
(e.g., employee commitment, overtime hours worked, the deliv­
ery of quality public services, etc.). For example, Marvel (2015)
found that female teachers working for female principals work
more overtime hours compared to when they work for male
principals and when male teachers work for female principals.
Similarly, Hong (2021) examined ethnic minority representa­
tion at different hierarchical levels in policing and found no
direct effect of leadership representation on police performance;
rather, leadership representation moderated the impact of
street-level representation on crime outcomes. Both findings
suggest that diverse leaders may impact actual public service
delivery by impacting street-level employees with shared demo­
graphic characteristics. However, the reverse can also be true,
in that when there are negative experiences or perceptions
under leaders of color, this may also lead to negative work
outcomes.
Racial socialization processes can shape people’s identity in
potentially positive and negative ways, affecting the values one
holds and subsequent behavioral choices derived from those
values. As such, in examining the behavioral foundation of
representative bureaucracy, Webeck and Lee (2022) point out
important questions regarding the importance of role identities,
such as a work identity or a managerial/leadership identity,
and how they interact with one’s personal identity (see also
Headley 2022a). Taken together, we maintain that it is vital to
understand how racial representation at the leadership level
impacts street-level bureaucrats’ perceptions of race and diver­
sity within the organization (irrespective of whether such per­
ceptions are a function of symbolic or active representation).
We focus specifically on the views of Black and White firefight­
ers, as they represent the largest racial diversity groups present
in the study. Further, scholars have argued for the importance
of not taking for granted the perceptions and experiences of
White bureaucrats since that reifies current racialized patterns
or structures as the norm (Feit 2024; Portillo, Humphrey, and
Bearfield 2022).
Current study: fire services
Like other emergency medical services, fire departments
respond to emergency calls (Henderson and Sowa 2019). In
2020, fire departments responded to approximately 36,416,000
calls for service (Fahy et al. 2022), representing a steady yearly
increase since 2010. These calls for service range from medical
aid, false alarms, mutual aid (coordination with other emer­
gency services), hazardous material spills, and natural disasters
(Clare et al. 2019). However, in 2020, the largest response from
fire departments was geared at providing medical aid or a res­
cue call, with fewer than 4% of all calls being fires (Fahy et al.
2022). Community outreach and education also make up a
significant part of the fire service job, especially when not
responding to emergency requests (Andrews, Ashworth, and
Meier 2014).
Fire departments are historically known to be places where
women and people of color have faced discrimination. Research

wise, Andrews, Ashworth, and Meier (2014) did not find
evidence to support a link between representation and fire
service performance, but did find that gender and racial repre­
sentations were both positively associated with organizational
effectiveness (as indicated by performance audits) as well as for
non-core discretionary tasks (e.g., providing guidance to com­
munity organizations). Further, fire departments serve a mix
of residents from diverse backgrounds (Henderson and Sowa
2019). Thus, racial mismatch between the composition of
departments and the makeup of communities served might
undermine organizational effectiveness. Exploring the inner
workings of departments, such as the presence of Black lead­
ership, and how this might shape outcomes within agencies,
especially for employees, is important. In public administration
writ large, less scholarly attention has been paid to fire services
and even less to how representation occurs within this public
organization among street-level workers and top-level leader­
ship. To address this gap, we analyzed 47 in-depth qualitative
interviews to understand the role of leadership representation
in employees’ perceptions of diversity and representation in a
fire department with a Black fire chief.
Methods
Conducting a qualitative study, we focused on a fire depart­
ment in a medium-sized city in the southeastern part of the
United States. This study was approved by the (Omitted) Uni­
versity Institutional Review Board. The first author conducted
47 in-depth interviews in person with firefighters from one
department between May and September 2021.
The first author recruited participants after consulting with
the Fire Chief and the Human Resource Management officer.
Following a sampling-for-range approach (detailed in the
next section), the researcher received a complete list of all
fire department personnel broken down by race, ethnicity,
gender, and rank on the job. After identifying the sample that
fit the criteria, the human resource manager scheduled inter­
views for participants three times a week. The interviewee
provided verbal and written consent as part of the interview
process. Each interview took place at individual fire stations
in rooms that provided seclusion and confidentiality to
increase transparency and elicit more open responses. The
first author conducted interviews while the firefighters were
on their shift, and if they were forced to leave during an
interview, the interviewee returned later in the day to com­
plete the interview.
The first author used a semistructured interview guide that
probed the role of mentorship in the fire department, collabo­
ration with citizens to provide services, mental health chal­
lenges on the job, and the role of diverse representation in the
department. Our analysis is motivated by four questions from
the interview guide: What does representation mean to you?
What does diversity within the department mean to you? How
often does race come up in your conversations with your col­
leagues? How often does race come up in conversations with
your supervisor? Interviews ranged from 26 min to 75 min. In
the findings section, a pseudonym was assigned to each respon­
dent to protect their identity.
Sampling strategy and characteristics
While most fire departments in the United States are primarily
White, this sample was driven by a sampling-for-range
approach emphasizing oversampling firefighters of color. Spe­
cifically, the first author identified Black as a subcategory that
needed to be oversampled (Small 2009; Weiss 1995) to under­
stand their unique experiences on the job. There is no consen­
sus on the specific number of participants needed in qualitative
studies that primarily use interview data; rather, interviewers
are encouraged to continue interviews until “saturation” is
reached (meaning that no new information is derived from
additional interviews, see Creswell and Poth 2016). Authors
such as Thomas and Pollio (2002) argue that there needs to be
6 to 12 participants if redundancy appears after 6. Alterna­
tively, Creswell and Poth (2016) assert that there could be
between 5 and 25 participants. Following the logic of both
scholars, this study completed interviews once theoretical sat­
uration was achieved, with a sample of at least 15% of the

Analysis
Using NVIVO (14) software, researchers analyzed transcripts
from each interview and conducted a content analysis through
a deductive process (Yin 2015). This research was informed
by the representative bureaucracy literature (Kennedy 2014),
drawing insights from the relational demography literature
(Joshi, Liao, and Roh 2011) where relevant, to build the inter­
nal representation conceptual framework. Deductively, we
started the analysis by doing a line-by-line reading of the inter­
views and organizing the data by the prior codes we identified
from the study objectives. Those study objectives sought to
understand how firefighters view race, representation and
diversity within the fire department. Once each response was
identified, we coded these responses as positive or negative
valence. For example, associations related to the positive
valence included firefighters mentioning the ability to see
themselves in the departments for a long time, once they met
the chief or supervisor (who was a person of color) and want­
ing to move into leadership roles because of that supervisor
and seeing the department as a space that cares about racial
diversity. Associations related to negative valence included
firefighters highlighting preferential treatment over merit and
not seeing race because of the familial ties in the department.
After organizing the themes into these positive and negative
categories, we then allowed descriptive categories to emerge
from the interviews and refined them through an iterative
comparison of Black and White firefighters in the department
(Glaser and Strauss 1998; Miles and Huberman 1994; Ryan
and Bernard 2000). For instance, we coded (a) instances in
which Black firefighters reported having a supervisor who
looked like them and provided them encouragement toward
advancement, as well as (b) White firefighters stated prefer­
ences for work ethic being most important for advancement
both as related to personnel processes. By doing this, we were
able to construct broader theme matrices across participants
that we used in analyzing the interviews (Ryan and Ber­
nard 2000).
As a check on the coding process, two additional graduate
students read every fourth interview and achieved approxi­
mately 95% agreement on their coding. In the findings below,
we expound on the major themes and provide specific quotes
that illustrate the themes that were most consistent in the inter­
views. Quotes have been edited for clarity and conciseness with
words in parentheses indicating inserts or removals.
Findings
In the mid-2010s, the current chief made a big push to increase
diversity within the fire services, both in terms of ethnicity and
gender representation. While the push for more representation
for women within the fire services hadn’t yielded more tangible
results, there were big increases in terms of racial diversity.
Between the years 2010 and 2020, the new firefighters who
entered the department were overwhelmingly from underrep­
resented backgrounds. Prior to 2010, only 2% of the fire
department identified as non-White. By the time of the inter­
views, over 25% of the total fire service was non-White.
In terms of recruitment, the chief noted that he utilized both
the nearby college campus, affinity groups and public adver­
tising campaigns to recruit more diverse recruits. First, the chief
noted that they set up booths at the local university so they
could attend job fairs and table on  campus. Second, the chief
reached out to sororities on campus, the Women’s Club of (city
name omitted), the local chapter of both The National Orga­
nization for Women and The Oasis Center for Women and
Girls. While the chief was not able to change promotion poli­
cies in the department, he did alter the hiring policy by offering
both a financial and educational bonus that was the first of its
kind within the city.
These findings from this study reveal how racial diversity in
leadership impacts employees’ perceptions of racial diversity
and representation in a fire department with a Black fire chief.
Across Black and White firefighters, interviews suggest that
respondents held diverging views about diversity and represen­
tation concerning hiring and promotion. Specifically, Black
firefighters perceived greater opportunities for professional
advancement and hiring that mirrors the community, whereas
White firefighters perceived an emphasis on race as an undercut
to meritocratic processes. Second, when it came to having dis­
cussions about race and diversity in the workforce, White fire­
fighters avoided discussions of race in the organization and
prioritized the importance of familial ties to the organization
over differences in racial identity. Black firefighters discussed
either selectively choosing when (e.g., diversity training) and
with whom (e.g., their Black peers) to discuss issues of race
within the department or avoiding discussions of race alto­
gether. They often noted that discussions did not translate
department-wide due to a lack of widespread appetite.

You have some people feel that you only got the job because

Conversations about race
While both White and Black firefighters mentioned the role of
race specifically as it related to personnel practices, many
avoided discussions of race with each other in the department
altogether. White firefighters viewed their strong ties and
family-like bonds with each other as superseding racial differ­
ences. This collective identity of being a “firefighter” allowed
them to embrace similarities and minimize differences; thus,
there was no need to engage in discussions of race. As such,
nearly all (90 percent) of the White firefighters interviewed
expressed either a preference not to discuss issues of race in the
department or did not see a need to do so. On the other hand,
Black firefighters were often acutely aware of their individual
racial identity, but discussions of race frequently depended on
the organizational subculture (perceived as one where caution
was warranted versus an environment of openness) or the spe­
cific situational context (if there were cultural opportunities
present, e.g., diversity training or peers of color). While a
majority of Black firefighters shared that they selectively dis­
cussed racial issues at work, a third of the Black firefighters
also preferred to avoid discussions of race and diversity, often
due to exercising caution or feeling uncomfortable.

Discussion
Applying the theory of representative bureaucracy to under­
stand internal representation within the department, that is,
leveraging a public agency with a Black leader, we explored the
impact of leadership representation on street-level bureaucrats’
perceptions of representation and diversity. While traditional
representative bureaucracy literature focuses on the impact of
representation primarily from bureaucrat to public, we built
on a smaller body of research that examined how leadership
representation impacts street-level bureaucrats within those
organizations (e.g., Grissom and Keiser 2011). By focusing
inward, within the organization, from bureaucrat to bureaucrat
(leadership to frontline employee), this qualitative inquiry of
firefighters and their interactions in the department illustrates
the benefits and limits of leadership representation from the
perspective of employees.
Several themes emerged across Black and White firefighters’
interviews regarding racial diversity and representation. First,
both groups of firefighters arrived at different conclusions
about how race mattered in hiring and promotion. Black fire­
fighters felt empowered working under a Black fire chief
because they perceived it led to a more diverse organization
and more opportunities for professional advancement. Further,
Black leadership demonstrated a commitment to creating a
department that was representative of the community they
serve. Thus, our finding is in line with prior research that found
Black employees are often more likely to be satisfied with their
organization’s attempts at creating a diverse and inclusive envi­
ronment (Trochmann, Stewart, and Ragusa 2023). In contrast,
White firefighters perceived an overemphasis on racial diversity
and representation as undermining meritocracy. White fire­
fighters perceived their department as being focused on “racial
quotas” and expressed the importance of individual work ethic
and ability as paramount in the hiring and promotion process,
often implying that diversity replaced merit.
Our findings echo earlier research that argues that diversity
initiatives can also trigger adverse spillover effects related to
majority groups’ perceptions of preferential treatment and
unfairness in a process that is supposed to be merit-based (Leslie
2019). Portillo, Humphrey, and Bearfield (2022) note that racial
differences exist in who is perceived as having managerial quality
or belonging in certain positions. They further argue that dis­
cussions of racial representation in the workforce are often met
with concerns that “benefits might be achieved at the expense
of White(s)” (p. 596). This interpretation helps shed light on
our results regarding White firefighters questioning the merit
process and equating efforts to enhance diversity with perceived
quotas, though further empirical investigation is needed.
Second, both groups of firefighters discussed how they
avoided discussing issues of race and diversity within the
organization, but the degree to which this happened differed
by race. White firefighters especially minimized racial differ­
ences amongst their colleagues, choosing instead a colorblind
approach (Leslie et al. 2020) that prioritized kin-like bonds.
For White firefighters, their Black counterparts were like
“brothers and sisters.” Thus, White interviewees appealed to
the collective identity of “firefighter” by minimizing racial
differences. While identities are malleable depending on the
context one is embedded in Webeck and Lee (2022), for White
firefighters, the salience of the professional identity super­
seded one’s personal identity, whereas Black firefighters often
discussed their personal racial identity. On the contrary, many
Black firefighters discussed selectively discussing issues of race
and diversity depending on how they perceived the subculture
or context they were embedded in. Black firefighters felt most
comfortable discussing it when there were clear opportunities,
such as during diversity trainings, designated meetings
devoted to unpacking racial tensions in the world, or around
leaders and peers who shared their race. Unlike the majority
of White firefighters, only a few Black respondents intention­
ally avoided conversations about race altogether. While these
Black interviewees acknowledged that questions and topics
surrounding race might be on everyone’s mind, it was best if
they were left unspoken due to a lack of comfort or fearing
repercussions.
Prior literature has distinguished between identity-blind and
identity-conscious ideologies.
The former deemphasizes demographic differences, whereas
the latter centers and invites individuals’ unique identities to
the table (Leslie et al. 2020). Both colorblindness and meritoc­
racy are identity-blind ideologies that minimize differences, but
colorblindness ignores differences and prioritizes a common
identity, whereas meritocracy focuses on equitable treatment

across different groups. Leslie et al. (2020) found that color­
blind ideology was negatively related to support for diversity
policies. This can help explain why we found that White fire­
fighters chose not to “see” race when it came to having discus­
sions internally, while simultaneously expressing pushback
against perceived racial quotas in hiring and promotion. Thus,
race was most salient for White firefighters when it posed a
material threat to them (e.g., regarding personnel processes),
but not as relevant when discussing the culture of the depart­
ment (e.g., having discussions about racial issues).
Methodologically, this is one of the few papers that uses
qualitative methods (in-depth semistructured interviews) to
understand internal representation between leaders and front­
line employees within an organization. Our results provide
theoretical implications for representative bureaucracy. While
this theory has seen substantial growth, the breadth of the work
has examined the relationship between the bureaucrat and the
citizen (An, Song, and Meier 2022; Bishu and Kennedy 2020;
Headley, Wright, and Meier 2021; Wright et al. 2023). These
studies provide insight into why and how representation occurs
in public interactions; however, we need to learn more about
how representation happens within organizations and between
bureaucrats (and whether it is active or symbolic representation
occurring). Our findings indicate that representation has dif­
fering effects within the organization between leaders and
frontline workers, depending on the race of the frontline
employees and how they perceive the acts of representation.
Further, we believe that these results indicate that future
representative
bureaucracy
models
that
explore
bureaucrat–citizen
interactions
should
account
for
bureaucrat-bureaucrat contextual variables going forward as
a potential mitigator or multiplier effect. While we could not
directly explore this next step of how it impacts the frontline
service delivery, representative bureaucracy theory often argues
that contingency factors within the organization shape the
effects of representation (Meier 2019). For instance, having
more Black colleagues can impact White employees through
contagion effects; however, if White employees perceive threats
to meritocracy due to internal representation, this hinders
potential contagion effects.
Conclusion
Developing a conceptual framework for internal representa­
tion, while also pulling on insights from relational demogra­
phy, we demonstrate how the leadership representation within
an organization can shape employees’ perceptions of the role
of representation and diversity. Furthermore, fire services pro­
vide a particularly compelling context for examining represen­
tative bureaucracy due to the critical nature of emergency
response and the direct, life-saving interactions that occur
between bureaucrats and the public. In this context, core
bureaucratic decisions are often mediated through administra­
tive processes (e.g., rules and regulations governing emergency
response), but other fire service provisions entail more discre­
tion, and the internal environment for firefighters can still
impact one’s overall work experience. By situating this study
within this context, we can isolate how dynamics of internal
representative bureaucracy may still be at play that mitigate or
exacerbate employee perceptions of the utility of representation
and diversity. We demonstrate that these perceptions are
important to understand, even when there may not be inequi­
table service delivery effects in urgent or life-threatening
bureaucratic interactions.
Practically speaking, our findings show clear distinctions in
views on the personnel process and discussions about race and
diversity. Further, we posit that perceptions of meritocracy can
hinder the positive impacts of representation in the organiza­
tional culture, warranting further study. Thus, future research
should investigate what role leaders should play in carefully
managing tensions within the workplace. Moreover, our frame­
work development allows future research to examine whether
these results may be similar to other contexts or policy areas
that are characterized by similar features, such as street-level
bureaucracies with strong organizational cultures, whether it
be policing or public schools.
This research is not without limitations. Future studies in
representative bureaucracy should continue to explore how all
forms of representation (passive, active, and symbolic) occur
within the bureaucracy for employees at different levels of an
organization. The focus on a single fire department allowed us
to examine perceptions within the same leader and organiza­
tional features and the qualitative nature of the study enabled
us to examine a novel case that demonstrates new conceptual
considerations.
This study demonstrates the importance of examining
intraorganizational dynamics regarding racial diversity and
representation and interrogating what it means for organiza­
tional change in an increasingly more diverse society. When
shared group identities are predominant and diverse group
identities are minimized, it may be more challenging to promote
organizational transformation due to the high-bond culture in
the workplace. At the same time, it is also important to acknowl­
edge that unintended consequences should be expected and are
inevitable features of organizational change efforts (Leslie
2019), but that does not mean they can’t be managed. Finally,
this study demonstrates that tensions will continue to arise
among majority and minority groups within organizations
when issues of diversity and representation are at play; thus,
effectively proactively anticipating and managing issues within
an organization is imperative (Houston, Kraimer, and Schil­
pzand 2023).

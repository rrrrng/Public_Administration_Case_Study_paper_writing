ABSTRACT
Kazakhstan is a post-­Soviet upper middle-­income country which claims to be a leader in regional governance in Central Asia.
A key component of its development strategy since independence in 1991 has been public administration reform. This paper
examines the post-­Soviet public administration legacy, its features, and how Kazakhstan has attempted to modernize. The coun-
try study considers the transition process from the Soviet period, factors which shaped the change, and the extent to which
Kazakhstan is now a “democratic unitary state.” Kazakhstan has made progress beyond a post-­Soviet model of public adminis-
tration. However, it cannot be described as a “democratic unitary state” but rather practices hybrid governance reflecting the de-
mise of the authoritarian bargain. The Kazakhstan country study illustrates how public administration reforms can gain traction
in non-­democratic settings and challenges the liberal democratic bias in much public administration theory.
1   |   Introduction
Kazakhstan is a Central Asian country which gained indepen-
dence in 1991 after the collapse of the Soviet Union. Since then,
it has been led by President Nursultan Nazarbayev who ruled
until March 2019 when his chosen successor Kassym-­Jomart
Tokayev assumed office. Kazakhstan is geopolitically signifi-
cant given its boundaries with Russia and China and is rich in
natural and mineral resources (oil, natural gas, uranium tita-
nium, zinc, and lead). It is a landlocked country with a landmass
of 2.7 m km2 and a population of just over 20 m people, making it
the ninth largest country in the world. Kazakhstan is an upper
middle-­income country with a GDP per capita of 12,918$ (cur-
rent US$ in 2023) and a human capital index of 0.6 (scale 0–1) in
2020 (World Bank country data, 2025). Kazakhstan's significant
economic challenge is its reliance on natural resources and vul-
nerability to oil price fluctuations which disrupt stable growth.
Despite economic progress, inequality remains a problem, spe-
cifically income inequality as well as disparities between and
within its regions (World Bank  2024). Corruption and gover-
nance challenges are prominent, erode public trust, and im-
pair the effectiveness of government operations. Historical and
political contexts play a significant role in shaping the state's
challenges today. Despite efforts towards democratization, the
Soviet-­style centralized governance mode left a lasting imprint
on the administrative systems where public administration
often continues to operate with a top-­down management style.
We return to this post-­Soviet legacy later.
Kazakhstan adopted a constitution in 1995 (modeled on the
French Constitution and since amended several times) and
committed to establishing itself as a democratic secular state
with the ideals of freedom and equality. Some 34 years beyond
independence, Kazakhstan is classified by Freedom House as a

The key research questions that we pose are:
i.	 To what extent has Kazakhstan transitioned from a Soviet
public administration model to one consistent with a
“democratic unitary state” as defined by its constitution?1
ii.	 What factors have shaped this transition?
To answer these questions, the paper is structured in four sec-
tions. First, we consider the detail of the post-­Soviet legacy in
general terms and the key features of the Soviet public adminis-
tration model. Second, we examine the transition process both
at a conceptual level and the factors shaping the changes. Third,
we provide examples which illustrate the key reforms associated
with the shift from the post-­Soviet public administration model,
a process of modernization. Finally, we reflect on the extent to
which the reforms constitute a public administration system
consistent with a “democratic unitary state.”
2   |   The Post-­Soviet Legacy
The post-­Soviet legacy is examined at two levels, the macro
or country level and the specific public administration model
which Kazakhstan inherited. At the macro-­level, the post-­
Soviet legacy is multi-­faceted in content, best characterized
as continuities with the past but also significant transforma-
tion since independence. Following independence, the coun-
try faced multiple social, political, and economic challenges.
Its social circumstances included a hugely diverse population
of ethnic Russians, Kazakhs, and other minority groups such
as Ukrainians, Uzbeks, and Germans. Stalin's deportations
and Soviet industrial ventures witnessed many non-­Kazakh
immigrants in Kazakhstan. As a result, the newly indepen-
dent government of Kazakhstan, led by President Nursultan
Nazarbayev, had to manage ethnic tensions, since many
Kazakhs spoke Russian, against a tide of nationalism sup-
portive of reviving their native language, while maintaining
social stability. Since then, Kazakhstan has engaged in build-
ing a separate national identity through the promotion of the
Kazakh language, cultural traditions, and Islam, which were
suppressed during Soviet times. Kazakh became the official
state language, although Russian is still widely used. There
have been sporadic tensions, but the political leadership
prides itself on maintaining ethnic harmony in an ethnically
diverse country. There were also concerns about possible se-
cession in north Kazakhstan, populated with a large Russian
demographic. The shift from a centralized planned economy
to a market-­based economy resulted in a large rural-­to-­urban
migration, with people seeking better employment opportu-
nities and, in turn, putting pressure on housing and health
services infrastructure.
Politically, post-­independence, Kazakhstan still retained ves-
tiges of the structures of the Soviet Union led by the same erst-
while political elite. Nazarbayev, for example, had been the First
Secretary of the Communist Party of Kazakhstan when he be-
came the president of the newly independent Kazakhstan. One

weapons in the early 1990s and joined non-­proliferation trea-

Soviet nuclear tests were carried out, now stands as a symbol of
Kazakhstan's new trajectory to global disarmament.
Economically, post-­independence, Kazakhstan struggled with
the transition from a centrally planned to a market economy.
The collapse of the USSR led to a disruption in supply chain
networks and the loss of Soviet-­era subsidies and production.
Attempts to privatize state assets, establish a new currency, and
create a functioning economy resulted in soaring inflation, the
demise of many industries, and heavy job losses in the 1990s.
This, even though Kazakhstan had rich but largely under-­
developed natural resources which lacked infrastructure and
foreign investment. Over the years, Kazakhstan's dependence
on natural resources has deepened. Although the country has
attracted significant foreign investment by privatizing state
assets and establishing itself as a key player in the global en-
ergy market, the sector remains prone to corruption and rent-­
seeking. The country's political and economic structures have
contributed to unequal wealth distribution, creating a large gap
between urban elites and the rural population. These inequali-
ties have been the cause of social unrest, while attempts to diver-
sify the economy have met with limited success. Kazakhstan's
Gini index has increased from a low of 26.8 in 2015 to 29.2 in
2021, the latest data available.
In sum, the transition from the Soviet era has left a legacy in
Kazakhstan (and other Central Asia countries) represented by
both continuity and change. In terms of continuity, there is clear
evidence of authoritarian governance, economic dependency
on natural resources, and geopolitical pressures from Russia
and China. In terms of change, Kazakhstan has managed to
maintain relative political stability, albeit enforced with a heavy
police hand, while simultaneously exhibiting aspects of mod-
ernization and national sovereignty. Not only did the Soviet leg-
acy manifest itself at political, economic, and social levels, but it
had a profound presence in public administration systems and
processes to which we now turn.
The newly independent Kazakhstan inherited a Soviet pub-
lic administration model which was highly centralized,
hierarchical, and bureaucratic, where the state, represented
by a single party system (Communism), exercised ultimate
control over all aspects of government (Mussagulova 2021a).
This included the provision of a wide range of free public ser-
vices (education, social welfare, housing, employment secu-
rity) to a basic level for all citizens, such that the state played
a pervasive and controlling role in people's lives. State enter-
prises were ubiquitous with limited involvement of the private
sector. The highly centralized nature of the public adminis-
tration model ensured top-­down decision making and little
opportunity for public participation (O'Connor et  al.  2019).
This, in turn, resulted in a culture of passivity which had an
enduring influence on the citizens of the newly independent
Kazakhstan.
Ministries and government departments were strictly hierar-
chical, maintaining oversight of all aspects of governance by
directing and controlling regional and local authorities. Power
resided in the executive branch of government, and the judi-
ciary was subordinate to the ruling Party, which meant that
laws reinforced state ideology and were used to suppress op-
position. Loyalty to the Party was a sine qua non for officials'
career advancement since it controlled appointments to key
administrative and political positions. Kazakhstan inherited
the nomenklatura system, a list of key positions from which
the Communist Party made leadership appointments to main-
tain its grip on power. The Soviet system, therefore, enabled
and embedded patronage networks and political-­bureaucratic
elite, where personal connections played a key role in career
progression (Peters et al. 2023). Many officials from the Soviet
era continued to work in key posts in the newly independent
Kazakhstan and, in so doing, sustained pre-­existing adminis-
trative norms and practices, a system of bureaucratic inertia.
In short, Kazakhstan inherited a public administration model
with both distinctive national features alongside those from
the Soviet period (Mussagulova 2021b).
3   |   The Transition
At a conceptual level, Kazakhstan adopted a social contract
model following independence, an agreement between the
state and its citizens where the former guarantees social
order and economic development in exchange for concessions
by the latter on their political freedoms and human rights
(Skyrms  2014). The model is rooted in Hobbes' social con-
tract theory in which individuals agree to surrender certain
freedoms to a ruler who holds absolute power in exchange for
security. Without strong central authority, Hobbes argues (in
Leviathan, Hobbes 1651) humans would exist in a perpetual
state of war. The idea of a social contract between citizens and
government evolved through the work of Locke (Two Treatises
of Government, 1689) who advocated for limited government
with the consent of the governed, and Rousseau (1964) who
promoted the ideals of direct democracy. So, while Kazakhstan
did not adopt a classical social contract framework along the
lines of Hobbes, there is evidence of an implicit social contract
forged between the First President (Nursultan Nazarbayev)
and his citizens, which directly influenced the state approach
to governance and state-­society relations during and after the
transition.

The implicit social contract came in the form of what was
described as the “authoritarian bargain” or an implicit agree-
ment between Nazarbayev and the people of Kazakhstan in
which the state provided economic benefits, stability, and
improved public services in exchange for limited political
freedom and compliance with government directives (Desai
et  al.  2009; Ibadildin and Pisareva  2020; Vortherms  2023).
This “bargain” comprised state guarantees of economic sta-
bility, a social welfare system that guarantees free healthcare
as well as pensions and benefits for the unemployed, families
with multiple children, and other vulnerable categories. In
return, citizens would accept limited political freedoms and
rights of participation and strong executive presidential rule.
This approach, rooted in Hobbes social contract theory, legit-
imized the ruling elite in Kazakhstan and provided resilience
for the authoritarian regime.
The authoritarian bargain evolved in three broad phases.
Following independence in 1991, the first phase was an ex-
clusive focus on the economy (1991–2000). Kazakhstan faced
hyperinflation, a sharp decline in GDP, and the collapse in
demand from key industries with economic ties to the former
Soviet Union. It was immediately challenged to transition from
a centrally planned to a market-­based economy through privat-
ization, attract foreign direct investment, and grow the energy
sector. Under such conditions, it was not a difficult choice for
citizens to opt for economic stability and a rise in their living
standards in lieu of limited political pluralism.
The second phase (2001–2010) witnessed a period of signifi-
cant economic growth largely due to rising global oil prices
and a large influx of foreign investment and revenue. This led
to an increase in GDP, a reduction in poverty, and an improve-
ment in living standards amongst Kazakhstan's population.
This included better core public services in health, education,
and social housing. The global financial crisis (2007–2008)
caused a slowdown in economic growth but was mitigated
by financial reserves accumulated during the oil boom.
Kazakhstan's standing in the international community was
evolving through its multivector foreign policy, which main-
tained a balanced relationship with Russia, China, and the
United States. However, with growth, social disparities and
inequalities surfaced in the form of elite-­level corruption and
wealth concentrated in certain sectors and regions. By the end
of this phase, underlying tensions were emerging, particularly
in the poorer regions.
Towards the end of the third phase (2011–2020) the authori-
tarian bargain witnessed economic slowdown due to oil price
fluctuations and rising inflation. The state's ability to deliver
its end of the bargain weakened, and, with that, came grow-
ing frustrations over rising fuel prices, corruption, and lack of
political freedoms. Although social welfare programmes were
implemented during this period to tackle poverty, inequality,
and social issues such as domestic violence, the most signifi-
cant event was the resignation of President Nazarbayev in 2019
and the election of Kassym-­Jomart Tokayev (Groce 2023). Since
2020, the authoritarian bargain has faced growing challenges.
Despite efforts to diversify its economy, Kazakhstan still relies
heavily on oil revenues, making it vulnerable to market price
fluctuations. The strains in the economic bargain came to the
fore in January 2022 through protests sparked by fuel price rises
but also reflecting wider grievances. Tokayev responded with a
heavy security response and limited political reforms. The crisis
allowed Tokayev to marginalize Nazarbayev's elite by removing
key figures from government and disassembling their business
networks.
4   |   Beyond the Authoritarian Bargain
While the protests in January 2022 were an overt manifesta-
tion of the breakdown in the social contract, other factors were
at play. The authoritarian bargain had relied on economic sta-
bility, measured political reforms, and dominance by a group
of elite stakeholders close to the President. The Soviet legacy
of centralized governance had led to bureaucratic inertia and
resistance to change when it was evident that the economy re-
mained too dependent on natural resources and hence suscep-
tible to external shocks. Although Kazakhstan had adopted
market reforms, key industries were still controlled by state
elite. Attempts to juggle economic liberalization and at the
same time retain a tight political grip on the business sector
proved difficult.
Young people who had not witnessed the early years of in-
dependence were becoming increasingly dissatisfied with
the lack of job opportunities and overt evidence of income
inequality. At the same time, they experienced frustrations
and political impediments to raising their voices, still less
holding political protests where freedom of expression came
with restrictions. Social media became a platform to air griev-
ances, albeit with government interference. Civil society
as a sector that could mobilize opposition to the vestiges of
the authoritarian bargain is weak or has been co-­opted into
the machinery of government. More generally, there were
push–pull factors. Russia's influence (pull) on Kazakhstan
as a member of the Eurasian Economic Union, and Chinese
engagement through the Belt and Road Initiative prioritized
economic cooperation over democratic reforms. At the same
time, Kazakhstan's partnerships with international organiza-
tions such as the OSCE and World Trade Organization pro-
moted a modernization agenda (push) including democratic
reforms. In sum, Kazakhstan's transition from the authoritar-
ian bargain has therefore been shaped by post-­Soviet anach-
ronistic governance practices, a challenge to elite domination,
economic imperatives, external influences, and growing soci-
etal pressures. These paved the way for a series of reforms to
which we now turn.
5   |   The Modernisation Agenda

mise of the authoritarian bargain but rather was part of the
external influences described above, in particular the global
New Public Management (NPM) agenda which swept devel-
oping countries and was widely promoted by donor organi-
zations. However, it is reasonable to suggest that there was a
confluence of events, as the authoritarian bargain unraveled
and change became necessary, NPM offered Kazakhstan (and
other countries) a melange of reforms from which to tackle

adaptation of the global modernization agenda in Kazakhstan,
where Soviet-­era practices and ideologies, institutional struc-

choice of these areas for investigation from the wider NPM
agenda is based on primary data collected through a probabil-

participants ranked the five most important internal prob-

the top problem facing Kazakhstan. Low competence of civil

5.1   |   Tackling Corruption
A key priority for Kazakhstan was to address systemic cor-
ruption in the public sector. President Tokayev has adopted
a zero-­tolerance policy towards offenders. Kazakhstan's ap-
proach to tackling corruption has, in part, been informed by
examples from other countries such as Singapore and Hong
Kong (Quah 2016; Gong et al. 2015) although former President
Nazarbayev emphasized modernisation “the Kazakh way,”
a reference to the context and pace of development of
Kazakhstan. Tokayev has taken a tough stance on the prob-
lem, updating and implementation of anti-­corruption laws.
He offers strong support to the government's Anti-­Corruption
Agency whose job is to implement preventative measures, in-
vestigate cases of corruption, and prosecute offenders. High-­
profile offenders are “named and shamed” as a deterrent to
others. This is supplemented by public awareness campaigns
which highlight the negative impact of corruption and pro-
mote a culture of integrity and good ethical behavior. While
there are examples of the various punitive measures put in
place including criminal convictions for both petty and grand
corruption, we focus on three preventative measures which
are prominent in the government's efforts to tackle corrup-
tion: open government; ethical standards and digitalization.

Open Government: Increased transparency, dialogue, and en-
gagement have been championed through open government
initiatives as a way to address corruption and build trust in
government. The opaque nature of government, particularly
in awarding large public sector contracts, provided a haven
for corrupt activities and rent-­seeking on the part of officials
and the business elite (Khamitov et al. 2023). Initiatives have
included websites on open data where citizens can access pub-
licly available government information which can be used to
hold state bodies to account on socio-­economic issues and as
a source of evidence for researchers and journalists. There
are similar websites on open legislation and open budgets
where citizens can discuss and provide inputs to draft legis-
lation, draft standards of public services, public budgets, and
reports on their implementation. An open dialogue platform
has also been established as a mechanism to provide citizens
with the opportunity to interact with government. This al-
lows citizens to: send requests to heads of government agen-
cies, participate in discussions on the activities of agencies,
and take part in polls or surveys conducted by state bodies.
Despite these ­examples of transparency, dialogue, and en-
gagement, researchers have found that Kazakhstan has “half-­
open government,” a reference to the fact that bureaucrats felt
threatened by these measures and are unaccustomed to listen-
ing and being expected to respond to the views of the public
(O'Connor et al. 2019). In other words, the transition from pas-
sive citizenry under the Soviet regime to a more active public
has caused officials to adopt ex-­ante behavior and engage in
token openness.
An additional policy instrument in Kazakhstan's open gov-
ernment initiative was the creation of forums to increase cit-
izen participation in, and engagement with, the public policy
decision-­making process. Public councils, first introduced in
2016, aimed at strengthening democracy through citizen par-
ticipation in the decision-­making process proved little more
than window-­dressing as akims (mayors) populated the fora
with former public officials to control the agenda and activi-
ties (Knox and Janenova 2018). There followed the creation of
the National Council for Public Trust (2019) whose role was
to develop proposals and recommendations on public policy
issues with representatives of the public, political parties,
the business sector and civil society. Researchers who ex-
amined its work concluded: “overall, the evidence points to
the National Council being used as a state legitimisation tool
through government-­controlled citizen participation which of-
fers a veneer of respectability to external investors and foreign
observers” (Kurmanov and Knox 2022, 161). The Council was
integral to President Tokayev's mantra for Kazakhstan to be a
“listening state” but failed to achieve real impacts and was re-
formed into the Kurultai National Assembly in 2022 to bolster
public participation in governance and the decision-­making
process. The Kurultai comprises some 107 members and is a
formal gathering of selected citizens largely aimed at strength-
ening national identity through tasks such as improving state
symbols, strengthening the historical self-­consciousness of
the nation and promoting cultural heritage (Tokayev  2023).
This too has become little more than a highly controlled and
centralized gathering whose membership is self-­selected to af-
firm the decisions of the President.
5.2   |   Ethical Standards
The second preventative measure deployed by the govern-
ment to tackle corruption is the adoption of ethical standards
among officials. Kazakhstan introduced a new ethics code in
2015 replacing previous iterations which lacked legislative
weight. The code listed 14 general principles aimed at building
public trust in government organizations and the prevention
of unethical conduct and behavior among officials. The scope
of the code was wide-­ranging and covered the conduct and
behavior of officials at work and outside the workplace as well
as speeches to the public. Officials behaving ostentatiously
in public presented a poor public image and compounded the
perception of systemic corruption within the civil service. The
position of ethics commissioner was established in all central
and local government bodies. The commissioners were tasked
to report breaches of civil service, anti-­corruption and ethics
legislation to the (then) Agency for Civil Service Affairs and
Anti-­Corruption. Specifically, their role involved, inter alia,
“to analyse the causes of civil service corruption and breach of
the code of ethics and to make recommendations to the lead-
ership of the state body on their elimination” (translated from
Russian, The Kazakhstani Ethics Code, 2015). Commissioners
were deemed to be independent, but their powers of enforce-
ment were limited to notifying senior officials about breaches
of the code of conduct and making proposals to review the
position and duties of those involved.
Despite claims of serious intent, research found commission-
ers achieved limited success in the role for several reasons
(Janenova and Knox 2020). First, senior managers in the pub-
lic sector acted as gatekeepers in processing cases of unethical
or corrupt conduct of officials reported by the commissioners
and were reluctant to forward breaches onwards to the Agency
for Civil Service Affairs and Anti-­Corruption. Senior officials
viewed such cases as a reflection of poor stewardship in their
own organizations. Second, commissioners were placed in an
invidious position within departments and did not want to be
seen as “whistleblowers” against their colleagues. Third, over
time, they discovered that although their functions were exten-
sive, they had important responsibilities without power. This led
to inconsistences in the way in which they exercised their judg-
ment on what they deemed as breaches (Pelizzo and Knox 2023).
Finally, commissioners encountered problems in substantiating
breaches of the code, in particular the behavior of colleagues
outside the workplace. Researchers concluded that there is a
“lack of ownership and political commitment to the implemen-
tation of the ethics code” at both central and local government
levels (Janenova and Knox 2020, 193).
5.3   |   Digitalization
Kazakhstan has achieved significant success in digitalization.
It now ranks 24th (from 193 countries) in the 2024 United
Nations e-­government index, scoring 0.9009 (out of a possible
1) and ahead of countries such as France, China, and Canada.
Its journey to digitalization has been an example for other
post-­Soviet countries, beginning with the establishment of
one-­stop-­shops (known as one-­window centers) in 2005 as a


## Page 7

7
direct response to widespread corruption and a drive to im-
prove the quality of public services. The clear intention was to
make multiple public documents (passports, driving licenses,
land registration, marriage certificates, child registration, tax
services and others) available through accessible sites, thus
reducing the number of translations with corrupt officials
(Janenova and Kim 2016). This initiative quickly evolved into
the electronic provision of a wide range of services through
information portals and a dedicated e-­gov website. One recent
example of applying digital tools to combat corruption in the
judicial system (a sector renowned for bribery) is the use of
digital analytics, which uses artificial intelligence to reduce
the time for routine processing of large volumes of evidential
materials and make courts decisions more transparent. All of
this would suggest modernisation for the benefit of the citi-
zens of Kazakhstan.
However, digitalization has also been used for malevolent pur-
poses, to surveil, repress, and manipulate its citizens (Polyakova
and Meserole 2019). During the protests in January 2022, the
Kazakhstan government implemented a nationwide internet
shutdown. Typically, during election periods, internet blackouts
occur to prevent the coordination of protests, a practice that
runs in parallel with government monitoring of social media
platforms for dissenting voices, which results in targeting and
harassment. Kazakhstan has also implemented “Smart City”
projects that use technologies such as facial recognition, osten-
sibly to enhance public safety but are deployed to track polit-
ical activists and protestors. Kazakhstan has also introduced
laws on inciting online social discord and spreading extremist
content to stifle free speech, resulting in vague charges of pro-
moting hatred. In short, digital tools are being used to control
and suppress dissent, referred to as “digital authoritarianism”
(Kurmanov and Knox 2024).
So how has Kazakhstan performed in terms of tackling corrup-
tion? We draw data from the worldwide governance indicators
where “control of corruption captures perceptions of the extent
to which public power is exercised for private gain, including
both petty and grand forms of corruption, as well as ‘capture’
of the state by elites and private interests” (Kaufmann and
Kraay 2023). Scores range from −2.5 to +2.5 with higher scores
corresponding to better control of corruption (see Figure  2).
Relative to other Central Asian countries, Kazakhstan performs
best and over a 10-­year period Kazakhstan has improved from
−0.92 (in 2012) to −0.27 (in 2023). Clearly, there has been some
improvement over time, albeit that there is still a high level of
corruption.
5.4   |   Civil Service Reform
Immediately following independence little attention was paid
to reforming the public sector given the focus on tackling
the economic recession and maintaining political stability.
It wasn't until 1995 that a Presidential decree established the
legal and institutional framework for a modern civil service.
This decree appeared to have little impact and researchers de-
clared that public administration remained essentially Soviet
yet its importance to the wider reforms' agenda was seen at
critical (Emrich-­Bakenova 2009). Nazarbayev launched a de-
velopment strategy (Kazakhstan 2030) in 1997 which included
among its seven priorities building a professional civil service.
The document emphasized principles of professionalism, mer-
itocracy, efficiency, transparency and accountability to the
public. The strategy also promoted the use of modern infor-
mation technology to eliminate bureaucracy in government
bodies. The focus on public administration reforms contin-
ued with a new strategy launched in 2012 (Kazakhstan 2050)
within which creating “a professional state apparatus” was
singled out (Nazarbayev 2012, 25).
A new law (On Civil Service, 2015) marked the transition from
a mixed “position-­career” to a “career” model in civil service,
emphasizing merit-­based recruitment and transparent selec-
tion procedures with the involvement of observers and experts
to prevent “command moves” and enhance ethics within the
civil service. Additionally, the reforms created a structured ap-
proach to career progression and specialization within the civil
service, using Administrative Corps A and Executive Corps B
categorization to facilitate advancement. The Agency for Civil
Service Affairs was established (2019) to oversee and imple-
ment a unified personnel policy, ensuring coherence and uni-
formity in civil service management. Reforms progressed to

5.5   |   Representation
Kazakhstan formally declared adherence to the principles of
meritocracy in hiring and promoting public servants (Janenova
and Knox  2019). The new Concept Development: Public
Administration: 2024–2029 declared the principles of openness
in the civil service and stipulated future possibilities for indi-
viduals from the private sector to join the service. The Concept
also highlighted the need to simplify entry requirements for job
applicants to the civil service with the aim of improving access
to public sector jobs.
In the past, tribal associations were found to be important in
securing appointments and promotions (Schatz 2004) and neg-
atively affected the process of selection of candidates enter-
ing the civil service and subsequent promotion opportunities
(O'Connor et al. 2021). Furthermore, there is an underrepre-
sentation of some population categories in public administra-
tion. For example, gender representation is one such issue.
Although 55% of civil servants in Kazakhstan are females,
only 39% of managerial positions are held by women in the
public sector.2 Women are not proportionally represented
at higher ranks of government bodies or in the Parliament
(Kuzhabekova et al. 2018). Nevertheless, gender equality is-
sues have risen to the top of the agenda more recently. Some
current examples of positive changes include the introduction
of gender quotas for political parties in the Parliament, the
adoption of the law on domestic violence, and criminalization
of domestic violence.
5.6   |   Rewards and Benefits
Kazakhstan's civil servants receive monetary and non-­monetary
incentives to perform their jobs. The monetary benefits include
holiday pay and bonuses that range from 30% of total salary
in the regions to 57% in the central government (Ibrayeva and
Nezhina 2017). In terms of non-­monetary benefits, civil servants
are provided with training and skills development opportuni-
ties and performance-­based promotions. The recent Concept
Development: Public Administration: 2024–2029 introduced
horizontal promotion procedures, whereby civil servants can
be promoted to a higher category based on merit even when re-
maining in the same position through qualification for the next
level/category.
Low wages as compared to the private sector remain to be one
of the key factors negatively affecting talent recruitment and
the quality of human capital in the civil service (O'Connor
et al. 2021). According to a report by the Astana Civil Service
Hub (2023), Kazakhstan's public sector payroll fund is the
lowest as a proportion of GDP (3.52) among all Central Asian
countries, despite having the highest rate of public sector
employment in the region. During the same year, Russia's
public sector payroll fund stood at 9.62% and its public sec-
tor employment was 44.9% compared to Kazakhstan's 37.2%.
This difference in the public sector payroll fund is notable be-
cause both countries have similar GDP per capita in the re-
gion. The problem of low wages is recognized at the official
level as well—President Tokayev stressed in February 2024
that in order to increase the attractiveness of public service, it
is necessary to establish wages that are competitive with the
private sector.3 While the rewards and benefits system is far
from perfect and could be a significant demotivating factor for
civil service aspirants, lack of autonomy was also found to be
one of the constraining factors for the performance of the civil
service in the country (Mussagulova et al. 2023).
5.7   |   Politico-­Administrative Relations
The Civil Service Law distinguishes between political and
administrative employees. When transferred from one de-
partment to another, political civil servants were accustomed
to moving their teams with them (O'Connor et al. 2021). The
amendments in the Civil Service Law of 2005 were intended
to curtail the negative effects of such shuffling (Ibrayeva and
Nezhina 2017). Furthermore, there was an attempt to improve
the quality of human capital in the civil service by introduc-
ing Corps A and Corps B categories of civil servants. Broader
implications of this reform on the civil service remained lim-
ited as loyalty was found to supersede performance (O'Connor
et al. 2021, 3).
There were attempts to emulate the Singaporean approach in
building the civil service, for example, by integrating elements
of strategic human resource management. In 1993, Kazakhstan
established the Bolashak International Scholarship designed to
send talented young people to study abroad at top universities
worldwide. Amendments to the regulatory acts in 2009 granted
Bolashak alumni priority enrollment to the civil service by al-
lowing them to enter the service as cadre reserve without prior
working experience.4 Such changes delivered some results as
more political appointees at ministerial and other high levels
were educated abroad. In 2018, the scholarship administra-
tor reported that 57.7% of graduates who held the Bolashak
scholarship work in the public sector (including state-­owned
companies).5
When President Tokayev came to power in 2019, he initiated
the creation of the new Presidential Youth Cadre reserve pool
for high-­level public management positions.6 While the entry
process to this Cadre reserve pool is highly competitive, its
members are appointed to political and high-­level managerial
positions. The initiative has proved to be a successful mecha-
nism to introduce a new generation of public managers to the
public administration sector.
Research on this ongoing civil service reform agenda has
offered ambivalent results. Despite some governance im-
provements and increased efficiency in public service deliv-
ery, institutional reforms have not achieved their intended
impact due to bureaucratic inertia, insidious resistance from
the “old guard” within the civil service, and a lack of effective


## Page 9

9
mechanisms for public participation and feedback (Knox
and Janenova  2018). Even though reforms created more ac-
countable governance structures, it was deemed essential to
continue addressing other areas of critical importance, specif-
ically enhancing the rule of law and ensuring political plural-
ism (Burkhanov et al. 2021). Observers on progress made since
independence noted a paradox: the successful implementation
of many public administration reforms yet ongoing problems
in overall efficiency and transparency and limited impact on
the level of corruption (Baimenov et al. 2022).
Is the government of Kazakhstan more effective as a result of
civil service reforms and capacity building described above?
Government effectiveness “captures perceptions of the quality
of public services and the degree of its independence from politi-
cal pressures, the quality of policy formulation and implementa-
tion and the credibility of the government's commitment to such
policies” (Kaufmann and Kraay 2023). Here again, Kazakhstan
is a regional leader in terms of effectiveness, see Figure 3. Over
a 10-­year period, it has improved its score from −0.53 (in 2012)
to 0.15 (in 2023).
6   |   Discussion
Have the plethora of reforms aimed at tackling corruption and
modernizing the civil service transitioned Kazakhstan from
a Soviet public administration model to one consistent with
“democratic unitary state”? While the reforms agenda (tough
anti-­corruption laws, open government, enforcement of ethical
standards among officials, digitalization of public services and
the reform of the civil service) has good intent and can be linked
to a weakening or gradual breakdown of the authoritarian bar-
gain, it has not fulfilled its full potential. A public administra-
tion model consistent with a democratic state should balance the
needs of effective governance with the principles of democracy.
This demands a number of prerequisites.
Officials must be accountable to elected representatives and
operate in accordance with the rule of law. This should include
establishing a robust framework for the protection of human
rights and civil liberties. International organizations express
their concerns over the imprisonment of political activists, the
violation of freedom of speech and LGBT+ rights. The public
administration system should be open and transparent in terms
of policies, procedures, and public spending. Citizens should
have the right to access information about government activi-
ties and participate in decision-­making processes. Given the
history of passivity among the citizens of Kazakhstan, there is
a greater need for a vibrant and independent civil society when,
in fact, many NGOs are captured by the state. Officials should
be responsive to the needs of the public and feedback on pub-
lic services. Decentralization, empowering local government
(maslikhats), would help address this issue, but centripetal ten-
dencies in Kazakhstan are strong. The political and business
elite do not want to cede power to lower tiers of governance.
Officials should adhere to ethical standards and avoid conflicts
of interest, acting impartially with integrity and fairness. While
upholding these democratic values, the public administration
model should strive for efficiency and effectiveness in delivering
public services.
Kazakhstan's post-­Soviet transition does not meet these exacting
standards to be described as a unitary democratic state. Reform
proposals outlined in the strategy documents Kazakhstan 2050
and Concept Development: Public Administration 2024–2029
include the language of reforms that would make a radical shift
from post-­Soviet public administration. The latter, for example,
emphasizes service orientation and client focus; professional-
ization of the civil service; transparency and meritocracy; and
continuous learning and accountability, all of which align with
principles of democratic governance. However, there are signifi-
cant challenges in the form of entrenched bureaucratic practices,
resistance to change for those with vested interests in the status
quo, and a lack of political commitment to implementation.
There are a number of wider lessons from the Kazakhstan case
study. First, the use of social contracts as a way to transition
to democratic governance has key limitations. Scholars have
looked at the use of social contracts in other contexts (South
Sudan, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Yemen, and South Africa) and
concluded that “when elite politics are inclusive, but society is
excluded, crises will generate mass social mobilization against
those in power” (Zahar and McCandless 2020, 124). One of the
explanatory factors cited for renewed protests in 2019 in the
Arab world (Algeria, Sudan, Lebanon, and Iraq) was that the

social contract, which had previously provided the basis of au-
thoritarian stability, came to a breaking point (Csicsmann and
Rozsa 2022). Research on Tunisia's political transition process
showed how the social contract unraveled because “the ex-
pected and hoped-­for inclusive socio-­economic outcomes did
not sufficiently or expediently follow” (McCandless 2025, 1). In
the case of Belarus (during the period 1994–2006), Lukashenko
made use of a social contract in a perverse way to sustain author-
itarianism by offering socioeconomic rewards to his supporters
and imposing sanctions on dissenters (Trantidis 2021, 130). The
Kazakhstan case provides additional evidence that a social con-
tract could not be sustained because citizens witnessed the con-
tinuance of elite dominance and significant social and economic
inequalities.
The second observation from this case study is on the transition
process from authoritarianism to Kazakhstan's self-­declared po-
sition as a “democratic unitary state.” In fact, we demonstrate
that Kazakhstan has a façade of democracy in which it can point
to elections, a constitution and legal framework for governing
but the reality is elections are manipulated, opposition is sup-
pressed, and there is a lack of independent press (Merritt 2004).
Even the conditions for Dahl's polyarchy model, political contes-
tation and inclusive participation, a form of governance which
does not constitute “true” democracy, are not met in Kazakhstan
(Dahl 2008). In other words, the requirements for a successful
transition to democracy do not exist. Huntington's seminal work
(Huntington 1991) on why democratic transitions fail is prescient
here. He argues that history, economics, leadership and exter-
nal factors contribute to the success or failure of democracy. For
Kazakhstan, the most salient factor was the use of the country's
resources to forge a social contract in the first instance, and the
weakening of the contract through significant social inequalities
promoting public protests and, as a result, reforms.
The third reflection on the Kazakhstan case study is that re-
forms can be misused and contribute to a façade of democ-
racy. Digitalization, discussed above, is a good example of this.
Kazakhstan has been accorded international legitimacy in its
use of digital governance, ranking 24th (from 193 countries)
in the UN's e-­government index, yet we have shown how it is
also used for malign purposes, surveillance, repression, and
manipulation of its citizens. Similarly, there are claims of open
government in Kazakhstan with mechanisms to support these
assertions when the reality is that the processes and procedures
in place to secure access to “publicly” available data are cumber-
some and the validity of the information dubious.
In fact, rather than a transition to a “democratic unitary state”
we suggest that the post-­Soviet public administration model ad-
opted by Kazakhstan can be considered as a good example of
hybrid governance. This is where the current political and ad-
ministrative system has features of authoritarianism, market-­
led economic policies, and selective democratic institutions
which sit alongside tribal, familial, or regional networks of in-
fluence (a mix of formal and informal institutions). A hybrid re-
gime is taken from concept of “competitive authoritarianism,” a
type of regime which is different from traditional authoritarian
regimes (Levitsky and Way 2010; Hale 2011; Petrov et al. 2014;
Carothers  2018). Kazakhstan is an example of a competitive
authoritarian regime which holds regular elections, but the
outcome is known because “the playing field is heavily skewed
in favour of the incumbents” and competition is unfair (Levitsky
and Way 2010, 5). Beyond competitive authoritarianism at the
political level, we argue that Kazakhstan is characterized by
public administration hybridity where Soviet-­era practices and
ideologies, institutional structures and bureaucratic cultures
influence modern governance practices. Yet hybrid governance
can deliver regime stability and political pragmatism in cir-
cumstances where public administration institutions are weak
and exercise centralized control. The combination of authori-
tarianism and elements of democracy in Kazakhstan has been
described as “soft authoritarianism” which has resulted in sta-
bility and regime resilient but ultimately suffers from long-­term
legitimacy, public accountability and institutional development
(Isaacs 2018; Schatz 2008).
Typically, in the context of Kazakhstan, we witness several
features of hybridity. The political system is highly central-
ized, with significant power located in the executive branch of
government. Kazakhstan has adopted the trappings of politi-
cal pluralism with a democratic system (bicameral elected par-
liament and multi-­political parties), but opposition parties are
weak or highly managed and face obstacles to participation.
There has been limited political liberalization under President
Tokayev, who has promoted the mantra of a “listening state”
and introduced reforms in local governance, political partici-
pation, and human rights. However, these reforms are limited
and do not alter the centralized nature of the state in which
media freedom is curtailed and independent civil society is
highly restricted in the activities it delivers. The transition to
a market economy has been partial, evidenced by the domi-
nance of large state-­owned enterprises in the energy, telecom-
munications, and transport sectors controlled by a ruling elite
(Orazgaliyev and Akhmetzharov 2020). Despite advice from
major international financial institutions (World Bank, IMF,
Asian Development Bank) to diversify the economy, this has
met with resistance from vested business interests close to
the ruling elite. The dominance of the state in key economic
sectors has also impeded the development of independent reg-
ulatory bodies. There are examples where public sector offi-
cials oversee sectors in which they have direct vested interests
(Loftus 2023). This represents a clear conflict of interest and,
more generally, results in weak institutional capacity to effec-
tively regulate the market.
Patronage and informal networks persist which embed corrup-
tion and rent-­seeking. Kazakhstan has cultivated a moderate
external image through participation in international organi-
zations such as the United Nations, World Trade Organisation
(WTO), and Organisation for Security and Co-­operation
(OSCE), and hosted high-­profile events such as EXPO, World
Nomad Games, OSCE summit, and jubilee summits of the
Shanghai Cooperation Organisation and Organisation of Islamic
Cooperation. This global-­facing external image is at odds with
the authoritarian regime in Kazakhstan. The country's political
leadership justifies a hybrid model, authoritarianism juxtaposed
with selective features of modernization, on the grounds of
nation-­building and stability. Authoritarianism is necessary to
maintain social harmony in a multi-­ethnic society, build politi-
cal stability, and encourage economic growth. This post-­Soviet
narrative has been central to growing and maintaining the

within a volatile Central Asia region.
7   |   Conclusion
Returning to our research question, the evidence does not sup-
port claims of a democratic unitary state or compatible public
administration model. At the macro-­level we see clear evidence
of hybrid governance, a mix of authoritarianism and democratic
elements. There is still a consolidation of powers at the center
of government although President Tokayev has claimed that his
constitutional reform efforts have shifted the country from “a
super-­presidential system to a presidential system with a strong
Parliament.”7 The impact of these reforms and the extent to
which they encourage genuine competitive political processes
remains to be seen. Hybridity is also evident at the economic
level. Initiatives such as Kazakhstan 2050 Strategy and Nurly
Zhol (“Bright Path” in English) are aimed at attracting foreign
investment, particularly in the energy sector but regulation, cor-
ruption and inefficiency in government act as impediments, not-
withstanding claims by the President of a “New Kazakhstan.”
Central to this vision is reducing social inequality yet the Gini
index shows increasing inequality from a high of 26.8% in 2015
to 29.2% in 2021, the latest data available (where 0% = perfect
equality and 100% perfect inequality).
When we look at efforts to democratize and modernize state
institutions, again we witness hybridity. The legacy of Soviet-­
era bureaucracy is still clearly evident in cumbersome adminis-
trative procedures and patronage networks which have proved
difficult to change. Despite capacity building efforts in the civil
service, progress is slow. Participants tend to be early career of-
ficials whose energy and enthusiasm to make change are stifled
by a wider culture of civil servants trained in the Soviet era and
resistant to modernization, not least because it stymies their
efforts in rent-­seeking opportunities. Public administration re-
forms have therefore been layered over existing resilient Soviet
structures and processes. Mixed modernization and Soviet-­era
practices play out in civil service reforms which have introduced
significant changes to enhance efficiency and professionalism.
These include fewer politically appointed civil servants, access
to training and development opportunities, and more open re-
cruitment and promotion procedures based on merit. Positive
results were mainly achieved through open government initia-
tives primarily based on e-­government and digitalization, which
enhanced transparency and citizen engagement. However, tra-
ditional practices continue to hinder the development of an ef-
ficient, transparent, and meritocratic civil service. One example
is that career advancement is still based largely on loyalty and
connections rather than merit (O'Connor et al. 2021). Despite
recruitment and promotion reforms to enhance government
effectiveness, nepotism and high ministerial turnover impede
progress.
This country study of Kazakhstan suggests a modest contribution
to public administration knowledge in several ways. First, it re-
sponds to Chan's (2024) call for empirical research beyond liberal
democracies in which he argues for the adaptation of public ad-
ministration theories to account for the distinct institutional and
political contexts of authoritarian regimes. Second, Kazakhstan
offers a real-­world example of institutional layering, a concept
derived from historical institutionalism where public sector re-
forms are introduced without tackling deep structural transfor-
mation (Mahoney and Thelen 2010). When gradual overlapping
layers of institutional change occur, the result is a form of hybrid
government as in Kazakhstan. This country study also provides
some understanding of hybridity which Peters et al. (2023) ar-
gues is crucial for comparative public administration research,
especially as democratic backsliding becomes more common.
Third, Kazakhstan offers further knowledge on the role of pol-
icy transfer and the adoption of Western public administration
practices in an authoritarian regime (Karini 2024). In so doing,
it provides insights into how Kazakhstan reconciled interna-
tional pressure from donors, local legitimacy needs, and control
exercised by elites, a form of adaptive governance (Janssen and
Van Der Voort 2016). Finally, the case study demonstrates how
public administration reform can occur without full democratic
transition. Kazakhstan adds to existing research on countries
such as China and Singapore which successfully implemented
civil service and e-­government reforms without political liberal-
ization (Nana 2024; Lim 2014). Kazakhstan continues to grapple
with its post-­Soviet legacy overlaid by modern public adminis-
tration practices: well-­trained early career officials, digitaliza-
tion, and ambitions to emulate international standards. These
changes sit beneath the dead hand of centralized control, and
systemic problems of corruption and patronage which are prov-
ing hugely resistant to change.

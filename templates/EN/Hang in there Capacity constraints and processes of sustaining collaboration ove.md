Abstract
Sustaining collaboration over time is vital for its effectiveness and long-term success but presents challenges, especially for actors with capacity
constraints. This study introduces “capacity tensions” as a central challenge in collaborative efforts, referring to the strain that arises when the
capacity needed for effective engagement exceeds the capacity actors have available. This gap creates competing demands, as actors must
balance what they can realistically contribute with what the collaboration needs to remain viable and successful. Focusing on a nonprofit col­
laboration that, despite ongoing capacity constraints, persisted with notable achievements, this study investigates how actors navigate and
manage these tensions to sustain their efforts. By analyzing 165 interviews conducted over eleven rounds, participant observations, and archival
documents from 2016 to 2020, this study identifies strategies of internal accommodation and external orientation. These strategies enabled ac­
tors to “hang in there” by assembling different contributions; tailoring work processes; searching for relevant opportunities; and creating spin-off
projects. Examining these strategies across three phases of collaboration over 5 years, this study proposes a process model that offers insights
into sustaining effective collaboration despite capacity tensions. These findings provide valuable guidance for practitioners and scholars striving
to build sustainable and resilient collaborations.
Key words: collaboration sustainability; capacity constraints; capacity tensions; nonprofit collaboration; process model.

Collaboration has emerged as a powerful strategy for
tackling complex social problems. By engaging in collabo­
ration, actors share resources, broadly defined, to produce
synergistic gains that individual organizations cannot achieve
alone (Ansell and Gash 2008; Bryson, Crosby, and Stone
2015; Emerson, Nabatchi, and Balogh 2012; Gray and Purdy
2018). Despite its potential, however, collaboration often
becomes burdensome, struggles to sustain itself, and may be
terminated before achieving its intended outcomes (Rigg and
O’Mahony 2013; Takahashi and Smutny 2002; Zuzul 2019).
The challenges of managing diverse and often contradicting
demands, the absence of established governance structures,
and the need for building trust and shared understanding re­
quire substantial capacity from actors to lead and sustain col­
laboration effectively (Huxham and Vangen 2013).
But what happens when actors with limited capacity join
collaboration? Can they participate effectively, or do they
withdraw or fail to achieve their purposes due to insufficient
capacity? While existing literature emphasizes the importance
of capacity for successful collaboration, evidence indicates that
actors with varying levels of capacity—including those with
limited capacity—engage in collaboration to enhance their
capacity (Guo and Acar 2005; Wiechman, Alonso Vicario,
and Koebele 2024). This raises the question of how such ac­
tors manage to join and sustain collaborative efforts. Even
actors with ample capacity may struggle to sustain collabo­
ration, depending on how much they can commit. Although
the literature acknowledges capacity building as crucial for
collaboration (Emerson, Nabatchi, and Balogh 2012; Stadtler
and Karakulak 2020), how exactly actors with limited ca­
pacity address these demands and continue collaborating re­
mains undertheorized.
This study seeks to answer the following question: How do
actors with capacity constraints navigate and sustain collab­
oration? Using grounded theory and rich longitudinal data
from action research on an interorganizational collaboration
known as Spark (a pseudonym), I unpack the dynamics of
sustaining collaboration despite ongoing capacity challenges.
Spark is a collaboration among seven nonprofits that started
in 2016 with a mission to reduce racial income and wealth
inequities by supporting minority-owned businesses (MOBs)
in the Twin Cities area of Minnesota. Comprising mostly small
and under-resourced organizations, Spark often struggled to
secure full participation, yet it successfully maintained itself
over several years with notable achievements.

Complexity of leading and managing
collaboration
Collaboration
refers
to
formalized
joint
working
arrangements between independent organizations to achieve
shared purposes (Bryson, Crosby, and Stone 2015; Gray 1989;
Huxham and Vangen 2013). With its emphasis on intentional
and cooperative efforts across organizational boundaries, the
term is often used interchangeably with collaborative govern­
ance (Ansell and Gash 2008), network management (Silvia
and McGuire 2010), or interorganizational and intersectoral
partnerships (Suárez and Esparza 2017). Collaboration holds
the promise of creating a whole that is greater than the sum
of its parts, but the complexity of working across boundaries
often introduces resistance, confusion, and failure.
The challenges of collaboration are well-documented in
the literature. Effective collaboration relies on synthesizing
diverse perspectives, interests, and expertise to create in­
novative and synergistic outcomes. However, this diversity
also complicates coordination and alignment, as reconciling
differing objectives with the collaboration’s overarching pur­
pose requires extensive dialogue, negotiation, and compro­
mise (Quick and Feldman 2014; Wiechman, Alonso Vicario,
and Koebele 2024). This process, though crucial, is often
fraught with misunderstandings, conflicts, and inefficiencies,
leading to what Huxham and Vangen (2013) describe as col­
laborative inertia, which can undermine the collaboration’s
effectiveness (Rigg and O’Mahony 2013; Zuzul 2019).
Beyond finding common ground, effective collaboration
demands concerted action to produce intended outcomes.
Coordinating the efforts of multiple organizations—each
operating within its own constraints—involves aligning com­
plementary resources (Sowa 2009), orchestrating different
timelines (Jarvenpaa and Välikangas 2022), navigating con­
flicting accountability demands (Lee 2022), and leveraging
the strengths of each participant while mitigating weaknesses
(Page et al. 2021). The success of such efforts depends on the
collaboration’s ability to maintain engagement and respond
to emerging challenges, without losing sight of the purpose
of the collaboration. This dynamic process requires stra­
tegic coordination and a robust framework for governing the
complexities inherent in collaborative work.
Effective governance mechanisms are essential to guide
decision-making, allocation of tasks and resources, and con­
flict resolution in a fair and functional manner (Cheng 2019).
Given that actors come from different organizational systems
with distinct hierarchies, decision-making processes, and ac­
countability mechanisms, these must be integrated into a
new governance system that provides “the basic protocols
and ground rules for collaboration” (Ansell and Gash 2008,
p. 555). Addressing power imbalances and encouraging
broad participation are particularly important, as larger or­
ganizations may naturally exert more influence, potentially
marginalizing smaller participants. Deliberate efforts must be
made to balance power dynamics and ensure that all voices
are heard and valued (Purdy 2012). Strong leadership is vital
in this context, as it can help cultivate a collaborative spirit,
facilitate the development of a shared vision, and guide the
group through the complexities of aligning diverse interests
within an inclusive governance framework (Crosby and
Bryson 2005).
In sum, leading and managing collaboration involves
navigating a complex interplay of activities to develop shared
understanding, leverage complementary strengths, and build
effective governance mechanisms. These tasks require active
management, continuous engagement, and the ability to re­
solve conflicts and adapt to new challenges. This suggests
the fundamental role of capacity within collaboration, as it
underpins the ability to manage these processes effectively and
sustainably. Adequate capacity helps collaborations respond
to changes, address issues proactively, and maintain effective­
ness over time, thereby achieving long-term objectives and
fostering a resilient partnership (Spekkink and Boons 2015;
Stadtler and Karakulak 2020; Suárez and Esparza 2017).
Capacity tensions and implications for
sustaining collaboration
Capacity can be understood as the ability to perform work
(Eisinger 2002). While definitions of capacity vary across
levels of analysis in public administration research, it is gen­
erally considered to be a multidimensional concept including
organizational infrastructure, financial, human, and knowl­
edge resources, as well as external environmental factors
(Christensen and Gazley 2008). In collaboration, the concept
encompasses both tangible and intangible assets that enable
participating actors to effectively participate in, contribute to,
and benefit from collaborative efforts (Gray 1989; Spekkink
and Boons 2015).
An important yet often overlooked aspect of capacity in
collaboration is its dual role and somewhat contrasting expec­
tations. On one hand, stronger capacity is a desired outcome
of collaboration. Successful collaborations can significantly

increase problem-solving capacity and enable participants to
tackle challenges beyond a single organization’s reach, such
as expanding social service delivery system (Stadtler and

promoting inclusive public management (Suárez and Esparza
2017), or responding to national crises and emergencies (Simo
and Bies 2007). In other words, collaboration combines indi­
vidual actors’ capacities to create a collaborative capacity that
supports collective activities and performance to achieve the
collaboration’s mission (Emerson, Nabatchi, and Balogh 2012).
The pursuit of stronger capacity extends to the organiza­
tional level as well as the collaboration level, as actors seek
to enhance their own capacity through collaborative efforts.
Studies suggest that collaboration offers numerous benefits
to participants (Guo and Acar 2005; Sowa 2009). From a
resource-dependence perspective, collaboration allows actors
to access complementary or new resource bases that reduce
environmental uncertainties (Pfeffer and Salancik 2003).
Institutional perspectives suggest that collaboration can help
actors gain legitimacy by aligning with participatory govern­
ance norms and expectations (DiMaggio and Powell 1983),
while network theory emphasizes the social benefits of collab­
oration, such as information sharing and accumulated trust
(Gulati, Wohlgezogen, and Zhelyazkov 2012). In sum, effec­
tive collaborations should develop strong capacities both at
the collaboration level to achieve shared purposes and at the
organizational level to benefit participants (Head 2008).
On the other hand, capacity is often seen as a “prerequi­
site” for collaboration (Seo and Bryson 2022). Collaboration
cannot succeed only with good intentions. Actors need ad­
ministrative and social capacity—that is, the ability to
manage operational tasks and build relationships, trust, and
communication channels—to endure the demanding process
of collaboration and nurture its development (Spekkink and
Boons 2015; Sydow 2004). Indeed, some scholars empha­
size the organizational capacity of participating actors as
the crucial component of collaboration for broadening the
breadth and depth of partnerships, generating synergistic
gains, and increasing the long-term viability (Simo and Bies
2007; Suárez and Esparza 2017). Alternatively, insufficient
capacity can hinder meaningful participation and adversely
affect a collaboration’s potential. For example, Kim and Peng
(2018) found that limited human resource capacity prevents
smaller nonprofits from collaborating effectively. Similarly,
Stadtler and Karakulak (2020) showed that collaborations
that focused on capacity building were more successful in
overcoming emergent implementation challenges, while
collaborations that failed to develop capacity did not succeed
in the long run.
The dual role of capacity in collaboration suggests that
tensions may arise from the gap between the capacity needs
of collaboration (what it takes to participate in collaboration)
and the capacity actors have available (what actors can af­
ford), particularly for those with limited capacity. If capacity
is both something that collaboration is supposed to build and
something that is required to participate in the first place,
then what happens to organizations that lack sufficient ca­
pacity? Research indicates that actors with ample capacity
are more likely to invest in and benefit from collaboration
by fostering its development and generating greater gains
(Jang, Feiock, and Saitgalina 2016; Snavely and Tracy 2002).
In contrast, for resource-constrained actors, participating in
collaboration that consumes their already limited capacity
would be counterproductive. Recent work calls for more at­
tention to this tension actors face in collaboration (Kim and
Peng 2018; Seo and Bryson 2022), yet little is known about
how varying levels of capacity shape different collaborative
experiences, particularly for those who may be at a disadvan­
tage due to limited capacity. Specifically, there are two key
limitations in our current understanding.
First, much of the literature assumes that actors possess a
baseline level of capacity necessary for effective participation.
However, not all actors entering a collaboration are equally
equipped (Kim and Peng 2018). Actors with limited ca­
pacity may join collaboration to gain from capacity-building
opportunities, but their lack of resources, time, expertise, or
organizational infrastructure can create barriers to mean­
ingful participation. While the literature emphasizes the im­
portance of building capacity and encouraging inclusive
participation, it often focuses on procedural fairness, such
as democratic decision-making processes (Ansell and Gash
2008; Choi and Robertson 2014) and balancing power dy­
namics (Purdy 2012), rather than practical challenges faced
by capacity-constrained organizations. A more nuanced
exploration is needed of how actors with varying levels of
capacity, especially those with constraints, navigate collabo­
ration and work towards achieving the intended outcomes.
Second, the literature does not adequately address the tem­
poral dimension of sustaining collaboration for capacity-
constrained
actors.
Collaboration
demands
ongoing
commitment and substantial investment (Head 2008; Jaspers
and Steen 2020), which creates a temporal gap between
the immediate, resource-intensive activities and the delayed
benefits that accrue from such efforts (Seo and Bryson 2022;
Seo, Bryson, and Crosby 2023). For actors with limited ca­
pacity, justifying sustained investment without immediate
returns can be particularly challenging. While existing re­
search acknowledges that collaboration requires “contin­
uous” management, it often falls short of addressing the
specific challenges faced by capacity-constrained actors. For
example, Emerson, Nabatchi, and Balogh (2012) emphasize
the iterative process of principled engagement, where on­
going dialogue and reflection build consensus and facilitate
collective action. However, they tend to focus on the quality
of interaction and alignment among actors, rather than the
difficulties of “maintaining” engagement for those with lim­
ited capacity. Similarly, Ansell and Gash (2008) advocate for
cultivating facilitative leadership and celebrating small wins
for momentum and empowerment, yet there remains a lim­
ited understanding of how actors with capacity constraints
can effectively apply such strategies and sustain collaboration
over time.
Methods
This study uses a longitudinal case study and grounded theory
to investigate how actors with limited capacity navigate and
sustain collaboration (Glaser and Strauss 1967; Yin 2017).
The research focuses on Spark, a collaboration in Minnesota,
US, that started with seven nonprofit organizations serving
different MOB groups. Spark seeks to create an innovative
support system to address Minnesota’s racial income and
wealth disparities. Research shows that MOBs are growing
faster than white-owned businesses and hold strong potential

hindered coordination and impact. To address these issues,
the seven organizations agreed to work together. Appendix A

clients, main services, and total revenue.
Process studies make use of time bracketing to help un­
derstand what happens in a case (Cloutier and Langley
2020; Langley 1999). I separate the case into three periods,
each with a different focus on collaborative work: Phase 1
(2016–2018)—Building the infrastructure; Phase 2 (2019)—
Strengthening the collaboration; and Phase 3 (2020)—
Utilizing the collaboration. During Phase 1, actors built the
foundation of collaboration, such as shared norms and gov­
ernance structures. Starting the first leadership meeting as a
“Minority Business Development Cohort” in October 2016,
the group explored what they wanted to achieve through col­
laboration. In 2017, they launched workgroups and branded
the collaboration as “Spark” to better represent it to funders
and clients. In 2018, they adopted a joint Memorandum of
Understanding (MOU) to solidify their governance structure.
In Phase 2, the focus shifted to strengthening collaboration.
Spark created a strategic plan and engaged in joint advocacy
for MOBs at the state legislature. During this time, the group
prioritized establishing shared leadership and a sustainable
governance system. In Phase 3, Spark’s priority pivoted to
crisis management following the global pandemic and civil
unrest after George Floyd’s murder in May 2020, which dis­
proportionally affected MOBs. By utilizing its collaborative
platform, Spark significantly increased support for MOBs
and secured major funding to launch a joint loan program
tailored to minority business needs.
Spark is well suited to understanding the processes of
sustaining collaboration. First, Spark consists mostly of small
and under-resourced organizations and struggled with var­
ious kinds of capacity constraints in maintaining collabora­
tion. The time and effort needed to make Spark operational
posed a heavy burden on participants. Second, as a voluntary
collaboration, Spark could have dissolved if participation
had become too burdensome. Still, Spark sustained itself for
several years with some meaningful achievements, indicating
its success in supporting members’ continued participation.
Third, this study benefited from exceptional access to longi­
tudinal data collected in real time, offering rich insights into
Spark’s development over multiple years. Thus, Spark serves
as a “revelatory case” that provides a valuable opportunity
to understand how actors navigate the challenges of ongoing
participation and maintain collaboration (Yin 2017).
Data collection
The data for this paper come from action research in which
I served on a two-person developmental evaluation team
to help Spark move forward (Coghlan 2011; Patton 2010).
I have studied Spark’s convening organization as part of a
larger research project on organizational change since August
2015. When Spark began in October 2016, I and a colleague
were invited to observe and document the collaboration
process in real time and provide periodic feedback to enhance
the speed and effectiveness of their collaborative endeavors.
Through active and prolonged engagement in the field, we
were able to build trust with the actors in Spark, gain in­
sider perspectives, and gather comprehensive and valuable
information on the collaboration process, thus facilitating
the building of a credible and holistic account of the case
(Creswell and Miller 2000).
Multiple sources informed this study. First, 165 in-depth
interviews over eleven rounds were conducted with twenty-
seven actors in Spark (see table 1). Beginning in May 2017,
the evaluation team conducted quarterly interviews with all
Spark meeting participants—those who regularly attended
meetings, represented Spark’s member organizations, or
were deeply engaged in the collaboration work—who were
mostly CEOs and key staff of each member organization.
Additionally, we held monthly interviews with the CEOs of the
convening organization through the larger research project.
This difference in interview frequency reflects the convening
organization’s crucial role in designing and implementing
the collaboration. To ensure a balanced perspective, we also
conducted periodic interviews with other key actors across
different organizations, including external consultants hired
by Spark for meeting facilitation and project management.

Data analysis
I used a grounded theory approach to develop a theory on
how actors sustain collaboration despite the challenges of on­
going participation (Glaser and Strauss 1967; Locke 2001).
Data analysis involved a complex, nonlinear, and iterative
process of constant revisiting and reinterpretation of data.
The first step was to build a base narrative of the collabora­
tion.1 A timeline of Spark’s development was first created with
the dates and overviews of key events, such as each Spark
meeting, decisions made, field trips the group took, and com­
munity events hosted by Spark. Then, a comprehensive nar­
rative was constructed in chronological order with multiple
overlapping time brackets. Each section had an approximate
time period, detailed descriptions of events, and references to
supporting documents. The base narrative served as a tool
for combining different pieces of data and understanding the
overarching story of collaboration development (Feldman et
al. 2004; Jones and McBeth 2010).
Second, I created a summary for each interview and organ­
ized a table listing the main topics discussed by each member
organization across the eleven rounds of interviews. This step
aimed to track the evolving perspectives of actors and ex­
amine any changes or consistencies over time. From this ex­
ercise, I noticed a pattern that appeared in almost all rounds
of the interviews: Actors expressed a strong commitment to
Spark and actively engaged in strategizing, often diagnosing
the collaboration’s current state and identifying areas for im­
provement. Yet, the same actors frequently voiced frustration
with slow progress and the lack of tangible outcomes. This
pattern made me ask the following questions: How can these
conflicting attitudes be explained? What are the underlying
mechanisms that made actors both frustrated and committed
at the same time? Also, given that Spark is a voluntary collab­
oration, what makes actors stay in collaboration despite their
continued frustration? These questions guided the subsequent
stages of analysis.
In the third stage, I conducted open coding of interview
transcripts, reflection notes, and archival data using NVivo
(Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton 2013; Glaser and Strauss
1967). I searched for themes that broadly captured actors’
perceptions toward the collaboration and what they did
based on the perceptions, while remaining open to emergent
themes. The coding was conducted in an iterative manner
as I kept looking for new codes that would support or chal­
lenge my existing codes, revisiting relevant literature for addi­
tional insights, and refining codes, until it reached theoretical
saturation (Locke 2001). Then, I clustered the themes into
higher-order categories to distill the theoretical implication
of the findings beyond the specific context of the case (Gioia,
Corley, and Hamilton 2013), which resulted in broad themes
around challenges of participation, particularly around ca­
pacity tensions; threats to sustainability; coping strategies;
and implications for sustaining collaboration. The overview
of the final coding structure is shown in fig. 1.
As I continued to refine the coding structure and make sense
of emerging findings, I also focused on recurring patterns in
the temporal progression of the case and developed a process
model that illustrates how actors persist and sustain collabora­
tion with various strategies (Langley 1999). Following Howard-
Grenville, Metzger, and Meyer (2013), I performed a final
round of finetuning codes as I developed the model, and linked
it to the Spark case. To ensure the credibility of the findings, I
conducted triangulation and verified my interpretations were
supported by several data sources (i.e., different interviewees)
and methods (i.e., interviews, observations, and documents).
Also, I constantly reviewed the data and revisited my emerging
theory, searching for both confirming and disconfirming evi­
dence (Miles and Huberman 1994). In this process, I often en­
gaged in peer debriefing with colleagues who have expertise on
this topic or the case. Finally, I carefully documented the steps
I took throughout the data collection and analysis process,
logging all research activities and decisions as an audit trail
(Creswell and Miller 2000; Yin 2017).
A process model of managing capacity
tension and sustaining collaboration
In this section, I first present a general process model of
addressing capacity tensions (fig. 2), and then apply it to ex­
amine how different phases and patterns of addressing these
tensions influence the sustainability of collaboration, using
the case of Spark. At the core, the model captures the per­
sistent tension between capacity needs and availability, and
how coping strategies enable actors to “hang in there” de­
spite ongoing strain. Capacity tension refers to the inherent
challenges that arise when the capacity needed for effective
collaboration exceeds what actors can realistically commit.
This discrepancy creates competing demands, as participating
organizations must invest time and various resources even if
they lack sufficient capacity to fully meet these demands. For
actors with limited capacity, the tension is particularly signifi­
cant, as it forces continuous negotiation and balance between
their contributions and the collaboration’s requirements to
remain viable and successful. Without effective management,
capacity tension can lead to frustration, inertia, or even the
collapse of collaborative efforts. As capacity needs and avail­
ability evolve, specific experiences of collaboration may vary,
but the underlying tension remains constant and impacts
overall outcomes and sustainability.

The process model features two categories of strategies for
addressing capacity tension. Internal accommodation focuses
on optimizing the use of existing capacities within the col­
laboration. First, actors assemble different contributions by
acknowledging and capitalizing on each other’s strengths and
understanding the overarching vision of the collaboration. A
principle of reciprocity is essential both in terms of mutual
respect and contributions. In the case of Spark, there was a
strong emphasis on that every member organization had im­
portant roles to play regardless of its size or power and that
everyone will play the roles when and where they can. Second,
actors tailor work process to foster meaningful engagement
and broader participation to leverage the diverse strengths of
every participant. This includes offering customized support
while also establishing structures that facilitate involvement.
In Spark, this involved restructuring group meetings and pro­
viding individualized support to elicit active participation.
Meanwhile,
external
orientation
involves
seeking
opportunities in the surrounding environment that the collab­
oration can leverage. This manifests in two forms: Searching
for relevant opportunities and creating spin-off projects.
First, actors may explore the external landscape for existing
opportunities that align with the collaboration’s strengths. The
most salient example from Spark is its continuous pursuit of
various grant opportunities. Second, actors can leverage what
they already have to launch smaller-scale, semi-independent
projects for immediate outcomes while developing the collab­
oration. For example, actors in Spark often initiated spin-off
partnerships outside of the main collaborative efforts to ben­
efit from existing programs within member organizations.
Tensions may not be completely resolved, but effective use
of these strategies at both the collaborative and individual
levels allows actors to persevere and continue engagement.
This persistence is crucial to navigating capacity tensions and
sustaining collaboration. Because collaboration often requires
significant effort and time to become fully operational and
fulfill its promise, capacity-constrained actors might struggle
to stay involved. These coping strategies help actors manage
the tensions by making the most use of their existing capacity
(internal accommodation) and providing a sense of accom­
plishment (external orientation), enabling them to remain
committed despite challenges. By staying engaged, actors
contribute to building the collaboration’s infrastructure and
gain a clearer understanding of the collaboration’s situation
and needs, which informs capacity dynamics in future phases.

Challenges of capacity tension.
Seven organizations that serve various groups of MOBs

Discussion
Beginning with the puzzle of how actors with limited ca­
pacity navigate and sustain the capacity-consuming process
of collaboration, this study reveals the persistent tensions
arising from the gap between capacity needs and availability,
and identifies strategies that allow actors to move forward.
By effectively managing capacity tensions, Spark sustained
the collaboration until it became fairly well-developed and
produced tangible outcomes. This study extends the literature
in three key areas. First, it demonstrates and conceptualizes
the inherent tensions around capacity and the challenges of
sustaining participation. Second, it theorizes the temporal
dynamics of collaboration development by focusing on the
gap between the initial investment and delayed benefits at the
center of capacity tensions. Third, by exploring interactions
among actors with varying capacity levels, it uncovers the
micro-level practices and processes that foster shared leader­
ship in collaboration. Here I elaborate on these contributions
and their implications for future research.
Inherent tension around capacity in collaboration
This study explores how actors navigate the complex process
of collaboration, focusing on the experiences of small,
­under-resourced organizations that face persistent challenges
in maintaining involvement. The concept of capacity tensions
highlights the challenges arising when the capacity needed for
collaboration exceeds what actors can provide. In nonprofit
settings, where resources are often limited and external funding
is uncertain (Seo, Bryson, and Williams 2025), these tensions
are especially salient, making sustained participation a cen­
tral challenge. Since no organization has unlimited capacity to

dedicate to collaboration, capacity tensions are an inherent fea­
ture of collaborative processes (Kim and Peng 2018; Seo and
Bryson 2022).
While capacity is recognized as a major motivation for col­
laboration (Gray 1989; Guo and Acar 2005) and a necessary
condition for meaningful participation (Spekkink and Boons
2015), most studies address either the need for capacity or its
availability, without adequately examining the tension arising
from these two aspects. By empirically investigating both,
this study uncovers an undertheorized dimension of collab­
oration: the difficulties of actors not only to join but also to
maintain their involvement despite limited capacity.
Existing literature typically assumes a baseline capacity
among actors and focuses on challenges faced by actors al­
ready securely “at the table,” conceptualizing challenges pri­
marily as reconciling differences or coordinating complex
processes. For example, concepts such as “principled engage­
ment” and “capacity for joint action” (Emerson, Nabatchi,
and Balogh 2012) emphasize deliberate and structured
exchanges among actors and the overall functionality of
collaboration. Discussions on addressing power imbalances
often center on procedural fairness among those already
participating (Choi and Robertson 2014; Purdy 2012), rather
than considering those struggling to maintain their place. In
contrast, the strategies revealed in this study—internal ac­
commodation (assembling different contributions and tai­
loring work process) and external orientation (searching for
relevant opportunities and creating spin-off projects)—are
specifically aimed at helping those who are “struggling to stay
at the table.” These strategies uniquely foreground the role of
capacity in collaboration and the need for enabling actors to
remain engaged despite ongoing capacity tensions.
These strategies operate through multiple mechanisms.
Their action-oriented aspects align closely with behavioral
mechanisms, as they involve tangible efforts to adapt and
sustain participation. Simultaneously, the ongoing need to in­
terpret and navigate collaboration dynamics highlights a cog­
nitive and social dimension, with actors constantly reassessing
and adjusting their contributions. Furthermore, attention to
formal and informal governance systems introduces a struc­
tural component, where adjustments to meeting formats or
decision-making structures facilitate sustained engagement.
While the importance of adaptation and managing the
environment has long been recognized in organizational
“moves” (Pentland 1992), these often assume that actors
possess the capacity to adapt or manage effectively. In col­
laboration, however, limited capacity fundamentally alters
these processes. Internal accommodation strategies go be­
yond mere adaptation by managing scarce resources so that
even capacity-constrained actors can continue to contribute
meaningfully. Similarly, external orientation strategies extend
beyond traditional environmental management by enabling
smaller actors to secure incremental gains and sustain their
participation despite limited capacity. These strategies ac­
knowledge the harsh reality that for many actors, the primary
challenge is to simply “hang in there” long enough to make
meaningful contributions on the arduous journey of collabo­
ration that ultimately can result in substantial benefits.
By highlighting these strategies, this study offers new
insights into how capacity tensions are navigated, focusing on
the challenge of simply remaining engaged in collaboration—a
challenge often taken for granted. This focus on sustaining
participation is crucial because, without it, the more visible
challenges of coordination and implementation cannot be ef­
fectively addressed. Overlooking the role of capacity can lead
to mistakenly attributing the failure of collaborations (or the
exit of some actors) to a lack of incentives or insufficient co­
ordination, rather than recognizing underlying constraints.
Thus, this study shifts the emphasis to the need for strategies
that help actors endure and remain engaged, ultimately
leading to more resilient and inclusive collaborations.
Hang in there. . . until when? Temporal dynamics of
sustaining collaboration
This study highlights the crucial role of the temporal dimen­
sion in navigating capacity tensions within evolving collabo­
ration. Collaboration is a dynamic process, unfolding through
distinct phases, with shifting challenges and changing ca­
pacity needs (Sydow 2004). Managing the tensions requires
ongoing learning and adaptation. Although existing literature
hints at these dynamics, (e.g., Seo, Bryson, and Crosby 2023;
Suárez and Esparza 2017; Takahashi and Smutny 2002), this
study provides an in-depth, phase-by-phase examination of
how actors grapple with capacity tensions over time.
Understanding the temporal dimension is particularly relevant
to capacity tensions. Collaborations often follow an “invest first,
benefit later” model, which creates a time gap between when
actors invest their resources and when they begin to see returns.
This dynamic exacerbates capacity tensions, especially for
smaller actors who must persist until benefits materialize. While
prior studies acknowledge the role of “small wins” in building
momentum (Ansell and Gash 2008), it remains relatively silent
on how capacity-constrained actors achieve these milestones.
This study demonstrates how internal accommodation and
external orientation strategies allow actors to persist through
challenges, essentially “buying time” for the collaboration to
mature. By dividing the analysis into three phases, it uncovers
a recurring pattern of capacity tensions and adaptive
strategies across collaboration development (Langley 1999).
In Phase 1, internal accommodation was vital for building the
collaboration’s foundation and ensuring participation from
smaller organizations, while external orientation supported
visibility and early resource mobilization. In Phase 2, as the
focus shifted toward producing tangible outcomes, both
strategies became equally important. Actors worked inter­
nally to build shared leadership and stronger governance
while pursuing advocacy efforts and external relationship
building. Phase 3 highlighted the agility of Spark as it pivoted
to crisis management during the COVID-19 pandemic and
racial justice protests. External orientation became crucial for
securing additional resources and policy recognition, while
internal adjustments facilitated active participation.
Although the emphasis of these strategies shifted across phases,
their interplay remained critical throughout the collaboration’s
development. Rather than one strategy dominating the other,
both internal accommodation and external orientation were
continuously employed together. This synergy created cumu­
lative effects, allowing the collaboration to progress despite
ongoing challenges (Head 2008; Sydow 2004). For example,
Spark’s achievements in Phase 3 were the result of continuous
efforts from the previous phases. While these strategies do not
eliminate capacity tensions, they help actors remain engaged and
advance collaboration, as illustrated in the process model.
By situating collaboration within the context of time—a per­
spective often advocated but rarely implemented (Cloutier and
Langley 2020; Langley 1999)—this study captures the evolving

Hang in there. . . for whom? Toward shared
leadership in collaboration
This study deepens our understanding of shared leadership
in collaboration, particularly when there is a significant dis­
parity in capacity among participants. Shared leadership2,
often described as a decentralized approach where multiple
actors assume leadership roles, is highly relevant in collab­
orative efforts involving diverse capacities (Crosby and
Bryson 2005; Ospina et al. 2020; Quick 2017). This study
demonstrates how shared leadership evolves as a dynamic
outcome of both practical necessity and the coping strategies
employed to sustain collaboration.
In Spark, shared leadership emerged as actors persevered to
bridge the temporal gap between initial investments and even­
tual rewards. While “hanging in there” is central to this discus­
sion, a detailed analysis distinguishes the different challenges
faced by organizations based on their capacity. For smaller
organizations, the challenge lies in justifying their upfront in­
vestment of limited capacity when returns are not immediate.
For these actors, “hanging in there” involves enduring the
often uncertain and prolonged period before benefits mate­
rialize, maintaining their presence despite immediate strains.
Their persistence is vital to remain engaged and ultimately
contribute to the collaboration’s success.
In contrast, larger organizations with greater capacity
face the challenge of supporting smaller actors without
dominating the process. While they have the resources to sus­
tain their participation, meaningful collaboration requires
the engagement and input of all participants, including those
with limited capacity. For these larger actors, “hanging in
there” means exercising patience and providing strategic
support to help smaller organizations progress at their own
pace. This approach fosters an inclusive environment where
all participants can contribute meaningfully over time, thus
achieving the collaboration’s overarching purposes.
For Spark, larger organizations provided essential early
support to smaller ones, facilitating their participation de­
spite severe capacity limitations. This support, delivered in a
respectful and non-dominating manner, laid the groundwork
for shared leadership, where each organization played distinct
yet complementary roles. The notion of mutual contributions
with staggered timing, as evident in Spark, is significant in
this context. Smaller organizations may need time to build
their capacity before fully engaging, while larger organiza­
tions might contribute initially to support the collaboration’s
development. This staggered contribution approach fosters
inclusivity and sustainability, allowing all participants to con­
tribute meaningfully at their own pace.
These findings align with existing literature on the impor­
tance of prosocial behaviors in eliciting broader participa­
tion, especially in the early stages of collaboration (Bryson,
Crosby, and Stone 2015; Choi and Robertson 2019; Emerson,
Nabatchi, and Balogh 2012). This study adds to the literature
by illustrating the micro-level practices and strategies that en­
able these prosocial behaviors to take root and produce ripple
effects over time. By broadening participation and leadership
within the collaboration, these strategies sustained Spark and
contributed to its long-term success. The dual processes of
“hanging in there”—self-sustainability for smaller actors and
fostering collective engagement for larger ones—emphasize
the critical role of leadership not just in guiding collaboration
but also in supporting and nurturing the capacity of all actors
involved, ultimately building collaboration that thrives over
time (Huxham and Vangen 2013; Purdy 2012).
Conclusion
This study examines how actors address capacity tensions
and sustain collaboration through a process model. To map
the boundaries of the findings, I discuss collaboration settings
to which they are best suited and suggest an agenda for future
research. First, the findings and process model speak directly
to voluntary nonprofit collaborations, where actors lead
efforts without predefined directions and designated resources
(Huxham 1991; Jang, Feiock, and Saitgalina 2016). Spark
started with a loosely defined mission of better-supporting
MOBs and spent considerable time clarifying its objectives
and strategies. This open-ended approach intensified the
challenges of sustaining participation, especially when tan­
gible benefits were not immediately evident. While these
challenges are pertinent to any collaboration, they are par­
ticularly pronounced in nonprofit settings where limited
capacity and resource constraints frequently shape collabo­
rative endeavors (Seo, Bryson, and Williams 2025). Future
research should investigate how different collaboration types
and starting conditions influence actors’ experiences and
strategies for sustaining collaboration.
Second, Spark benefited from the early involvement of
committed actors, particularly from larger organizations with
greater capacity. These actors played vital roles in mitigating
capacity constraints and advancing the collaboration.
Their prosocial behaviors, such as spearheading efforts and
facilitating broad participation, were essential in sustaining
the collaboration during its early formative stages (Bryson,
Crosby, and Stone 2015; Choi and Robertson 2019; Gray
and Purdy 2018). This study highlights how these behaviors
can generate ripple effects toward shared leadership and sus­
tainable collaboration. However, strong leadership is not
guaranteed in every collaboration. Future studies could ex­
plore how collaborations lacking dedicated actors manage
various challenges in sustaining collaboration and whether
such efforts foster prosocial behaviors among participants.
Finally, Spark capitalized on unique external circumstances
presented by the pandemic and civil unrest following George
Floyd’s murder, and the subsequent surge in support for
MOBs during the latter part of the study period. Actors swiftly
transformed Spark meetings into platforms for crisis manage­
ment and leveraged their collaborative efforts to engage funders
and policymakers. While these achievements were facilitated by
external conditions, they were also a result of Spark’s continued
efforts to establish the collaboration’s foundations well before

ABSTRACT
Research has shown that administrative burdens significantly influence benefit uptake across various welfare programs in the
U.S. and beyond. However, much of the existing research has focused primarily on program take-­up, leaving a gap in our un-
derstanding of how burdens affect the ongoing use of welfare benefits. To address this gap, we utilize a regression discontinuity
design to analyze how the introduction of substantial fees on interpreting services affects usage of healthcare services among
Danish immigrants. Our data comes from highly reliable, detailed registries that track the weekly use of general practitioners by
Danish immigrants. We find no effects of the fees on healthcare utilization and no evidence that the fees disproportionately affect
disadvantaged groups. Additional survey-­based analyses suggest that doctors may use their discretion to mitigate the fee's impact
on immigrants, highlighting how frontline workers can potentially reduce the effects of burdensome policies.
1   |   Introduction
Administrative burdens have been defined as people's experi-
ences of policy implementation as onerous (Burden et al. 2012).
Burdensome experiences are not only unpleasant, but also
critical because they can impede access to essential public
benefits (Herd and Moynihan 2019). Research shows that ad-
ministrative barriers significantly affect benefit uptake across
various welfare programs in the US (Fox et al. 2020; Fox, Feng,
and Reynolds  2023; Rauscher and Burns  2023) and beyond
(Heinrich 2016). Yet, previous research predominantly focuses
on program take-­up (Halling and Baekgaard  2024), leaving a
gap in understanding how burdensome experiences affect the
day-­to-­day usage of welfare benefits. Accessing welfare benefits
and utilizing them are fundamentally different processes. The
initial access often involves navigating complex application pro-
cedures, while ongoing use requires regular compliance with
administrative requirements. Therefore, the impact of admin-
istrative burdens may vary significantly between these stages.
We address this gap by analyzing how the introduction of a
formal policy design that includes tangible monetary demands
affects weekly healthcare utilization among immigrants. More
specifically, we examine how the introduction of fees for inter-
preting services in Danish healthcare encounters affects the
use of general practitioner (GP) services. The fees were intro-
duced on July 1, 2018, for all immigrants who have been liv-
ing in Demark for more than 3 years (The Danish Ministry of
Health 2018).
We propose and test two competing hypotheses regarding the
impact of interpreting fees on immigrant access to GP services.
First, drawing from the administrative burden framework
(Herd and Moynihan 2019), we suggest that fees for interpreting

In addition to providing insight into how administrative bur-
dens affect regular usage of welfare services, this article makes
at least three other contributions to the literature on adminis-
trative burdens. The first contribution is empirical and meth-
odological. While previous research has used rigorous causal
designs, such as field and survey experiments, to examine
the effects of administrative burdens on welfare take-­up (e.g.,
Lasky-­Fink and Linos 2023; Linos, Quan, and Kirkman 2020),
our study uses exceptionally detailed administrative data that
few other studies have access to. This data enables us to metic-
ulously track immigrants' behavioral responses to the policy on
a weekly basis. Crucially, the strength of our analysis lies in our
ability to attribute these behavioral changes to the policy itself,
made possible using a robust causal design.
A second contribution of this study is to analyze and discuss a
racialized burden within the European context. In their recent
paper, Ray, Herd, and Moynihan (2023) define racialized burdens
as “the experience of learning, compliance, and psychological
costs that serve as inequality reproducing mechanisms” (p. 139).
While several studies have documented discrimination against
ethnic minorities in their interactions with European wel-
fare states (e.g., Olsen, Kyhse-­Andersen, and Moynihan 2020;
Andersen and Guul 2019; Pedersen, Stritch, and Thuesen 2018),
few have examined the impact of policies explicitly designed to
increase the difficulty of these interactions for ethnic minorities.
As will be discussed later, this is precisely the political motiva-
tion behind implementing interpreting fees.
Third, we contribute to the emerging literature linking ad-
ministrative burdens to street-­level bureaucracy (e.g., Bell and
Meyer 2024; Bell et al. 2021). Our results show that despite po-
litical intentions to make health encounters more burdensome
for immigrants, doctors use their discretion to largely neutralize
the effects of the policy. This is consistent with previous stud-
ies showing that frontline workers actively work to minimize
citizens' experiences of administrative burdens (Halling and
Petersen 2024; Bell and Smith 2022).
We begin by introducing the legislation on interpreting fees in
Danish health encounters to provide the necessary context for
understanding the case. Next, we discuss the political and racial
origins of the policy using the administrative burden framework.
We then hypothesize, based on this framework and studies of
welfare take-­up, that interpreting fees will reduce the use of GP
services. We also present a competing hypothesis, based on the
literature on street-­level bureaucrats, suggesting that the fees
may have no effect. Finally, we present the results, followed by a
discussion and concluding remarks.
2   |   Background: Interpreting Fees in Danish
Health Encounters
The law that implemented interpreter fees in Danish healthcare
settings took effect on July 1, 2018. Since then, immigrants who
have lived in Denmark for more than 3 years must pay a fee
whenever an interpreter is used during their interactions with
health authorities. The only exceptions to this rule are children,
individuals unable to learn Danish due to physical or psycho-
logical illness, and people from the Faroe Islands, Greenland,
and the German-­speaking minorities in Denmark. For GP en-
counters, the fees for using professional interpreters are DKK
191 (USD 28) for video or telephone interpretation, while it costs
DKK 334 (USD 48) for in-­person interpretation (The Danish
Ministry of Health 2018). Fees must be paid for each consulta-
tion, which typically lasts 10–15 min (Kjeldsen 2019).
GPs play a crucial role in the Danish healthcare sector as they are
the primary entry point for any Danish resident seeking medical
care. Access to most specialists requires a referral from a GP. As
a result, nearly all Danish residents have a GP and visit them on

2.1   |   Previous Studies on the Interpreting Fees
A study suggests that the introduction of fees was associated
with a reduction in the use of interpreters (Michaëlis, Krasnik,
and Norredam  2021). This study used aggregated data on in-
terpreting services provided by the Danish regions and showed
that the use of professional interpreters fell by up to 40% after
the introduction of fees. However, the authors cannot draw
causal conclusions based on their data and design and therefore
cannot definitively attribute the decrease in interpreter use to
the new legislation. Nevertheless, it seems unlikely that the de-
cline would have occurred without the introduction of interpret-
ing fees.
Another study surveyed immigrants in Danish language schools
to assess the impact of the legislation. Of 486 respondents, 164
reported needing an interpreter after three years in Denmark.
Of these 164 respondents, 42% said they had been unable to visit
a doctor or hospital because of the interpreting fees, while 73%
said they had used friends or family as interpreters (Nielsen and
Jervelund 2023). While these results are interesting, it is import-
ant to acknowledge that they are based on a cross-­sectional sur-
vey. Therefore, they cannot be used to draw causal inferences
about the impact of interpreting fees, which is one of the pri-
mary goals of this article.
3   |   Interpreting Fees as Administrative Burdens
Administrative burdens are individuals' experiences of policy
implementation as onerous (Burden et al. 2012). The definition
highlights that administrative burdens are experiential, which
means they vary considerably between individuals (Christensen
et al. 2020). Moynihan, Herd, and Harvey (2015) separate these
onerous experiences into three types of perceived costs that indi-
viduals can encounter when interacting with the state: learning
costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs. Learning costs
represent the time spent learning about public benefits; compli-
ance costs represent the time and resources spent complying
with requirements from the state; and psychological costs rep-
resent stress, stigma, and loss of autonomy from participating
in programs.
3.1   |   Interpreting Fees as Politicized, Racialized
Burdens
Before discussing the impact of interpreting fees on health-
care use, we draw on theoretical frameworks that explore the
political (Peeters 2020; Moynihan, Herd, and Ribgy 2016) and
racialized aspects of administrative burdens (Ray, Herd, and
Moynihan  2023). Situating the policy within this context, we
connect it to the broader literature on administrative burdens,
which should help understand its wider implications.
A central argument in the administrative burden literature is
that burdens are often intentionally constructed by policymak-
ers to achieve specific policy goals (Herd and Moynihan 2019,
19). Thus, the ideology of political actors shapes the nature and
extent of barriers across different policy areas. For instance,
Moynihan, Herd, and Ribgy (2016) found that access to Medicaid
tends to be easier in states with Democratic control than in those
governed by Republicans. This finding is supported by subse-
quent research on “burden tolerance” (Baekgaard, Moynihan,
and Thomsen  2021), which demonstrates that conservative
politicians, frontline workers, and members of the public are
more supportive of administrative burdens in welfare programs
(Baekgaard, Moynihan, and Thomsen  2021; Bell et  al.  2021;
Baekgaard, Halling, and Moynihan 2024).
It is easy to assume that fees on interpreting services, like many
co-­payments in healthcare, were implemented to manage de-
mand and save money (Zeckhauser 2019). However, the rhetoric
of successive governments suggests that this was not the pri-
mary policy goal. Instead, it is a fairly clear example of a policy
that deliberately imposes administrative burdens on a marginal-
ized group. When the policy was introduced in the 2017 Finance
Bill by the right-­wing government, Health Minister Ellen Trane
Nørby emphasized that immigrants would be expected to learn
Danish within 3 years of their arrival. After the 2019 election,
the newly elected government led by the Social Democrats con-
tinued to support the policy, despite reports that the adminis-
trative costs of redeeming fees by far exceeded revenues. Social
Democratic spokesperson Rasmus Horn Langhoff was clear
about the policy's intent: “This is not about earning money. It's
about making sure that everyone who lives here in Denmark
learns Danish and becomes an active member of Danish soci-
ety” (Mansø and Pedersen 2019).
Hence, this is an example of policymakers explicitly using ad-
ministrative burdens to achieve a policy goal. Further, it is
noteworthy that the burdens are applied in a policy area that is
unrelated to the primary goal. Specifically, the goal is to promote
language learning, which falls within the broader domain of im-
migration policy, but the burdens are imposed in the healthcare
domain. This illustrates the creativity of policymakers in mak-
ing it more difficult to be an immigrant in Denmark. Therefore,
it can also be characterized as an example of what Ray, Herd,
and Moynihan (2023) call “racialized burdens.” These are ex-
periences of learning, compliance, and psychological costs that
serve as mechanisms for reproducing inequality. Racialized
burdens can arise from seemingly neutral rules or intentional
policies that distribute resources, status, and punishment un-
equally. In Denmark, as in other places, immigrants are gen-
erally disadvantaged compared to the general population. The
imposition of fees targeted specifically at this group can thus
serve as a mechanism for perpetuating and even exacerbating
existing societal inequalities.
3.2   |   Burdensome Experiences From
Interpreting Fees
The most obvious administrative burden that individuals face is
the fee itself (USD 28–48). Fees on public benefits can be a sig-
nificant compliance cost that may affect access to these benefits.

For example, Heinrich et al. (2022) describe how fees on birth
certificates mean that some people cannot afford the certifi-
cates, which in turn affects their access to public benefits (15–
16). In this context, it is necessary to consider whether the fee
is substantial enough to affect GP utilization. At least two rele-
vant comparisons should be considered here. First, interpreting
fees can be viewed as a form of healthcare co-­payment; a topic
extensively studied for nearly 50 years. Numerous empirical
studies, including field experiments and other robust research
designs, demonstrate that higher prices reduce the demand for
healthcare services, particularly those with price-­elastic de-
mand like GP services (Landsem and Magnussen 2018; Olsen
and Melberg 2018; Manning et al. 1987; Kiil and Houlberg 2014).
For example, Landsem and Magnussen (2018) find that a fee of
about USD 17 (NOK 172) reduces GP visits by 10%–15% among
Norwegian adolescents. Given that the interpreting fees are sig-
nificantly higher, they should have the financial potential to af-
fect GP use.
Second, it is important to consider how much of immigrants'
income the fee represents. This will vary significantly and will
not be a substantial share for immigrants with high salaries.
However, a relatively large proportion of immigrants, under 60%
of nonwestern immigrants in 2018 (Stender and Petersen 2023),
are not employed and rely on integration benefits. For a person
under 30 without children, these benefits are DKK 6553 (USD
949) per month. Consequently, the lowest interpreting fee of
USD 28 (for video interpretation) constitutes nearly 3% of a
person's monthly income on integration benefits. While paying
such a fee occasionally might be manageable, it becomes a sub-
stantial burden if one needs interpreting services multiple times
a month.
Fees can also create considerable learning costs. Individuals
must spend time and resources to become familiar with the
new legislation. This includes finding out whether one is ex-
empt from paying the fee, whether a child can be used as an
interpreter, and how to pay the bills. This task is particularly
burdensome because Denmark's five regions have implemented
the legislation in different ways, creating confusion and thereby
potentially higher learning costs.
Finally, the fee can also trigger psychological costs. These in-
clude stress from dealing with compliance or learning costs
(Herd and Moynihan 2020). The fee may also stigmatize af-
fected individuals to some extent. Paying a fee to understand
the content of interactions with the state may signal to in-
dividuals that they are undeserving of help (Schneider and
Ingram  1993), and such signals are potentially stigmatizing
(Nisar 2018).
3.3   |   Take Up of Benefits
The potential administrative burdens faced by individuals as a
result of the new legislation are critical in themselves. However,
facing administrative burdens can also influence whether indi-
viduals take up the welfare benefits to which they are entitled.
Take-­up refers to the extent to which eligible individuals partic-
ipate in welfare programs and claim the benefits to which they
are entitled. As noted by Currie (2004), high take-­up is critical
to the effectiveness of social programs, yet many individuals
fail to enroll due to a variety of barriers. Recent research on ad-
ministrative burdens shows that they are one such barrier (Fox
et al. 2020; Daigneault and Macé 2020).
One of the key findings from this literature is that rule com-
plexity and extensive documentation requirements are signifi-
cant barriers to welfare take-­up. For example, Fox, Feng, and
Reynolds  (2023) show that simplifying administrative pro-
cesses—by automating enrollment and linking eligibility to
other programs—can significantly increase program participa-
tion. Deshpande and Li (2019) show how closures of social secu-
rity field offices, which increases compliance costs because of
longer travel times, […] lead to a 16 percent decline in the num-
ber of disability recipients in surrounding areas (213). Lasky-­
Fink and Linos (2023) demonstrate that reducing learning and
psychological burdens improves benefit take-­up. Their field ex-
periments with rental assistance programs show that providing
clear information and using stigma-­reducing messages signifi-
cantly increases application rates. Collectively, these studies
highlight that the presence of administrative burdens in welfare
programs greatly affects program take-­up.
While accessing welfare benefits and using them are distinct
processes, it is reasonable to expect, based on the administra-
tive burden and take-­up literature, that interpreting fees will
result in affected immigrants using general practitioner ser-
vices less frequently. This leads us to formulate the following
hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1.  Immigrants who are required to pay interpret-
ing fees will use general practitioner services less frequently.
4   |   Frontline Implementation of Interpreting Fees
The arguments above focus primarily on how immigrants react
to the implementation of the new rule regarding interpreting
fees. However, these fees are not automatically imposed on im-
migrants, but are instead administered by doctors. Therefore,
doctors serve as crucial intermediaries, making it essential to
discuss how they are implementing the rule in order to fully un-
derstand its potential consequences.
4.1   |   Frontline Employees and Administrative
Burdens
One of the key topics in the street-­level bureaucracy literature
is how frontline workers exercise their discretion in interactions
with citizens. According to Tummers and Bekkers (2014), dis-
cretion is “the perceived freedom of street-­level bureaucrats in
making choices concerning the sort, quantity, and quality of
sanctions, and rewards on offer when implementing a policy.”
The extent to which discretion is used to exacerbate or mitigate
experiences of administrative burdens may depend on whether
individuals act as “state agents” or “citizen agents” (Maynard-­
Moody  2003). State agents strictly follow state-­defined rules
and act in their own self-­interest, which can lead to practices
such as cream-­skimming, where frontline workers avoid serving

citizens who are difficult or unpleasant (Maynard-­Moody and
Musheno  2000). In contrast, citizen agents base their actions
on normative perceptions of citizens' needs. When acting as
citizen agents, frontline workers are willing to bypass rules, go
the extra mile, and sacrifice official success to help those they
perceive as needing help (Maynard-­Moody and Musheno 2000).
This concept is somewhat similar to Watkins-­Hayes' (2009) no-
tion of “social workers,” who use their discretion to advocate for
clients and provide comprehensive services. Conversely, state
agents resemble “efficiency engineers,” who prioritize quick and
efficient case processing.
Several empirical studies have examined how frontline
workers handle and perceive administrative burdens. Bell
et al. (2021) demonstrate that workers' interpretations of bur-
dens are influenced by their political ideologies. Conservative
frontline workers tend to support stringent administrative
policies, viewing them as essential for preventing fraud
and ensuring that only deserving clients receive benefits.
Conversely, liberal workers generally oppose such burdens,
arguing that they undermine social equity and restrict cli-
ent access. In another study, Bell and Smith  (2022) investi-
gate how state-­imposed administrative burdens are managed
by frontline workers. They find that frontline workers who
adopt a “student support official” role use their discretion-
ary power to help students overcome these burdens, thereby
enhancing program access. However, the impact of frontline
workers is often limited by the administrative capacities of
their agencies, as those serving larger numbers of eligible cli-
ents often lack the resources to mitigate burdens effectively.
Additionally, Bell and Meyer  (2024) reveal that reducing
frontline workers' workloads can improve minority students'
access to college financial aid programs in Oklahoma. Wiley
and Berry (2018) report that nonprofits take significant mea-
sures to absorb administrative burdens internally, making
interactions less cumbersome for citizens. Moreover, Halling
and Petersen (2024) find that Danish caseworkers respond to
citizens' communication of psychological costs by reducing
the demands placed on them, thus acting as “citizen agents.”
This supports Jilke and Tummers' (2018) finding that front-
line employees are more inclined to prioritize assistance for
citizens in need. Collectively, these studies highlight the
critical role of frontline workers in shaping the impact of ad-
ministrative burdens on citizens. They also illustrate that, in
general, frontline workers tend to act in ways that minimize
the burdensome experiences of citizens.
4.2   |   How Doctors Implement Interpreting Fees
To discuss the role of doctors in the implementation of inter-
preting fees, it is necessary to assess the extent of their dis-
cretion. On the one hand, the law explicitly requires doctors
to arrange for an interpreter without consulting the patient's
preference (PLO 2018). This is intended to limit doctors' dis-
cretion by providing clear guidelines. On the other hand, the
law also allows relatives or acquaintances to serve as inter-
preters in “uncomplicated cases.” This introduces a signifi-
cant degree of discretion for doctors, as they are responsible
for determining what qualifies as a complicated or uncompli-
cated case. As a result, doctors effectively become the primary
decision-­makers as to whether or not to use a professional
interpreter.
The next step is to explore the incentives doctors have for hir-
ing or not hiring a professional interpreter for consultations.
Doctors face a delicate situation when making this decision.
Based on their interviews with Danish doctors implement-
ing the policy, Davidsen et al. (2022) describe the dilemma as
follows:
Legally it was the GPs' responsibility to procure the services of
a professional interpreter when there were language problems.
The GPs, however, also felt a responsibility towards the patient
and did not feel that they could book an interpreter, which
the patient would have to pay for, without asking the patient
(Davidsen et al. 2022, 7).
On the one hand, doctors have the Hippocratic Oath and
their responsibility to provide the best possible treatment
for patients. Professional interpreters can play a crucial role
in achieving this. Studies show that language barriers can
affect both access to and quality of healthcare (van Rosse
et al. 2016; Clarke and Isphording 2017), and professional in-
terpreters are seen as the best solution for overcoming such
barriers (Karliner et  al.  2007; Flores  2005). In one survey,
Latino parents identified language issues as “the single great-
est barrier to health care access for their children” (Ku and
Flores 2005, 436). Other studies find that non-­native speak-
ers struggle to comply with treatment regimes (Gerrish 2001)
and are at higher risk of experiencing medical errors (Wilson
et  al.  2005). This all illustrates that there are good medical
reasons for hiring a professional interpreter.
On the other hand, as the aforementioned studies demonstrate,
many frontline workers, such as doctors, aim to help citizens by
reducing their administrative burdens. In this case, this would
mean permitting acquaintances or relatives to serve as inter-
preters or proceeding without an interpreter, despite the asso-
ciated risks previously discussed. This approach would relieve
immigrants of the administrative burdens associated with inter-
preter fees while still ensuring that they receive the necessary
medical care.
However, several other factors may also discourage doctors
from hiring professional interpreters. One key consideration is
the spontaneous nature of GP visits, which often occur on short
notice. In such cases, it is typically more convenient to rely on
an informal interpreter rather than navigate the administra-
tive hurdles of securing a professional one (Li, Pearson, and
Escott 2010; Davidsen et al. 2022). Additionally, there is no direct
financial incentive for doctors to use professional interpreting
services. Their role is limited to arranging the interpreter, while
the financial transactions, including fees and any potential reve-
nue, are solely between the patient and the regional authorities.
In fact, doctors may have a financial disincentive to hire a pro-
fessional interpreter, as doing so could discourage immigrants
from seeking future consultations. Since doctors are paid on a
“per visit basis,” a reduction in visits could negatively impact
their practice's income. Additionally, if immigrants avoid seek-
ing medical care, their health could suffer, providing a potential
medical rationale for not hiring a professional interpreter.

5   |   Differential Reactions to Interpreting Fees
It is highly likely that immigrants do not respond uniformly to
the implementation of interpreting fees. The idea that adminis-
trative burdens are distributive and can contribute to inequal-
ity is a central tenet of the administrative burden framework
(Christensen et  al.  2020; Herd and Moynihan  2019). The key
finding from research on this topic is that individuals from
marginalized or low-­resource groups generally struggle more
with state-­imposed barriers, leading to more experiences of
compliance, learning, and psychological costs (Halling and
Baekgaard  2024). For example, studies have shown that in-
dividuals with low self-­efficacy (Thomsen, Kjærgaard, and
Jensen 2020) or limited skills in interacting with public services
(Döring and Madsen 2022; Masood and Nisar 2021) experience
greater administrative burdens when dealing with the public
sector. Additionally, other research has demonstrated that ad-
ministrative burdens are unevenly distributed among different
population groups. For instance, individuals with lower lev-
els of education (Chudnovsky and Peeters  2021), those from
ethnic minority backgrounds (Olsen, Kyhse-­Andersen, and
Moynihan  2020), and people with lower incomes (Heinrich
et al. 2022) face more burdens in their interactions with public
services.
We consider three types of disadvantages that may affect immi-
grants' reactions to interpreting fees. First, individuals who are
temporarily out of work have, on average, lower incomes than
those in the labor market, which makes it more difficult to pay
the fee. In addition, participation in social assistance programs
often comes with significant compliance demands (Baekgaard,
Mikkelsen, et al. 2021). As individuals on social assistance must
navigate these requirements to maintain their benefits, they
have less time and resources to handle other compliance issues.
This can increase the burden of interpreting fees (Christensen
et al. 2020).
Second, nonWestern immigrants tend to have lower levels of
English proficiency than those from Western countries. This
often means that they need interpretation services more often.
As a result, on average, nonWestern immigrants may be more
affected by interpreting fees than their Western counterparts.
Third, asylum seekers are among the most vulnerable groups
in any society. A 2008 report indicated that nearly half of asy-
lum seekers in Denmark had been tortured before arriving.
The trauma of such experiences, combined with the challenges
of fleeing to a new country, often results in significant mental
health problems (Masmas et al. 2008). Research has shown that
individuals with mental health problems face greater adminis-
trative burdens (Bell et al. 2023). Consequently, asylum seekers
may experience a more pronounced impact from interpreting
fees compared to other groups.
Overall, it is expected that disadvantaged immigrants will be
more significantly affected by interpreting fees. This expectation
can be supported regardless of which of the first two hypoth-
eses is supported. If Hypothesis 1 is supported, disadvantaged
immigrants may experience a greater impact of fees than their
nondisadvantaged counterparts. Conversely, if Hypothesis 2 is
supported, disadvantaged immigrants may still experience sub-
stantial effects from interpreting fees, even if the impact of the
fees is not significant on average.
Hypothesis 3.  Interpreting fees will lead to a greater reduc-
tion in general practitioner service use among disadvantaged im-
migrants compared to non-­disadvantaged immigrants.
6   |   Data and Identification Strategy
The analysis is based on data from official population registers
covering all immigrants living in Denmark.1 The main source of
data is the National Health Insurance Service Registry (NHISR).
In the NHISR, it is possible to track whether all individuals vis-
ited their GP in a given week. We use this to construct an un-
balanced panel dataset that captures the number of times all
immigrants visited their GP, week by week, during 2015–2019.
The main independent variable is an indicator that captures
whether the immigrant has resided in Denmark for more or less
than 3 years in a given week. We measure the length of stay in
Denmark by subtracting the day of entry into Denmark from the
week of the medical visit.
6.1   |   Identification Strategy: The Regression
Discontinuity Design
We use a sharp regression discontinuity design (RDD) to ex-
amine the impact of interpreting fees on service usage. In the
RDD, a forcing variable divides subjects into a control and a
treatment group around a threshold, effectively mimicking a
randomized experiment in a small bandwidth on either side
of the threshold. In our application, the forcing variable is
the length of stay in Denmark, as those above the three-­year
threshold are required to pay for interpreting services (treat-
ment group), while those below the threshold are exempted
from payment (control group). We analyze this as a sharp RDD
because all immigrants below the threshold are exempt from
payment, while those above it are required to pay. This results
in an immediate change in treatment status precisely at the 3-­
year threshold for all individuals.
The key assumption in any RDD is that outcomes are continuous
at the threshold in the absence of an intervention. Here, this means
that medical visits are continuous at the three-­year threshold in
the absence of the new legislation. If this assumption holds, the
RDD yields unbiased estimates of the local average treatment ef-
fect. The assumption fails if subjects sort around the cut-­off and
thereby manipulate their treatment status. We assess whether ma-
nipulation occurs by conducting a density test, also known as the
McCrary (2008) test, using the Stata rddensity package (Cattaneo


## Page 7

1025
et al. 2018). For our main RDD specification, the null hypothesis
of no manipulation is not rejected, indicating that manipulation
is not present (T = −1.05, p = 0.29). Further, manipulation of treat-
ment status is difficult in this case. It would require immigrants
to change how long they have resided in Denmark. While it is
theoretically possible that immigrants could leave Denmark and
then return later, it is unlikely that immigrants would spend so
much time and effort to avoid paying a fee. Leaving and then re-
turning to Denmark is an onerous process that requires both time
and money, and for most immigrants, this will not measure up to
the small benefit of free access to interpreting services in health
encounters. In addition, as shown in Figure 1, there is no apparent
discontinuity in the length of stay of immigrants around the three-­
year threshold.
Another common issue with RDDs is low statistical power,
as the analysis focuses on a narrow window of observations
(Wing and Cook 2013). However, this is less of a concern in
our study due to the availability of highly detailed adminis-
trative records. For example, our main specification includes
data from 35,206 individuals and 1,339,397 weekly observa-
tions, which provides ample statistical power to detect policy-­
relevant effect sizes. Table A1 provides additional descriptive
statistics about the sample.

7.1   |   Results Over Time: Interrupted Time Series
Analysis

this effect. As a supplementary analysis, we therefore conduct
an interrupted time series (ITS) analysis. The ITS analysis is a

8   |   Concluding Discussion
This study adds to the literature on administrative burdens by
showing that policy designs involving direct monetary costs do
not necessarily affect citizens' regular use of welfare benefits.
Specifically, we find that the introduction of interpreting fees in
the Danish health healthcare system did not lead to a decrease
in general practitioner visits among immigrants affected by the
legislation. Furthermore, there is no evidence that the fees dis-
proportionately affected disadvantaged immigrants.
Before relating these findings to the literature on administra-
tive burdens, it is important to address their generalizability.
Fees for interpreting services represent a specific case, and there
are obviously certain limitations to the external validity of our
findings. A couple of different aspects of the policy is import-
ant to consider here. First, since interpreting is a supplementary
service rather than a primary service (healthcare), our results
may not generalize well to scenarios involving fees for primary
services. The literature on healthcare co-­payments, which has
consistently shown that such fees affect healthcare utilization,
supports this distinction (Kiil and Houlberg 2014). Fees or other
burdens associated with primary care services may discourage
individuals from seeking these services altogether. In contrast,
when burdens are imposed on supplementary services, indi-
viduals, frontline workers, or other actors may find ways to
circumvent these rules. In this study, frontline workers played
a critical role in mitigating the impact of interpreting fees, but
in other contexts it may be citizens themselves or other actors
who play this role. Therefore, our findings are most applicable
to situations where frontline workers have some discretion in
implementing burdensome policies and can use this discretion
to mitigate the impact on citizens.
Second, we examine the use of healthcare, a key welfare ser-
vice for most citizens. This focus is advantageous because the
results have significant implications for individuals' lives and
well-­being. However, it also presents a potential limitation, as
healthcare is arguably a less susceptible context for the effects of
fees than most other areas of welfare. Due to the essential nature
of healthcare services, individuals may be less likely to reduce
their utilization despite the imposition of fees. As a result, it is
plausible that a similar policy could have a more pronounced
negative effect on the utilization or uptake of other types of
welfare services that may not be as critical or indispensable as
healthcare.
Third, the policy we study specifically targets immigrants, which
narrows the focus of our findings. As a result, it is most appro-
priate to generalize these findings to immigrants and, by exten-
sion, potentially other disadvantaged social groups. Immigrants
often face unique barriers to accessing welfare services, such as
language difficulties and unfamiliarity with bureaucratic pro-
cesses, which may influence how they respond to policies such
as interpreting fees. The lack of a decrease in healthcare use
among immigrants suggests that these barriers were not exacer-
bated by the introduction of fees in this particular case.
We also acknowledge some limitations of our study. One lim-
itation is our inability to account for the ethnic match between
doctors and patients. Research on representative bureaucracy
suggests that public employees often seek to actively represent
and improve outcomes for citizens who share their ethnic back-
ground (Meier 2019). It is possible that doctors from ethnic mi-
nority backgrounds are more inclined to assist immigrants in
navigating interpreting fees than their majority counterparts.
Unfortunately, our data does not include information on the
ethnicity of the doctors, which prevents us from exploring this
potential dynamic.
Another limitation of our study is the lack of alternative out-
come measures. While healthcare usage is both significant
and relevant, it is not the only outcome that interpreting fees
may affect. As discussed earlier, inadequate interpretation can
reduce the quality of healthcare, potentially harming patients'
overall health. Therefore, it would be of great interest to assess
whether interpreting fees have a negative impact on immigrant
health. Additionally, the increased reliance on family members
for interpretation may have adverse effects on them as well. For
example, if children are used as interpreters, they might miss
school or work, leading to potential negative consequences for
their education or income.
In conclusion, by leveraging detailed administrative data to
track immigrants' behavioral responses on a weekly basis, our
study provides robust insights into the impact of administrative
burdens on the use of welfare benefits. While prior research
has consistently shown that administrative burdens can sig-
nificantly impede access to welfare programs (Fox et al. 2020;
Fox, Feng, and Reynolds 2023; Rauscher and Burns 2023), our
findings suggest that in the specific policy context we examine,
financial burdens did not lead to a reduction in welfare use.
Although various contextual factors may explain these results,
our study highlights the importance of not assuming that the
presence of administrative burdens necessarily reduces the use
of welfare services.
Our findings also underscore the critical role of frontline work-
ers, in this case, general practitioners, in mitigating the effects of
administrative burdens. The survey data suggests that doctors,
in tandem with immigrants, have found ways to minimize the
burdens associated with interpreting fees, thereby neutralizing
the anticipated negative effects. This aligns with previous stud-
ies that show that frontline workers actively work to minimize
citizens' experiences of administrative burdens (Halling and

ABSTRACT
Over the past three decades, the amount of quantitative data in general and performance indicators specifically within street-­
level organizations has increased immensely. Although quantitative data is often perceived as information about and for practice,
it must also be seen as part of practice itself. Through a bottom-­up approach to numbers using ethnographic data from Danish
public employment services targeting vulnerable clients, this article examines what happens when “numbers are put on the
table” in street-­level organizations, going beyond categories of “gaming” or “perverse effects.” This is done by introducing an
empirically derived typology of “reactions to numbers” that includes three domains: emotions, attitudes, and actions. The re-
search contributes to a better understanding of numbers' organizational lives and performance management systems seen from
a street-­level perspective.
In recent decades, the proliferation of quantitative data along
with accompanying demands for counting, measuring, compar-
ing, and evaluating social and work-­related phenomena, prac-
tices, and performances of organizations and individuals has
risen dramatically across most Western countries (Espeland
and Sauder  2007; Hoeyer  2023; Mau  2019; Rottenburg and
Merry 2015). The widespread introduction of performance man-
agement (PM) systems and the use of performance indicators
(PIs) within street-­level bureaucracy (SLB) organizations is part
of this megatrend (Soss et al. 2011).
Quantitative data comes with a promise (Hoeyer 2023) of pro-
viding neutral knowledge, evaluation, and feedback to and
about practice. However, such data has also become an integral
part of practice itself. As reflexive beings, SLB employees and
managers attribute meaning and respond in multitudes of ways
to the vast amount of numerical information presented to them
in graphs, spreadsheets, tables, and benchmarks.
The overall goal of this article is to present an empirically rich in-
vestigation of what happens in SLB organizations when “numbers
are put on the table”. Based on ethnographic data from Danish
public employment services (PES) targeting vulnerable clients, it
moves beyond concepts of perverse or unintended effects to argue
for a more nuanced and ambivalent story of what numbers do in
organizations. This is crucial, not least because numbers are a
pervasive and powerful language and a language of power, that
draws organizational attention and resources by merely existing.
While public administrators, producing the numbers, may be-
lieve, or hope, that the data is used intentionally for cool-­headed
feedback and learning about quality of work and performances, it
is often introduced into organizational dialogues and meetings in
a more haphazard fashion generating paradoxical (Hoeyer 2023)
and diverse forms of data-­coping rather than pure data-­learning.
Furthermore, the article not only explores and critiques
but also contributes with a reparative critique (Monrad and

1   |   Quantitative Data and Frontline Reactivity
Research on quantification has drawn attention to how numbers
are increasingly used “to describe social phenomena in countable
and commensurable terms” (Merry 2016, 1), and how processes
of quantification have become deeply integrated into SLB organi-
zations and society more broadly (Stone 2020). Quantitative data
proliferates through graphs, charts and benchmarks, making
us increasingly see the world through numbers (Hoeyer 2023).
Scholars have highlighted how this is connected to a ‘tsunami of
accountability and transparency’ (Espeland and Sauder 2007, 2)
resulting in an “audit society” (Power 1997), an “audit culture”
(Strathern 2000), or a “metric society” (Mau 2019).
These developments are seen as driven by both technological
and digital advancements as well as ideological and cultural
developments—particularly within public governance. Here,
they intersect with values of New Public Management (NPM)
and demands for evidence-­based practice, transparency, and
accountability (Desrosières  2015; Frost  2010; Hoeyer  2023;
Merry 2016).
Performance management (PM) in the public sector is part of
this broader quantitative megatrend. PM is broadly defined as ‘a
system that generates performance information through strate-
gic planning and performance measurement routines and that
connects this information to decision venues, where, ideally, the
information influences a range of possible decisions’ (Moynihan
2006 in Gassner et al. 2022, 108). In this ideal PI, always in the
shape of numbers, provide feedback for learning, improvement
and objective performance evaluation (Behn  2003; Jakobsen
et al. 2018). However, as many scholars have noted, PM systems
rarely work ideally in practice. Although quantitative data may
at first sight seem “appealing because they claim to stand above
politics” (Merry  2016, 3), they also “change the world simply
by existing” (Mau 2019, 340). They reshape agency itself (Soss
et al. 2011, i204), not least because SLB employees and man-
agers are reflexive beings (Espeland and Sauder 2007). Rather
than using quantitative data solely for feedback and learning,
SLB professionals construct meaning and react to the fact that
they are being measured and to their expectations of how other
people will use the data.
Moynihan et al. (2020) argue that PM systems often suffer from
a paradox: While the purpose of PM and PI is to improve the
quality and efficiency of work, such effects depend on frontline
workers, who generally express skepticism or dislike toward
such systems, perceiving them as both undermining autonomy
and providing unreliable, insufficient, and decontextualized in-
formation about work practices.
This literature on quantification and PM has primarily fo-
cused on broad developments in society, policy, ideology, and
governance. Research on PM and SLB-­organizations has typ-
ically focused on either administrators' and managers' use of
PIs and the implementation of PM or on so-­called unintended
consequences such as gaming, creaming, and perverse ef-
fects (Brodkin 2007; Gassner et al. 2022; Jakobsen et al. 2018;
Jakobsen and Mortensen  2016; Larsen  2013; Moynihan
et al. 2020; Soss et al. 2011; van Berkel 2017). However, less
attention has been paid to the perspective of frontline workers
and managers and to their day-­to-­day interactions with and
reactions to such quantitative data. Some exceptions include
Dahler-­Larsen (2014, 2016) who drawing on interviews with
frontline workers from Danish high schools, language schools,
and universities, argues that the concept of “unintended con-
sequences” rests on the false premise of clear-­cut intentions
behind indicators and measurements. He instead suggests
acknowledging the multiple and often conflicting intentions
behind indicators emerging from many different positions
(people, institutions, ideas) as well as the many and often con-
tradictory consequences for frontline workers and managers.
Though the intention behind many measurements probably is
to remove uncertainty, it is, he argues, an empirical fact that
they often provoke uncertainty, debate, critique, ambiguity,
and affective reactions ranging from uneasiness to pride.

the Israeli police are not per se against PM systems but react

Thus, this article presents an exploratory study that seeks to
answer the following question: How do SLB frontline workers
react to and engage with quantitative data in their daily work
practices, and what can we learn about reactivity to quantifica-

1.	 The object of the study: It adopts a bottom-­up perspective,
starting from SLB employees' experiences and interpreta-

of street-­level organizations, where policies and govern-
ance are transformed into practice. As SLB research has
shown, policymaking is multilayered (Lipsky 2010; Hupe
et al. 2015; Zacka 2017). The same can be said for quanti-
tative data: numbers also take on a life of their own within
organizational layers as professionals and managers apply
various coping strategies to different types of data in differ-
ent contexts.
2.	 The methodology of the study: Most existing research on
SLB professionals' responses to PM systems and quantita-
tive indicators has relied on interviews, surveys, or doc-

and Sauder  2007; Jakobsen et  al.  2018; Larsen  2013;
Petersen 2020; Soss et al. 2011). This article takes us deeper
into organizational life by exploring SLB employees' and
managers' immediate reactions to quantitative data as it is
presented in organizational meetings, dialogues, emails,
and other settings. Through longitudinal ethnographic
fieldwork approach, it opens the “black box” of SLB organi-
zations (Brodkin 2017; Hupe et al. 2015) and captures first-
hand data engagements (Hoeyer 2023, 120), shedding light
on organizational dynamics when numbers are “put on the
table”. In short, quantitative data is studied qualitatively.
3.	 Theory development: Moving beyond the dominant con-
cepts of learning, gaming or unintended consequences,
the article presents an empirically derived typology of
“domains of reactions to numbers” encompassing issues of
emotions, attitudes, and actions. This typology is substan-
tiated and illustrated through rich ethnographic material.
2   |   Introducing the Setting: PES That Count
Since the mid-­1990s, activation policies targeting unemployed
citizens have become central in most Western countries
(Andersen and Larsen  2024; van Berkel  2017). These policies
have since expanded to include citizens with complex health,
social, or substance abuse issues. This focus on activation and
work rehabilitation has developed in parallel with the values
and principles of governance from NPM. It entails demands for
documenting both the effects and efficiency of various activation
policies and instruments, as well as documenting adherence to
process regulations (van Berkel 2017; Gilbert 2001).
The Danish PES has been chosen as an extreme case (Yin 2018)
of this trend toward quantification and PM, boasting one of the
most comprehensive and robust datasets globally (Andersen and
Larsen 2018). This is partly due to Denmark's Civil Registration
System, which tracks all citizens over time across interventions
and partly due to the governance of the Danish PES sector since
the mid-­2000s through what has been labeled “decentral cen-
tralization” (Caswell et al. 2024; Caswell and Larsen 2017). In
this model, local municipal job centers were given full responsi-
bility for implementing activation policies but were also tightly
constrained by numerous regulations dictating how those pol-
icies were to be implemented (Nielsen et  al.  2020). Frontline
workers in the PES have therefore been required to register and
document many of their daily practices, such as dialogues and
meetings with clients, and the referral of clients to various ser-
vices, job-­qualifying internships, and medical assessments. In
other words, frontline workers produce the very data that is later
used to evaluate their performance.
The Danish Ministry of Employment meticulously collects and
analyzes this data, transforming it into graphs, spreadsheets,
rankings, and benchmarks that are both publicly accessible and
regularly sent to municipalities. This data is not made public
solely for naming and shaming; it also carries implications for
financial reimbursements from the state to the municipalities
(Andersen 2020; Nielsen et al. 2020) and for the risk of being
placed under state administration. Both job centers and unem-
ployed clients may face sanctions for non-­compliance with leg-
islative demands. Scholars describe this governance structure as
marked by ‘triple mistrust’: mistrust in municipal job centers,
frontline workers, and clients (Caswell et al. 2024). This concept
of mistrust is critical when seeking to understand reactions to
extensive quantitative data, especially since the data is required,
analyzed, and used by the same state agencies perceived as
‘mistrusting’ from a SLB perspective. However, it is worth not-
ing that while clients and job centers can be sanctioned, indi-
vidual case workers are not penalized—neither sacked nor pay
docked—for failing to meet benchmarks. Moreover, although
the state threatens underperforming municipalities with state
administration, this has rarely been enforced. Thus, the Danish
PES case presents a paradox: on the one hand, performance data,
rankings, and other forms of measurement attract significant

3   |   Data, Methodology and Analytical Approach
The data for this analysis stems from ethnographic fieldwork
conducted as part of a qualitative study on reflective practice in
Danish PES from 2017 to 2022 (Kongsgaard 2022, 2024). I pre-
viously had 15 years of practical experience working first as a
PES frontline worker for two years, followed by several years as
a team manager, senior manager, and educator in a not-­for-­profit
service provider working for Danish job centers. As an educator
and supervisor, I worked closely with many SLB managers and
professionals, supporting them in navigating the many dilem-
mas of their work. The task of integrating vulnerable individuals
into the labor market is complex and fraught with challenges.
Like other forms of SLB work, it involves balancing demands
and resources (Lipsky  2010), navigating moral dilemmas
(Zacka 2017), and handling conflicting policy and administra-
tive logics (Caswell and Larsen 2017; Nielsen and Monrad 2023).
Moreover, clients' problems are often complex and intercon-
nected, and thus must be reconstructed through discretion
rather than treated as objective facts (Caswell and Dall 2022).
These processes of balancing and case formation take place
within collegial reflective practices. However, I found that such
practices were not well studied academically. I therefore set out
to examine them empirically and to write about organizational
reflective practice and dilemma coping in a doctoral thesis.
In this exploration of inter-­collegial reflexivity, I became, as
Douglas-­Jones et al. (2021, 12) puts it an accidental ethnographer
of data, stumbling upon (Brinkmann 2014) the organizational
pervasiveness of all sorts of quantitative data and allowing myself
to be surprised and puzzled by (Hulst and Visser 2025) by these
phenomena. Measurements, benchmarks, goals, performance
indicators, sheer enumerations and similar metrics consistently
appeared in meetings, presentations, emails, daily dialogues,
and even media coverage. Numbers were seemingly everywhere.
Even early in the morning, when a frontline worker turned on
the computer, she/he was greeted by the interface with a “Well
done, you have conducted xx client interviews this month, keep
up the good work”. Many SLB professionals and managers ex-
pressed feeling overwhelmed or hijacked by the volume of data
flowing into the organization both intentionally and inciden-
tally. I recognized these feelings from my own experience as a
frontline worker and manager. In my research, I showed how the
use of quantitative data often obstructed learning and displaced
reflections about quality of work with reflections about how to
cope with data and with other's interpretations of it.
Although quantitative data was initially only one of several
topics related to reflective practice in my research, I have since
become increasingly interested in understanding the organiza-
tional life of such data in SLB settings. I continue to observe that
managers and frontline workers frequently complain about the
growing volume of data. They are typically not opposed to quan-
titative data per se, but struggle to make sense of what the num-
bers mean, why particular data arrives at a given moment, and
whether the data can be trusted. They know that quantitative
data does not simply convey “truth,” but they are also acutely
aware that it has real-­world implications. Here I align with
Douglas-­Jones et al. (2021, 9), who observe that “Anthropologists
find themselves needing to make sense of ‘data’—and not only
their own—as an emergent ethnographic object.”
The ethnographic data was collected through 5 months of organi-
zational fieldwork (Ybema et al. 2009) in a Danish job center. The
job center was selected as a “typical” medium-­sized Danish job
center, organized like many others and grappling with the same
dilemmas. I drew on my practical background and on discussions
with practitioners from other job centers in labeling it as typical.
At the site, I followed approximately 35 SLB frontline workers
and managers across three departments in an open-­plan office,
all tasked with helping unemployed clients with complex health
and/or social issues return to work or education and conducting
employability assessments. Due to the complexity of their cases,
all employees were skilled in providing integrated services and
conducting interviews, coaching, and counseling for clients fac-
ing a broad range of physical, social, and mental challenges. Their
daily tasks included meetings with clients, phone calls, docu-
menting progress and interventions, referring clients to job search
courses, internships, reskilling programs, medical assessments,
psychologists, and training. They also wrote assessments and
made determinations about employability, motivation, client cat-
egorization, and eligibility for state benefits. In addition to legally
required documentation, the job center management imposed ad-
ditional mandatory registrations, such as the number of company
dialogues, the number of clients hired through employer requests,
and more. This data was also collected and used—sometimes in-
tentionally, sometimes not—by management.
As is typical in Danish PES, the frontline workers came from di-
verse professional backgrounds, including social work, pedagogy,
occupational therapy, and entrepreneurship. I followed both
managers and frontline workers and participated in as many for-
mal and informal meetings as possible across the organization.
Fieldnotes were taken during observations of daily routines, par-
ticipation in around 100 formal and informal collegial meetings,
30 client interviews, and 80 formal and informal semi-­structured
interviews (Kvale and Brinkmann 2015) with SLB profession-
als and managers. Only 13 interviews were audio recorded and
transcribed. The organization did not permit audio recording of
meetings. Thus, any deliberations cited in this article should not
be seen as verbatim transcripts but as reconstructed interactions
based on detailed notes. All conversations occurred in Danish,
and all translations were made by me. I also collected notes from
reviewing approximately 700 pages of documents, including cli-
ent assessments, daily emails, meeting agendas, and minutes. In
total, I produced 280 pages of fieldnotes.
Many interviews took place as informal conversations during
lunch, breaks, walks, and car rides. These were semi-­structured

been slightly altered to ensure anonymity.
For this article, I revisited and re-­coded the ethnographic data
related to SLB professionals' reactions to and interactions with
quantitative data. Here, “reactions” are broadly defined as ac-
tions, thoughts, or emotions expressed by professionals and man-
agers shortly after encountering quantitative data—either during
its presentation or in the following days. Naturally, I cannot assert
with positivistic certainty that these reactions were solely caused
by the presentation of data, as they may also relate to other orga-
nizational processes, some of which may not be visible to either
observer or participant. I therefore apply a pragmatic approach
to the concept of “reaction,” labeling various actions, emotional
expressions, and thoughts as reactions to numbers when infor-
mants themselves made that connection, or when they explicitly
linked them to a concrete instance where numbers were “put on
the table.” Other concepts—such as process, dynamic, or interac-
tion pattern—could also have been appropriate.
Furthermore, the domains of “emotions” and “analytical
stance” also require comment. Obviously, as an observer, I had
only access to expressions, not to internal emotions. I therefore
discussed interpretations with informants in informal follow-­up
interviews and in-­depth interviews later on, validating my un-
derstanding with them. The goal was not to identify specific
emotions with precision but to label responses that appeared
more emotional than analytical. “Taking an analytical stance,”
by contrast, refers to cognitive engagement with the data. In
practice, emotional and analytical responses often overlap and
are not always easy to distinguish.
Based on this data, an empirically driven typology of reactions to
quantitative data was developed through an iterative (Hammersley
and Atkinson 2007) and abductive process following Tavory and
Timmermans' (2014, 35) definition of abduction as an “inferential
procedure through which people in their everyday life as well as
social-­scientists-­at-­work produce novel generalizations about the
world they live in.” Part of an abductive strategy is to test and
discuss hypothesis, analysis and categories with communities of
inquiry (ibid.) of both the research community and practice com-
munity. Using my cultural capital as an experienced practitioner, I
did both. This process involved the following steps:
1.	 Reviewing original fieldnotes to identify situations where
quantitative data was presented to SLB professionals.
2.	 Labeling different types of reactions both observed in
meetings and afterward, resulting in 17 initial empirically
grounded categories.
3.	 Refining these categories through discussions with PES
professionals as co-­analysts (Down and Hughes 2009) and
fellow researchers, and creatively (Hulst and Visser 2025)
integrating insights from relevant theories and research
the social implications of quantification, PM systems, and
emotions in organizations, and so forth.
4.	 Re-­coding and consolidating the categories into 11 distinct
reactions grouped into three domains: emotional, analyti-
cal, and action-­based (see Table 1).
These categories are neither exhaustive nor mutually exclusive.
Some excerpts could be interpreted in multiple ways, and some
cases exhibit more than one reaction (e.g., both joy and anxiety). In
the table, reactions are ordered by their relative frequency within
each domain (e.g., refutation appeared more frequently than
learning). While exact frequency cannot be determined in a quali-
tative study, the ordering is based on actual counts from fieldnotes
and verified through follow-­up dialogues with participants. The
goal is not to quantify reactions precisely or assign them rigidly
to fixed categories, but rather to offer an ‘orienting heuristic, an
animating set of concepts […] to help scholars identify similarities
and differences’ in actors' responses to quantitative data (Espeland
and Sauder 2007, 33), grounded in relevant theories and analytical
deliberations with both researchers and practitioners. Therefore,
the findings are illustrated through relatively lengthy empirical ex-
cerpts. On the one hand, this helps the reader understand what oc-
curred and why a particular label was applied; on the other hand,
it also invites the reader's own interpretations and categorizations.
4   |   Findings: Attracted by Numbers
A major finding during fieldwork—and the impetus for this
article—was that numerical data acted like a magnet, draw-
ing attention whenever presented. Meeting agendas or collegial
case deliberations were often hijacked once numbers were in-
troduced. Rather than continuing the discussion of a specific
dilemma, case, or theme and using numbers as just one infor-
mational input, deliberations began to orbit around those num-
bers: how to interpret them, what they meant, who might use
them, and how to respond. This is precisely what happened in
Excerpt 1. Once the numbers were “put on the table,” the re-
mainder of the meeting revolved around this data—specifically,
the percentage of clients in on-­the-­job training—without return-
ing to the broader question of how to help more clients into jobs
or revisiting earlier frontline experiences.

5   |   Expressing Emotions
Emotions are part of organizational life (Clough 2008; Swan and
Bailey  2004). Given the widespread attribution of quantitative
data as truth-­tellers with real consequences concerning, for in-
stance, resource allocation, quality assessments, and rankings,
numbers connect intimately to SLB professionals' emotions
(Dahler-­Larsen  2016; Hoeyer  2023, 121). In the research data,
emotional responses to the presentation of quantitative data were
abundant. Skipping detailed conceptual refinements, emotions
are here broadly defined—based on informants' own descrip-
tions—as subjective, instinctive, or intuitive feelings distinct
from reasoning or knowledge in reaction to a person, object, or
situation (Oxford Languages and Merriam-­Webster). I make no
claim to know what the individuals “really” felt—only how they
expressed their emotions in the context of encountering numbers.
These emotional expressions were categorized into four groups:
5.1   |   Anxiety
Anxiety was the most prevalent emotional reaction to quantitative
data. These responses—among both frontline workers and man-
agers—were primarily linked to concerns about how others (espe-
cially higher-­level managers, senior officials, or politicians) might
interpret and act upon the data. Managers often spoke of the “risk
of being put under administration” by the Ministry of Employment
due to non-­compliance with process regulations—a real concern
(Beskæftigelsesministeriet 2018). The leading manager of this job
center had, just 6 months before fieldwork, been summoned for a
personal meeting with the minister following poor performance
on interview frequency with clients on sickness benefits.
Frontline workers also frequently expressed anxiety. For exam-
ple, when the number of unemployed clients with immigrant
backgrounds dropped significantly, this might have appeared as
a positive sign of successful integration. Yet it provoked anxiety
among staff, who feared layoffs or reassignment (as fewer clients
could result in a reduced need for staff). The following excerpt
2 from a team meeting of professionals working with sickness
benefit clients illustrates the ambivalence often present in re-
sponses to numbers, where both positive emotions (such as joy)
and anxiety were experienced simultaneously.
This excerpt is one of many where initial joy quickly gave way to
anxiety over how performance data might be misunderstood—
even when the numbers were positive.

Despair was another common reaction, typically manifesting as
a sense of futility, absurdity, or powerlessness in relation to data
that felt unchangeable. The following excerpt 3 from a manage-

5.3   |   Joy
Joy followed by collegial recognition and celebration consti-
tutes the third emotional reaction. It is well documented how
teams celebrate with pizza, champagne, and expressions of grat-
itude (Espeland 2015) or cake and candy, as is often the case in
Denmark, when reaching targets or achieving favorable rankings.
This is exactly what Excerpt 2 above illustrates: team members ini-
tially applauded and expressed joy at their good performance. The
same slide was shown a week later to the entire job center, again
met with applause and framed as a team success.
While most expressions of joy were sparked by individual client
success stories, a significant share stemmed directly from quan-
titative data showing strong departmental or organizational per-
formance Excerpt (3).
However, sometimes celebrations can be ambiguous. In one no-
table  2019 case, a Danish job center promised workers in the
sickness benefits department a free ice cream bar if they reduced
the number of clients by a set deadline (Boysen 2019; Færch and
Møller 2019). The story went public, and both the manager and
city mayor apologized. The criticism was not about celebrating
performance per se, but about celebrating ambiguous results. The
goal—reducing the number of sickness benefit recipients—could
be achieved either through successful return-­to-­work outcomes
or by reclassifying clients into another benefit category. The job
center was widely criticized for incentivizing actions that could
compromise due process.
As Dahler-­Larsen (2022) notes in his analysis of a Danish high
school, even professionals who oppose rankings and perfor-
mance indicators may still find it difficult to resist celebrations
when “the numbers are good”—paradoxically reinforcing the
importance of numbers.
5.4   |   Motivation
Motivation (and even pride) was the fourth, and least common
emotional reaction. A minority of professionals reported feeling
inspired by performance data. When asked about their motiva-
tion, several mentioned the satisfaction of helping the munic-
ipality save money by getting more clients into jobs As one of
the frontline workers said, “Numbers don't lie. I think these
rankings are important.” Another said: “It's natural to want to
know how we're doing. I wish we had more specific numbers
showing the difference we all feel we're making every day.” This
suggests that SLB professionals are not uniformly skeptical of
quantitative data—numbers can also fuel pride and purpose
(Dahler-­Larsen 2016).
In summary, SLB professionals often react emotionally to the
presentation of quantitative data. Instances of anxiety and de-
spair far outnumber those of joy and motivation. While the for-
mer may be considered “negative” and the latter “positive,” such
labeling depends on perspective. In some motivational theories,
anxiety may even be considered productive.
However, from the point of view of frontline workers and
managers—and from a job satisfaction or learning perspec-
tive—anxiety and despair should be categorized as negative
emotions (see also Behn 2003, 592, on how excessive data can
inhibit learning). Moreover, only one of the four reactions—mo-
tivation—focused attention on service quality. The other three
diverted attention: either toward managing negative emotions
or toward superficial celebration of “good numbers” without
deeper inquiry into what they might actually say about quality
(Behn 2003).
6   |   Taking an Analytical Stance
The second domain of reactions includes instances where
SLB professionals adopt an analytical stance toward the data
presented. In these cases, they cognitively engage with the
data—either to refute it, avoid it, or learn from it. This category
is inspired by literature such as Gassner et al. (2022), who dis-
tinguish between defensive explanations and productive sug-
gestions as responses to PM systems, and research on enabling
and learning outcomes associated with PIs in Petersen (2020),
Jakobsen et al. (2018), and Raaphorst (2024).

6.1   |   Refutation
By far the most common analytical stance among both frontline
workers and managers was to challenge or refute the numbers
presented. They did this by referring to contextual conditions not
captured by the data, expressing mistrust in how the data had
been collected, or mistrusting the agencies producing it. These re-
actions can be interpreted both as challenges to the validity of the
data and as defensive mechanisms for avoiding its implications.
Excerpt 1 illustrates this: caseworkers explained the team's poor
performance (16% vs. 32%) by pointing to contextual factors—
for example, clients awaiting further clarification (Caseworker
A), or clients who were harder to place (Caseworker B). Later,
Caseworker A questioned the data's reliability by expressing
doubt in the agency producing the figures.
The following excerpt 4 shows another example of refuting
quantitative data through contextualization. In a team meeting,
the team, responsible for assisting unemployed immigrants,
discusses a recently published ranking of “integration perfor-
mance”, comparing municipalities on indicators such as employ-
ment rates, education levels, and Danish language proficiency.
Initially, Caseworker A expresses a desire to learn (which we re-
turn to later), but the others quickly shift the focus to explaining
away the difference: company size, sample size, and client mo-
tivation. The goal here is not to assess the accuracy of these ex-
planations or whether the ranking accounts for these variables,
but to highlight a typical pattern: the numbers are refuted rather
than accepted as meaningful feedback; often starting with the
phrase “but isn't it just because….”
A frequently expressed form of refutation came from managers
who voiced mistrust toward numbers produced by STAR. The
municipality had its own internal analytics team generating
performance data, which often differed from STAR's numbers—
sometimes due to differences in methodology, and sometimes
inexplicably (see also Gassner et al. 2022, 114; Soss et al. 2011).
Though STAR's data had to be taken seriously, managers pri-
vately admitted to both frontline workers and to me that they did
not trust the numbers as accurate reflections of practice. In this
way, quantitative data was paradoxical (Hoeyer 2023): simulta-
neously to be taken seriously and to be suspected.
These forms of refutation are consistent with findings in Gassner
et al. (2022), who classify such responses as context-­related and
PM-­system design–related defensive explanations. Similarly,
Dahler-­Larsen (2016) and Abildgaard and Nickelsen (2016) show
how data intended to promote certainty instead often generate
critique, defensive narratives, and debate. While administrators
might hope that “putting numbers on the table” would settle dis-
putes, the opposite often occurs—numbers spark new discussions.
6.2   |   Avoidance
Instead of criticizing the data, some reactions took the form of
active avoidance—seeking to deflect attention from the data
altogether. Such a stance of avoidance is seen in the following
excerpt 5, where a manager in an interview expressed a widely
held sentiment regarding municipal performance rankings from
the Ministry of Employment.
6.3   |   Learning
Numbers occasionally sparked curiosity and a desire to learn.
As seen in Excerpt 4, Caseworker A proposes contacting a
better-­performing municipality to learn from it. Many frontline
workers and managers also requested more localized data. One
team even created a “secret” spreadsheet, well known inter-
nally, but hidden from higher-­ups due to concerns over potential
misuse. The team, including its manager, used the data to track
internal metrics like workload and client diagnoses, with the
goal of gaining a clearer overview and learning from practice.
This same spirit of inquiry appears in the following example
Excerpt (6).

7   |   Acting
The third domain constitutes instances of street-­level bureau-
crats and managers acting to do something about either prac-
tice or the numbers themselves. This section draws on literature
about gaming and perverse effects (Brodkin  2007; Dahler-­
Larsen 2014; Soss et al. 2011) as well as on Espeland's (2015)
work on narrative reactions to numbers and performance re-
gimes. I here differentiate between four types of actions: (a)
actions to show that something is done, (b) actions to improve
practice, (c) actions to make the numbers look better (gaming),
and (d) actions to control the narrative of the numbers.
7.1   |   Actions to Show Action
A frequently occurring reaction, typically seen from managers,
was to call for immediate action, without further reflecting on
what the numbers might mean. Something had to be done, not
least to show that something would be done. The following case
illustrates this.
Admittedly, this excerpt (7) could be interpreted as an exam-
ple of an “action to make practice better”. However, I primarily
interpret it as one of many similar instances where “bad per-
formance numbers” were followed immediately by a meeting
and an actionable plan to “at least do something” to show that
something was done. This interpretation is supported firstly by
the speed with which the manager reacted. Instead of exploring
the numbers and possible explanations, dilemmas and solutions,
he spent his weekend setting up new procedures. Secondly, he
told me, as seen in the excerpt but also on several other occa-

vein, managers on several occasions, typically at management
meetings, produced new goals (in the shape of numbers) and
new action plans of how to reach the goals without truly believ-
ing in the value of such goals and plans. “We do it because it
makes us look more competent,” as one of them told me.

Of course, many actions were also meant to actually improve
practice. Excerpt 6 can be seen as one of many instances of how
quantitative data were taken as valid information about prac-
tice, reflected on, and used as impetus for further investigation
and instigation of changes to improve practice. In these cases,
the data was not refuted nor was it the aim to “just do some-
thing”. Excerpt  8 contains another typical example of using
numbers to improve quality of work. In this case a former de-

7.3   |   Gaming the Numbers
Many actions were aimed not at improving practice, but at im-
proving the numbers themselves. Such gaming strategies can be
interpreted both as a way of cheating and as a way of coping with
the inherent gap between demands and resources (Lipsky 2010).
It is well known (personal experience and dialogues with job
center managers) how Danish job centers for instance conduct
information meetings for groups of client but then register the
meetings as individual interviews to meet demands of individ-
ual interview frequency, or how they contract with large com-
panies to place several clients at a time in on-­the-­job training
to meet performance indicators about ‘on-­the-­job training’ but
without bringing clients into real jobs. Such practices have even
been labeled “on-­the-­job training factories” by STAR itself (see
for instance Rambøl and Metrica 2018, 12).
The following excerpt (9) from the data illustrates such a dialogue
about getting the numbers right. The meeting has been sum-
moned because this specific team was lagging behind on regis-
tered client interviews. The excerpt begins after the manager has
asked what they can each do to better the registered frequency.
I take the case as an instance of negotiating the extent of gaming al-
lowed. Case worker B insists that they should meet process require-
ments suggesting that ‘there is always something to talk about’,
whereas case worker A insists on his right to do discretion and de-
cide when to talk with whom. Case worker C suggests a more prag-
matic approach of meeting demands by registering phone calls as
interviews which is accepted by the manager. In this way the num-
bers are gamed, not for personal gain (as I read it) but to cope with
the gap between demands and resources. The research data shows
many examples of gaming by changing registration practices. In
one instance, the front-­line workers were even asked indirectly to
stop categorizing too many clients into a specific client category to
meet the performance targets of a specific project aiming at reduc-
ing the number of clients in this specific category.

8   |   Conclusion and Discussion
“The existential question remains: ‘Can a measurement
of human affairs be completely independent from the
way it is used?’” (Desrosières 2015, 342)
Rephrasing Groucho Marx's famous line about sex, I think quan-
titative data is here to stay. Technological advancements, per-
vasive data promises and enduring demands for holding SLB
organizations accountable for outcomes, efficiency and doing
‘what works’ will most likely increase the prevalence of num-
bers presented in beautifully designed colorful graphs, charts,
and tables in the foreseeable future. As reflexive beings, frontline
workers and managers will just as likely continue to react to and
interact with this data.
The findings in this article demonstrate first and foremost that
quantitative data takes on a life of its own in SLB organizations.
While numbers may sometimes be regarded by both administra-
tors and frontline workers as neutral information and account-
ability devices, they also get attributed with socially constructed
meaning. Whereas previous research has primarily focused on
the use of performance indicators on a governance and man-
agement level, this article adopts a frontline perspective show-
ing how frontline workers (and managers) interact with, ascribe
meaning to, and cope with the multitude of numbers presented
to them sometimes deliberately and sometimes incidentally.
Furthermore, the findings expand and nuance our understand-
ing of so-­called unintended consequences. Much of the previous
literature has focused on concepts such as gaming or perverse
effects. In this article, we get a deeper insight into organizational
life and the many kinds of reactivity to numbers both emotion-
ally, analytically, and practically. This contribution is both em-
pirical and theoretical. The theoretical contribution consists of
a suggested typology ordered around three domains of reactions
to numbers illustrating how such reactions manifest in practice.
This typology may serve as a comparative tool and a sensitizing
concept (Blumer  1954) for studying local organizational con-
texts across professions and disciplines. It may also be refined
and adapted for local analytical purposes when approaching
quantitative data qualitatively (Raadschelders  2011). In line
with the article's reparative ambition, the typology can also be
used (as has already been tested) in workshops with managers
and SLB professionals to enhance data literacy (Mau 2019) and
encourage local dialogues about how to use, produce, request,
and interpret data.
There are several implications of this understanding of reactiv-
ity to numbers. First, it helps both researchers and practitioners
acknowledge that numbers are not ‘cold facts’ about practice,
but part of practice itself that takes on a life of its own once
generated. Thus, more data does not necessarily translate to
more learning or better services; it may instead result in more
resources spent on frontline data-­coping, which may, contrary
to what was intended, have negative implications for job satis-
faction, motivation, and quality of services.
Secondly, this raises the question of which data to generate and
ask for. We need to, as Hoeyer (2023) has put it, make better use
of data instead of just asking for more data. Data producers and
users must ideally engage in dialogues about which data to gener-
ate, for what purposes (c.f. Behn 2003), and how to use and inter-
pret it alongside other data sources. I here concur with Jakobsen
et al. (2018, 25) suggesting dialogue as the relevant metaphor for
developing locally relevant performance indicators that can de-
liver on both external accountability and internal learning and
motivation, thus contributing to an internal learning regime.

meaningfully. Particularly, managers must develop awareness

Thirdly, we must consider better and worse ways of sharing and

ings in this article, “raw numbers” presented without context
appear to be the most problematic, often triggering significant
analytical, emotional, and practical coping. In many cases, data
was “delivered” without interpretation and without inviting
frontline workers into the interpretive process. As shown, quan-
titative data does not speak for itself; it is riddled with ambiguity
and open to multiple interpretations. A more fruitful approach
would be to create reflexive arenas of interpretation. Managers
and data producers could offer preliminary, qualitative inter-
pretations alongside raw figures—whether in emails, presenta-
tions, or frontline meetings. These should not be positioned as
definitive “truths,” but as invitations to dialogue. Ideally, this
would enhance data engagement, learning, and quality of work.
Finally, it may be helpful to shift attention from the numbers
themselves to the people communicating them. Drawing on
rhetorical theory, particularly Aristotle's concept of ethos, the
credibility of the communicator plays a key role (Gabrielsen and
Christiansen 2010). Ethos depends on the communicator's per-
ceived wisdom, competence, and empathy. Accordingly, atten-
tion should be given to how managers, administrators, scholars,
and peers communicate quantitative data, and how these com-
municators of data are perceived by the recipients. I find some
indications in the research data that SLB professionals react dif-
ferently depending on who presents the data (see also Behn 2003;
Moynihan et al. 2020; Petersen 2020), thereby linking reactions
to numbers to relations of trust, faith, and psychological safety
(Destler 2017; Porter in Abildgaard and Nickelsen 2016, 159).
The research suffers from some limitations that can also set
the direction for further research. First of all, it is a single case
study. While the study integrates theory and empirical findings
from other SLB organizations, comparative studies are needed
to explore how quantitative data is interacted with in different
professional settings, and which variables such as occupational,
institutional, management style, governance regime, and work-
ing environment might help explain similarities and differences.
Secondly, we need to know more about different kinds of quan-
titative data. For instance, do professionals react differently to
externally imposed data versus data co-­created within the orga-
nization (as suggested by Jakobsen et al. 2018)? How do reactions
vary between historical data and real-­time data? Can some of
the frustration and ambivalence around quantitative data be at-
tributed to the fact that data are old? Will this change if SLB or-
ganizations in the future get access to real-­time data either due to
technology (e.g., AI) or due to manual ways of collecting data lo-
cally, making data more meaningful for the professionals? These
are questions of distance—temporal, spatial, and positional.
Third, the ways quantitative data are presented compared to
other kinds of data deserve attention. It can be argued that quan-
titative data has an aesthetic advantage, which translates into a
rhetorical advantage (Hoeyer 2023, 120; Latour in Abildgaard
and Nickelsen 2016, 160), in so far as it can travel across settings
in well-­designed, colorful, and creative charts, where results and
overview can be presented literally in a snapshot supported by
digital software. Qualitative data still travel primarily through
narratives or more comprehensive presentations lacking the
smoothness of quantitative data. The article has only touched
very briefly on this question.
Four, though reactions were followed over time, the article still
zooms in on immediate thoughts, emotions, and actions, but
does not follow long-­term implications of data use on issues such
as organizational efficiency, work quality, and motivation.
Finally, there is the question of AI technologies, which was not
an issue at the time of data collection but is now. The use of AI
technologies may both add to the claim that we live in an age
of quantification by producing increasingly more numbers and
bring about new ways of gathering, analyzing, and presenting
all kinds of data. Will this development alienate SLB profession-
als further from the data, or will it bring about new ways of con-
necting with the data?
To conclude, in an era of intensive data sourcing and quantifica-
tion, we need to advance our understanding of how quantitative
data intervenes in SLB organizations and how data coping is
done. While SLB professionals often feel overwhelmed and out-
numbered by the volume and potential implications of quantita-
tive data, it is not inherently negative. Numbers are also valuable
tools for feedback and learning. One way or the other, it seems
that numbers count in many ways.

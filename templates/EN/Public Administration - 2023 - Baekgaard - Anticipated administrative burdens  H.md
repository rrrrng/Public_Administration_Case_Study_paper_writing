Abstract
Administrative burden research claims that target group
members are likely to experience learning, compliance, and
psychological costs when interacting with government pro-
grams. We argue that the mere anticipation of such interac-
tions may translate into experiences of administrative
burden. Utilizing a large-scale dataset with responses from
2276 Danish social benefit recipients, we estimate how
proximity to upcoming compulsory meetings with street-
level bureaucrats—a common condition in means-tested
benefit programs—affect the recipients' experiences of bur-
dens. We find that the shorter the time to future meetings,
the more benefit recipients experience stress and stigma,
but the less they experience learning costs. The findings
suggest that welfare recipient experiences of burden are
likely to fluctuate over time and that psychological costs
increase as recipients have to make mental and practical
preparations for complying with government demands.

INTRODUCTION
State-enacted compliance demands imposed on participants in means-tested programs, such as compulsory registra-
tion tasks and meeting attendance requirements, have a range of behavioral consequences that potentially hamper
policy success (Bhargava & Manoli, 2015; Daigneault & Macé, 2020; Fox et al., 2020, 2023; Heinrich, 2016;
Moynihan et al., 2015). Scholars of administrative burden have argued that such behavioral changes are likely
explained by the various costs that target group members experience during onerous bureaucratic encounters
(Christensen et al., 2020; Herd & Moynihan, 2018; Peeters, 2020).
While there is some evidence that compliance demands may impose costs on target group members
(e.g., Baekgaard, Mikkelsen, et al., 2021; Baekgaard, Moynihan, & Thomsen, 2021; Hattke et al., 2020), there is little
research on temporal aspects of administrative burden. In many cases, however, compliance demands are scheduled
ahead of time. Meetings are planned, unemployed benefit recipients are told to show up for job training programs at
specific times, and deadlines will have to be kept to continue being eligible for benefits.
We argue that the mere anticipation of encounters with frontline personnel is likely to matter for experiences of
administrative burden among benefit recipients. This is particularly the case for compliance demands that involve
encounters with public authorities entitled to sanction noncompliance with for instance financial penalties. Given
the potentially severe consequences of such sanctions, we hypothesize that the phase directly preceding compulsory
meetings might increase stress from fear of receiving a sanction or stigma from making the recipient identity more
salient. Moreover, it forces benefit recipients to prepare for the meeting and thus to bear the costs of learning about
their rights, opportunities, and obligations. Taken together, our argument highlights a temporal perspective on
administrative burden, according to which many costs are already experienced—and in the case of learning costs also
dealt with—even in advance of the actual compliance demand.
To test our expectations, we utilize a dataset with survey responses from 2276 Danish benefit recipients that
contains information about the timing of scheduled future meetings with the job center as well as their experiences
of administrative burdens. We find that the shorter the time to future meetings, the more benefit recipients experi-
ence stress and stigma, and—less consistently—a reduced capability of mastering their own situation. However, we
also find that learning costs decrease, suggesting that such compliance demands may—at least in the phase preceding
them—simultaneously increase some costs, whereas other costs become less pronounced. Overall, the findings con-
firm the central claim made by administrative burden scholars that state-enacted compliance demands are associated
with costs (e.g., Christensen et al., 2020), but additionally demonstrate that their impact on citizen experiences are

ADMINISTRATIVE BURDEN: DISTINGUISHING BETWEEN STATE
ACTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
Administrative burden is defined as the “experience of policy implementation as onerous” (Burden et al., 2012). In
doing the administrative burden framework prominently aims to shed light on individual perceptions and experiences
(Baekgaard & Tankink, 2022; Herd & Moynihan, 2018; Moynihan et al., 2015). Unlike concepts addressing similar
phenomena, such as ordeals, sludge, or most definitions of red tape, administrative burden establishes a clear distinc-
tion between the individual experiences of costs, and the compliance demands assumed to cause such experiences
(Christensen et al., 2020; Madsen, Mikkelsen, & Moynihan, 2022).
Experiences of administrative burden fall into three broad categories (Herd & Moynihan, 2018; see
Barnes, 2020a for an extension of the cost categories). Learning costs are the experiences of burden associated with
acquiring and understanding information about the terms and conditions of a service or a program. Compliance costs
are the experiences of burden associated with the process of getting access to, or staying eligible for, services and
benefits. They may, for instance, come about as a consequence of having to spend time and money on complying
with benefit demands. Finally, psychological costs involve experiences of, for example, stress, stigma, and autonomy
loss when subject to demands or restrictions.
By anchoring the concept to individual experiences, administrative burden research opens for a wider perspec-
tive on the causes and consequences of learning, compliance, and psychological costs. At the same time, the tripar-
tite division of costs enables a more nuanced understanding of the types of effects caused by specific compliance
demands. For instance, it seems plausible that asking recipients to show up at their local public office to provide doc-
umentation about their financial situation would be more stigmatizing than if recipients had to do it online from
home. At the same time, if caseworkers at the local public office stand ready to assist the recipients in filling out the
paperwork, it would likely reduce the learning costs that would otherwise be present if one had to do everything
alone from home.
Despite the focus on experiences of administrative burden in the literature, the question of whether experiences
of costs are temporal in nature has been subject to little empirical scrutiny (see, Barnes, 2020b for a notable excep-
tion). However, many encounters between citizens and the state are recurrent in nature and involve a time perspec-
tive. This applies to the welfare benefit recipient who has to apply for program participation, later on for
recertification, and maybe over time opts in and out of benefits. It also applies to benefit recipients and citizens more
broadly who engage in ongoing communication with public authorities and frontline workers as a part of the pro-
cesses of paying taxes, communicating with public schools, participating in compulsory meetings with street-level
bureaucrats to maintain eligibility for benefits, and many other examples. To better understand how compliance
demands and citizen-state encounters more broadly might trigger experiences of costs, there is a need for knowl-
edge about the temporal nature of costs. Without attending to the temporal nature of costs, we might miss out on

EXPECTATIONS ABOUT HOW UPCOMING MEETINGS INFLUENCE
COSTS
Compliance demands may come in many forms. Yet, a common characteristic of compliance demands is that they
require an effort of target group members to get access to benefits or services. One prominent example is documen-
tation and recertification requirements where benefit recipients will have to provide proof of eligibility in order to
get access to benefits or to update their status on a regular basis to uphold eligibility for benefits (Brien &
Swann, 1999; Currie & Grogger, 2002). Another example is requirements in active labor market policies (Gray
et al., 2020; Maibom et al., 2017), where citizens will have to participate in training programs or deliver some sort of
work in return for benefits. The “utility job” requirement in Danish employment policies described in Baekgaard,
Moynihan and Thomsen (2021) is an example of the latter. A third example is meeting attendance requirements,
where benefit recipients have to participate in meetings with street-level bureaucrats to apply for or maintain eligi-
bility for benefits (e.g., Bitler et al., 2003; Maibom et al., 2017; Soss, 1999).
While all the compliance demands mentioned here may potentially be associated with experiences of costs,
meeting attendance requirements are particularly interesting for our purposes. Widely used across different sub-
domains of social welfare, meeting attendance requirements is an example of compliance demands with a potential
to particularly influence individual experiences (Barnes & Henly, 2018; Bisgaard, 2020; Danneris, 2018; Masood &
Nisar, 2020). Typically, it is compulsory for recipients to participate in such meetings while the caseworker has dis-
cretion to sanction and insight into sometimes very sensitive personal information. Compared with many other com-
pliance demands, these features make meeting attendance requirements a case of relatively strong control. Unlike
other forms of compliance demands, such as registration tasks, job search requirements, or recertification, meeting
attendance requirements per definition involve a very direct encounter between benefit recipients and caseworkers
who are responsible for monitoring recipient eligibility and sanction noncompliance. If recipients look to these meet-
ings and fear they are unable to document their standing or uncertainty about the outcome of the meeting, it is likely
that merely awaiting the encounter affect their experiences of administrative burden. For instance, the phase pre-
ceding the encounter can increase stress and stigma as their identities as recipients of benefits become more salient.
Yet, the phase might also provide recipients with opportunities to seek information about their rights and obligations
as beneficiaries and thus make them feel more acquainted with the conditions of their benefits.
To form expectations about how anticipating such meetings with caseworkers influence experiences of costs
among benefit recipients, we draw on the existing, and largely qualitative, literature on citizen–state interactions,
such as policy feedback and welfare studies (Järvinen & Mik-Meyer, 2003; Soss, 1999).1 A common finding across
these studies is that target group members face a series of compliance costs when attempting to fulfill formal
requirements of public programs (Auyero, 2011; Soss, 1999). For instance, Daigneault and Macé (2020) report how
participants in the Quebec Supplement to the Work Premium experience notable compliance costs when they search
out information, fill out forms, or engage in other aspects of the claiming process. Similarly, Barnes and Henly (2018)
show how childcare subsidy recipients face compliance costs while waiting for lengthy bureaucratic processes, inac-
cessible caseworkers, or delayed eligibility decisions. As Currie (2006) argues, such onerous experiences may be
important to whether potential and active recipients decide to apply for and stay on benefits (see also Brodkin &
Majmundar, 2010). Moreover, recipients might also experience compliance costs from having to gather and organize
information that proves their eligibility to caseworkers at the meeting. In other words, simply preparing for meetings
can require a real effort of the recipients themselves that strengthens their feelings of onerousness. In sum, the mere
anticipation of mandatory meetings might therefore increase the saliency of compliance costs and as a consequence

the extent to which compliance costs are experienced even prior to taking part in the meeting. Thus, we

Hypothesis 1. As the time to an upcoming meeting becomes shorter, experiences of compliance costs

The existing literature also suggests that overcoming compliance demands affects the psychological well-being
of recipients (Baekgaard, Mikkelsen, et al., 2021; Baekgaard, Moynihan, & Thomsen, 2021). For instance, studies of
welfare programs show that recipients perceive compulsory bureaucratic encounters as relations of power and that
feelings of autonomy loss arise when they have to adjust their identity to the caseworker's image or negotiate their
standing in the eyes of the caseworker (Dias & Maynard-Moody, 2007; Lipsky, 1980; Soss, 1999, 2005). Moreover,
stress arises when recipients feel miscategorized and spend their scarce resources on activities unadjusted to their
most pressing individual needs, attending frequent meetings with shifting caseworkers, or participate in programs
lacking clear trajectories, particularly in situations where noncompliance is sanctioned (Caswell et al., 2011;
Danneris, 2016; Danneris & Nielsen, 2018). Finally, stigma can result from highly institutionalized interactions with
the welfare system, fingerprinting, or publicly waiting in line at municipal welfare offices that all risk messaging to
recipients lower civic standing, threats to self-identity, or lack of self-sufficiency (Barnes, 2020a; Bartlett et al., 2004;
Schneider & Ingram, 1993; Soss, 1999; Stuber & Schlesinger, 2006).
Drawing on the well-established framework of Basic Psychological Needs Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2017), we argue
that individuals essentially experience satisfaction when their actions are driven by volition, integrity, and willingness,
and the opposite whenever they feel pushed or under pressure from controlling external interventions (Ryan &
Deci, 2017; Vansteenkiste et al., 2020). More generally, we argue that meeting attendance requirements can be seen
as a controlling external regulation that potentially hampers the basic psychological need of autonomy. This is partic-
ularly the case if recipients perceive the process to be without clear goals or the caseworker to be unresponsive to
their needs, but simultaneously allowed to sanction noncompliance (Vansteenkiste & Van den Broeck, 2018). This
feeling of constraint might also extend to recipients' perceived ability to act on the situation. Hence, feeling less
autonomous from spending time and resources on having to comply with future meeting attendance requirements
might very well translate into a more general diminished sense of ability to be able to change their own life to the
better.
Hypothesis 2. As the time to an upcoming meeting becomes shorter, experiences of autonomy loss
will increase.
Hypothesis 3. As the time to an upcoming meeting becomes shorter, recipients feel less able to con-
trol their own lives.
Similarly, external compliance demands can affect recipients' experiences of stress by making them feel that
their behavior is regulated by external demands rather than their own interest, goals, and values (Weinstein &
Ryan, 2011). Stress arise from the conflicting directions of the individuals' own autonomous orientation and the con-
trolled orientation of the external compliance demands they are subject to (Vansteenkiste et al., 2004). Moreover,
stress can also arise from having to collect and organize information to document eligibility. The impact of these con-
trolled orientations only become more likely whenever benefit recipients depend on the social benefit system for
vital resources, such as income (Moynihan et al., 2015).
Hypothesis 4. As the time to an upcoming meeting becomes shorter, experiences of stress will
increase.

Recipients might not only experience meeting attendance requirements as an external regulation unsupportive
of their individual autonomy. They can also be interpreted as signals of mistrust in the willingness of benefit recipi-
ents to do an effort to gain employment, and make benefit recipients inclined to believe that they have attributes
that devalue them in the eyes of others (Major & O'Brien, 2005). In this case, compliance demands will not only pose
threats to autonomy but also the other basic psychological need of relatedness, which is rooted in the feeling of
being connected and significant to others (Krug et al., 2019; Ryan & Deci, 2017; Ryan & Ryan, 2019; Vansteenkiste
et al., 2020; Weinstein et al., 2017).
Hypothesis 5. As the time to an upcoming meeting becomes shorter, experiences of stigma will
increase.
Although recent research suggests that individuals face learning costs when complying with demands of public
services (for instance, see Jilke et al., 2018; Ritzel et al., 2020), we consider it less likely that the phase preceding a
meeting will increase said costs as the meeting will offer an opportunity to deal with doubt and confusion about
rights and obligations by asking questions. Also, the importance of being able to document eligibility at the meeting
may push recipients to engage with program rules and conditions prior to the meeting. On this basis, learning costs
will decrease with proximity to the meeting as recipients obtain more knowledge about rules and conditions of their
benefit and simultaneously becomes more reassured that remaining information can be obtained during the meeting.
Thus, we hypothesize that:
Hypothesis 6. As the time to an upcoming meeting becomes shorter, experiences of learning costs
will decrease.
Note that the hypothesis focuses on the timing of learning cost experiences rather than the sum of learning costs
experienced by an individual over the course of being a benefit recipient. A benefit recipient may experience less
severe learning costs close to a meeting exactly because they have had to deal with prior learning costs to prepare
properly for their upcoming meeting.

CASE SETTING: MEETING ATTENDANCE REQUIREMENTS FOR
DANISH SOCIAL BENEFIT RECIPIENTS
To test how meeting attendance requirements trigger costs among benefit recipients, we need a case where such
demands are frequent and salient to benefit recipients, where absence from attending meetings is sanctioned, and
where we are able to isolate the effect of meeting attendance requirements from that of other demands and from
the general situation of being an unemployed benefit recipient. To this end, we rely on the case of meeting atten-
dance requirements targeted at Danish social benefit recipients.
The Danish social benefit scheme is a means-tested program targeted at uninsured individuals temporarily
unable to provide the means necessary to meet their needs due to unemployment. Compared with other groups of
unemployment benefit recipients, social benefit recipients typically face more serious financial and social challenges
as they, for instance, receive markedly lower benefits and cannot possess assets exceeding 10,000 Danish Kroner
(around 1630 USD) prior to entering the program.2 In order to stay eligible for benefits, recipients have to live up to
a number of requirements. One of these is the regular attendance in meetings with caseworkers employed at the job
center, the local municipal authority responsible for determining eligibility for benefits and for planning how to get
people back to work.
The frequency of these meetings varies with unemployment length. Within the first week of becoming a social
benefit recipient, the recipient has one compulsory meeting with case workers from the job center. Afterwards, the

recipient must attend a minimum of four meetings in the following 6 months of unemployment. Once this period is
over, the meetings depend on the recipient's behavior (compliance or noncompliance) and needs and thus become
less frequent.
At a typical meeting, caseworkers check up on the recipient's job search-related activities and discuss a plan for
how to improve competences and increase chances of getting a permanent job. Recipients may be informed about
possible sanctions (e.g., reduction of benefits) in case they do not comply with benefit requirements. To arrange the
meetings, recipients are typically notified weeks ahead of their next meeting and given the option to choose
between available time slots. Recipients are obliged to book and participate in the meetings unless unusual circum-
stances, such as health problems, prevent participation. If the recipients do not show up for the meetings or book
them within the set time range, their benefit can be subtracted with a daily rate until they contact the job center
again. As the time span of available meeting time slots is narrow, typically within the same week, the actual meeting
itself is largely exogenous to the experiences of benefit recipients.
Along with the compulsory registration of job search-related activities, job training, work requirements, or the
other compliance demands imposed on social benefit recipients in Denmark, requirements of meeting attendance
entail an element of control. For some, this may be felt even harder because it involves attendance of both benefit
recipient and caseworker either at the job center or via video call.3 Particularly, as the caseworker has full insight into
the recipients' online portfolio containing their registered job search-related activities with the right to sanction the
recipients if their behavior is not deemed “active” enough. For others, meetings may however be perceived as help-
ful insofar as caseworkers provide them with some useful feedback on how to improve their situation and are avail-
able for questions about how to understand the rules and demands that apply. In the following, we discuss the data
collection.

DATA AND PARTICIPANTS
To test our hypotheses, we rely on a survey sent to recipients of social benefits in Denmark.4 We contacted each
invited participant via the Danish e-Boks system, which is a secure platform for digital mail contact between citi-
zens and universities, insurance companies, and public authorities. To decrease the likelihood that estimates are
dependent on the timing of the data collection, data were collected in three waves between February 23, 2020
and November 27, 2020.5 Within each wave, respondents were divided into three groups that were randomly
assigned to be invited for the survey either 6 days before the payment of their benefits, the day after the payment
of their benefits, or �2 weeks after the payment of the benefits. This procedure was repeated for each of the
three waves throughout the data collection period. The first wave ran from late February to mid-March 2020, the
second from late September to mid-October 2020, and the third wave from in late October to mid-November
2020.
Before each wave, we drew a random sample of unique respondents from a complete list of current social bene-
fit recipients distributed to us by The Danish Agency for Labour Market and Recruitment. We invited between 5704
and 6992 individuals for each wave, and 19,068 individuals in total. The final sample for this study consists of 2276
full and partial responses, amounting to a response rate of 11.9%.6 A comparison with Denmark's National Registry's
data on the target population show female, younger, and long-term unemployed respondents are slightly over-
represented in our sample (see Table 1).
In the survey invitation, we informed the respondents about the research project's broader aim of gaining
more knowledge about how social benefit recipients' experience of life on benefits. We also made it clear that
responses would be kept anonymous, and that participation was entirely voluntary and would not have any
impact on their benefit situation. Moreover, we underlined that no government authorities were involved in the
project. Finally, we did not present any hypotheses to the respondents in order to avoid the risk of demand
effects.

Mastery
To measure mastery, we rely on the Pearlin Mastery Scale (Pearlin & Schooler, 1978). The scale is measured with
seven items, such as: “What happens to me in the future mostly depends on me” and “There's no way I can solve
some of the problems I have” (reversed). As such, the scale has much in common with the autonomy concept but in

scale ranging from “Completely disagree” (0) to “Completely agree” (4). Coefficient omega is 0.82.

Learning costs
Learning costs were also based on Madsen and Mikkelsen (2022) and consisted of three items. Examples include: “It
takes too long to learn the rules that apply to me as a social benefit recipient” and “It is easy to get an overview of
the possibilities (e.g., further education or courses) I am entitled to as a social benefit recipient” (reversed). Each item
was rated on a 5-point scale ranging from “Completely disagree” (0) to “Completely agree” (4). Coefficient omega

Controls
Finally, we include a set of additional variables to minimize the risk of confounding. First, we include socio-
demographic characteristics to control for the respondent's age, gender, ethnic origin, educational level, cohabitation
(i.e., sharing the home with a partner), and children living in the household. Moreover, we adjust for length of unem-
ployment length as existing research into unemployment consistently finds associations between length of unem-
ployment and some of our dependent variables, such as stress (Kim et al., 2019; McKee-Ryan et al., 2005; Paul &
Moser, 2009). In addition, we add two measures of recipients' self-reported physical health impairments and whether
they can rely on social support from family and friends, as both are also likely to influence our dependent variables.
Finally, it is possible that people's experiences of receiving benefits fluctuate over the course of a month. For
instance, they may be more concerned about their situation and experience more stress and less autonomy in times
of scarce resources (Madsen, Baekgaard, & Kvist, 2022). Empirically, we would expect resources to be scarcer in the
period right before the monthly payout of benefits. To account for the possible confounding effects of financial scar-
city, we thus also control for the timing of the survey invitation as enlisted above. Table 2 contains descriptive statis-

DISCUSSION OF LIMITATIONS
While we believe that our estimation strategy is well-suited to test our hypotheses, we also note some limitations. Most
importantly, our estimation strategy is not based on random assignment to meetings, and hence we cannot completely
rule out the possibility of reverse causality and selection bias. For instance, it is possible that individuals feeling more
capable of navigating the social benefit system or more focused on regaining employment (which are also the ones feel-
ing less stressed, feeling less stigma, etc.) are more inclined to utilize caseworker meetings and thus on average more
likely to ask for a meeting with the job center in the first place. If so, such recipients would be more likely to have a
meeting scheduled at any given time. However, we consider this highly unlikely. Most importantly, social benefit recipi-
ents are severely restricted in their possibilities of asking for and arranging meetings. Furthermore, as shown in Supple-
mentary Appendix F, all levels of the independent variable are largely balanced on both background characteristics and
job search motivation indicating no systematic patterns in who has an upcoming meeting scheduled in the calendar in
our data. Finally, taking into account recipients' recent meeting activity does little to alter our main results.7
Another issue might arise from measurement. Learning costs, compliance costs, and mastery were estimated as
single-factor constructs in confirmatory factor models as they are independent theoretical constructs, whereas
stress, stigma, and autonomy loss were estimated in a three-factor model as they are all subsumed under psychologi-
cal costs (Herd & Moynihan, 2018). However, it is possible that our findings are sensitive to model specifications.
We therefore ran our regressions with two additional specifications of the dependent variables: Additive indexes
(Supplementary Appendix D) and factorized scores of all six dependent variables derived from one confirmatory six-
factor model (Supplementary Appendix E). As shown in Figure 1, running the regressions with additive indexes
shows significant results on stigma, mastery, and learning costs (p < 0.05). While the factorized scores from a six-
factor model do not substantially change our main results besides from improving estimates on autonomy loss
(p < 0.1), it does show less convincing results on learning costs (p > 0.1). Apart from these slight deviations, however,
estimates are overall stable across estimations, suggesting that the main model does indeed provide a valid picture
of how upcoming meetings influence experiences of administrative burden.
Finally, while our study is based on real-life data and has high ecological validity, it is still worth discussing the gen-
eralizability of our findings. Importantly, some of our data were collected during a period of coronavirus disease 2019
(COVID-19) lockdown, where a series of compliance demands were relieved or temporarily suspended: Physical meet-
ings were converted to online meetings during most of the period, active job search and participation in job training
was not compulsory during most of the period, and so forth. These circumstances create a complex set of scope condi-
tions that may have influenced how meetings translate into burdens. On the one hand, we would expect that online
meetings all else equal are associated with less experiences of burden because having to show up physically is more
intimidating. In this sense, our estimates may be considered conservative. On the other hand, the lockdown also meant
that few jobs were available and may have caused an even stronger sense of meaninglessness of having to participate
in compulsory meetings. While there thus both are arguments that speak for inflated and conservative estimates on
psychological costs due to the timing of our data collection, it is also important to note that the same is not the case for
learning costs, which are likely to be largely unaffected by these circumstances. Thus, we feel confident about the main
conclusion that learning costs are differently affected than psychological costs by meeting attendance requirements.
Moreover, do our findings generalize to other policy settings where meeting attendance requirements also are
used? While we also expect meeting attendance requirements to matter in other settings, they may not necessarily
matter in the same way as in our study. For instance, the stigma associated with said requirements may very well
depend on the (expected) content of meetings and the broader policy context in which the requirements are embedded.
In some policies, meetings are communicated more as a control device towards undeserving clients such as immigrants
and the long-term unemployed, whereas in others it is communicated as an intended helping hand. Thus, findings on
for instance stigma may be more pronounced in some settings and less in others. Likewise, our results do not necessar-
ily entail that other types of compliance demands will translate into experiences of compliance, psychological, and learn-
ing costs in a similar way. As we show, compliance demands can both increase and decrease the experience of costs

and the direction of these effects might very well differ across contexts. Similarly, the effects of other compliance
demands will depend on the nature of the demand itself. For instance, fingerprinting is likely to impact more severely
on the basic psychological needs of relatedness and increase the experience of stigma. On the other hand, activation
courses will more likely matter to compliance costs and autonomy loss, and particularly if the recipient perceives the
course to be onerous and not improving their progression towards employment. The same likely applies to registration
requirements, which on the contrary can reduce learning costs as the process itself encourages the recipient to gather
knowledge about the social benefit system. It is an important point for future research to look further into the effects
of specific compliance demands and to theorize how different demands may trigger different types of costs.

CONCLUSION
Our study finds that benefit recipients experience more stress and stigma the closer they get to upcoming compul-
sory meetings, while they on the other hand experience less learning costs. This evidence suggests that compliance
demands in two important respects play a more complex role for the experience of costs than commonly stated in
the literature on administrative burden (Herd & Moynihan, 2018). First, people do not need to experience compli-
ance demands to be affected by them. The mere anticipation of compliance demands in itself is enough to trigger
costs. Thus, compliance demands may have broader consequences than hitherto suggested in the literature. This
begs additional questions for future research: To what extent are experiences of administrative burden mainly a
question of anticipation, and to what extent is it a consequence of actually having to comply with demands? Does
the importance of anticipations perish over time as people become better acquainted with complying with demands?
And more broadly: How does the repeated interactions between benefit recipients and government influence

experiences of burden? Answering such questions will add to our understanding of how compliance demands influ-
ence social welfare recipient's experiences of administrative burden and to our understanding of the temporal
aspects of administrative burden. Second, the evidence suggests that while social benefit recipients experience psy-
chological costs when awaiting interactions with the job center, the process of complying with compliance demands
simultaneously creates an opportunity to get better acquainted with the conditions of their benefit and thus to
reduce learning costs. Thus, anticipating mandatory meetings influences when respondents deal with learning costs.
This has some important implications for research. For instance, research may underestimate the learning costs asso-
ciated with a given compliance demand if it only studies the experiences of such costs while people are dealing with
compliance demands directly, as many of the costs may have been experienced and dealt with beforehand. As such,
the finding illustrates the benefits of a temporal perspective on learning costs. Future research may build on the find-
ing of Nielsen et al. (2021) that some citizens are unlikely to seek out new information when interacting with govern-
ment to understand these dynamics in more detail. Relatedly, Bell et al. (2022) find that emotions influence the
likelihood of seeking information. These insights may be used to explore in further detail how emotions and anticipa-
tion interact in shaping learning costs. For instance, those who experience shame may be less inclined to seek out
new information and hence their learning costs may not diminish at an early stage to the same extent as for those
feeling less shame. While our argument concerns how citizen reduces learning costs by gathering additional informa-
tion, future research may also want to explore the complementary role of government in this process. For instance,
comprehensive dissemination of information prior to meetings may assist citizens in reducing learning costs.
By demonstrating how awaiting a relatively simple and trivial requirement influences people's experiences of bur-
den even in advance of meetings, our findings also have important implications for practice. Most importantly, they
illustrate that requirements are not neutral. They potentially matter both before, during, and after the actual encounter.
Hence, it is relevant to consider how to uphold legitimate requirements while simultaneously reducing experiences of
burdens. In the case of meeting attendance requirements, one option could be to make it voluntary if people have to
participate in online meetings or meetings with physical presence. Another strategy could be to ensure that people
meet the same caseworker over time to reduce the uncertainty about how the caseworker behaves and to reduce frus-
trations about repeatedly experiencing having new caseworkers who have little knowledge about the specific case.

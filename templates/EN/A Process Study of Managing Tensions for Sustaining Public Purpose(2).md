Abstract
Purpose-oriented networks (PONs) face multiple tensions, but although their existence is well-documented, there is limited research on how
network tensions emerge and how they are managed over time to enable network sustainability. We conducted a longitudinal process study of
a public PON in the natural hazards field in Norway to explore how network participants experience and manage network tensions. The study
identifies four tension-managing practices that help sustain networks. The analysis reveals how network tensions are interrelated and (re)emerge
as the network unfolds, and how the participants’ use of these practices yields different outcomes but enables them to sustain the network over
time. The study answers recent calls in the public administration literature for process studies to better understand network phenomena and
contributes a “networks-as-practice” perspective on managing network tensions. This perspective helps to reconcile and extend the dominant
network governance and management perspectives because, by focusing on practice, it captures how structures and actions mutually shape one
another—an interaction that is fundamental to network processes and dynamics.
Key words: public purpose-oriented networks; network tensions; managing practices; network sustainability, process study.
Introduction
Networks, broadly defined as “a group of three or more orga­
nizations connected in ways that facilitate achievement of a
common goal” (Provan, Fish, and Sydow 2007, p. 482), have
received much attention in the public administration literature
as a key means to cope with the challenges public managers
face (Agranoff and McGuire 2001; Isett et al. 2011; Lecy, Mer­
gel, and Schmitz 2014). However, networks are prone to ten­
sions (Koppenjan and Klijn 2004; Ospina and Saz-Carranza
2010; Provan and Kenis 2008; Vangen and Huxham 2012),
which are contradictory demands and forces inherent in net­
works (Berthod and Segato 2019). Among the number and
variety of tensions identified in the literature, Provan and Kenis
(2008) offer a set of core tensions that remain an important
building block for scholars on the issue: stability versus flexi­
bility, inclusivity versus efficiency, and internal versus external
legitimacy. Managing these network tensions is a central object
of managerial attention, especially in public purpose-oriented
networks (PONs) (Berthod and Segato 2019), because failure
to do so reduces goal achievement, effectiveness, and sustain­
ability of networks (Provan and Kenis 2008).
While the need for managing tensions is well documented in
the extant literature, few studies have addressed the processes
and specific social practices network managers use to cope with
tensions in PONs (Auschra and Sydow 2023; Berthod and
Segato 2019). Practices here refer to the actions people take
that recursively produce and reproduce the structures that con­
strain and enable these actions (Giddens 1984). The network
literature is dominated by structural and configurational
approaches (Auschra and Sydow 2023) and has developed
along two key perspectives. On the one hand, scholars within
the “network governance perspective” have focused on the
impact of different governance modes (Klaster, Wilderom, and
Muntslag 2017; Moynihan 2009; Provan and Kenis 2008; van
den Oord et al. 2023) and characteristics of ties between net­
work actors (Kapucu and Garayev 2016) for the effectiveness
of networks and network governance (Raab, Mannak, and
Cambré 2015; Siciliano, Carr, and Hugg 2020). On the other
hand, scholars within the “network management” perspective
have highlighted tasks, activities, and behaviors as essential
components of network management (Agranoff and McGuire
2001; Bryson, Crosby, and Stone 2015; Ospina and
Saz-Carranza 2010). These approaches have provided invalu­
able insights into network governance and network manage­
ment, but they fail to explain the “messy dynamics and
intricacies of establishing and maintaining a network as a pro­
cess in which actors engage with multiple tensions simultane­
ously” (van Duijn, Bannink, and Ybema 2022, p. 662).
Against this backdrop, this study addresses the following
research questions: How do network tensions emerge in a public
PON, and how do network participants manage these tensions
to sustain the network over time? To answer these questions, we
conducted a longitudinal process study on a public PON in the
natural hazards field in Norway. The network was established to
reduce the negative impacts of natural hazards by improving the
coordination and collaboration between key public organizations

This study makes several contributions to the public admin­
istration literature on PONs. There is a dearth of empirical
longitudinal studies on the processes of network evolution,
dynamics, and sustainability (Provan and Lemaire 2012), but
more recently, there have been a few such studies in the public
network literature (e.g., Berthod et al. 2017; Lemaire, McK­
eague, and Sedgwick 2024; van Duijn, Bannink, and Ybema
2022). Our study falls within the stream of research that uses
longitudinal studies to disentangle the underlying processes and
practices of network phenomena (Cloutier et al. 2016; Quick
and Feldman 2014). In response to recent calls for process and
practice-based studies of PONs (Auschra and Sydow 2023;
Berthod, Grothe-Hammer, and Sydow 2017; Berthod and
Segato 2019), we extend understanding of how network sus­
tainability is linked to managing tensions and network dynam­
ics. Building on a practice perspective (Orlikowski 2010), this
study offers a “networks-as-practice” perspective that clarifies
the relationship between tensions, practices, and outcomes
enabling network sustainability. Practice refers to the idea that
social structures “are best expressed and studied via socially
embedded and recurrent activities that constitute a bridge
between agency and structures” (Berthod, Grothe-Hammer,
and Sydow 2017, p. 302). This helps to reconcile and extend
the network governance and network management perspectives
by highlighting the consequential and relational nature of prac­
tices and how they form a mutually constitutive relationship
with network structures.
Within this overall contribution, this study provides two
major insights. First, it demonstrates the dynamic nature of
network tensions and how they (re)emerge as the network
“comes into being” (Berthod and Segato 2019). Furthermore,
while previous studies tend to confine the analysis to individual
tensions, this study reveals how network participants face
­multiple, interrelated tensions. Second, we identify four
tension-managing practices that network participants, and not
only managers, use to manage tensions as they (re)emerge. The
study shows that the outcomes of these practices are only pro­
visional and might trigger new tensions; however, they still help
participants sustain the network over time. While the findings
are especially relevant for public PONs, they also provide
insights into public networks in general, since tensions and
their management are inherent to such networks (Provan and
Kenis 2008).
Managing network tensions in PONs
Network tensions are contradictory demands and forces that
lead to competing choices and point to the potentially para­
doxical forces that networks face (Berthod and Segato 2019).
They are shared among network participants, unlike tensions
at the individual level (Lee 2022). They may arise within the
network (inward) or between the network and external actors
(outward) (Ospina and Saz-Carranza 2010). A key source of
these tensions resides in the ambiguous, complex, and dynamic
nature of networks, such as membership structures (Huxham
and Vangen 2000), goal setting (Huxham 2003), and account­
ability (Agranoff and McGuire 2001).
Extant literature has identified several network tensions (see,
for an overview, Berthod and Segato 2019). A particular focus
has been put on the tension between the self- and collective
interests (Thomson and Perry 2006) and the degree of goal
congruence (Huxham 2003; Vangen and Huxham 2012).
Related to these is the tension between unity and diversity
(Ospina and Saz-Carranza 2010; Saz-Carranza and Ospina
2011; Vangen and Winchester 2014), differentiation and inte­
gration (van Duijn, Bannink, and Ybema 2022), and between
autonomy and accountability (Berthod et al. 2017; Huxham
and Beech 2003), reflected in the web of different accountability
relationships in networks (Lee 2022; Lægreid and Rykkja
2022; Romzek et al. 2014; Waardenburg et al. 2020). However,
despite the number of network tensions identified, Provan and
Kenis (2008) provide a typology of network tensions to which
many network scholars adhere: the tension between
efficiency–inclusiveness, internal–external legitimacy, and
flexibility–stability. These three generic tensions partially
include and overlap with many of the tensions described above.
The first relates to the trade-off between the involvement of
multiple and diverse organizations and the need for efficient
decision-making (Provan and Kenis 2008). Including multiple
organizations is key in dealing with complex societal problems
since it brings different perspectives and knowledge to the table
(Dawes, Cresswell, and Pardo 2009; Feldman et al. 2006).
However, this might complicate the problem definition
(Waardenburg et al. 2020), where ongoing explorations of the
problem might lead to little action (Huxham and Vangen 2005)
and, ultimately, pre-termination (Vangen, Hayes, and Corn­
forth, 2015). Thus, the inclusiveness–efficiency tension is
related to the tension between unity and diversity. As Ospina
and Saz-Carranza (2010) note, efficiency is constrained with
greater diversity. This also relates to the tension addressed by
Klaster, Wilderom and Muntslag (2017) between pursuing and
achieving short-term goals, which focus on efficiency and estab­
lishing enduring network relations, which implies inclusiveness.
Provan and Kenis (2008, p. 14) argued that “the outcome of
this problem is that a small subset of participants may end up
doing most of the work, producing a drop in enthusiasm,
increased levels of frustration, and a gradual shift toward a
greater centralization of governance.”
The second tension arises when attempts to improve internal
network legitimacy negatively influence external legitimacy and
lead to an inability to meet external expectations, and vice versa
(Provan and Kenis 2008). This relates to the lack of traditional
hierarchical authority in networks (Agranoff and McGuire
2001), and ambiguities in expectations and to whom the col­
laboration is accountable (Bryson, Crosby, and Stone 2015).
Provan and Kenis (2008) argue that this tension has multiple
dimensions, including a tension between individual and collec­
tive legitimacy and between a focus on building internal net­
work interactions and building the credibility of the network
externally. Thus, this tension relates to several other tensions
identified in the literature, such as the differentiation–integration
tension (van Duijn, Bannink, and Ybema 2022) and tensions
between hierarchical and vertical accountability (Lee, 2022).
Human and Provan (2000) find that network sustainability
increases when participants adopt a dual legitimacy-building
strategy that combines inside-out and outside-in strategies in

The third tension relates to the recognition that, while a
network is established to enhance flexibility, stability must be
established to sustain and facilitate collaboration in the net­
work (Provan and Kenis 2008). Thus, this tension is also
related to the tension between short-term goals and enduring
network relations (Klaster, Wilderom, and Muntslag 2017).
According to Lemaire, McKeague, and Sedgwick (2024), a key
source of this tension is the “ebb and flow” of network partic­
ipants. Several studies confirm stability as a key condition for
network effectiveness (Provan and Milward 1995), for exam­
ple, in emergency management, where long-term relationships
across crises are critical for preparedness (Kapucu, Garayev,
and Wang 2013; Moynihan 2009; Siciliano, Carr, and Hugg
2020). However, stability may also lead to inertia and collab­
oration “fatigue” (Huxham and Vangen 2005). Similar to the
finding about legitimacy, scholars recognize the value of both
stability and flexibility for network sustainability and effective­
ness (Lemaire, McKeague, and Sedgwick 2024).
Because network tensions are an essential aspect of network
life, their management is a central issue for effective network
management and network sustainability (Berthod and Segato
2019; Provan and Kenis 2008; Saz-Carranza and Ospina
2011). Traditionally, the literature has focused on structural
and configurational approaches to networks (Auschra and
Sydow 2023), resembling two perspectives in the literature. In
the “network governance” perspective, “the use of institutions
and structures of authority and collaboration to allocate
resources and to coordinate and control joint action across the
network as a whole” is prioritized (Provan and Kenis 2008, p.
3), for example, different network governance modes and their
effects (Berthod et al. 2017; Klaster, Wilderom, and Muntslag
2017; Moynihan 2009; Provan and Kenis 2008; Raab, Man­
nak, and Cambré 2015; van den Oord et al. 2023). Within the
“network management” perspective, scholars have been more
concerned with identifying the key components of effective
network management (Agranoff and McGuire 2001; Koppen­
jan and Klijn 2004; Ospina and Saz-Carranza 2010) and com­
bining these into integrative frameworks (Bryson, Crosby, and
Stone 2015; Emerson, Nabatchi, and Balogh 2012; Thomson
and Perry 2006; Vangen, Hayes, and Cornforth 2015).
For example, much attention has been given to the activities
of network managers to manage tensions, like Ospina and
Saz-Carranza (2010), who identified six collaborative practices
that helped managers of immigrant networks in the US to man­
age tensions related to unity–diversity (inward) and
confrontation–dialogue (outward): facilitating interaction;
cultivating personal relationships; promoting openness and
participation (inward work); managing credibility; multi-level
work; and cultivating multiple relationships (outward work).
The authors admit that these practices largely overlap with the
network activities identified by other scholars (e.g., Agranoff
and McGuire 2001) but add to previous conceptualizations by
demonstrating the paradoxical nature of these tensions. In a
later study, they showed that “embracing tensions” (p. 357)
seemed to be the preferred strategy in the networks studied
(Saz-Carranza and Ospina 2011). This complies with other
scholars who argue for a paradox lens, highlighting a “both/
and” mindset instead of an “either/or” one (Huxham and
Beech 2003; Lee 2022; Vangen 2016; Waardenburg et al. 2020).
Despite the valuable contributions provided by these per­
spectives in identifying different practices for managing ten­
sions, they do not address how network participants are able
to sustain the network while balancing these tensions over time.
Critics have argued that they fail to explain how networking
actors navigate and negotiate the challenges inherent in a net­
work (van Duijn, Bannink, and Ybema 2022). Instead, the
critics call for a shift to a process view of networks to better
understand their complexities and what managers do to man­
age conflicting demands (Auschra and Sydow 2023; Berthod
and Segato 2019; van Duijn, Bannink, and Ybema 2022; Van­
gen 2016). A process view is arguably better fitted to capture
how networks, and more particularly how PONs are “per­
formed at the crossroads of multiple, contradictory forces and
the reciprocal consequences of managerial actions” (Berthod
and Segato 2019, p. 228). As Vangen (2016) states, tension
management is better considered as a continuous effort in
which “actions and choices will trigger new situations and
tensions ad infinitum” (p. 267). Thus, a process view indicates
that a PON “comes into being” when “agents carry out its
constitutive processes through recurring practices” (Berthod
and Segato 2019, p. 227).
These arguments align with calls in the general
inter-organizational literature for a better understanding of the
dynamics of inter-organizational relationships and networks
(Majchrzak, Jarvenpaa, and Bagherzadeh, 2015; Sydow and
Berends, 2019). However, empirical studies adopting a process
view are rare in the literature on managing network tensions,
especially in the public domain (Berthod and Segato, 2019).
Thus, in this study, we explore how multiple tensions emerge
and interrelate as a PON unfolds and the practices that network
participants—beyond merely network managers—use in their
efforts to manage these tensions to sustain the network over
time. As Provan and Lemaire (2012, p. 642) observed, a key
reason why networks fail “may be a lack of consideration of
how emergent relationships typically form, are strengthened,
and ultimately are sustained.”
Methods
This study draws on a longitudinal process study of a public
network in the field of natural hazards in Norway called the
Natural Hazards Forum (NHF). A process study enables cap­
turing “how and why things emerge, develop, grow, or termi­
nate over time” (Langley et al. 2013, p. 1). This design was
useful for our purposes because we aimed to explore how net­
work tensions emerged and how participants managed these
tensions to sustain the network over time. NHF resembles a
PON, “being bounded, self-referencing collectives comprised
of actors who consciously affiliate to the collective around some
shared purpose” (Nowell and Milward 2022, p. 22), which in
the NHF case was reducing societal risks from natural hazards.
We considered the NHF a revelatory case because it involved
multiple and quite diverse organizations. While all were in the
public sector, they had different roles and responsibilities con­
cerning natural hazards, including reporting to different min­
istries. Thus, we expected NHF participants to face multiple
tensions as the network unfolded. Since we were allowed access
to the case from the beginning of 2016 to the present, we could
explore these processes in real time. This enabled the building
of a theory (Yin 2018) of managing tensions in public PONs
(Berthod and Segato 2019).

Research context
In 2012, the Norwegian Public Roads Administration
(­Norwegian: SVV), the Norwegian Railway Directorate
(­Norwegian: JBD), and the Norwegian Water Resources and
Energy Directorate (Norwegian: NVE) joined a 4-year R&D
project called Natural Hazards—Infrastructure, Floods, and
Slides (Norwegian: Naturfare-Infrastruktur-Flom-Skred
(NIFS)). The partners considered the project a success and
stated in the final report that “the good collaboration that has
been established should be continued” (Myrabø et al. 2016, p.
74) and suggested “a fixed structure to be established from
2016 […], and where other parties will be included” (p. 71).
This initiative was endorsed by the top managers of the three
partners, as illustrated in presentations at the final seminar in
April 2016. It was acknowledged that the new initiative should
be coordinated with similar initiatives. Consequently, the Nor­
wegian Directorate for Civil Protection (Norwegian: DSB) was
contacted because it was responsible for following up on Nor­
way’s commitment to the Sendai Framework for crisis man­
agement and for a network dedicated to this topic. The parties
agreed to merge the initiatives into the NHF, which would also
answer the call in a white paper for a national strategy to
coordinate and facilitate cooperation between organizations in
the field of natural hazards (Stortingsmelding, 15. (2011–2012)).
NHF started in 2016 with eight public organizations with
distinct responsibilities and roles in the field of natural hazards.
In the following years, four other organizations were included,
for a current total of twelve organizations (Supplementary
Appendix A). Some of these collaborated outside the NHF,
such as the SVV, NVE, and MET on warning systems, whereas
others were more independent and met primarily in the NHF.
The network relied on participating organizations to fund their
own time and activities, and sub-projects that were established
with workgroups dedicated to specific topics were funded ad
hoc. The network was managed by a steering group with rep­
resentatives (primarily department directors) from the twelve
members, who met four times a year on average, and a secre­
tariat involving representatives from NVE, SVV, and DSB,
convening approximately every month. Network governance
was aligned with shared governance (Provan and Kenis, 2008).
In addition, the top managers of the organizations met once a
year to discuss the results and guide the way forward. At the
end of the study, NHF was recognized as playing an important
role in the battle against natural hazards in Norway (Storting­
smelding, 27 (2023–2024)).

Data collection began with an exploratory phase, with one
of the authors attending the final seminar of NIFS in April
2016, when NHF was launched. As a follow-up and to learn
more about the need for collaboration to cope with natural
hazards, we conducted two interviews with two representatives
from each of the initiating organizations that had also been
involved in the NIFS project. As NHF was new, we also inter­
viewed other experts in the field of natural hazards to learn
about the challenges and need for collaboration in the broader
field. They did not participate in NHF but had extensive knowl­
edge. One of these interviews included two interviewees who
represented colleagues in different expert areas. The joint inter­
views were relatively similar to those with one interviewee but
benefited from the dialogue between the interviewees because
they could complement each other’s information. In this phase,
the questions were more open-ended and included themes
about the reasons, needs, ambitions, actions, and challenges of
collaboration in the field, as well as questions related to the
strategies and work on natural hazards in the interviewees’
home organizations. Data collection continued with archival
data, including 126 reports generated by NIFS, which we
scanned through for information about the collaboration. This
provided an in-depth understanding of natural hazard chal­
lenges and collaborative efforts to cope with them, including
the development of new solutions and guidelines.
In the second data collection phase, after NHF was formed,
we interviewed some of the representatives in the steering group
and in the secretariat, and one to two representatives of some
of the most active sub-projects. This was to gain insights into
the organization and management of the network, as well as
the activities in the projects. In total, we conducted fifteen indi­
vidual interviews, including two interviews with external
experts who were involved in sub-projects but were not mem­
bers of NHF. In line with the process study research design, we
shifted the focus from more general questions related to col­
laboration to asking the interviewees’ views on the network,
including what they were doing and the challenges they faced,
with a focus on emerging network tensions. Thus, our unit of
analysis was the “whole network” (Provan, Fish, and Sydow,
2007). To address network dynamics and align with the process
study design, we focused on key events such as discussions and
decisions about network composition, network scope, and key
activities. We developed a more formalized thematic interview
guide with similar questions across the interviews, even if they
were slightly tailored to each interview depending on the inter­
viewee’s involvement and if s/he had been interviewed previ­
ously. The questions were informed by inter-organizational
collaboration and network literature (e.g., Berthod and Segato,
2019; Provan and Kenis, 2008; Vangen, Hayes, and Corn­
forth, 2015).
In the third data collection phase, we conducted follow-up
interviews with the secretariat to capture changes as the net­
work unfolded. We also extended the interviews to cover all
steering group members, more sub-project members, and exter­
nal field experts who could broaden the scope and provide
views on the NHF from outside. This interview round also
included an evaluation of the network in 2021, initiated by the
steering group to develop a more formal strategy and action
plan. The evaluation was based on interviews with the mem­
bers of the steering group and the secretariat (twelve interviews
in total). An own interview guide was developed, which
included questions about the status of the network, interview­
ees’ views on how the network was organized and worked,
perceived challenges and opportunities, and future develop­
ment and ambitions. This round included new interviewees,
since some of the steering group participants had been replaced.
In the fourth data collection phase, we conducted follow-up
interviews with secretariat participants to obtain updated infor­
mation about the status of the network. One was a group
interview with all the representatives, allowing them to reflect
on the status of the network and on what had happened since
the evaluation. Supplementary Appendix C provides an exem­
plary interview guide for the general interviews across the data
collection phases and the interview guide used for the evalua­
tion in 2021.
In addition to the interviews, access was granted to the NHF
project database, where minutes from meetings of the steering
group, subprojects, and top managers were available. From
2020 to 2024, one of the authors observed the steering group
and annual top manager meetings, taking detailed notes on the
discussions and decisions regarding the structure, management,
and activities of the network. These were primarily
non-participant observations that assisted in gaining a valuable
understanding of the conversation style and topics of the meet­
ings. On two occasions, preliminary results from the study were
presented, providing opportunities for feedback.
Analytical process
Data analysis was performed in four iterative steps. After
uploading the data to NVivo, the first analytical step was to
create a thick chronological description of the NHF develop­
ment. This description provided an overview of how the net­
work developed, the key events, and when the changes
occurred. The second analytical step involved probing the data
for network tensions based on Provan and Kenis (2008). This
was done by looking for situations in meetings where partici­
pants discussed issues that created contradictory demands and
where possible solutions were ambiguous. We drew on the
interviewees’ own words to reflect, describe, and explain such
situations. In addition to the three network tensions identified
by Provan and Kenis (2008), the analysis revealed tensions
related to accountability (Agranoff and McGuire, 2001; Lee,
2022). The public sector is organized in sectoral silos of respon­
sibility and hierarchical authority (Lægreid and Rykkja, 2022),
and networks with their horizontal accountability relationships
challenge and complement these vertical relationships. This
tension between vertical and horizontal accountability (Lee,
2022) also relates to the recognition that networks operate with
limited authority and opportunities to force compliance and
has been related to legitimacy (Bryson et al., 2015). Based on
our analysis, we chose to focus on the vertical–horizontal
accountability tension as a separate tension to highlight the
interrelatedness between tensions, since the lack of authority
in the NHF led to the prioritization of daily tasks in home
organizations. This subsequently reduced activity levels and
internal legitimacy.

the four types of network tensions and open coding to reveal

scaling and scoping, mobilizing top managers’ support, and

The fourth analytical step involved matching the occurrence
of tensions and management practices as the network unfolded.
We relied on a temporal bracketing strategy (Langley, 1999).
Temporal bracketing permits “analyzing how the changing
context from previous periods impacts subsequent events in
current periods” (Langley et al., 2013, p. 7). We decomposed
the overall process of NHF, where tensions were particularly
salient and required attention and management by participants,
into three phases comprising 2-year periods. The first two
phases, formation (2016–2017) and maturation (2018–2020),
comply with traditional network life cycle models (Berthod and
Segato, 2019). However, in contrast to many other models,
NHF did not enter a decline phase resulting in termination, but
entered a revitalization phase, helping network participants
sustain the network. This started in 2021 and continued
throughout 2023. When data collection ended in 2024, it
seemed that the network had matured again. The analysis
revealed that the practices yielded different outcomes in and
across the phases, driving the overall development of the NHF.
Through open and axial coding, these outcomes were coded
into three types: diminishing, amplifying, and balancing ten­
sions, as illustrated in Table 3. While some practices helped
participants diminish the occurrence and impact of tensions in
one phase, some tensions reemerged and were even amplified
in another. In addition, the analysis revealed a third outcome,
balancing, where tensions persisted but were managed and
solutions were accepted by members.

Emerging tensions

natural hazards to reflect the complexity of the issue. Many

group was considered essential to provide variations in
perspectives.

Phase 2: maturation of NHF (2018–2020)
(Re)emerging tensions
Differences between parties were soon revealed in terms of engage­
ment and expectations. Some were satisfied with participating in
meetings to exchange information, while others, particularly ini­
tiating organizations, were increasingly worried that the network
was becoming a “talking club.” The differentiating participation
practice implied that the parties could voluntarily assign represen­
tatives to sub-projects depending on their interests and resources.
There was also a lack of basic funding, as one interviewee argued:
“Shared funding would be challenging, since it requires that all
have to succeed, although we have different priorities” (Steering
group member, May 2021).
Thus, efficiency–inclusiveness and flexibility–stability ten­
sions soon re-emerged. The network largely depended on NVE,
SVV, and DSB, which each allocated 50 percent of employee
resources to the secretariat, creating an imbalance in responsi­
bilities and efforts. These positions were supposed to circulate
among the parties, but “no one was knocking on the door,”
according to the steering group leader. For steering group rep­
resentatives, relying on a core group of people was efficient:
“Much of the success can be attributed to these guys,” and
“the secretariat does an amazing job.” However, it was also
recognized that the core group, especially NVE, was powerful,
possessing all the central positions: “They [NVE] are still in
the lead and the others just have to follow” (Steering group
member, June 2020). The other parties became relatively pas­
sive, and the participants’ frustration emerged because their
level of activity decreased. A steering group member expressed
concerns regarding this development:
The steering group, well, they show up, I’ll give them that,
but I don’t think they do much more. They’re happy with
the secretariat coming up with an agenda, so there is a lack
of engagement, and I’m a bit tired of that. (Steering group
member, May 2020)
Other participants agreed: “So, I think people were maybe
more optimistic in the beginning than now… People had more
expectations that the different members would be more active”
(Project member, February 2019). Declining engagement was
attributed to a lack of economic resources, as perceived by
many interviewees, as a major drawback. With a busy working
day, the daily chores within home organizations were priori­
tized over projects in NHF, and the steering group had few
opportunities for sanctions: “There are no strong demands
from anyone” (Project member, June 2020). With no formal
accountability relationships, either horizontally or vertically,
the levels of engagement and activity decreased, influencing the
legitimacy of the collaboration. Some members began to ques­
tion whether it was worth continuing their involvement.
Practices
Responses to tensions that emerge during the maturation phase
evolved. These responses were not a result of deliberate deci­
sions. Forming joint projects was key to the network from the
beginning, and even if some projects had stalled (for reasons
from both within and outside the network), they were consid­
ered critical for creating commitment, increasing the level of
activity, and providing flexibility to address relevant topics. It
was also recognized that joint projects could address more
complex topics, thereby enhancing the legitimacy of the net­
work. This issue scaling, which shifted from the low-hanging
fruits in the beginning to more complex topics, assumed that
“challenging problems have proved to require some maturing”
(Project member, January 2020).
One such topic concerned the funding of preventive measures
against natural hazards, which was a primary concern for the
parties. However, it was recognized that issue scaling by
addressing more complex topics like funding increased the
potential for conflicts: “I don't think we have come there yet,
but maybe we're getting closer when we investigate these big
issues, like funding, which interfere with members’ budgets and
involve other sectors such as insurance” (Secretariat member,
January 2020). Nevertheless, given the importance of preven­
tion, the steering group decided to address the issue at the 2018
top manager meeting to mobilize top management support.
The meeting resulted in a joint statement signed by all the top
managers declaring that “too few resources are used for the
prevention of natural hazards on existing buildings and infra­
structure” (Top Managers’ statement, 2018). The discussion
continued in subsequent top managers’ meetings, and in 2019,
according to the joint statement: “Each organization ensures
that the issues are sufficiently anchored in the organizations’
letter of allocation [provided by the different ministries] and
other governing documents” (Statement, 2019). The steering
group was asked to establish a joint project on this topic. Most
parties participated with representatives from their home orga­
nizations, and the project generated much engagement and
enthusiasm. It also included external experts such as FNO and
resulted in a joint report recommending alterations to the
national insurance scheme for natural hazards.
The report was presented at the top manager meeting in
2020. The intention was to conclude the meeting with a joint
statement regarding the need for a new funding scheme. How­
ever, instead of using it for internal purposes only, it was pro­
posed that the statement be sent to the Ministry of Justice, given
the legal aspects of the issue. However, the joint statement
sparked heavy debate among top-level managers, revealing the
surfacing accountability tensions. While many managers were
enthusiastic and wanted immediate ratification, others were
more reluctant regarding the “responsibility principle” and
questioned how far NHF could go in their recommendations.
The counterargument was that “we have to be bolder to cope
with these issues” (Top manager, meeting November 2020).
An agreement to send a joint statement to the Ministry of
Justice was finally reached in March 2021, after internal hear­
ings and adjustments.

Phase 3: revitalization of NHF (2021–2023)
(Re)emerging tensions
The “funding prevention” project created enthusiasm and
demonstrated the value of the network. However, after final­
izing the project, engagement decreased, and tensions (re)
emerged. The levels of engagement and activity were considered
difficult to sustain among member organizations, leading to
efficiency–inclusiveness tensions, which also influenced the
internal–external legitimacy tension, and revealed (re)emerging
flexibility–stability tensions. It was recognized that NHF was
good at establishing projects that addressed important topics,
but the next step, “where do we go from here?” was still miss­
ing. Participants referred to this as the “emperor’s new clothes”
analogy.
[…] it’s perhaps a bit like the “Emperor’s New Clothes.”
We have a strong voice, but I don’t think we have used it.
So, there’s huge potential, I think. If we do more together,
it will have an impact. (Steering group member, May 2021)
It was also acknowledged that real changes were difficult to
achieve.
My intention and hope for NHF were that we would be able
to change… if some laws and regulations hamper sensible
management, that we could come as far as to address this
and say, “This is just nonsense, why don’t we stop it?” But
these organizations are big professional mastodonts, and it’s
difficult to get under their skin, so you can’t just say, “Let’s
do it another way.” (Steering group member, May 2021)
These tensions were largely attributable to a lack of resources
and basic funding, which had continued to be a topic of dis­
cussion. While most agreed that this hampered the level of
activity, opinions differed on whether NHF should pursue a
more formal mandate from the government with subse­
quent funds:
A network appointed by the Government will often have a
time-constrained mandate and allocation, while we see that
the tasks and challenges are increasing. At the same time,
being appointed, for example, for specific parts, would have
made the NHF more visible. (Steering group member,
June 2021)
Some argued that the lack of a formal mandate enabled NHF to
“keep its free voice.” In the top managers’ meetings, several
managers emphasized that NHF should not be too formalized
or institutionalized but should remain a coordinating body.
However, while the top managers’ meetings were established
because their support was considered important for legitimacy
reasons, some participants questioned the value of this support
as top managers were reluctant to grant any funds to NHF: “It
would have been much easier if we had the money and resources,
[…] but the top managers haven’t given us that” (Project mem­
ber, March 2022). Tensions between vertical and horizontal
accountability continued to surface at top managers’ meetings:
In NHF, we trust each other and can speak freely. But imme­
diately after we write something, we need to be very careful.
The directors are resolute: “Work jointly on the natural haz­
ards issue, make it better, but do not spend time meddling
with the different organizations’ responsibilities.” (Secretariat
member, March 2022)
Practices
To cope with (re)emerging tensions, launching new joint proj­
ects was considered key because it proved to be a helpful man­
agement practice. To manage the reemerging tensions related
to flexibility–stability, at the beginning of 2021, the steering
group leader and secretariat proposed establishing a new proj­
ect to evaluate NHF (steering group meeting minutes, February
2021). Five years had passed, and the group agreed that it was
time to evaluate the network. The evaluation was accompanied
by a task force with a mandate to develop a new strategy and
work plan for the period of 2022–2026. This was run as a joint
project for which a broad group of participants across organi­
zations was gathered. This is also a way to increase the level
of activity, which has remained a major concern. The level of
activity had again dropped for various reasons: some work­
group leaders had changed jobs, leaving a vacuum in the
respective projects; the COVID-19 pandemic, which signifi­
cantly interfered with planned activities; and the fact that the
steering group leader was about to retire. To manage these (re)
emerging efficiency–inclusiveness and flexibility–stability ten­
sions, the group decided to launch another joint project on a
relevant topic. This concerned the mapping of cross-sectoral
issues and identifying those “falling between the cracks” and
with no clear responsibilities. The project included broad par­
ticipation among participants and their home organizations
and was endorsed by top managers at the annual meeting.
Furthermore, the network was considered more important
than ever and enjoyed a high level of internal and external
legitimacy: “If NHF hadn’t been established yet, it would have

We identified three primary outcomes of managing the tensions
that (re)emerge in this phase: diminishing, balancing, and
amplifying. The “issues with no responsibility” project con­
tributed to diminishing the efficiency–inclusiveness tension,
which, like before, was reduced while the project was con­
ducted. This time, the result of the project was presented to a
broader audience through open webinars, including local com­
munities and municipalities. The evaluation and strategy proj­
ect also increased the levels of enthusiasm and activity, and
subsequently helped balance the tension related to flexibility
and stability, which persisted but was manageable through the
launch of new projects. Moreover, the project helped highlight
the need for the NHF, which was reinforced by the issue scop­
ing connecting the NHF’s work to climate change, thereby
reducing legitimacy concerns:
I believe and hope that [the evaluation and strategy work]
can revitalize NHF and help connect it to current paradigm
shifts in society […] so it’s extremely good timing. Our ambi­
tion now is to make it more vital, make more out of it, and
modernize it so that more people see its value. (Project mem­
ber, March 2022)
The “issues with no responsibility” project was presented in
the top managers’ meeting in spring 2023. The ambition was
to conclude the meeting with a joint statement from the top
managers. The statement would be publicly announced and
used by member organizations to address the need for action.
However, discussions occurred again around the joint state­
ment, where it was argued that it was not ambitious enough
and that these joint statements needed to be better prepared to
ensure a clear message. The vertical–horizontal accountability
tension remained at the surface and was amplified through
continuous concern over the lack of funds and how it nega­
tively impacted the level of activity. NHF has been increasingly
recognized externally, for example, in various white and green
papers. While this created legitimacy, it accentuated concerns
that NHF could not meet the expectations of action. In a
steering group meeting in February 2024, the project leader of
one of the projects asked the group to help organizations sign
a “letter of intent” to ensure commitment and secure funding.
However, the suggestion received mixed responses, and refer­
ences were again made to the parties’ differences and sectoral
responsibilities.
Discussion and conclusion
This study aimed to understand how participants in public
PONs manage network tensions for network sustainability.
The study of NHF helped us to conceptualize the
“tensions-practices-outcomes” processes across three different
phases of NHF (Table 4). We found that network participants
had to manage emerging and interrelated tensions simultane­
ously and identified four key tension-managing practices that
enabled them to do so: differentiating participation, issue scal­
ing and scoping, mobilizing top managers’ support, and joint
projects. The outcomes of these practices varied from dimin­
ishing, amplifying, and balancing tensions, showing that these
efforts were only provisional, and tensions tended to re-emerge.
Nevertheless, these practices enabled network sustainability,
conceived as “the extent to which organizations within a net­
work continue to engage in substantive collaborative activities
over time” (Kapucu, Garayev, and Wang 2013, p. 105).
In doing so, we answer recent calls in the public administra­
tion literature for process studies of PONs (Berthod and Segato
2019) and contribute to theory-building on managing tensions
in PONs with a “networks-as-practice” perspective. This per­
spective enables us to disentangle the underlying processes of
network tensions, their management, and the practices that
constitute these processes. A practice perspective recognizes
that “it is through the situated and recurrent nature of everyday
activities that structural consequences are produced and
become reinforced or changed over time” (Orlikowski 2010,
p. 35). As such, we comply with other scholars who argue for
understanding a network as “a verb, not a noun” (Nowell and
Milward 2022, p. 30), and argue that this perspective helps to
reconcile and extend the network governance and management
perspectives that dominate the public administration literature
(Auschra and Sydow 2023) by showing the consequential and
relational nature of practices and how they form a mutually
constitutive relationship with network structures. Berthod,
Grothe-Hammer, and Sydow (2017) similarly argued that
practice-based research is valuable, but remains scarce in net­
work studies, mainly because of the methodological challenges
involved in studying practice. Our study demonstrates that it
is possible to study network practices and their recursive rela­
tionships with network structures through longitudinal quali­
tative network research, which helps unpack the intricacies of
networks and tension management (van Duijn, Bannink, and
Ybema 2022). This is not only important from a theoretical
point of view but also for network practitioners, providing
them with important insights “enabling effective reflection in
practice” (Vangen 2016, p. 263). Within this overall contribu­
tion, we discuss two major insights from this study.
Network tensions are complex and dynamic
phenomena
Previous research has demonstrated the multi-level and
multi-relational nature of network tensions by showing that

network participants must manage the duality of tensions both
within the network and toward external actors, including their
home organizations (Human and Provan 2000; Lee 2022;
Lemaire, McKeague, and Sedgwick 2024; Ospina and
Saz-Carranza 2010; Waardenburg et al. 2020). However, our
study extends the notion of tension complexity (Huxham and
Beech 2003) by demonstrating how participants face and man­
age multiple interrelated tensions simultaneously. For example,
the discussion about whether to include the private sector in
the NHF, which reflected the efficiency–inclusiveness tension,
was related to legitimacy issues (e.g., retaining the societal mis­
sion/goal focus but excluding relevant organizations). Further­
more, including members that differed in terms of resources
and capacity while leaving much of the work to a few (i.e., the
secretariat) for efficiency reasons triggered flexibility–stability
tensions after the activity level dropped. This influenced inter­
nal legitimacy because people began to question the value of
the network.
This study shows that these tensions were triggered by miss­
ing formal accountability relationships in the network. Without
funds, it was difficult to make demands and keep participants
accountable, and without demands and control, the commit­
ment and obligation to prioritize NHF activities during a busy
workday decreased. While vertical–horizontal accountability
tensions are highly prevalent in public networks, there is sur­
prisingly limited research on this tension (Berthod and Segato
2019). The current study highlights their importance as a root
cause of many other tensions and indicates that there might be
a hierarchy among network tensions, in which some are more
fundamental than others.
The study shows that tensions are interrelated but also indi­
cates a need for further scrutiny of network tensions and their
demarcations. Although the literature largely converges around
the importance of the three generic network tensions identified
by Provan and Kenis (2008), several types are considered in
the literature (Berthod and Segato, 2019). We included the
vertical–horizontal accountability tension because of its impor­
tance and because it turned out to have some specificities com­
pared to the others. However, studying this tension in more
depth could reveal that accountability and, for example, legit­
imacy are more closely related than considering them as two
different types of tension. A further discussion of this issue is
beyond the scope of this article, but it warrants further
investigation.
Managing network tensions is key to
network sustainability
With a few exceptions (e.g., Lee, 2022; van Duijn, Bannink,
and Ybema 2022), the literature has mainly focused on net­
work managers (Provan and Kenis 2008) and not on other
network participants’ enactment of tensions (Berthod and
Segato 2019). In contrast, our study demonstrates that man­
aging network tensions requires the joint efforts of network
participants. The four tension-managing practices largely
resemble activities and behaviors identified by other scholars,
like “differentiating participation,” which includes what
Agranoff and McGuire (2001) referred to as “activation.”
However, with the emphasis on differentiating, this practice
captures the need for both inclusiveness (which activation
primarily refers to) and efficiency, helping to manage this
tension. “Issue scaling and scoping” is similar to “framing”
and “mobilizing” (Agranoff and McGuire 2001), since this
practice involves determining what the network should focus
on and how (framing) and using this to create a commitment
to the network’s goal and scope (mobilizing). However, this
practice captures the network participants’ ability to manage
tensions, such as those between vertical and horizontal
accountability. A similar argument can be made for “mobi­
lizing top managers’ support,” which the NHF participants
used to manage internal and external legitimacy tensions and
vertical and horizontal accountability tensions. Finally, the
“joint projects” practice resembles “synthesizing” (Agranoff
and McGuire 2001) and “facilitating interaction” (Ospina
and Saz-Carranza 2010), which highlight the important task
of creating favorable conditions for interaction. However,
joint projects not only refer to the facilitation of arenas for
interaction but also to how network participants use and
enact this practice.
Thus, in contrast to previous notions of network activities,
we reveal that the four practices are situated, recurrent, and
consequential in terms of how the network unfolds and is sus­
tained. Our “network-as-practice” perspective helps reveal
how managing tensions is highly related to the structural fea­
tures of the network, such as maintaining a shared governance
mode, the network composition being confined to public orga­
nizations only, and the role of accountability relationships in
NHF. We also show how the practices enabled the participants
to sustain the network while managing tensions over time.
Network sustainability is an issue that is still poorly understood
(Provan and Lemaire 2012), despite recent studies of network
dynamics (Berthod et al. 2017; Lemaire, McKeague, and Sedg­
wick 2024). Although the participants’ joint efforts may not
fully explain why the NHF has been sustained, they likely
played a significant role.
The study demonstrates how managing network tensions is
a continuous effort, given the dynamic nature of tensions, and
that the outcomes of these efforts are provisional (Huxham and
Beech 2003) and sometimes unintended (Berthod and Segato
2019). In NHF, some tensions were managed through deliber­
ate choices that helped diminish them, while others persisted
but were considered manageable. For example, differentiating
participation is a way of balancing the efficiency and inclusive­
ness tension. The tension did not disappear but was considered
manageable. This finding is similar to Ospina and Saz-Carranza
(2010), who found that tensions are not necessarily resolved,
but addressing them can help strengthen the network through
sensemaking processes. The findings also showed that some
practices that helped manage tensions could trigger and even
amplify others. For example, differentiating participation in
mobilizing various organizations implies that they are not
required to engage because of differences in resources and
capacity. This decreased the level of activity and unintention­
ally amplified flexibility–stability tensions (Berthod and
Segato 2019).
The study illustrates that some practices were re-employed,
indicating a learning process, and that tensions might provide
opportunities (Lee 2022). This was particularly true for joint
projects, which yielded multiple purposes and became an
important recurrent practice. However, joint projects also cre­
ated new and reemerging tensions as resources were required,
such as the vertical–horizontal accountability tensions that
emerged and triggered other tensions. These tensions persist
on the surface and continue to challenge the network. These
findings illustrate the paradoxical nature of network tensions,

meaning that they cannot necessarily be “resolved,” but must
be dealt with regardless (Berthod and Segato 2019; Lemaire,
McKeague, and Sedgwick 2024; Saz-Carranza and Ospina
2011). Furthermore, the study confirms previous studies show­
ing how tensions might enhance the collaboration depending
on the response strategies employed (Lee, 2022), thereby indi­
cating a further step from the “both/and” mindset of tensions
to a “more than” one (Putnam, Fairhurst, and Banghart 2016).
Practical implications, boundary conditions, and
limitations of the study
This study offers key insights for practitioners in managing and
participating in public PONs. The parties in NHF were all from
the public sector and shared the overall responsibility for cop­
ing with natural hazards. However, they faced network ten­
sions similar to those in cross-sectoral networks (Provan and
Kenis, 2008). Thus, this study demonstrates that participants
in PONs, who are likely to have much in common, should
expect and be prepared to face and manage tensions. However,
perhaps the most important factor is for network participants
to realize that tensions are seldom settled, as they tend to
re-emerge and require continuous attention and joint efforts
beyond those of network managers. Indeed, a “both/and”
approach is needed to manage network tensions, and given the
opportunities provided by tensions to learn and enhance the
collaboration, this should be extended to a “more than” mind­
set in which tensions are deliberately used for development.
For example, the joint projects in NHF created learning arenas
where participants could learn about each other and build trust
that supported the management of (re)emerging tensions.
Therefore, it is important to understand that tensions are not
equal to conflicts. Despite these tensions, the atmosphere at
NHF was collaborative and respectful, with no open conflicts.
As the network unfolded and the participants engaged in man­
aging tensions, the basis for collaboration strengthened, which,
in turn, enabled network sustainability.
Despite these insights, this study is subject to certain bound­
ary conditions and limitations. Being a public PON without a
formal mandate or formal accountability relationship meant
that the NHF participants did not face many sanctions, at least
not formally, even when their level of activity decreased. This
also means that terminating the collaboration was not a high
risk for the organizations involved. However, the fact that par­
ticipants managed to sustain the network over time despite
facing tensions might provide insights relevant to other types
of public networks about managing network tensions over
time. Even if NHF was a PON, and scholars warn against
comparing apples to oranges (Nowell and Milward 2022), we
assume that most public networks have some goal or purpose,
and therefore believe these findings are relevant to various types
of public networks.
Another issue relates to the context of NHF, as the abilities
of the NHF participants to sustain the network might be
attributed to the characteristics of Norway, with its strong
public sector and collective mindset. This may not be the case
in other countries. As stated in previous literature, the network
form of organization in public administration developed
because of the failure of the public sector to provide public
services (O'Toole 1997). Nevertheless, long-term networks for
preventing natural hazards beyond immediate emergency
responses to incidents are likely to involve public agencies, also
in countries with a weaker public sector. Finally, being a single
case in a specific context means that the study is subject to the
usual limitations of single-case studies, where no statistical
generalizations can be made. To compensate for this limitation,
this study draws on previous research to enable analytical gen­
eralization (Yin 2018).
The above limitations provide avenues for future research.
First, process studies of other types of public networks in other
contexts could help test and further elaborate the complex
nature of tensions and how network participants can manage
them. This could also reveal types of tensions other than the
four that we focus on and help integrate our knowledge of
tensions and how they relate. Second, a multiple-case study
with several public PONs could use our findings as a starting
point to identify patterns of how participants manage tensions
and the situated practices they use. Finally, it would be inter­
esting to compare networks such as NHF, with only public
sector participants, to cross-sectoral networks to reveal the
differences and similarities that can be attributed to the inclu­
sion of different interests, such as the public and private sectors
and communities.

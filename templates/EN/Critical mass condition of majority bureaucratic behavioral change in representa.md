Critical mass condition of majority bureaucratic behavioral
change in representative bureaucracy: a theoretical
clarification and a nonparametric exploration
Danyao Li

*Corresponding author: danyaoli@usc.edu
Abstract
Representative bureaucracy theory examines how bureaucrats’ demographics impact outcomes for clients with shared identities, with
“critical mass” posited as an enabling condition. Yet empirical evidence is mixed regarding where this threshold stands. To reconcile these
inconsistencies, this study emphasizes the need to first clarify the mechanisms that underpin critical mass requirements. Specifically, I attend to
how majority behavior changes due to enhanced representation and evaluate corresponding critical mass condition. Nonparametric analyses of
traffic stop data in two states find that, in Washington, the critical mass where White officers show reduced bias towards Black drivers occurs
when Black officers constitute 6–9 percent of the force. In South Carolina, similar shifts occur at 9–11 and 19–23 percent Black representation.
While findings indicate improved policing towards Black drivers, increased representation still falls short of achieving full parity between Black
and White drivers. No significant critical mass is observed for Hispanic representation in either state.
Key words: representative bureaucracy, critical mass, nonparametric estimation, law enforcement.
Literature on representative bureaucracy examines how the
demographic characteristics of bureaucrats can be converted
into substantive effects for client groups who share the same
identity (Meier 1993b; Mosher 1968). One stream of re­
search highlights the concept of “critical mass,” considering it
a condition for the successful translation of passive to active
representation. Drawing heavily from Kanter’s seminal work
on critical mass (Kanter 1977a, 1977b), representative bu­
reaucracy scholars routinely hypothesize that there exists a
threshold and that active representation may only occur after
the composition of the minority group reaches that point. The
intuition is that members of the minority group will be able
to act in ways that promote their identities, preferences, and
interests when they feel empowered by a sufficiently large
group of peers who hold similar backgrounds within the or­
ganization. Empirical evidence regarding where that critical
mass is, however, is decidedly mixed, varying across policy
contexts, levels of examination, and estimation techniques
(Meier 1993a; Atkins and Wilkins 2013; Andrews and Miller
2013; S. Grissom, Rodriguez, and Kern 2017; Nicholson-
Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty, and Fernandez 2017).
In part, the inconsistency in extant empirical literature
results from a lack of concrete theorizing such that the under­
lying mechanisms that convert demographic representation
to positive outcomes are largely unspecified when expecta­
tions about critical mass are developed. I argue that, while
critical mass may often be viewed as an overarching organi­
zational parameter, it is deeply rooted in intraorganizational
processes that occur at more granular levels, including group
and individual dynamics. To better understand its relevance
to representative bureaucracy theory, it is crucial to explicate
the specific mechanisms where representation happens prior
to any empirical exploration. For example, this study asks the
questions: Whose behavior alters as minority representation
within the organization grows? And is a critical mass required
for that behavioral change to occur?
I attend to how majority bureaucratic behavior adjusts
when minority presence in an organization increases to a crit­
ical level. Representative bureaucracy literature has outlined a
theoretical prediction of it (Lim 2006) but it has gathered only
limited empirical evidence so far and thus remains a largely
underexplored area (see Atkins and Wilkins 2013; Li 2021;
Meier and McCrea 2022 for exceptions). Very few studies
have ever investigated the question of the minimum level of
representation above which majority group members are more
willing to shift their behavior towards minority clientele.
Using traffic stop data from Washington (2009–2016)
and South Carolina (2005–2016), this study evaluates the
critical mass beyond which White police officers change po­
licing behavior in stop encounters with minority citizens.
Theoretically, this analysis adds clarity to the conversation on
critical mass, representative bureaucracy, and more broadly
speaking administrative behavior, by explicating the behav­
ioral change mechanisms underpinning the critical mass re­
quirement. Methodologically, it uses a nonparametric strategy
that makes no prior assumptions about the functional form of
the relationship of interest and thus has great potential to add
more precision to the empirical literature.

Critical mass and performance: where does
empirical evidence stand?
The notion of critical mass was developed by Kanter (1977a).
In Men and Women of the Corporation, Kanter conducted
case studies of 20 saleswomen’s experiences with working in
a male-dominated environment (a multinational Fortune 500
firm with a 300-person sales force). The aim was to better
understand how numerical gender composition impacts
intraorganizational processes. She first used the term “token”
to define the subgroup that represents less than 15 percent of
the overall workforce. Tokens lack power and are in a dis­
advantaged position to initiate changes in the organization.
As a result, they attempt to minimize their differences with
others and keep a low profile. Kanter argued that not until the
presence of tokens reaches a critical mass of 15 percent can
we observe them starting to break silence. She also suggested,
however, that for tokens to assert their social identities and ad­
vocate for their preferences, an even higher proportion should
be obtained (Kanter 1977b). Although some have questioned
the arbitrary nature of her drawing of the cutoff points, many
turn to Kanter’s early work as a framework to study group
size and conformity behavior of minority members, and 15
percent has often been assumed to represent a turning point
(see Meier and Xu 2022 for a review).
Building upon Kanter’s seminal work, scholars of diver­
sity and generic management attempt to tackle the question
of critical mass empirically. For example, business research
posits that corporate decision-making and subsequent perfor­
mance are products of the board composition. It contends—
and generally finds evidence—that an increase in the number
of minorities on corporate boards results in a buildup of crit­
ical mass that will substantially change power balance and
add to firm performance (Bear, Rahman, and Post 2010;
Campbell and Mínguez-Vera 2008; Fredette and Sessler
Bernstein 2019; Mahadeo, Soobaroyen, and Hanuman 2012).
When exploring how large that critical mass is, many of those
studies measure the composition as a “ratio.” However, some
of them end up finding—and sometimes explicitly build their
hypotheses on—the phenomena of “at least three.” Namely,
having three members of the minority group on a corporate
board constitutes a tipping point of change (Joecks, Pull,
and Vetter 2013; Konrad, Kramer, and Erkut 2008; Torchia,
Calabrò, and Huse 2011). Put differently, critical mass is
seen in those works as the size of the minority group, and
three is the magic number. This conceptualization is some­
what different from Kanter’s critical mass and draws from
Asch’s (1955) research on conformity. The famous Asch ex­
periment concludes that a majority size of three would suffice
for the full force of group pressure to be felt and that a fur­
ther increase in the size adds little to the overall effect (Asch
1955). The question of what constitutes a critical mass that
can produce meaningful changes in intergroup processes, as
well as the most accurate modeling strategy to estimate such
a threshold, has been the subject of an ongoing debate (see
Bond 2005 for a review).
Within representative bureaucracy scholarship, critical
mass research similarly draws heavily from Kanter’s founda­
tional work. Empirical studies, however, have produced dif­
ferent findings of where the threshold point lands. Previous
results vary by levels of the organization (i.e., representation
in managerial positions vs. at the street level), data type (i.e.,
aggregate vs. individual), service contexts, and estimation
techniques.
Meier (1993a) was among the first to empirically test if a
numerical threshold is required for Latina principals to posi­
tively impact educational outcomes of Latina students. Using
Florida public school district data, he found that the levels of
critical mass vary depending on the outcome in question, with
many estimates falling between 16 and 26 percent. Atkins and
Wilkins (2013) later found that Black teachers in Georgia
public schools need to obtain a critical mass of at least 20 per­
cent before any significant decrease in Black teen pregnancy
rates can be observed. In another study, Atkins, Fertig, and
Wilkins (2014) use a nationally representative longitudinal
survey of adolescents and find that when Latinx teachers con­
stitute about 40 percent of the school, Latinx student’s self-
reported college expectations begin to increase. This study
also examines cross-group representation and predicts that
when Black teachers make up 70 percent, beyond the sample
range, school connectedness reported by Latinx students
will begin to rise. Using a national representative sample of
schools in the U.S., Grissom, Rodriguez, and Kern (2017) find
that at least 20–30 percent Black/Hispanic teachers is needed
to increase the proportion of Black and Hispanic students
being placed into gifted programs.
In policing context, Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty,
and Fernandez (2017) hypothesize a quadratic relationship
between Black officer representation and the death of Black
citizens at hands of police. In their analysis of the 100 largest
U.S. cities in 2014 or 2015, the authors estimate an inflection
point at approximately 42 percent, after which the number
of police-involved homicides of Black citizens begins to drop.
They also acknowledge that in their sample the very lim­
ited number of police departments with high levels of Black

Some studies do not find supporting evidence for critical
mass. For example, when evaluating the relationship between
gender representation and the performance of fire authorities
in England, Andrews, Ashworth, and Meier (2014) find no
critical mass point among managerial positions. Andrews and
Miller (2013) similarly do not find nonlinearities in how fe­
male representation on police forces affect domestic violence
arrests in England. Using data on national departments in
South Africa, Fernandez, Koma, and Lee (2018) demonstrate
a linear relationship between Black/female representation
and departmental performance. Agyapong (2018) even finds
that after certain inflection points, additional shares of female
teachers negatively impact girls’ performance on math and
science exams in Ghana.
With regards to statistical approaches, a method commonly
used in the literature to identify critical mass point is to es­
timate the model with the representation variable squared
(Meier 1993a; Andrews and Miller 2013; Atkins, Fertig, and
Wilkins 2014; Fernandez, Koma, and Lee 2018; S. Agyapong
2018; Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty, and Fernandez
2017). Another technique is to separate the percentage of
representation into adjacent ranges and then add a series of
categorical indicators in the model to capture nonlinearities
(Atkins and Wilkins 2013; Grissom, Rodriguez, and Kern
2017).
Missed opportunities in estimating critical
mass
To date, it has been challenging to reach concrete conclusions
regarding the existence and location of critical mass points.
This may in large part arise from a lack of thorough examina­
tion of the intraorganizational dynamics that underscore the
need for critical mass. Past research has mostly treated crit­
ical mass as an enabling organizational feature, assuming it
is monolithic. This approach may have inadvertently skewed
empirical tests to some degree.
This study takes a more nuanced approach. To develop more
sophisticated hypotheses, I propose to dissect the evolving
intraorganizational dynamics, following enhanced represen­
tation, into granular levels. First, it is essential to understand
how a heightened presence of minority members in the organ­
ization may impact the behavior of different organizational
subsets, such as majority and minority groups, and whether a
critical mass is needed to initiate these group-level behavioral
modifications. This step, however, is often overlooked in prior
studies. Second, having identified these behavioral shifts, the
next level of inquiry should seek to distinguish the specific
reasons or catalysts for such changes. Unfortunately, most ex­
isting literature has not sufficiently addressed these nuanced
levels of investigation.
In addition to the lack of theoretical clarity, the practical
constraint of data—namely, not having granular individual
data—has prevented researchers from observing bureaucratic
behavior and administrative outcomes at the micro level. For
example, studies using aggregate data, by construction, are
unable to distinguish the actions of bureaucrats from dif­
ferent groups, and thus cannot disentangle if critical mass
points exist correspondingly to specific mechanisms under­
lying representation.
This study posits that, to better understand how critical
mass may apply to representative bureaucracy theory, it needs
to be anchored in a framework where intraorganizational
processes, such as those two layers of complexities highlighted
earlier, are explicitly delineated and articulated. A clarified
theoretical framework allows for a systematic examination
of the intricacy and subtly of critical mass. Through this lens,
it may become feasible to bridge the discrepancies in existing
findings. That said, it is important to acknowledge that the
current study only addresses the first layer of inquiry, focusing
uniquely on the behavioral change of majority bureaucrats.
It is unable to unfold the nuances in the second tier, due to
data constraints. Nonetheless, the examination of majority
bureaucrats’ behavior, conditioned by critical mass of mi­
nority representation, offers a novel and valuable contribu­
tion to representative bureaucracy scholarship.
Is critical mass relevant? Mechanisms of
representation matter
As noted in the previous section, I argue that the relevance of
the critical mass notion to representative bureaucracy theory
depends on the specific mechanisms at play. Representative bu­
reaucracy scholars have outlined several plausible pathways
that connect passive and active representation, the first being
minority bureaucrats’ role adoption of being a “representative.”
When right institutional conditions present (e.g., discretion; a
significant presence of similar others), they are more likely to act
in a way that promotes substantive interests of clients sharing
similar demographic backgrounds (Keiser et al. 2002; Meier
and Nigro 1976; Selden 1997). Second, drawing from diver­
sity management literatures that focus on intraorganizational
processes, such as interactions among co-workers within work
teams (see Williams and O’Reilly 1998; Van Knippenberg,
De Dreu, and Homan 2004; Van Knippenberg and Schippers
2007 for reviews), some hypothesize that the presence of mi­
nority members indirectly affects the behavior of majority
bureaucrats, changing their treatment of minority clients (Li
2021; Meier and McCrea 2022). Alternatively, minority rep­
resentation within public agencies may change the attitude
and behavior of those receiving the public services, leading
citizens to view these agencies as more legitimate and thus to
co-produce with them more willingly (Gade and Wilkins 2013;
Riccucci, Van Ryzin, and Li 2016). While three mechanisms
above indicate an individual-level behavioral change following
enhanced representation, another possibility exists that it may
also generate changes with regards to organizational policies
that contribute further to administrative outcomes (Atkins and
Wilkins 2013; Roch, Pitts, and Navarro 2010).
Note that, when examining critical mass, empirical studies
as cited in previous section operate implicitly or explicitly
with the assumption that the first mechanism of representa­
tive role adoption underlies the production of active represen­
tation. In this study, however, I instead attend to the second
path—the behavioral change initiated on the part of majority
bureaucrats as a result of accumulation of minority members,
and then examine its corresponding critical mass condition.1

The tipping behavior of majority members may occur as a
product of several scenarios, theoretically speaking. Germane
to the purpose of this study, a requirement of critical mass
is suggested by each of these scenarios. First, the presence of
minorities in the organization acts as check and constraint on
majority members, increasing the risk that their discriminatory
behavior against minority clients will be observed, reported,
and held accountable (Juris and Feuille 1973; Thompson 1976;
J. Nicholson-Crotty and Li 2023). If a check-and-constraint
process is at work, a significant presence of minorities is needed
to raise the majority’s concern to a level where they may begin
to curb biased behavior consciously. Second, increased minority
representation may change procedures, policies, acceptable be­
havior, norms and even cultures within the organization, which
in turn regulate majority members’ actions (Atkins and Wilkins
2013; Roch, Pitts, and Navarro 2010). One can reasonably ex­
pect that a substantial number of minorities—especially in key
positions with managerial power—is necessary for the purpose
to change organizational factors that are notoriously difficult
to shift, such as organizational culture.
Another scenario happens when majority members change
their behavior following intergroup contact with minority
colleagues. Contact thesis that predicts the significance of inter­
personal contact in reducing prejudice between groups (Allport
1954) serves as the basic logic for this scenario. The hypothesis
has been substantiated by compelling evidence over decades
in social psychological research (see Pettigrew and Tropp
2006; Paluck, Green, and Green 2019 for reviews). More re­
cent empirical studies find additional evidence supporting the
actual behavioral shift following prejudice reduction (Carrell,
Hoekstra, and West 2019; Mousa 2020). Drawing insights
from contact theory, literatures on diversity and management
focusing on social dynamics among co-workers at the team
level often predict that positive outcomes will be borne out of
several processes enabled by intergroup contact. These included
but are not limited to learning, reduction in intergroup threat
and anxiety, and improved sympathy for others (Ashikali and
Groeneveld 2015; Groeneveld and Meier 2022; Lorbiecki and
Gavin 2000; Mohammed and Linda 2004; Worchel, Andreoli,
and Folger 1977). In parallel, public administration scholars
also find supporting evidence that the contact with minority
colleagues changes majority bureaucratic behavior. For ex­
ample, Atkins and Wilkins’s (2013) qualitative interviews of
public school teachers in Georgia indicate that White teachers
intentionally seek advice from their Black co-workers be­
cause they are cognizant that racial congruence with Black
students matters for improved teacher-student interactions.
Quantitative tests are relatively rare, but a recent study by Li
(2021) demonstrates that White police treat citizens of color
in a similar way to White citizens when they work alongside
more minority colleagues. If majority members’ behavior
alters because of bias reduction and other plausible processes,
as outlined above, that stem from intergroup contact, it is then
a credible hypothesis that the organization will need to attain
a sufficiently large number of minorities in order to sustain
that intergroup exposure.
To summarize, regardless of the specific scenario, it is rea­
sonable to expect that there is a threshold level of minority
representation required to bring about a change in majority
members’ behavior. However, how large the critical mass
should be is a priori unknown. Given the inconclusiveness
in empirical literatures and a lack of strong theory predicting
the precise location of the inflection point, this study does not
delineate a hypothesis that forecasts the specific site of critical
mass but predicts the following:
Hypothesis: Higher levels of passive representation will
lead to changes in majority bureaucrats’ behavior towards
minority clientele only in the presence of a critical mass of
minority members in the organization.
Data, variables, and methods
Data
Expectations above are tested in the context of law enforce­
ment with the use of individual-level data that allow an inves­
tigation of majority police behavior in two U.S. states. These
data, collected originally by Stanford Open Policing Project,2
are drawn from the states of Washington (WA) and South
Carolina (SC). The choosing of states is mostly bounded by
data availability. Although law enforcement data is becoming
more accessible for public and scholarly use, the level of de­
tail provided depends on the reporting protocols of individual
agencies. Of all public-use datasets at the Stanford Project,
these two states serve as the best places available to explore
the topic of critical mass.
Each entry in the original data is a stop record. These
data include the population of traffic stops made by State
Police over multiple years, spanning 2009 to 2016 in WA,
and 2005 to 2016 in SC. Analysis in this study first limits the
sample to encounters that happened between White officers
and Black, Hispanic and White drivers only.3 Stop-level data
are subsequently structured by officer identification number
and year, and then further categorized by the driver’s racial/
ethnic group. Thus, the resulting dataset provides informa­
tion on the annual traffic policing activities and performance
of each White officer with respect to each racial/ethnic group
of drivers. These include a total number of stops, how many
stops led to a vehicle search, and the number of stops in which
contraband was found following the search.
Methods and variables
Given that the location of critical mass is a priori unknown,
this study adopts a nonparametric strategy to investigate the
possible nonlinear effect of minority representation on po­
licing outcomes. A nonparametric approach is well suited for
this purpose in that it is agnostic about functional form and
thus is less subject to misspecification errors. For more flex­
ibility, in operation it uses the procedure of semiparametric
regression that estimates the variable of interest (i.e., repre­
sentation) nonparametrically while simultaneously allowing
for other variables to enter the model linearly.4 The equation

profiling in law enforcement as well as to differential practices
such as affirmative action intended to change the racial com­
position of a police force. Control variables enter the model
in a linear fashion.

the nonparametric approach—the primary focus—to uncover
finer nuances of critical mass conditions.

Further discussion: critical mass in quantity
versus in quality
While the results presented above add evidence to the suppo­
sition that a minimum quantity of minority presence is needed
to generate behavioral change of majority bureaucrats, there
is an interesting finding in this study that the break point
where White police behavior changes does not seem to be
an excessively high number. This is particularly noteworthy
when considering the empirical setting of policing and police
culture, where one may reasonably expect that a remarkably
high level of minority presence is required to convince White
officers to make any behavioral adjustment.
Sociopsychological research that takes interest in “quan­
tity” and “quality” of intergroup contact offers an initial
explanation. That is, both the frequency of contact and the
quality of exchange play a role in individual behavioral ad­
aptation and intergroup relations, although their relative

Conclusion, contributions, and limitations
This study seeks to assess the critical mass condition in rep­
resentative bureaucracy. While prior research has produced
varied findings about where the break point is, this study
aims to bring more clarity and precision to the literature.
First, I argue that the underlying mechanisms through which
passive representation is converted into substantive benefits
needs to be specified prior to any hypothesizing and test of
critical mass. While previous literature has not been able to
offer an adequate level of clarification, this study does so
and attends specifically to the mechanism whereby majority
bureaucrats are impacted by higher levels of minority repre­
sentation. Therefore, it makes a theoretical contribution to
representative bureaucracy theory and advances our under­
standing of the relevance of critical mass in studying repre­
sentation behavior, and administrative behavior in a broader
sense. While the current analysis only tests for one critical
mass condition that corresponds to a specific representation
mechanism, the theoretical framework proposed is valuable
for future studies to avoid potential conflation of multiple
critical mass points.
Second, the modeling techniques used in previous work are
subject to, to varying degrees, misspecification because they
impose assumptions about the functional form of the relation­
ship of interest, whereas that shape is a priori unknown. This
study adopts a semiparametric framework that estimates the
effects of minority representation nonparametrically but flex­
ibly allows linear effects of other variables. Nonparametric
estimations let the data speak for themselves and thus add
precision to the empirical literatures. It constitutes a method­
ological contribution.
Using traffic law enforcement data from WA and SC that
allow one to observe individual-level policing behavior as
well as organizational-level characteristics, it finds evidence
in favor of critical mass requirement in representative bu­
reaucracy. In WA, Nonparametric estimations identify a crit­
ical mass at 6–9 percent Black representation where White
officers’ search accuracy of Black drivers increases. In SC,
equivalent effects arise at 9–11 and 19–23 percent Black rep­
resentation. Interestingly, when comparing Black and White
drivers’ outcomes at the same thresholds, increased repre­
sentation is found to make positive changes towards more
equitable policing, yet full parity between groups remains elu­
sive. In both states, White officers’ search activities remain
almost identical for both Black and White drivers but hit rate
patterns diverge. In WA, their search performance for Black
drivers improves but declines for White drivers. In SC, both
groups experience substantial gains in search accuracy, but
the effects for Black drivers are smaller. No robust evidence of
critical mass is found for Hispanic representation.
This study takes a first cut at a broad research topic, with
several limitations that need to be acknowledged and they
inform future research inquiries. First, the scope of current
research is restricted by data availability of two states and
is limited to a single context of traffic policing. The police
organizations under examination offer a relatively narrow
range of representation. A longer spectrum that may be found
in contexts outside law enforcement will allow one to fur­
ther test the supposition of multiple critical mass points as
suggested by Hindera and Young (1998). Findings in SC in
this study where the range of Black representation is relatively
wide seem to be an early indication of future research in this
direction. More importantly, future work that tests critical
mass in other public service sectors will offer a great oppor­
tunity to build stronger theories that more comprehensively
consider how contextual factors, such as degree of hierarchy,
professionalization, segregation, history, and culture, may im­
pact the location of critical mass.
Second, although this study innovatively tests critical mass
point of one prominent representation mechanism, many
more questions remain to be answered. For one, the alterna­
tive mechanism of active advocacy by minority bureaucrats
cannot be robustly tested in current study due to data limi­
tations. To be specific, law enforcement nationwide is heavily
dominated by White police and these data are no exception.
White officers constitute, on average, over 89 percent of po­
lice in WA and 86 percent of police in SC. This naturally lends
more statistical power to the estimations on their behavioral
change. That said, research in next steps should purse the
investigation when a sufficiently large amount of individual
data is available. Subsequent studies should also investigate
critical mass across other representation mechanisms, such
as symbolic representation and organizational policy change,
since there are ample theoretical reasons to expect that critical
mass may be needed and vary in those situations (see Meier
and Xu 2022 for a review). Levels of analysis will differ—so
do data requirements—depending on types of representa­
tion in examination. More granular data will be needed to
test mechanisms regarding individual behavioral adjustment
while the test for organizational policy change would require
collection of aggregate data over time. Another limitation of
current analysis is that it is unable to tease apart the relative
contributions of each scenario or combination of scenarios
to White officers’ behavioral shift. As noted earlier, it only
tackles one layer of intraorganizational dynamics and is un­
able to delve deeper into finer intricacies. Future research that
uses interviews with officers and a more nuanced qualitative
analysis of their decision-making processes may help to ad­
dress this limitation.
Third, with inadequate information to measure the nature
and content of interactions among officers, this study cannot
differentiate the role of “quantity” and “quality” of intergroup
contact. It however merits future investigation, as discussed in
previous section. Last, the split results for Black and Hispanic
representation this study finds are intriguing; however, it goes
beyond the scope of current research to investigate if and
how critical mass differ contingent on demographic group in

Mechanics of Semiparametric Estimation
Equation (1) can be alternatively stated in a simplified
matrix-vector form:
Y = F (Rep) + Xγ + ϵ

(2)
where Y is a NT × 1 vector with elements of yit. F is the
unknown function f (·) of interest. X includes both elements
of Xit, αi and θt in previous equation. ϵ is the error term
and is assumed to have zero correlation with Rep (i.e.,
E (ϵ | Rep) = 0).
The semiparametric model is fitted using Robinson’s double
residual semiparametric regression estimator (Robinson
1988). In the first step, conditional expectation is applied on
both sides of Equation (2) that results in Equation (3):
E(Y|Rep) = F (Rep) + E (X | Rep) γ

(3)
By subtracting (3) from (2), we have:
Y −E (Y | Rep) = [X −E (X | Rep)] γ + ϵ

(4)
If the conditional expectations are known, parameter γ
can be estimated by fitting the above Equation (4) using ordi­
nary least squares (OLS). If unknown, however, they need be
estimated by calling on some consistent estimators gy (Rep)
and gx (Rep). To approximate the unknown conditional ex­
pectations g, for example, Verardi and Debarsy (2012) pro­
pose to use local polynomial fit. Alternatively, it can be fitted
with other methods such as series estimation (Baltagi and Li
2002). Either way, by estimating E (Y | Rep) and E (X | Rep)
using some nonparametric function g and replace them in
the above equation, two residual components are attained,
namely ϵ1 = Y −ˆg (Y | Rep) and ϵ2 = X −ˆg (X | Rep).
Next, by regressing ϵ1 on ϵ2 using OLS, a consistent estima­
tion of ˆγ is obtained, which is plugged back into Equation (2)
to calculate the error component as below:
ˆU = Y −Xˆγ = F (Rep) + ϵ

(5)

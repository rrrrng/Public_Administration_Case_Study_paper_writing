Abstract
The provision of public services by for-profit and non-profit organizations is widespread in OECD countries, but the jury is still out on whether
outsourcing has improved service quality. This article seeks to nuance existing debate by bringing to the fore variation in service quality between
different types of non-public providers. Building on theories of dimensional publicness and incomplete contracts, we argue that different forms
of non-public ownership are associated with varying intensity of incentives for profit maximization, ultimately affecting service quality. Using res-
idential elder care homes in Sweden as our universe of cases, we leverage novel panel data for 2,639 facilities from 2012 to 2019, capturing the
ownership type of the care home operators, against a set of indicators pertaining to inputs, processes, and outcomes. The results suggest that
non-public providers with high-powered incentives to make profit, such as those owned by private equity firms and publicly traded companies,
perform worse on most of the selected indicators compared to private limited liability companies and nonprofits. Our findings that the intensity
of quality-shading incentives is not the same for all non-public providers have important implications for government contracting and contract
management.
Introduction
The question of whether public and private organizations op-
erate differently, and the implications of this for the provision
of public services, has been part of the public administration
research agenda since at least Simon et al. (1956). The exam-
ination of this question has involved the publicness frame-
work that argues that all organizations are public in some
way (Bozeman 1987). The dimensional publicness literature
has shown that the extent to which organizations are exposed
to political and economic authorities has an impact on their
internal structures and processes, and, eventually, perfor-
mance (Heinrich and Fournier 2004; Miller and Moulton
2014; Moulton and Bozeman 2011; Su 2017). This article
contributes to this literature by bringing to the fore the var-
iation in the degree of publicness among those providers of
social services that are owned by anyone or anything other
than the government (thereafter non-public) and examining
how this variation affects service quality.
Today, non-public providers command a considerable share
of government expenditure on goods and services, amounting
on average to almost 9% of GDP in OECD countries (OECD
2020b). In Sweden, where the government spending on social
services is joint highest in OECD (OECD 2020a), the share of
non-public providers in this sector is almost 20% (Statistics
Sweden 2022). They also exhibit a range of legal ownership
forms, including but not limited to, sole proprietorships,
different forms of partnerships, limited liability companies,
corporations, and also non-profit organizations. Given their
prominence and variation in organizational form, the ques-
tion of whether there is a link between provider ownership
and service quality is important for public sector managers,
who make a hugely consequential decision on whether to
provide a service in-house or to contract it out to a for-profit
or non-profit provider, for policymakers making judgments on
the optimal combination of providers and also for individuals
making life choices about child- or elder-care.
The benefits and drawbacks of allowing non-public
providers to deliver publicly-funded social services have been
widely discussed in academic and policy circles. Advocates
usually build on the classic arguments that private owner-
ship and competition drive prices down and improve service
quality (Shleifer 1998), whereas in nonprofits the advantage
of private property is hinged on “... the organization’s legal
commitment to devote its entire earning to the production of
services” (Hansmann 1980, p. 884). Critics point to various
deficiencies associated with the outsourcing of public service
provision, such as incomplete markets and contracts, or the
low measurability of the quality of complex services (Brown,
Potoski, and Van Slyke 2018; Hart, Shleifer, and Vishny 1997;
Romzek and Johnston 2002). There is also a substantial em-
pirical literature on the quality and cost performance of
public and non-public public service providers (e.g. Broms,
Dahlström, and Nistotskaya 2020; Dahlström, Nistotskaya,
and Tyrberg 2018; Hood and Dixon 2015; Petersen et al.
2022; Stolt, Blomqvist, and Winblad 2011). Although impor-
tant, this literature has focused on the differences between
two broad categories of providers: public and non-public,
therefore underestimating the importance of potential varia-
tion between different forms of non-public providers.
In contrast to such a strict dichotomy adopted by the lit-
erature on the determinants of service quality, we build on
insight from the publicness literature that instead highlights
the extent of organizational exposure to economic (market
forces) and political (government) authority (Andrews,

a fifth of all nursing homes in Sweden are operated by non-
public organizations, and their share is higher in some of the
more populated municipalities. The results of our analyses re-
veal striking differences in performance between non-public
providers. First, nonprofits consistently outperform for-

reveals that nursing homes owned by private equity firms and
publicly traded companies most often end up at the lower
end of the service quality distribution, compared to private
limited companies.
Literature Review
The scholarship on dimensional publicness argues that
“public” and “private” are not dichotomous categories, and
that much can be understood about organizations, including
their performance, by examining the extent of their expo-
sure to economic and political authority (Andrews, Boyne,
and Walker 2011; Bozeman 1987). An important implica-
tion of this research is that the difference between public
and non-public service providers may not be the most im-
portant for explaining how providers perform on service
quality, which points to the need to examine more closely
the extent of publicness within these categories. And while
there is a growing body of empirical research on the effects
of variation in publicness on organizational performance
(Andrews, Boyne, and Walker 2011; Heinrich and Fournier
2004; Moulton and Bozeman 2011; Su 2017), the more
subtle differences between organizations in the less public
end of the publicness continuum has remained understudied.
Similarly, the literature on the effects of outsourcing public
services remains predominantly focused on the difference
between in-house and contracted provision, albeit without
clear consensus on the matter as some empirical studies find
market providers to be associated with better quality perfor-
mance, (Bergman et al. 2016; Blix and Jordahl 2021; Castle,
Engberg, and Liu 2007; Holum 2018; Stolt, Blomqvist, and
Winblad 2011), but not others (Dahlström, Nistotskaya, and
Tyrberg 2018; Forder and Allan 2014); while a third group
finds mixed results (Broms, Dahlström, and Nistotskaya
2020; Petersen et al. 2022; Winblad, Blomqvist, and Karlsson
2017).
There are also empirical studies focusing on the quality
effects of providers of specific organizational forms, albeit
no attention being paid to variation in publicness. First, the
increased presence of providers linked with private equity in-
vestment has generated a scholarly and public debate about
the role of private equity (hereafter PE) ownership in the pro-
vision of social services in such diverse settings as the United
States, the United Kingdom, and Sweden (Duhigg 2007;
Garside 2017; Gupta et al. 2021; Plimmer 2019; Winblad,
Blomqvist, and Karlsson 2017). The PE business model,
described in detail in Gompers, Kaplan, and Mukharlyamov
(2016) and Kaplan and Stromberg (2009), is centered on lev-
eraged buyouts of mature businesses, with the goal of selling
them at a profit within a relatively short time frame. PE owner-
ship is deemed to be associated with exceptionally high-pow-
ered incentives to maximize company value, which may be
either beneficial or detrimental to service quality. On the one
hand, PE’s focus on improving the management practices of
target companies and easy access to credit may boost quality.
For example, Bernstein and Sheen (2016) report a positive
impact of private equity buyouts in the restaurant industry,
as restaurants become cleaner, safer, and better maintained.
On the other hand, PE’s focus on cost-cutting measures, their
short-term horizons, and weak relationships with target
firm stakeholders (Kaplan and Stromberg 2009) could affect
quality adversely. Eaton, Howell, and Yannelis (2020) argue
that quality deterioration after PE buyouts is more likely
in sectors with extensive government subsidies and where
quality is difficult to specify, such as the social services sector.
Given this theoretical ambiguity, there is a surprising paucity
of empirical research on the quality effects of private equity
investments in the social services sector. In other words, nei-
ther theory, nor existing empirical studies (Gupta et al. 2021;
Harrington et al. 2012; Stevenson and Grabowski 2008;
Winblad, Blomqvist, and Karlsson 2017) has so far given a
clear answer to policy-makers and citizens whether PE organ-
izations are suitable providers of social services.
The second strand of this empirical literature focuses on
nonprofits, and presents a rather strong case for a quality
advantage of nonprofits, compared to for-profit organiza-
tions. The argument is predominantly rooted in Hansmann’s
(1980) idea that since nonprofits may not lawfully distribute
generated profits to those in control of the organization (the
non-distribution constraint), they are less likely to behave
opportunistically and sacrifice quality for profit. Another po-
tential source of quality advantage of nonprofits lies in the
alignment of the preferences of principal and agents that is
derived from the mission-oriented nature of nonprofits, which
should have positive consequences for service quality (Besley
and Ghatak 2005).
Most empirical studies that have specifically examined the
impact of provider ownership on the quality of care in nursing
homes (Amirkhanyan, Kim, and Lambright 2008; Chou
2002; Harrington et al. 2001; O’Neill et al. 2003), including
systematic reviews (Bach-Mortensen and Montgomery 2019;
Barron and West 2017; Comondore et al. 2009; Hillmer et
al. 2005), report supportive evidence for the quality advan-
tage of nonprofits hypothesis, but there are also some that

We aim to contribute to the literature by highlighting a
common mechanism for variation in service quality among all
types of non-public providers—the extent of organizational
exposure to economic authority, which informs the intensity
of incentives to maximize profit—and by empirically testing
this proposition using seven years of data from Swedish
nursing homes.
Theory
The argument about the quality-improving effects of market
provision of social services rests on the idea that private
owners are the residual claimants of profit, which incentivizes
them to “innovate and become efficient” (Shleifer 1998, p.
135–137). In other words, the possibility to generate an ap-
propriate profit pushes non-government economic actors, in-
cluding non-public providers of services to improve quality
and reduce costs. However, a powerful counterargument was
laid down by the cost-quality trade-off framework (Hart,
Shleifer, and Vishny 1997). Having started from the obser-
vation that “the quality of service the government wants
often cannot be fully specified” in a contract, Hart and his
colleagues formally showed that contractual incompleteness
enables “significant opportunities for cost reduction that
do not violate the contracts, but... can substantially reduce
quality” (Hart, Shleifer, and Vishny 1997, p. 1128, 1152). In
other words, because service quality cannot be unambigu-
ously described in a contract and comprehensively evaluated
upon delivery, non-public providers are more likely to pursue
cost reduction, sidelining quality (Brown, Potoski, and Slyke
2016; Hart, Shleifer, and Vishny 1997). Governments at all
levels buy goods and services “whose attributes and perfor-
mance requirements are hard to define” (Brown, Potoski,
and Van Slyke 2018, p. 739). The quality of social services,
such as elder care, is challenging to specify for at least two
reasons. First, it has a large number of intangible aspects—for
example, relational features—that are difficult to fully specify
and monitor. Second, the sheer number of relevant aspects of
service quality makes it impossible to spell them all out in a
contract (Brown, Potoski, and Slyke 2016). Thus government
procurement of social services on the market inevitably leaves
the buyer with an incomplete contract and the provider with
ample opportunities for quality-shading.
The cost-quality trade-off framework (Hart, Shleifer, and
Vishny 1997) suggests that all non-public providers face
incentives to engage in cost reduction at the expense of service
quality. Informed by dimensional publicness theory, we ex-
pand this framework by arguing that the strength of quality-
shading incentives is not equal for all non-public providers,
but depends on the extent of organizational exposure to ec-
onomic authority. While in the publicness literature expo-
sure to economic authority is most often conceptualized in
terms of legal ownership (Anderson 2012; Bozeman 1987;
Bozeman and Moulton 2011), we focus on the strength of
profit motive as an angle, through which a further distinction
between the degrees of publicness could be captured (Rainey,
Backoff, and Levine 1976).
We assume that profit-maximization incentives have at
least two dimensions: the preferred margin of profit and the
preferred time when a profit occurs. Every type of non-public
organization that operates on the public service markets can
be categorized on these dimensions. For example, if private
equity (PE) ownership is associated with high margins of
profit in a very short time and publicly traded (PT) with high-
margin short-term profits, then private limited companies
(PL) may prefer lower profits that are realized not immedi-
ately, but over a long period of time. Based on this, we argue
that PE and PT providers have a particularly high intensity
of profit-maximizing and, consequently, quality-shading
incentives, than PL. Nonprofits (NP) are situated at the very
bottom of the ladder of profit-maximizing incentives. Below,
we discuss this argument in more detail for each of the four
types of ownership (for more details and examples, see online
Appendix A).
PE firms typically acquire full control of a mature busi-
ness with the single aim of increasing the asset’s market value
and selling at a profit (Barber and Goold 2007; Jensen 1989;
Kaplan and Stromberg 2009). To achieve this, PE companies
apply a range of financial, governance, and operational en-
gineering tools to control costs. Because PE operate with
borrowed moneys on relatively short terms (usually up to
ten years), they seek to sell their asset within a short period
(Kaplan and Stromberg 2009, p. 123). Thus, PE ownership
is associated with aggressive profit maximization in terms
of both the size and pace of profit realization, which in the
context of the provision of complex social services is likely
to give rise to quality-shading incentives (Eaton, Howell, and
Yannelis 2020; Hart, Shleifer, and Vishny 1997).
PT companies have a similar profit-maximization profile,
stemming from their ownership structure. PTs typically have
a diverse ownership structure, with institutional investors
often being the largest shareholders. Shareholders evaluate
the efficiency of their investments on the basis of returns
accrued, resulting in capital movements on the stock market
to the most profitable companies. While movements of cap-
ital from less to more profitable companies is a normal fea-
ture of well-functioning financial markets, recent decades
have seen a trend toward investors having increasingly
shorter time horizons and a demand for quicker returns on
investment (Sampson and Shi 2023). For example, between
2003 and 2012, PTs from the S&P 500 index used more
than 90% of their earnings to either buyback their own
shares from the open market or pay dividends (Lazonick
2014, p. 46). This short-termism is associated with a change
in the practice of financial reporting by PTs, from annual
to quarterly, that saw light in the 1990s. Statements about
revenue and spending—quarterly earnings reports—hold a
very large influence on share prices, thereby incentivizing
PT managers to pursue strategies of short-term profit max-
imization. Daniel Vasella, the former chairman and CEO of
Novartis AG, described these short-term pressures and their
implications for firm’s behavior: “Once you get under the
domination of making the quarter... you start to compro-
mise in the gray areas of your business... such as incremental
improvements to your products, customer service, employee
training...” (Vasella 2002). Thus, bowing to the pressures of
quarterly capitalism (Barton 2011), PTs are faced with weak

When it comes to PL firms, the intensity of their incentives
for profit maximization is, arguably, the lowest among all
for-profit companies. Since such companies are subject nei-
ther to the “buy to sell” business philosophy of PE, nor to the
pressures of quarterly capitalism, their profit-maximization
profile is likely to be different to that of PE and PT. Although
profit is usually considered to be the main motivation for busi-
ness creation, many small business owners are motivated to
start a business on the basis of non-financial factors, such as
personal or lifestyle considerations (Walker and Brown 2004).
Since a typical PL is a small business, profit-maximization
seems to not be the main motivation for many PL owners. In
other words, the exposure of such organizations to economic
authority is low. Even assuming that PL owners desire as high
a profit margins as PE or PT owners, their expectations in
terms of the timing of profit realization are likely to be dif-
ferent. A PL owner runs a business as a longer-term opera-
tion and, therefore, may prefer lower, but more stable profits,
occurring over a long period of time. Furthermore, PL is more
likely to care about quality than other for-profits because their
usually small size puts them in a greater financial jeopardy in
the case of a loss of an important customer. For example, in
a public service provision quasi-market such as Sweden, the
financial health of many PL providers depends on whether
they manage to satisfy the quality requirements of the pur-
chasing public authority. Similarly, within, for example, a
voucher system, in which customers respond to the deteriora-
tion in quality by “voting with their feet,” PL bears a higher
financial risk than PE or PT. Taking these reasons together, we
argue that the intensity of quality-shading incentives among
for-profits is the lowest in PL, in comparison to PE and PT.
Finally, in NP profit motive is the weakest of all non-public
organizations. While both for-profits and nonprofits may law-
fully pursue profit, there is also a cardinal difference between
the two in the amount of freedom they have with regard to
how they can use it. While the former can, and routinely do,
forward profits to the company’s owners/shareholders, the
latter are barred from distributing any profits the organiza-
tion makes to people who exercise control over the organiza-
tion, and has to “devote its entire earnings to the production
of services” (Hansmann 1980, p. 844). In other words, due to
the non-distribution constraint, nonprofits lack actors who
have a share in control of both the organization and its re-
sidual earnings. Consequently, as predicted by Hansmann
(1980, 2000), and formally shown by Glaeser and Shleifer
(2001), founders/directors/trustees of nonprofits have weak
incentives to maximize profits and to engage in ex post
expropriations of customers, such as lowering the quality
of the products/services after the purchasing agreement is
concluded. Furthermore, nonprofits are dedicated to pursuing
mission-oriented goals (Besley and Ghatak 2005) and there-
fore are more likely to attract individuals with intrinsic moti-
vation to work hard in order to achieve organizational goals
(Park and Word 2012) and more likely to enjoy alignment
of preferences between founders/directors and their rank-
and-file personnel. In the parlance of organizational studies,
nonprofits may have a higher mission valence, generating
higher motivation (Rainey and Steinbauer 1999) and greater
cohesion (Boyd and Nowell 2017; Kim 2004) of staff. For
these reasons, a nonprofit that, for example, posits good
quality care to elder people as its organizational goal, is more
likely than a for-profit company to attend to less tangible,
relational features of care, than to cut down on care quality
in order to achieve other, more measurable, goals. Table 1

Based on these considerations, our hypotheses predict that:
H1: Among all non-public providers, nonprofits perform
better on quality than for-profit providers.
H2: Among all for-profit providers, PL providers perform
better on quality than PE/PT providers.
The Case: Residential Care Homes in Sweden
For most of the post-war period, Sweden has been a model
case of a mature welfare state, in which social services and
education were almost exclusively funded and provided by
the state and/or municipalities (Gingrich 2011). Remaining
mostly publicly financed, the provision of welfare services is
open to non-public organizations following reforms in the
1990s. As of today, quasi-markets operate in key welfare
services such as primary and secondary education, health
care, and elder care (Blix and Jordahl 2021; Blomqvist 2004).
Since breaking with the monopoly of public service provision,
marketization of social services and education in Sweden has
gone faster, and reached further, in Sweden than in compa-
rable countries. Compared to Norway, for example, elder care
provided by for-profit organizations is about three times more
common in Sweden (Ågotnes, Jacobsen, and Szebehely 2020).
However, the majority of elder care is still provided by public
organizations, which is a sharp contrast to, for example, the
United States where public nursing homes care for less than
ten percent of all residents in such facilities (Amirkhanyan
2008). As in other Scandinavian countries, in Sweden the
non-profit sector plays a small role in elder care provision
(Blomqvist and Winblad 2019).
Elder care has been a responsibility of the country’s
290 municipalities since a comprehensive reform in 1992
(Ädelreformen; Government bill 1990/91:14) that dramat-
ically expanded the role of municipalities in its provision.
Today, elder care accounts for more than half of municipal so-
cial service budgets. Senior citizens receive care either in their
own homes (hemtjänst) or in long-term care facilities (särskilt
boende). The budget for the latter makes up about 60% of
the total municipal elder care budget (Statistics Sweden 2021).
Residential care homes—the subject of this study—provide
complex services to the senior citizens of the municipality
when their needs cannot be met through assistance in their own
homes. Such needs are individually assessed by municipalities,

Nationally, the share of care home residents living in
facilities run by non-public organizations is about one in five
(Socialstyrelsen 2020). Furthermore, they operate in about
a third of the municipalities and their share of the local
market varies from zero, often in sparsely populated and
rural municipalities, to a majority, often in the municipalities
in the country’s metropolitan areas (Broms, Dahlström, and
Nistotskaya 2020; Jordahl and Öhrvall 2013).
The non-public organizations that operate residential care
facilities are heterogeneous. The first distinction can be made
between for-profits and nonprofits.
a.	 NP (nonprofits) are the smallest group of providers
and their presence is geographically concentrated: 20%
of care homes in Stockholm and 12% in Gothenburg,
the country’s two largest municipalities, are run by
nonprofits (Suvanto et al. 2017). Most of these organi-
zations operate one or a few facilities, but a few larger
nonprofits operate several facilities. Nonprofits most
often take on such legal forms as foundations or idea-
tional associations (ideella föreningar) with a strong mis-
sion and a set of values. For example, Bräcke Diakoni
is a foundation that started in 1923 with the mission of
achieving a more benevolent society.

(Ågotnes, Jacobsen, and Szebehely 2020; Erlandsson et al.
2013), contains ample internal variation and can be classified
into three groups:
b.	 PL (private limited) ownership ranges from sole
proprietorships, partnerships, or limited companies,
running one or a few facilities, to larger entities like
the Norwegian-owned Norlandia and Danish-owned
Förenade Care, two of a small number of foreign actors
on the market;
c.	 PE (private equity) is a type of ownership that largely
disappeared from the Swedish residential elder care mar-
ket in a wave of initial public offerings (IPOs) during the
2010s. Previously, it was, however, a substantial category,
dominated by a handful of companies—Attendo, Humana,
and Vardaga (previously Carema Care)—that controlled a
large share of the national private residential elder care
market (Ågotnes, Jacobsen, and Szebehely 2020);
d.	 PT (publicly traded) ownership consists of the former
PE-owned entities after going public, alongside the noted
Swedish investment company Investor, which for a long
period was a majority shareholder in Aleris, one of the
largest providers.
Data
To obtain a viable sample of Swedish residential elder care
facilities and information about their main attributes and
care quality, we rely on data from two interconnected surveys
carried out by the Swedish National Board of Health and
Welfare (Socialstyrelsen). The Facility survey documents the
characteristics of individual facilities, such as size, care pro-
file, ownership, as well as a number of care-related attributes,
particularly pertaining to staffing, and is available from 2012
to 2019, except for 2013. The Resident survey measures
residents’ experience and satisfaction with services at their fa-
cility, and is available from 2014 onwards. For more informa-
tion on data sources see online Appendix B. The resulting
dataset consists of 14,613 observations of 2,639 distinct
facilities for seven years between 2012 and 2019, with a mean
coverage of 5.5 years per facility.
To capture the ownership status of providers, we matched
facilities recorded as for-profit and non-profit in the Facility
survey with information from the Business Register of
Statistics Sweden and the Capital IQ database (for a de-
tailed discussion see online Appendix B). The bulk of this
classification was automatic, but we manually cross-checked
observations with missing ownership information and with
deviating information on ownership in the Facility survey and
the Business Register data.

The overwhelming majority of care homes (N = 11,956

Figure 1 visualizes the uneven spatial distribution of non-
public facilities across the country. The figure is a stylized
map of Sweden, where each hexagon is one of the 290
municipalities, and the thick lines are the borders of the 21
counties. While constituting a majority (56%) of nursing
homes in Stockholm county in the east, they also make up
around a third (Uppsala, Östergötland, Gotland) or a quarter
(Skåne, Halland) of all residential care homes in several other
large or densely populated municipalities in the south, while
being less common in the north.
Given the multi-dimensional nature of service quality, it
is difficult to observe “true” quality (Bauer and Johnston
2020; Broms, Dahlström, and Nistotskaya 2020; Van
Slyke 2007). As a solution to this problem, researchers fre-
quently use Donabedian’s (1988) input-process-outcome
framework for evaluating the quality of elder care (Broms,
Dahlström, and Nistotskaya 2020; Comondore et al. 2009;
Winblad, Blomqvist, and Karlsson 2017) as measures on
three dimensions produce a more complete picture of service
quality. Following this approach, we use five empirical
indicators. The first three indicators capture input-related
aspects: staff density, nurse density, and staff education. As
a process-related indicator, we employ the share of residents
with an updated action plan. The final measure, capturing
one type of outcome, is a question from the Resident survey
about their satisfaction with care. Ideally, we would like to
employ a wider range of quality indicators, particularly those
pertaining to objective resident quality of life and health
outcomes, such as bed sores or infection rates, to make a
truly robust evaluation of the quality performance of dif-
ferent ownership forms. Such data is, unfortunately, not
available in the present context, which is an important lim-
itation of this study. Appendix B describes the variables and
their data sources in detail, and Appendix D discusses how
these indicators relate to each other in our data. Figure 2
plots the distribution of the outcome variables.
Estimation Strategy
The nature of our data presents both opportunities and
challenges for estimating the hypothesized relationships be-
tween provider ownership and service quality. On the one
hand, the fact that our sample approaches the total popu-
lation of nursing homes over a seven-year period enables
an empirically comprehensive analysis, poised to produce
findings generalizable for the realm of modern Swedish
elder care. On the other hand, analysis of observational data
requires meeting a demanding set of assumptions. While re-
verse causality is not a serious threat to identification in our
case, we discuss the assumption that no relevant confounders
are absent from the empirical model in detail below.
We identify three sets of relevant confounders. First, there
are broad place-based demographic and socioeconomic
factors that may affect both provider ownership and service
quality. Larger and wealthier municipalities may attract more
non-public providers (see figure 1) and specific types of non-
public providers (see online Appendix E), but also affect the
quality of care through a number of channels, for example,
by having better-trained civil servants supervising outsourced
nursing homes (Dahlström, Nistotskaya, and Tyrberg 2018).
We, therefore, control for a vector of variables (γ): municipal
population size; mean income; their interaction term, since
the combination of large and wealthy municipalities may be
particularly attractive to for-profits; and ideology, capturing
political orientation of the governing party/coalition, which
may affect both the propensity for non-public service pro-
vision and care quality. Because the facility-level data is col-
lected in the first half of the year, but municipal-level data is
compiled at the end of year, we use municipal data in year t−1.
A second set of municipal-level confounders concerns the
senior citizens and the organization of residential elder care
in municipalities. The share of senior citizens in the popula-
tion may affect the structure of the local markets for elder
care (in terms of the overall presence of non-public providers
and the market shares of different types of ownership), which
informs the level of competition, and is in turn consequential
for quality (Besley and Malcomson 2018; Broms, Dahlström,
and Nistotskaya 2020). Regarding the organization of
care, as a growing group of municipalities have adopted
the “choice system” policy, aimed specifically at improving
care by opening up to non-public provision, this should be
accounted for. We, therefore, control for a vector of variables
(δ): 65+ and 80+ are the shares of the municipal population
above the indicated threshold; market exposure is the share
of non-public facilities (in linear and quadratic forms, as per
Besley and Malcomson 2018), and whether a municipality
has a choice system in place. With the exception of the latter
two, all variables are measured in year t−1.

Third, facility-level characteristics may also affect the re-
lationship between ownership and our quality indicators.
There may be a difference between those non-public organi-
zations that outright own care homes and those that operate
publicly-owned care homes for a fixed-term under contract
with the municipal authorities. Non-public “operators” ar-
guably have a different set of preferences than non-public
“owners” that may affect care quality. The overwhelming
majority of “operators” in our data are for-profits, suggesting
that outsourcing is either more attractive to for-profit organ-
izations or that nonprofits are worse equipped to win such
contracts (the latter tendency has been noted in the Swedish
context, see Blomqvist and Winblad 2019; Jutterström and
Segnestam Larsson 2020). Furthermore, facility size and care
profile may affect profit margins and, consequently, variation
in the willingness to bid for a contract between profit- and
non-profit organizations. Size and care profile are also rele-
vant for nurse and staff density indicators. For example, there
are likely more nurses in dementia care facilities than in service
flats. We, therefore, introduce a set of facility-level controls (ζ
): operator is a dummy variable if the facility is operated but
not owned by a non-public organization; care profile is a set of
dummies for type of care offered by the facility (general care,
dementia care, short term care, and service flats); and size,
measured through the number of beds, including its quadratic
and cubic terms to account for non-linear relationships. By
including facility-level characteristics as controls we assume
that they are not affected by provider ownership. Cognizant
that this assumption may not hold, we run analyses without
these potential post-treatment covariates, resulting in sub-
stantively unchanged findings (Appendix F). Table 2 reports
summary statistics.

3.4%, change status between non- and for-profit. Therefore,

basis for testing H1 through this technique. Meanwhile, 67
care homes, or 12.7%, transitioned between the PL and PE/
PT categories, permitting a meaningful application of the
within estimator for intra for-profit comparison (H2).

The results for H1 show that NP systematically (p 0.05
or higher) outperforms all for-profit categories on two out
of three input indicators: staff density and staff education.
Substantively, facilities operated by NP have nearly two more
care workers for every 100 residents than PL, and around
three more compared with PE and PT. Care workers at NP
facilities are also better trained: the share of staff with ad-
equate education is 3.5 percentage points higher than at
PL, and 6 to 8 percentage points higher than at PE and PT.
Moving to the third input indicator, the advantage of NP on
nurse density is only statistically significant in relation to
PT, to the magnitude of about one nurse per 200 residents.
Nonprofits systematically outperform PE and PT providers
on resident satisfaction by around three percentage points,
but the difference between NP and PL providers is statistically
non-significant for this outcome-related indicator. While these
observations are in line with what was hypothesized, there is
a systematic advantage of PE and PT providers on process, as
more residents (circa 1.75 percentage points) in PE and PT
have updated plans, compared to NP. As with satisfaction and
nurse density, the NP-PL difference on our process indicator
is statistically non-significant.
In sum, these results point to a systematic advantage of
NP over PE and PT on the selected measures, except for the
process indicator, but the difference between NP and PL is ev-
ident only on two out of five measures. When NP is compared
to all for-profits as a single category (not reported), NP sig-
nificantly outperforms for-profits on staff density, staff educa-
tion, and satisfaction, but under-performs on updated plan,
with no statistically significant difference on nurse density.
Taken together we treat this evidence as supportive of H1.

Robustness
To further check the accuracy and stability of our findings, we
conducted two sets of robustness tests. The first set considers
a number of feasible alternatives to the estimators and mod-
eling choices for the main analysis. We take the following
steps:
A)	Acknowledging that our outcomes of interest are likely
to depend on their own previous values, we introduced
lagged dependent variables (LDVs) to the pooled OLS
model. This produced coefficients very similar to those in
the main analysis.
B)	By means of the between-estimator, which draws on var-
iation in facility-level means, we consider the notion that
the limited amount of temporal variation in provider
ownership, especially in the NP versus for-profit compar-
ison (H1), could be a sign that the data is best treated
as cross-sectional. Results are reminiscent of the main
results, although the loss of statistical power that this ap-
proach causes produces comparatively wider confidence
bands that sometimes cross the statistical significance
boundaries.
C)	Given that Breusch Pagan Lagrangian Multiplier tests in-
dicate the presence of unobserved facility-specific effects
across the models for all quality indicators, we employed
the random effects estimator, drawing on within- and be-
tween variation in a partially pooled model. We noted
that Hausman tests show significant differences between
RE and FE estimates, indicating inconsistency of the RE
estimator, for two of the five outcomes (see online appen-
dix J). Again, differences to the main results are generally
negligible, with the most consistent exception being a
slight tendency of weakened significance for the NP ver-
sus for-profit comparison for satisfaction.
D)	By employing the Hausman-Taylor (HT) estimator, we
consider the possibility that the facility-level covariates—
most crucially, ownership type—may be correlated with
the unobserved facility-specific effect, by entering the
full set of facility-level covariates in ζ, as endogenous.
Although the HT-estimator can incorporate time invar-
iant regressors, given its similarity to the FE-estimator,
HT estimates are only reported for the PL versus PE
and PT comparison. The results produced by this pro-
cedure are highly reminiscent of those produced by the
FE-estimator.
Figure 5 reports the estimates for ownership from these
regressions, alongside the results from the main analysis.
For ease of comparison across dependent variables, the
coefficients are reported as the standard deviations of respec-
tive dependent variable.

Taken in their totality, the results provide support for our
hypotheses. Regarding H1, nonprofits outperform for-profits
on four out of five quality measures, three pertaining to input
and one to outcome. At the same time, the process, captured
through the updated care plans, revealed itself as a forté of
for-profit providers. A similar picture emerged with regard
to the quality effects of for-profit forms of ownership (H2),
where PL outperforms PT and PE, albeit that the effect sizes
are generally smaller compared to the nonprofit/for-profit
comparison.
Conclusion
Outsourcing of the provision of publicly funded social services
to non-public organizations has spurred a discussion about
their comparative performance vis-à-vis public providers.
Much of the debate on public versus contracted provision is
presented as being diametrical with one form being superior
and the other inferior, disregarding heterogeneity of organiza-
tional forms of contractual provision. This article’s objective
is to closely examine heterogeneity of non-public providers
with regard to service quality.
Drawing on the insights from dimensional publicness
(Bozeman 1987) and incomplete contract (Hart, Shleifer, and
Vishny 1997) theories, we hypothesized that nonprofits are
likely to provide higher service quality than other non-public
providers (H1) and that within the for-profits, private limited
(PL) providers outperform private equity (PE) and publicly
traded (PT) providers (H2). We tested these hypotheses using
seven years of data from Swedish nursing homes and found
that care homes run by nonprofits have, on average, higher
staff density and better educated personnel, as well as more
satisfied residents, but for-profits performed better on the
selected process indicator. We also found that PLs outperform
PE and PT companies on the input indicators and also on sat-
isfaction, but no statistically significant differences are found
with regard to the process. Furthermore, a comparison be-
tween all forms of ownership, including public, showed that
public provision often sits in the middle of the performance
spectrum between the non-public organizational forms with
the highest (PE and PT) and lowest profit-maximization
incentives (NP).
Our article makes several important contributions to the
public administration literature on government contracting
and contract management. Theoretically, we extend the cost-
quality trade-off framework (Hart, Shleifer, and Vishny 1997)
by arguing that the intensity of quality-shading incentives is
not the same for all non-public providers of public services.
This is in contrast to even the most recent relevant public
administration studies on quality-shading in the provision of
public services as they do not go beyond the public/private
(Bauer and Johnston 2020) or in-house/contracted out dis-
tinction of providers (Elkomy, Cookson, and Jones 2019).
Empirically, by focusing on the heterogeneity of non-public
providers, the article has painted a more nuanced picture of
quality performance in the provision of public services than
has previously existed. For example, our results regarding the
intermediate performance of public providers, as between the

consider higher ex-ante demands on service quality, closer
monitoring of organizational performance, and other quality-
assurance measures.
Several caveats, which lead to new research avenues, need
to be noted. First, our claim that some ownership forms are
more likely to cut costs and therefore compromise on quality
would be more persuasive if a comparison of the costs across
providers is undertaken to offer assurance that costs are in-
deed influenced by ownership type. By directly observing only
ownership and a number of service quality indicators, but not
costs, this article has had to assume an important component
of the causal mechanism—differential cost patterns. A natural
extension to this study would be to collect appropriate meas-
ures of costs at the facility level and examine their effects on
quality performance.
A second important limitation concerns the quality
indicators we have used in this article. Although they cap-
ture all elements of a well-established model for evaluating
service quality, the article lacks an extensive set of measures
on resident quality of life and health, such as bed sores or
infection rates. The inclusion of such measures into our anal-
ysis would have provided a fuller picture of performance. To
illustrate, private equity firms may be able to reduce staff/
nurse density, but still deliver sufficient health outcomes for
residents. The lack of robust and varied indicators of service
quality is a serious problem, and it is in the interests of all
stakeholders—authorities, residents and their families, and
academic community—to implement a series of indicators
adequately measuring multiple aspects of service quality.
Third, this article has explored only one dimension of
publicness, namely the exposure to market forces, keeping
the influence of political authority constant. The influence
of political authority is an important theoretical dimension
of publicness and also an important feature of our empirical
milieu as Swedish local authorities vary on many relevant
attributes, including the regulatory framework for elder care
provision (Blix and Jordahl 2021) and quality of public ad-
ministration that manages outsourcing contracts (Dahlström,
Nistotskaya, and Tyrberg 2018), that should be explored
in further research. Related to the latter issue, we acknowl-
edge that the context of our study is somewhat particular
due to the high presence of public providers in the residential
elder care sector, which may limit the generalizability of the
findings, even in the face of considerable spatial variance of
public and contracted provision.

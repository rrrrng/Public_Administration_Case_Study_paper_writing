Abstract
The challenges of implementing public policies and interventions have long been recognized, and a wide range of well-documented barriers fre­
quently hinder effective implementation. We develop a framework of implementation support approaches, distinguishing between two types:
fidelity and professional responsibility. We test the framework in a large-scale, preregistered field experiment involving 250 Danish schools imple­
menting an evidence-based reading intervention. Regression results show that, at the family level, a professional responsibility approach empha­
sizing discretion led to behavioral changes in program take-up and, to some extent, use. A plausible mechanism is increased encouragement from
teachers in this group, as indicated by the parents. Somewhat unexpectedly, teachers most appreciated the fidelity approach. Overall, the findings
suggest that even minor changes in the framing of implementation support have detectable consequences, and while the fidelity approach may
ease teachers’ workload in the short run, granting greater discretion through a professional responsibility approach ultimately enhances benefits
for the target group.
Key words: implementation; support; field experiment; teachers.
Introduction
Implementing public policies and interventions is notoriously
difficult (Sandfort and Moulton 2020; Thomann, van Engen,
and Tummers 2018; Winter, Mikkelsen, and Skov 2022). Tra­
ditionally, two theoretical frameworks have competed in the
study of implementation: the top-down framework, with a
focus on political control and implementation as planned, and
the bottom-up framework, with a focus on the implementing
frontline workers and their discretion to implement as needed
given their work context (Lipsky 2010; Sabatier 1986). These
two frameworks are quite different considering who defines
successful implementation and what it means, the acceptance
of divergence from the original plan or policy, and the trust in
frontline professionals (Hill and Hupe 2022; Winter 2012).
They do agree, however, that implementation requires support
to overcome implementation barriers and succeed (Fox et al.
2022; Nilsen et al. 2013; Powell et al. 2012; Pressman and
Wildavsky 1973; Sabatier and Mazmanian 1980). They are
also similar in the sense that as general frameworks they have
proven difficult to test empirically.
In this study, we extract core aspects distinguishing the two
frameworks and develop this into testable theories. More spe­
cifically we argue—in line with Sandfort and Moulton (2015,
2020)—that core structures in the multilayered public admin­
istration are important, but are also malleable and subject to
human ingenuity and discretion. Therefore, the approach to
implementation support may matter for how programs are
implemented. From the top-down framework we develop an
approach emphasizing fidelity in the implementation process,
and from the bottom-up framework we develop an approach
emphasizing employees’ professional responsibility for adapt­
ing the program to their specific context.
We define implementation support as resources that imple­
menting organizations or managers can attach to the interven­
tion with the aim of assisting leadership and staff with the
implementation (Albers et al. 2021). Seen from a top-down
perspective, such implementation support would be expected
to focus on minimizing the discretion that creates variation in
implementation through control and adherence to protocol.
We call this approach to implementation support “fidelity.”
If the implementation problem is instead seen from a
bottom-up and human agency perspective as a lack of adapt­
ability of the intervention to complex environments and dis­
cretion for frontline employees, the ideal implementation
support would focus on the delegation of responsibility to
maximize discretion at the point where the problem is most
immediate (Elmore 1979, 605), and there should be profes­
sional responsibility for the work carried out (Sandfort and
Moulton 2020; Yukl and Becker 2006). We call this approach
to implementation support “professional responsibility” (PR).

We test the effect of the two fundamentally different
approaches to implementation support in a large-scale, prereg­
istered1 field experiment aimed at implementing an
evidence-based reading intervention, READit, in public schools.
The intervention is rolled out in 250 public schools in Denmark
(≈17,000 students). The READit intervention has been shown
to be effective in the Danish school context (Andersen and
Nielsen 2016), but it has also proven hard to scale (Andersen
and Hvidman 2024). Unlike most implementation studies,
which are often underpowered and rarely provide causal evi­
dence from randomized trials (Miller, Smith, and Pugatch
2020), we conduct a nationwide field experiment, offering
more leverage in terms of both causality and power than most
studies to date (Glisson et al. 2010).
We compare three approaches to implementation support:
(1) business-as-usual (BAU) support, in which there are a few
simple support materials and a hotline mimicking BAU support
when scaling up educational interventions; (2) fidelity, where
the focus is on adherence to intervention protocol and an exten­
sive set of support materials, simulating the control approach
to implementation; and (3) PR, which offers freedom of choice
among the same extensive set of implementation support mate­
rials that is offered in the fidelity condition as well as a focus
on discretion, professional identity, and own responsibility,
mimicking the human agency approach to implementation. In
testing a set of implementation support tools that are almost
identical against a BAU support, we discover that the difference
between the two approaches, fidelity and PR, lies in the focus
on discretion and the framing of the support.
Following Sandfort and Moulton (2015), we study the imple­
mentation process by measuring implementation outcomes at
both the system level of professionals (teachers) and at the
target group level (families). Furthermore, we distinguish
between the perceptual and behavioral measures of the imple­
mentation process that are available in this study. In other
words, we study the whole implementation process of the inter­
vention at both levels, with perceptual as well as behav­
ioral data.
Starting with the behavioral measures, results show support
for the PR approach: the take-up of the program among fam­
ilies in this condition is larger, and as expected in one of our
two main preregistered hypotheses. The use of the online plat­
form also seems larger in the PR group than in the fidelity group
(significant at the 10 pct. level). Next, results from the percep­
tual measures show that teachers in the fidelity group—as
hypothesized—found the intervention more feasible than the
control group did. In contrast to our expectations, the teachers
in the fidelity group, not the PR group, also found the program
to be more appropriate in terms of supporting the skills of their
students and they were more committed to implement and
encourage families to participate. However, the families in the
PR group perceived more teacher encouragement.
The study has important implications for public administra­
tion and the study of implementation. First, the results show
that even rather modest changes in the approach to, or framing
of, implementation support create detectable differences in the
implementation process. Second, and more specifically, the
results highlight a mismatch between the fidelity approach
initially preferred by professionals and the professional

Theory
Implementation theory in public administration
In their classic study of a federally mandated economic devel­
opment program in Oakland, California, Pressman and
­Wildavsky (1973) highlight the many barriers to implementa­
tion. They view implementation as a linear process that depends
on a chain of links between stakeholders. Each stakeholder’s
input and the cooperation between them must be close to 100
percent to avoid a cumulative shortfall, or what they call an
“implementation deficit” (ibid., p. 107). When a project is
scaled up, both the content of the inputs and the degree of
cooperation between stakeholders likely need to be maintained
if the same outcomes as in small-scale pilots are to be achieved.
Building on these insights, two classic frameworks of policy
implementation were developed: the top-down and bottom-up
frameworks (Winter 2012). Top-down theories highlight the
importance of the political and democratic mandate behind
policies and adopt a planning-and-control perspective (Majone
and Wildavsky 1979, 165). In this view, what is politically
decided should be implemented, and if it is not, implementation
has failed. The solution is to focus on fidelity—that is, the
degree of exactness with which the policy or intervention is
implemented—and to use control procedures to secure this
fidelity. The central analytical question in the top-down tradi­
tion often becomes which factors or obstacles derailed the
intended implementation (Mazmanian and Sabatier 1981;
Winter 2012).
Bottom-up theories, by contrast, emphasize that many imple­
mentation failures occur when an intervention or policy reaches
the public employees at the frontline who must carry it out in
practice. Bottom-up researchers stress that these employees
often have very different perspectives and understandings of
the challenges compared to politicians and public managers,
who typically design and decide on the solutions. The standard
of evaluation is, therefore, not whether the decision was imple­
mented as originally designed, but whether there was attention
to the specifics of the context at the frontline. Since these
employees often end up exercising more influence over imple­
mentation than decision-makers, the bottom-up approach
accepts—and even expects—that implemented interventions
and policies will vary across contexts (Elmore 1979; Thomann,
van Engen, and Tummers 2018).
Several studies within the public administration literature
have attempted to bridge the two meta-theories (e.g., Matland
1995; Sabatier 1986; Winter 1990). A recent attempt by
­Sandfort and Moulton (2015) builds on theories of structura­
tion, pragmatic philosophy, and strategic action fields. The

argument is that structures such as resources and rules clearly
matter for implementation. At the same time, however, imple­
menters use their discretion to adapt these structures and align
them with local contexts.
According to Sandfort and Moulton (2020), many
approaches see the fundamental problem of implementation
as one of controlling the replication of an already tested inter­
vention. This control approach emphasizes the technical
aspects of implementation and external supervision to ensure
as little variation in implementation across organizations as
possible. If evidence-based interventions are to scale up prop­
erly, there is a need to control the implementation of them to
replicate findings. Moreover, there is a focus on the institutional
structures and resources that set the stage for the
implementation.
This control approach can be found in numerous implemen­
tation theories. A prominent example is implementation science
(Fixsen et al. 2015, 2009). This literature has generally called
for a focus not only on creating evidence for programs but also
on the implementation of such programs—especially fidelity
and implementation as intended by decision-makers and the
research template to replicate the conditions that made an inter­
vention evidence-based in the first place (Fixsen et al. 2015).
As such, implementation science fits in with the control
approach, and in some cases the result has been long check lists
with structural and management factors that need to be taken
into account (Damschroder et al. 2009). It has a strong focus
on fidelity and a disapproval of adaptation, or “tailoring”
(Albers et al. 2023; Fixsen 2025), and adjustments on
the ground:
[N]early all of the data indicate that high fidelity implemen­
tation of evidence-based programs produce better outcomes
for consumers (e.g., Landenberger and Lipsey 2005). Adap­
tation of core aspects of evidence-based programs does not
appear to be a good idea if the benefits of those programs
are to be preserved. (Fixsen et al. 2009, 535; Winter and
Szulanski 2001)
The other approach in the literature according to Sandfort
and Moulton (2020) is human agency. Crucial in this approach
is the application of frontline employees’ professional discre­
tion in the context at hand. An important insight from the
bottom-up studies of implementation is that too much control
may demotivate frontline workers, and it may dismiss the infor­
mation that frontline workers have about the individual clients
they serve (Elmore 1979; Tummers and Bekkers 2014). Hence,
the human agency approach emphasizes the necessity of dis­
cretion and the way individuals facilitate change within orga­
nizations and the social context that they inhabit.
Moreover, according to the empowerment literature, human
agency in work generally enhances the feeling of responsibility
for work tasks and increases motivation and job satisfaction,
which can help ensure implementation success (Thomann, van
Engen, and Tummers 2018; Yukl and Becker 2006, 212). In
this way, responsibility can be a product of human agency and
adaptation, which stands in opposition with the control view
that responsibility lies in being held accountable.
The main point is that both structures and human agency
matter to how policies and interventions are implemented.
Structures, like rules and resources, shape how action is directed
and are fundamental to how organizations are able to
implement. But so are the people responsible for implementing,
because they interact with those structures and reshape them
to match their social context. Using the theory of “strategic
action fields,” Sandfort and Moulton (2020) and Moulton and
Sandfort (2017) describe how actors engage in the structuring
of both tangible elements, such as rules and resources, as well
as more intangible elements, like routines and culture, to fit
them into their organizations during an implementation pro­
cess. Importantly, this view underscores how structures and
agency interact in practice. Implementers work within existing
rules and routines, but they also adapt and reorder them.
As such, structure and agency co-evolve over time and shape
the implementation process.
We focus here on a tangible resource in the form of imple­
mentation support and how the public administration and
managers can affect the structuring of this resource through
the approach they use to promote it (Sandfort and Moulton
2020, 151).
Implementation support and its tools
Despite the different, competing, and sometimes contradictory
approaches to implementation, we argue that many of them
share the fundamental notion that implementation requires
support to overcome barriers and succeed (Fixsen et al. 2015;
Fox et al. 2022; Nilsen et al. 2013; Pressman and Wildavsky
1973; Sabatier and Mazmanian 1980). In this section, we distill
various tools of implementation support that we argue are
compatible with most approaches. Implementation support is
a tangible, quite visible, resource, and also part of the system
in the organization. But it is also malleable and subject to
human agency and discretion. Therefore, we move on to con­
sider how the resource of implementation support is changed
and used in different ways, in part depending on the approach
that is taken to it by decision-makers.
In the literature, implementation support goes by many
names and meanings. It can be “prevention support systems”
embedded in its own organization, overseeing multiple imple­
mentation efforts across a policy domain (Mitchell, Florin, and
Stevenson 2002); it can be hiring and training staff (Powell
et al. 2012); it can be technical assistance (Katz and Wanders­
man 2016); it can be simple written support material designed
to help implementers in their daily work with overcoming bar­
riers; and much more. The overarching goal of implementation
support is, however, relatively clear: It should address the bar­
riers and facilitators in implementation and help maximize
implementation success (Albers et al. 2023).
Often, implementation support is called “implementation
interventions,” “implementation strategy,” “enablers,” or
“facilitators” in implementation science (Albers et al. 2021;
Katz and Wandersman 2016; Mitchell, Florin, and Stevenson
2002; Powell et al. 2012), whereas “implementation resources,”
“policy instruments,” “tools,” or “coordination” have been
common terms in policy implementation. However, in policy
implementation, implementation support is often considered a
part of the policy itself (Nilsen et al. 2013, 7).
There is no agreed upon definition of implementation support,
but Albers et al. (2021, 148) defines it as the following:
“[A]ny activities and processes aimed at assisting leadership
and staff of human and social service organizations and
­systems  in implementing, sustaining, and scaling RSIs
[research-supported interventions] for population impact.”

This definition points to both activities and processes that
can help staff and leadership implement, sustain, and scale an
intervention. However, it does not see the possibility of the
target group or end-users having an important role in imple­
mentation. Taking our cue from the coproduction literature
(McMullin 2021), we believe that in some policies and inter­
ventions, the target group can play a vital role in implementa­
tion. Therefore, implementation support must, and often does,
address the target group as well (Teuscher et al. 2017). In our
case, this could be assisting parents in signing up for an online
reading intervention.
Importantly, implementation support is not the specific
implementation actions, nor is it the staff carrying out
the  implementation itself. It can, however, be staff
members—sometimes called “implementation agents,” “pur­
veyors,” or “implementation support practitioners”—that are
responsible for assisting with the implementation, or it can be
the strategy behind recruiting specific staff members (Bührmann
et al. 2022; Fixsen et al. 2009). Mainly, it is the help imple­
menting staff receive through activities, processes, or concrete
material to implement a policy or intervention.

Two approaches to implementation support
We will argue that, instead of defining the tools of implemen­
tation support, the control and human agency approaches pri­
marily determine the degree of discretion in their use and
application.
The fidelity approach dictates that implementation support
focuses on control and informing implementers about the
expected actions. It is a focus on monitoring, supervision, adher­
ence to protocol, delivering data about implementation perfor­
mance, and reminders (Fixsen et al. 2009, 2015). A fidelity
approach places trust on leaders and “coaches” (Fixsen et al.
2009, 534) more than frontline professionals to secure intended
implementation. High fidelity is key within this approach.
In contrast to the fidelity approach, the PR approach focuses
on professional discretion, responsibility, creative adjustment
to the context, and “fixing the game” (Bardach 1977, 273;
Sandfort and Moulton 2015). This approach to support
emphasizes the delegation of responsibility to maximize dis­
cretion at the point where the problem is most immediate
(Elmore 1979, 605), and it includes discretion and the appli­
cation of implementation tools, knowledge sharing, and pro­
fessional responsibility for the work carried out (Thomann,
van Engen, and Tummers 2018; Yukl and Becker 2006).
Responsibility is here thought of as responsibility taken by the
professional employee because of the agency given—not
responsibility in the external accountability sense.
Figure 1 summarizes the theoretical argument of our two
different approaches to implementation support. As argued
above, the support tools might be the same in the two
approaches, but the framing and the discretion in “implement­
ing” the implementation support are different.
There is reason to believe that both the fidelity and the PR
approaches to implementation support can work. The fidelity
approach has a clear-eyed focus on supporting frontline work­
ers in replicating the results from previous trials. This also
means stressing the importance of using the supplied support
material and tools—and in the right way. The tools have been
selected with a clear goal in mind: replicating the positive
results that previous trials have produced. Therefore, it should
be used with fidelity.
Much evidence from the implementation science literature
points to the efficacy of controlling the implementation—including
the implementation support—this way. The central argument
is that if there is evidence for an intervention, then the essential
components need to be replicated to scale the evidence to other,
perhaps larger, contexts. And this kind of fidelity needs support
and supervision, not discretion, adaptation or tailoring
­(Fixsen 2025).
The PR approach receives its merit from public administra­
tion and professionalism literatures. If discretion and respon­
sibility is acknowledged as essential to performance in the
delivery of public services, the implementation support
approach should encourage it (Thomann, van Engen, and
Tummers 2018). Few would expect an implementation process
that follows the protocol in every aspect, in spite of the target
group’s different needs and special context, to work properly.

Evaluating implementation effectiveness at the
system and target group levels
Implementation outcomes are typically different than policy
outcomes (Ogden and Fixsen 2014, 5). The definition of
implementation effectiveness and success is a challenge in the
literature because goals are often vaguely defined and the
top-down and bottom-up approaches have different views on
what constitutes successful implementation (Winter 2012).
Following Sandfort and Moulton (2015, 12–16), we study
implementation effectiveness at two levels that are imperative
to activate for implementation to happen: change in systems
operations at the organizational level where the professionals
operate, and change at the target group level. At the same
time, we distinguish between changes in perceptions and
changes in behavior.
Whereas behavioral changes in the target group are crucial
for programs to have any impacts, changes in perceptions may
be seen as part of the mechanism that leads to behavioral
changes. We, therefore, focus our main hypotheses (H1 and
H2) at the behavioral level of the target group and have two
supplementary hypotheses about the mechanisms relating to
perceptions at the system level (MH1 and MH2).2
We expect that one of the reasons for the implementation
challenges is lack of implementation support. Thus, we
expect that both approaches will actively support the imple­
mentation of a program: the fidelity approach focusing on
planning and control, and the PR approach focusing on pro­
fessionalism, discretion, and responsibility. Both approaches
will be more successful than an approach without implemen­
tation support.
However, we also expect that the implementers with imple­
mentation support focusing on PR will be more successful.
Because there is more autonomy in the PR approach, a feeling
of responsibility for the implementation task and ensuing moti­
vation and satisfaction are expected (Yukl and Becker 2006,
212). Higher motivation among professionals is suggested to
lead to higher engagement and performance (Wright 2001).
We, therefore, preregistered the following two primary hypoth­
eses concerning behavior at the target group level (where the
“baseline group” is the BAU group):
H1: Use of the reading program among the families will be
higher in the fidelity support group than in the base­
line group.
H2: Use of the reading program among the families will be
higher in the PR group than in the fidelity support group.
To obtain a behavioral measure of parents’ use of the pro­
gram, we recorded their activity on an online platform that
was part of the program.

The two approaches may enhance implementation for dif­
ferent reasons. On the one hand, we expect that the fidelity
approach enhances teachers’ perception of the feasibility of the
program because of this approach’s focus on planning and
control, which might speak to the teachers’ perceptions of
whether the intervention is practically doable. On the other
hand, we expect the PR approach to enhance the teachers’
perception of how appropriate the intervention will be. This is
because of the PR approach’s focus on professional identity
and discretion in delivery, which might make teachers think
that the intervention is more compatible and fits better with
their local context. We, therefore, preregistered the following
mechanism hypotheses:
MH1: Teachers’ perceptions of feasibility of the intervention
will be higher in the fidelity support group than in the base­
line group.
MH2: Teachers’ perceptions of the appropriateness of the
intervention will be higher in the PR support group than in
the fidelity support group.
Design and data
The program: READit
The intervention that we support in different ways in this study
is READit, an evidence-based reading intervention in Danish
schools for children in first and second grade. It builds on pre­
vious studies (Andersen and Hvidman 2024; Andersen and Niel­
sen 2016) and consists of a shared book reading program that
emphasizes the importance of parents reading with their chil­
dren. The program supplies families with a bag of four books,
parent information on how to do shared book reading, and a
homepage where the families can find new books, instructional
videos, and inspiration for finding more on their own. The gen­
eral READit approach has been shown to be effective in the
Danish school context (Andersen and Nielsen 2016), but it has
also proven hard to scale (Andersen and Hvidman 2024).
READit emphasizes practical parental strategies, for example
parents are encouraged to ask questions before, during, and
after reading the book. Furthermore, READit adds a large dig­
ital component to previous versions with the addition of
e-books and instructions for parents and a support homepage
for participating teachers. The e-books and supporting material
have been translated into 11 languages, fairly matching the
presence of the largest language groups in Denmark.

The role of the teachers in implementing READit
Although READit is a family-based intervention, the teachers
are important implementation agents in the intervention. First,
contact persons3 and the children’s teachers have an important
role in having families sign up for READit. In other words, the
teachers’ first role in implementing READit is to secure a large
reach of the intervention. That is why we seek to support teach­
ers in this role through implementation support and use take-up
as an outcome (see below).
Second, the teachers have an important role in engaging the
families in the READit intervention. Part of READit lies in the
classroom, where teachers are supposed to engage students in
reading the material, read some of the available books in class,
and award stickers to the children based on the reading at
home. The teachers can also use the books in the bag or the
e-books on the homepage in the classroom to further the link
between home and classroom. We also seek to support the
teachers in this role by supplying extra material, ideas, and
information on the use of the READit platform for classroom
activities as a part of the implementation support. Therefore,
we also have the families’ use as an important downstream
outcome variable (see below).

a cluster-randomized controlled trial that randomly allocates
schools (n = 250) to one of three different levels and types of
organizational support: (1) BAU, (2) fidelity, and (3) PR.

Data
We recruited schools for the project by inviting 368 randomly
selected Danish schools to participate in the intervention via
email. The email was followed up by either another email or a
physical copy of the bag of books sent to the school, and finally,
we telephoned the schools that had not yet reacted to our invi­
tation. The study began in August 2022 and ended in May 2023.
We recruited 250 contact persons (in 255 schools).5 A few
dropped out during the intervention period. At the grade level,
first and second grade classes could sign up, and within them,
families and students could sign up.
Randomization
The randomization between the three groups was carried out
at the contact person level. Some schools have shared admin­
istrations, and thus, sometimes chose the same person as con­
tact person for the project in both schools. We, therefore,
randomized at the contact person level across all schools. The
contact persons may influence the implementation. Random­
izing at the contact person level will not confound the results,
though, because differences in skills and diligence of the contact
persons will be evenly balanced across the experimental con­
ditions in expectation.6
The randomization itself was a simple stratified randomiza­
tion with equal probability within strata. We stratified the ran­
domization by two variables: (1) sign-up process, and (2)
approval of data agreement. The sign-up process is measured
by how many contact attempts were made to the school or
contact person before they signed up to the project. We invited
them using various methods: mail, reminders, and telephone
calls. The stratification variable measures how many of these
methods it took for the school to sign up to the READit project:
(1) no reminder, (2) a reminder in the form of a physical sample
of the bag of books or an email, and (3) a telephone call.
5  We conducted a power analysis based on the 250 recruited schools. The
power analysis is conducted with a power of 80%, alfa of .05, ICC of .15.
With some assumed attrition at both class level (first and second grade) and
student level, we calculate power with 250 schools, 922 classes, and 17,877
students. The result of the power calculation indicates a minimum detectable
effect of .175 standard deviations when comparing averages (e.g., in our use
variable), and we at least need a treatment effect of 7.5 pct. points on the
take-up variable (at least increasing the assumed 23 pct. [Andersen and
­Hvidman, 2024] in the control group to 30.5 pct. in the treatment groups).
6  Randomization at contact person level only matters in five cases where the
contact person is the same for two schools.
Approval of data agreement is a variable that measures
whether the school approved the data documents before August
11, 2022 or not. The two variables used for stratification of
the randomization was chosen because they might measure
organizational capacity, thereby predicting our implementation
outcomes. With the two variables, we end up with six strata
that are taken into account in the analyses (Gerber and Green
2012). Table B1 in the supplementary file shows a balance test
on available background variables. There are no significant
differences between the experimental groups.
Measurement
We measure outcomes at both levels of implementation (see
fig. 2). At the level of systems operations, we have a number
of survey outcomes that are obtained from a pre-survey before
start of the intervention and a midway survey conducted when
the intervention was well underway among the teachers tak­
ing part in READit. The pre-survey was collected in
August–September 2022, and the midway survey was collected
in May–June 2023.
First and foremost, we measure appropriateness and feasi­
bility (Proctor et al. 2011) in these surveys to be able to answer
hypotheses MH1 and MH2 (see section D.1 in the supplemen­
tary file for the wording of all survey questions). Second, we
also present results on items about the perceived policy efficacy
of READit (Winter, Mikkelsen, and Skov 2022), discretion
when implementing READit (Tummers and Bekkers 2014),
commitment to implement READit (Cramer et al. 2021), and
satisfaction with the implementation support provided. Survey
measures with more than two items (feasibility, appropriate­
ness, policy efficacy, discretion) are converted to additive indi­
ces based on Cronbach’s alpha values and confirmatory factor
analysis (see section D.2 in the supplementary file for index
statistics). We also quantify the number of downloads of the
support materials that teachers have in each group, bearing in
mind that they had access to different materials across groups.
The behavioral measures at the systems operations level are
choice of material and teacher orders, both of which were mea­
sured on the online platform. Only the teachers in the PR group
had the opportunity to choose the implementation material, so
this measure is merely descriptive. Teacher orders are also
placed and registered on the online platform. These are extra
orders of the physical materials, offered free of charge, to be

At the target group level, we follow Cotton et al. (2020) in

turns out, the downloads of information and logins to the plat­

(see below). To further inform interpretation, we also present

not preregistered: whether parents ever got to the first page on
the online platform, and whether they ever opened an e-book.
As a secondary outcome, we preregistered that we would
measure the take-up (enrollment) of families at the schools into
the program, which is obtained from our own system. The
take-up is measured as the families’ own sign-up to the pro­
gram, which could be done until October 31, 2022. After this
date, teachers could sign children up as well. Families with
children who are registered at the schools in August are
included, and their treatment status depends on their registra­
tion in this period. We exclude families who appear in more
than one group due to children in different schools to avoid
spillover effects.
Furthermore, two surveys were sent out to the participating
families. These surveys contained one item asking the parents
to which extent they felt that their teacher had encouraged
them to participate actively in READit. It should be noted that
this outcome is not part of the preregistration since the oppor­
tunity to include it in the survey first arose after registration.
In terms of control variables (apart from the strata variables),
we use three basic background variables from the registries
when we analyze take-up and use of the program. First, we use
a ranking of parents’ income and education to pinpoint if the
child has high socioeconomic status (SES). An average ranking
of both parents’ education and income is calculated, and we
define high SES as being above the median in this measure.
Second, we use a variable reflecting whether the child has immi­
grant or descendant status. Third, we use a measure of the
number of children in the family. Slightly other variables are
used as control when we analyze survey data from parents
(education) and teachers (experience, age, gender, whether they
are a reading tutor).

Discussion and conclusion
This study has argued that there are two different approaches
to implementation support that build on the control and human
agency distinction apparent in the classic public administration
literature on implementation (Sandfort and Moulton 2020):
fidelity and professional responsibility. The fundamental dif­
ference lies in the view on discretion: Is it the challenge (fidelity)
or the solution (PR)? The common implementation support
tools are, we argue, often the same in the two, but the approach
and framing of the process are different. In the study, we syn­
thesize the different conceptualizations of implementation sup­
port, develop a framework wherein we operationalize how
classic control and human agency perspectives map onto spe­
cific implementation support approaches, and rigorously test
such theoretically derived interventions in a large-scale field
experiment.
The field experiment tests the two approaches and compares
them to a minimalist BAU support. Though comparable in
many respects, they were operationalized to address the pri­
mary fidelity concern in the control paradigm, and the focus
on professional discretion and responsibility in the human
agency paradigm (Sandfort and Moulton 2020).
The main results are found at the level of the target group:
the fidelity intervention did not have any measurable impact
on the families’ take-up and use of the program, which does
not support H1. However, the PR approach seemed to be most
effective because families in this group used the reading plat­
form more than both the BAU and the fidelity group. Results
also point to higher takeup in the PR group as well as higher
perceived teacher encouragement according to the parents,
when compared to the BAU group (both significant at the
10% level).
Results also show that both approaches to implementation
support—fidelity and PR—affected teachers’ perceptions at the
level of systems operations, where the professionals operate,
but in different ways. We see that teachers in the fidelity group
to a higher degree stated that the intervention was feasible and
appropriate, and they stated that they and the families had
higher commitment than teachers in the PR and BAU groups.
While it was expected that the fidelity group would express
higher feasibility (MH1), the higher appropriateness countered
our hypothesis MH2.
While additional research is needed to assess the robustness
and generalizability of these results, we believe they serve as
an important step in thinking about how the fidelity and PR
approaches to implementation support may be operationalized
and tested rigorously.
The reasons for these results can, as always, be due to various
limitations in both the design and setup, and they can also be
theoretical in character. First, despite the setup with an online
reading platform and the possibilities for measuring the activity
there, it is likely that much of the reading in the homes took
place in the old-fashioned hard-copy way. Our main measure
of the use of the platform—logins to the platform—might,
therefore, miss out on some of the activity happening in the
families, perhaps even missing out on larger differences between
the treatment groups. If we look at the teachers’ orders of
posters, stickers, and logbooks (see fig. 4), there also seems to
be some reading in old-fashioned books in the families based
on these orders that extend into early May of 2023. This is
much longer than there is activity on the online reading plat­
form. We do find differences in take-up and use, but if the
treatment effects have different mechanisms that affect online
vs hard-copy reading differently, there might be treatment
effects that we are unaware of.
Second, another possible mechanism that can explain some
of the results is the difference in likely perceived workload of
the intervention. On the one hand, teachers in the PR group
may have perceived less workload from the intervention, since
they could choose (or opt out of) the support themselves.
Teachers in the fidelity group, on the other hand, received the
message of having to use all the material, although they could

perceived appropriateness and feasibility, which partially sup­

A third limitation is that we do not observe the final impact
of the intervention: the reading outcomes for the students.
Much of the discussion on discretion and tailoring in public
professionals’ delivery of key services has been whether such
autonomy would water down program effectiveness (e.g., Fix­
sen 2025; Fixsen et al. 2009). Although the focus and main
outcome in the current study is successful implementation of
the intervention, future research should also focus on the
impact of the intervention on desirable outcomes—even if suc­
cess in this endeavor can both be attributed to an effective
implementation and an effective intervention.
A final limitation might be that we only frame how we think
implementation support should be used—with fidelity or with
professional responsibility. This also means that adjustment
and tailoring can take place in the fidelity group, just as teach­
ers in the PR group might feel supervised and controlled. This
can, for example, be seen in the lack of difference in the per­
ceived discretion in the two groups. The fidelity group might
experience as much discretion as the PR group, since we are
not in a position where we can force teachers to implement in
a certain way. However, based on our treatments we do see
some differences in outcome measures as well as measures of
possible mechanisms, for example in the commitment and par­
ents’ perceived encouragement from the teachers. This suggests
that even small changes in the communication of the imple­
mentation tools and approach can make a difference to how
implementation is carried out.
Our results merit some discussion on how to manage imple­
mentation and its support. First, the results point to differences
in the conditions under which each of the two approaches can
be assumed to perform best. Even though teachers on average
see the READit intervention as more feasible and appropriate
when supported with the fidelity approach, they seem to deliver
more when supported by the PR approach. One interpretation
could be that the fidelity approach impressed in terms of the
quantity of support, but lacked the more crucial discretion part
that nudged the teachers to take responsibility and tailor the
intervention to their target group. We perhaps see this tendency
in the results on perceived encouragement from the teachers,
when we ask the parents: it seems higher among parents in the
PR group. This encouragement may have been a part of the
teachers taking responsibility for the intervention’s success, and
therefore, the parents took more responsibility further down
the line.
This might be a tradeoff when designing policy and research
interventions. On the one hand, we want professionals who
believe in the feasibility and appropriateness of interventions
and work to implement with fidelity. But on the other hand,
we also want them to activate their professionalism and adjust
the intervention to the benefit of the target group. Professionals
who do not see the merit in an intervention or feel the imple­
mentation support is not adequate might only invest minimally
in it or reject it entirely (Oreg, Vakola, and Armenakis 2011).
Here, it seems the fidelity approach to implementation support
has an advantage. However, even if professionals’ beliefs are
quite important to public organizations, policies and interven­
tions are often directed at specific target groups. Without them
taking and using it, there is not much reason to engage in it in
the first place. In this case, the PR approach seems to have an
advantage.
This leads to the implementation process itself over time. In
the implementation literature, the implementation process is
often described as something happening in different
phases—e.g., an adoption phase and a sustainability phase
(Proctor et al. 2011)—with an ever-changing solution space
(Majone and Wildavsky 1979, 166). However, it is rarely con­
sidered whether there are changes in the demand for imple­
mentation support across these phases (Powell et al. 2017). For
example, could the fidelity approach’s planning-and-control
focus be better suited in the adoption phase? One reason could
be that when introducing new work procedures and ways of
doing things, it is best to be direct and focus on sticking to the
protocol. On average, staff are perhaps not always ready to
adjust new interventions to their own context from the
start—they need to understand the basics of it to begin with.
This might change as they familiarize themselves with the mate­
rial. Here, implementation support using the PR approach may
be a better fit and may ensure that sustainability is more likely
in the long run. This would be in accordance with Sandfort
and Moulton (2020)’s theory on how structure and agency
co-evolve over time.
Another discussion point is the question regarding the ana­
lytical generalizability from an external intervention program
like READit compared to public policies decided by politicians
or public managers. Even if what we can learn from the edu­
cation area to some extent can be generalized to other public
service areas, the fact that READit is a specific, educational
intervention that is not decided by politicians, but by local
leaders in schools in collaboration with researchers, does limit
the generalizability. There are certainly differences between a
specific externally provided intervention like READit and an
official policy coming from the government or local govern­
ment. Policies are often followed by strict, if unspecific, man­
dates set by politicians and managers that also influence other
things like working conditions and salary. This could, for better
or worse, prompt reactions from the professionals meant to
implement the policy.
Whereas READit is a special case of an external program
adopted by an organization, results show that even a small
change in the framing of the implementation support creates
changes in teachers’ and parents’ behavior. This suggest on the
one hand that fidelity and professional responsibility approaches
would indeed make important differences also in situations
where, for instance, fidelity would be backed up by stronger
top-down incentives. On the other hand, more political inter­
ventions may generate other dynamics which may dampen the
effects of fidelity and responsibility approaches.

ABSTRACT
Audiences that observe and interact with government agencies play a crucial role in shaping these agencies' reputations. However,
existing research often treats these audience networks as monolithic, overlooking the inherent diversity in their cognitive and
emotional processing of reputational information. This approach fails to account for the variations in how audiences experience
and evaluate agencies. To address this gap, we propose a new research agenda focused on the role of emotions in bureaucratic
politics. We introduce a novel theoretical framework of Reputation Learning, informed by Affect-­as-­Information Theory and
Affective Intelligence Theory, to explore the downstream effects of emotions as content and as process in shaping judgment for-
mation and information processing. Specifically, we identify emotion-­based components of bureaucratic reputation and examine
how emotions influence audience decision-­making processes and perceptions of government agencies. We conclude by outlining
four key contributions of this framework to advancing the study of emotions in bureaucratic politics.
1   |   Introduction
This article examines the influence of emotions on how mem-
bers of an audience network perceive, evaluate, and update
their opinions about the reputation of government agencies over
time. We highlight new research directions on the overlooked
role of audiences in bureaucratic reputation studies (Capelos
et al. 2016; Bustos 2021; der Veer and Arthur 2021). Bureaucratic
reputation scholarship refers to audiences that observe and en-
gage with government agencies as carriers of their reputation.
Audience networks encompass any entity capable of observing
a government agency and forming evaluative judgments about
it (Carpenter 2010). They are often seen as uniform, ignoring
the diversity in audience networks, their emotional responses
to reputational information, and the influence of inter–audience
power dynamics.
We start by reviewing how bureaucratic reputation literature
addresses audience networks, noting a shared definition of
“bureaucratic reputation” as “a set of symbolic beliefs about
the unique or separable capacities, roles, and obligations of an
organization, where these beliefs are embedded in audience
networks” (Carpenter 2010, 45). An audience refers to “[…] any
individual or collective that observes a regulatory organization
and can judge it” (Carpenter 2010, 33). Examples of audiences
include scientific and professional organizations, political and
judicial authorities, firms and not-­for-­profits organizations,
media outlets, the public, and similar entities. According to

Although audience is “[t]he central concept in a reputation-­
based perspective on regulation” (Carpenter 2010, 33), scholars
have yet to explicitly confront the way “[r]eputation can, by as-
signing expertise and status to government agencies, allow them
to define basic terms of debate, essential concepts of thought,
learning, and activity” (Carpenter 2010, 33; see also Maor and
Shoenfeld  2024). We introduce the term reputation learning,
defining it as the process by which audience members update
or maintain their perceptions and evaluations of a government
agency based on new reputation-­related information and direct
interactions with the agency.
We argue that fully understanding how “audience networks”
engage in reputation learning requires examining audience
members' needs, thoughts, and emotions—all of which shape
an adaptive motivational state (Reeve  2018). Our focus is
on emotions, defined as brief yet distinct physical and men-
tal responses to stimuli perceived as relevant to an individ-
ual's goals (Capelos  2013). Emotions encompass five key
processes: appraising of the stimulus' significance, physio-
logical changes preparing for action, cognitive adjustments
facilitating adaptation, action tendencies, and the conscious
experience known as “feeling” (Damasio 1994; Ekman 1992;
Frijda  1986; Lazarus  1991; Scherer  2000). Emotions impact
judgment formation, information processing, memory and at-
tention functions, and serve an adaptive role for individuals
(Capelos 2010, 2013; Keltner and Haidt 1999). Unlike overall
evaluations—enduring, automatically activated global atti-
tudes toward an object that range from very negative to very
positive—emotions such as anger, fear, pride, worry, concern,
joy, surprise, disgust, happiness, sadness, or enthusiasm are
episodic, object-­specific, and highly differentiated feelings
(Capelos 2010).
We examine the downstream effects of emotions in reputa-
tion learning, a process often viewed through a cognitive lens.
Building on the hot cognition hypothesis (e.g., Lodge et al. 1995),
which notes that social and political concepts are inherently af-
fective, we introduce a novel theoretical framework to generate
hypotheses on how emotionality shapes reputation learning
in audience networks. We draw on insights from two comple-
mentary theories of emotions that highlight their functions as
content and as process: Affect-­as-­Information (Forgas  2001)
provides insights on how emotions can serve as sources of in-
formation when making judgments and decisions. Affective
Intelligence Theory (AIT) (e.g., Marcus and MacKuen  1993;
Marcus et  al.  2000) notes that different emotions trigger dif-
ferent information processing pathways, which is useful when
seeking to understand how emotions experienced by audiences
can determine the way these audiences engage in information
processing.
These theories were selected for their distinct strengths in ex-
plaining the downstream effects of emotions in shaping infor-
mation processing and judgment formation—both of which are
critical in understanding audience evaluations of bureaucratic
reputations. Affect-­as-­Information Theory posits that emo-
tions serve as heuristic signals, offering quick evaluative cues
that shape judgments (Clore and Storbeck 2006). This frame-
work aligns with the (often) heuristic nature of audience as-
sessments of agency reputations, wherein emotions function as
shortcuts for interpreting complex or ambiguous information.
Complementing this, AIT provides a nuanced understanding
of how emotions that encourage heuristic processing, such as
enthusiasm or anger, serve a different function than those that
trigger systematic processing, such as anxiety or fear (Marcus
et al. 2000). This distinction enables us to theorize how audi-
ences engage with reputational information in distinct ways,
depending on their emotional states. Together, the two theories
offer a more comprehensive examination of reputation learn-
ing that expands beyond cognitive processes to account for the
downstream effects of emotions.
Here we also acknowledge the bidirectional relationship be-
tween emotion and cognition: emotions often come before
thoughts and act as a perceptual lens through which individ-
uals interpret situations, while emotions can also arise from
cognitive appraisals, as demonstrated by studies on attribution
(Capelos 2010, 2013; Zajonc 1980). However, unlike Cognitive
Appraisal Theory, which focuses on the antecedents of emotional
states (Lazarus 1991), the theories incorporated in our frame-
work address the downstream effects of emotions on decision-­
making and judgment formation. Specifically, we examine how
emotions, once elicited, influence the processing of reputation-­
relevant information. In this context, Affect-­as-­Information and
the AIT offer valuable and previously overlooked insights into
the functional role of emotions in reputation learning.
Summary
•	 Multiple audiences are carriers of government agen-
cies' reputation, yet their motivations might differ and
likewise their emotions, as distinct groups.
•	 Throughout a government agency's lifespan, audience
members' perceptions and evaluations of a govern-
ment agency can be revised in response to new rep-
utational information, experience with the agency,
and contextual attributes within inter-­audience power
dynamics. We introduce a new concept, reputation
learning, to term the ways this process evolves.
•	 Emotions can be studied as being integral to gov-
ernment agencies or incidental. Emotions function
as content, providing information that is circulated
among audience members, particularly in the absence
of other informational alternatives.
•	 Emotions may impact processes, determining the ways
in which audience members engage with information
to arrive at or update their evaluation (i.e., the learning
outcome) of the reputation of a government agency.
•	 Identifying and measuring emotions such as enthu-
siasm, anger, fear, hope, pride, gratitude, surprise,
disgust, happiness, or sadness and their functions as
content and process can shed light into the black box
of the process of reputation learning, enabling govern-
ment agencies to refine their bureaucratic reputation
management strategies.

Our theoretical contribution is twofold: we introduce a frame-
work that explains how emotions impact reputation learning
processes and outcomes and propose hypotheses on how spe-
cific emotions—such as enthusiasm, worry, anger, fear, or
joy—affect reputation learning. These emotions serve as con-
tent and process: they become pieces of information and can
also result in different processing styles, which, in turn, can
shape how impressions are formed and updated.
We aim to understand whether and how emotions can influ-
ence the way audiences evaluate the reputation of government
agencies. While research and practice have recognized emo-
tions in bureaucratic politics settings, there is still limited
understanding of the mechanisms through which emotions
influence reputation learning processes and outcomes of ob-
serving audiences. Scholarly research has given limited at-
tention to how individuals process and interpret information
under emotional conditions. Incorporating emotions into rep-
utation learning analyses offers a more thorough evaluation of
the extent and contextual factors shaping their influence, as
well as the distinct pathways through which this influence oc-
curs. This approach deepens our understanding of the causal
mechanisms driving different reputation learning processes
and outcomes—mechanisms largely overlooked in current
bureaucratic reputation literature. By addressing these dy-
namics, our work highlights the complex role emotions play
in shaping reputational perceptions—especially as bureau-
cratic politics becomes more emotionally charged (Maor 2016;
Rimkutė 2025).
By emphasizing emotionality to explain reputation learning
we advance the research agenda in four ways. Firstly, we go
beyond traditional rational choice models in bureaucratic pol-
itics, particularly principal-­agent (P-­A) frameworks. While
these models have evolved to include psychological and behav-
ioral dimensions (e.g., Bendor et  al.  2001; Grimmelikhuijsen
et  al.  2017; Simon  1997), they still offer limited insights into
how reputations are constructed and interpreted by audiences
outside of conventional P-­A relationships. They also fail to fully
address how emotions interact with cognitive processing, and
their combined impact on reputation learning outcomes. Our
approach goes beyond principal-­agent (P-­A) models, highlight-
ing the complex role emotions play in reputational judgments,
as content as well as process. If future empirical evidence sup-
ports our hypothesis that audience emotions influence cognitive
mechanisms (through facilitating or hindering) in reputation
learning, it will challenge Carpenter's (2010), 34 claim that “au-
diences overlap and blend into one another”. Second, we expand
the study of emotionality in the context of audience networks
interacting with government agencies, enhancing the empirical
validity of future analytical frameworks for studying bureau-
cratic reputations. Third, we explicate the significant impli-
cations of emotionality on bureaucratic reputation dynamics
through reputation learning. We invite scholars to examine the
causal effects of emotions within audience networks on repu-
tation learning. By providing a deeper understanding of the
complex relationship between emotion and reputation learning,
we seek to understand how agencies navigate these dynamics
to maintain positive public perception and effectively serve
their stakeholders. Finally, our contributions introduce a novel
research agenda, spanning bureaucratic politics and public
administration. While emotion science has advanced in deci-
sion theories (Lerner et al. 2015), and political contexts, its inte-
gration into public administration remains underexplored (e.g.,
Hattke et al. 2020; Davis and Pandey 2023; Rimkutė 2025).
The article is structured as follows: the first section reviews
decision-­making literature, incorporating recent formal mod-
els from politics and economics. The second section provides
an examination of reputational information. The third explores
the interplay between emotions and reputation learning within
established learning frameworks. The fourth outlines theoreti-
cal expectations regarding the role of audience emotionality in
reputation learning. Finally, the article concludes by outlining
four key contributions.
2   |   Decision-­Making and the Study of Members of
Audience Networks
There is increasing recognition that decision-­making is influ-
enced by a range of motivations. As a result, social scientists pri-
oritizing empirical plausibility over modeling quality are thus
expected to incorporate psycho-­social factors into their mod-
els. Traditional rational choice models, such as those by Von
Neumann and Morgenstern  (1944), and the political science
literature on bureaucracy, which often relies on P-­A models,
typically assume that the connections between voters and pol-
icies occur through established channels like parties, elections,
representatives, and the legislature. These models assume actors
hold beliefs that perfectly align with objective probabilities, cre-
ating an exogenous and objective standard. As a result, beliefs
serve as a rigid backdrop against which preferences are formed
(Minozzi 2013). Moreover, P-­A models emphasize formal struc-
tures and incentives to hold bureaucratic agencies accountable
and reduce information asymmetries, preventing “bureaucratic
drift” (e.g., McCubbins et al. 1987). Emotions and beliefs, how-
ever, are typically excluded from these accounts.
The need to incorporate psycho-­social factors in social science
models has gained recognition, even among scholars using for-
mal models in politics and economics. Political scientists have
integrated endogenous belief formation, which considers the
anticipatory effects of uncertainty and have empirically tested
this by allowing participants to choose their beliefs at the outset
of game theory models (e.g., Minozzi 2013). Economists in psy-
chological game theory have similarly incorporated motivations
into economic analysis, including preferences tied to one's own
or others' beliefs, such as emotions, reciprocity, image concerns,
and self-­esteem (e.g., Battigalli et al. 2019). While scholars in
these fields may use terms like emotion, motivation, and be-
liefs loosely, the key takeaway for us is that these advancements
highlight the urgent need to incorporate psycho-­social factors
into analytical frameworks of bureaucratic reputation.
Carpenter's (2001) seminar work on bureaucratic reputation
challenges the literature based on P-­A theory. He argues that
bureaucratic autonomy arises from the success of the “politics
of legitimacy” played by bureaucrats, which is often achieved
when bureaucrats cultivate a unique reputation rooted “in
a diverse coalition wrought from the multiple networks in
which they are engaged” (p. 353). Carpenter further asserts

Taking Carpenter's  (2001) notion of “multiple audiences” se-
riously requires moving beyond standard economic models
that emphasize utility maximization and conventional politi-
cal science models of choice that assume beliefs adhere to an
exogenous, objective standard. Instead, it calls for a focus on
emotions and sentiments, which serve distinct, endogenous
motivational functions. Carpenter's emphasis on reputation as
a subjective viewpoint held by members of audience networks
urges scholars to consider previously external factors, especially
when real-­life experiences demonstrate their impact on human
decision-­making.
In Carpenter's (2001) framework of the “politics of legitimacy”,
beliefs provide a flexible backdrop against which preferences
unfold. This setup of the “politics of legitimacy” is “flexible” so
that agencies can mold or ‘manage’ their audiences' expectations
and evaluations and, if successful, increase their bureaucratic
autonomy (see also Carpenter 2010, 33, 68). Carpenter's depar-
ture from standard rational choice models acknowledges the
significance of endogenous factors, including emotions and be-
liefs. Building on his insights, we further explore environments
with multiple audiences that survey as carriers of an agency's
reputation.
We make this argument for two reasons. First, we argue that
theorizing bureaucratic politics should recognize that the be-
havior of individuals involved in reputation building and pro-
tection (the top-­down dimension), as well as reputation learning
and derived behavior (the bottom-­up dimension) should be
driven by reward and information, rather than just the reward-
ing value of outcomes like utility maximization. To date, there
has been insufficient attention to incorporating belief forma-
tion among the multiple audiences shaping bureaucratic repu-
tation. A substantial reason for this gap is the discoveries that
human decision-­making involves cognitive biases (Tversky
and Kahneman  1981), reasoning is motivated, and cognition
is laden with emotions, in other words “hot” (e.g., Braman and
Nelson 2007; Kunda 1990; Taber and Lodge 2006). Desires can
shape beliefs, thereby rendering subjective beliefs possible.
Second, we anticipate that the emotions of audience members
may have significant implications for reputation learning and,
ultimately, for an agency's reputation-­management strategies.
For instance, concerns about image among members of a spe-
cific audience network could encourage pro-­social behavior
(Bénabou and Tirole  2006), leading to increased support for
governmental agencies involved in large-­scale social projects,
particularly when those network members identify with the
project's goals.
These, along with other endogenous components [e.g., curios-
ity, surprise minimization (Hartwig and Peters 2021)] direct our
attention to the active inference process shaping audiences' per-
ceptions of the agency's reputation and, ultimately, their deci-
sion to support the agency. Some decisions may be spontaneous,
while others may follow a reasoned process influenced by fac-
tors such as attitudes, subjective norms, social acceptability,
moral norms, a sense of duty, perceptions of others' actions and
thoughts, and perceptions of behavioral control. Considering
the complex nature of decision-­making regarding agency repu-
tation, further investigation is warranted to examine the endog-
enous beliefs held by multiple audiences that act as conduits for
agencies' reputations.
3   |   Reputational Information in Audience
Networks
Recent studies in bureaucratic reputation scholarship have
demonstrated that government agencies strategically engage
in legitimacy politics through their communication with ob-
serving audiences (e.g., Busuioc and Rimkutė  2020a, 2020b;
Varela Castro, et al. 2023; Carpenter 2001, 2010; Cerdeira and
Rimkutė 2024; Sharon et al. 2015; Lee 2022; Maor 2010, 2011,
2020; Maor et  al.  2013; Rimkutė  2020a, 2020b). Leveraging
novel machine learning techniques, recent contributions have
identified patterns and themes in agency communication, offer-
ing insights into the nature and purpose of these signals (e.g.,
Moschella et al. 2020). However, a significant gap remains in
understanding how reputational information—whether com-
municated directly by agencies or indirectly through audi-
ences—shapes the process of reputation learning. This process
influences how audience network members form and update
their perceptions of an agency, thereby influencing their de-
cision to maintain or alter their behavior toward it. In the re-
mainder of this section, we expand on this argument and outline
future research directions.
The relationship between a government agency and its audience
networks, which shapes the agency's reputation, unfolds in a
complex landscape often marked by conflicts in goals, interests,
or values. Within this context, reputational information—com-
prising agency–and domain-­specific issues, statements, and sig-
nals—is communicated based on the agency's information and
audience-­specific priorities. For instance, when an agency pro-
vides advice, its accuracy or inaccuracy can significantly impact
its reputation among target audiences.
Reputational information is communicated through direct and
indirect sources, including the agency itself, its parent depart-
ment, and other government entities. This information interacts
with audience members' experiences and their engagements
within and across different networks. Political segregation ex-
acerbates ideological and emotional polarization, fostering
hostility among audience networks and undermining political
civility and effective governance (e.g., Finkel et al. 2020; Iyengar
et al. 2018), potentially reducing cross-­network interactions.
For example, during the COVID-­19 pandemic, some government
agencies issued communications misaligned with emerging sci-
entific evidence. Dutch regulators, for instance, initially stated

Reputational information also involves direct experiences and
social interactions, shaped by homophily—the tendency to as-
sociate with like-­minded individuals—as well as demographic,
technological, and cultural factors. Recent research suggests
that social connections are influenced not only by homophily
but also by a preference for associating with individuals who
hold more extreme political views over moderates (Goldenberg
et al. 2022). Since government agencies' reputations vary based
on overall public trust in government and political ideology (Lee
and Van Ryzin 2020), reputation-­related processes within audi-
ence networks may be significantly affected by these dynamics.
Additionally, these processes can be influenced by conflicts
where actions benefiting one audience may disadvantage an-
other. Over time, the accumulation of these interactions fur-
ther shapes the agency's reputation within and across audience
networks.
In summary, reputational information permeates the daily
lives of audience network members, communicated through
formal and informal, direct and indirect channels. It inter-
acts with individuals' experiences with the agency and the
reputational information shared within and across audience
networks.
4   |   Reputation Learning: Information Updating by
Engaging Cognition and Emotion
We argue that reputational information plays a central role in
reputation learning, shaping and updating audience perceptions
of an agency and influencing their decision to maintain or revise
their behavior towards it. To clarify its theoretical grounding, we
position reputation learning within established learning frame-
works, such as experiential learning, organizational learning,
and adaptive learning. Traditional models focus on how agen-
cies refine internal processes and practices based on feedback
and outcomes (e.g., Moynihan 2008). However, empirical stud-
ies in public administration highlight adaptive learning as cru-
cial for agencies to respond to political, economic, and cultural
shifts and uncertainties, as well as stakeholder demands (e.g.,
Moynihan and Landuyt  2009). These studies emphasize that
learning extends beyond internal processes to include responses
to external feedback.
Reputation learning extends traditional learning theories by fo-
cusing on how external audiences form and update perceptions
of an agency's reputation. This bidirectional, reputationally
driven perspective emphasizes interactions between agencies
and their diverse audiences. Reputation learning focuses on how
external audiences—citizens, interest groups, or other relevant
stakeholders—adjust their perceptions based on new informa-
tion, observed agency behavior, and emotional responses. Given
that audience perceptions shape agency autonomy and legiti-
macy, reputation management becomes crucial (Carpenter and
Krause 2012; Rimkutė and Mazepus 2025). To further ground
reputation learning theoretically, we draw on psychological the-
ories of emotion, examining the content as well as the process
through which learning occurs via observation, interaction, and
information exchange among audience members.
First, considering content, one component of reputation learning
is the audience's initial interaction with the agency, which oc-
curs through various mediums such as text, signs, and symbols
disseminated by the agency or other sources. A recent example
from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) highlights the
challenges regulatory agencies face in managing public percep-
tions during health crises. In response to a critical shortage of
GLP-­1 receptor agonists—medications typically used for diabe-
tes management but increasingly popular for weight loss—the
EMA issued a press conference advising against non-­diabetes-­
related uses (European Medicines Agency 2024). However, the
message elicited diverse emotional and cognitive reactions: anx-
iety and fear among diabetes patients and healthcare providers,
and anger and frustration among those using the medication
for weight loss. The case illustrates the complexity of reputa-
tion management, as agencies must navigate not only the fac-
tual accuracy of their communications, but also the emotional
responses and pre-­existing perceptions of multiple audience
groups.
The above example demonstrates how information can in-
fluence audience perceptions and evaluations of an agency by
influencing their cognitive evaluative judgments within their
respective networks. As individuals process new information,
they assess whether adjustments to their prior perceptions and
evaluations of the agency are required and, if so, determine the
nature and direction of these changes. A second component of
reputation learning is affective. It can be driven by incidental
emotions—arising from an individual's broader emotional state
at the time of encountering information about the agency—as
well as integral emotions, which are experienced specifically to-
ward the agency itself. In this process, emotions can serve as
content, learned information, influencing how individuals inter-
pret and integrate new reputational cues, potentially leading to
shifts in their existing perceptions and evaluations.
Our second point involves how emotions influence the learning
process. Neurobiological research provides insights into the in-
terrelationships between attention, memory, and learning (e.g.,
Hare et al. 2011; Niv et al. 2012; Schönberg et al. 2007). Learning
can occur through two distinct pathways: deliberate and inad-
vertent. In deliberate learning, attention plays a crucial role in
determining how much individuals learn (e.g., Jr et al. 2009).
This type of learning is more likely to occur when individu-
als have ample opportunities to access information—such as
through traditional news media, which covers bureaucratic pol-
itics and current affairs—and when they are highly motivated to
pay attention to such content (e.g., Jerit et al. 2006).
In contrast, inadvertent learning may take place even when
attention is not actively engaged, such as when individuals

Studies show that inadvertent learning can occur through expo-

Emotional arousal, for example, can heighten cognitive pro-

stimuli are often perceived as more important or salient, leading
individuals to prioritize processing them over less emotionally
evocative information.
5   |   Theoretical Foundations: Emotions as Content
and Emotions as Process
We theorize that emotions play a dual role in the process of
reputation learning. First, emotions as content are part of
the information that is communicated, shared, and learned
throughout the reputation learning process. Second, emotions
influence the process, shaping the ways in which information
is communicated and shared within and between audience
networks. To build our theoretical model, we highlight key
insights from two influential theories: Affect-­as-­Information
Theory and the Affective Intelligence Theory. This account
is by no means exhaustive. However, these two theories illus-
trate how emotions—such as anger, fear, enthusiasm, joy, or
worry—which possess specific valence and arousal proper-
ties (Russell 1980), can function both as content and as de-
terminants of information processing in reputation learning
models.
Affect-­as-­Information Theory (Forgas 2001; Schwarz 2012) pos-
its that emotions provide content, serving as valuable sources of
information that guide evaluations and decisions, particularly
in complex or uncertain contexts. Emotions, can be linked to
the object of judgment (directly or indirectly), known as “inte-
gral” emotions, and they can be experiences which are unrelated
to the target of evaluation, known as “incidental” emotions.
Integral and incidental emotions play a heuristic role in shap-
ing how individuals perceive situations or objects. Integral
emotions, those linked to a specific target, provide embodied in-
formation about the valence (pleasant vs. unpleasant) and value
(positive/good vs. negative/bad) of that situation or object. In
this sense, when objects are experienced as the source of a pleas-
ant or positive emotional reaction, they take on positive affect
value, and when they are experienced as a source of an unpleas-
ant or negative emotional reaction, they take on negative affect
value. This affect value, which is conceptualized as an “affec-
tive tag” (Capelos 2010), provides additional information about
the object, complementing other non-­affective information
(Schwarz and Clore 1988). In situations of uncertainty or when
information is sparse, individuals may rely on their emotions as
a heuristic or shortcut for decision-­making, asking themselves
“How do I feel about it?”. Instead of engaging in complex cogni-
tive processing, individuals use their emotional responses as a
guide. This heuristic processing often occurs when individuals
lack the resources, ability, or motivation to engage in more de-
liberate thought. In these contexts, positive emotions typically
generate positive judgments, while negative emotions tend to
generate negative judgments, enabling people to make decisions
even when they do not have all the necessary information.
Studies also show that momentary emotions, not elicited by the
situation or object of relevance (“incidental”) can also impact
judgments. For example, enthusiasm—an emotion that may
arise in the presence of a situation or object without being caused
by it—can lead to more positive and optimistic interpretations.
Conversely, sadness can lead to more negative and pessimistic
judgments (Clore and Storbeck 2006). In the context of govern-
ment agencies, public anxiety about an economic downturns or
insecurity from international conflicts and crises can influence
perceptions of an agency's reputation, even if it has no direct role
in economic or security policy.
Affect-­as-­Information Theory emphasizes the evaluative va-
lence (positive or negative) of emotions, proposing that emotions
serve as general heuristics, regardless of their specific type. It
argues that integral and incidental emotions provide valuable
information about situations and objects, influencing percep-
tions, judgments, and behavior (Forgas 2001). The theory also
highlights mood congruency, where individuals tend to rely
more on emotions that align with the situation they are eval-
uating. This is particularly useful for understanding how the
overall affective tone, rather than specific emotions, shapes rep-
utational judgments (Schwarz and Clore 1983). For instance, a
general positive feeling toward an agency may lead to favorable
reputational assessments, regardless of whether this positive ori-
entation stems from pride, relief, or hope.
Turning to models that focus on the role of emotions in deter-
mining the decision-­making process rather than its content, AIT
emphasizes how distinct emotions—anxiety, anger, and enthusi-
asm—trigger different information processing systems. Unlike
heuristic processing driven by a lack of information or uncer-
tainty, AIT suggests that emotional states guide evaluations.
Specifically, AIT posits that two affective subsystems interact to
“make complex behavior and rational choice possible” (Marcus
et al. 2000, 46). “Anger and enthusiasm regulate the disposition
system, which governs implementation and development of
heuristic processes and behavioral routines [whereas] anxiety
regulates the surveillance system, which focuses attention, stim-
ulates effortful processing, and allows individuals to break out
of routines so that they may reconsider their behavioral strat-
egy in response to threats” (Groenendyk and Banks 2014, 162).
The distinction between the different information processing
avenues triggered by emotions is key to understanding how au-
dience engagement with agency communications can shift de-
pending on the emotional state of audiences. For instance, anger
over perceived regulatory failures may lead to dismissing agency
messages, whereas anxiety about health risks may prompt more
careful scrutiny of the same information. In public opinion stud-
ies, AIT predicts that anxiety and fear influence information
processing and learning in a deliberate and systematic way, by
taking in context-­specific information prompted by the surveil-
lance system. It also predicts that anger and enthusiasm promote
heuristic processing via shortcuts like pre-­existing impressions
or partisan cues, prompted by the disposition system (Marcus
et al. 2000). In reputation learning, this suggests that anxious
audiences, because of their anxiety, scrutinize agency commu-
nications for inconsistencies, while enthusiastic audiences, due

with their pre-­existing views.
Bringing the content and process models together allows us to
appreciate the dual function of emotions as (i) informational sig-
nals and (ii) determinants of processing styles. The appreciation
of the dual downstream effects of emotions provides a founda-
tional, albeit not exhaustive, lens to explore how audiences form
and update their reputational judgments about government
agencies. These two functions of emotions, and the two theo-
ries we outlined here which highlight them, are of course com-
plementary. While Affect-­as-­Information focuses on content
and downplays distinctions between discrete emotions, such as
anger and fear, by recognizing their shared negative valence,
AIT focuses on content and highlights their divergent cognitive
and motivational consequences. By integrating insights from
these complementary perspectives, we offer a novel framework
for understanding the multifaceted role of integral and inciden-
tal emotions as content and as process in reputation learning,
bridging the heuristic and systematic pathways through which
emotions shape judgments.
We posit those integral emotions—those tied to the subject of
evaluation—are felt during reputation learning by audiences,
particularly when audiences evaluate agency rulemaking pro-
cesses and outcomes that clash with the goals, interests, and/
or values, or are perceived as politicized. For instance, intense
integral emotions may be experienced and expressed by various
stakeholders, including politicians, bureaucrats, leaders of audi-
ence networks, and network members. These emotions manifest
across different stages of the policy cycle, during interpersonal
interactions, on social media, and in other public forums.
Furthermore, audiences may also experience incidental emo-
tions—unrelated to the agency's actions—that still influence
reputational judgments. Bureaucratic rulemaking processes
and outcomes can evoke strong emotions, particularly when
they involve existentially significant issues such as euthanasia,
abortion, and birth control. Similarly, regulations addressing
sensitive matters like security, environmental sustainability,
economic stability, or public health—like mass vaccination
mandates during a global pandemic—may amplify the salience
of incidental emotions during the evaluation process. Issues that
evoke strong emotions are also more likely to be prioritized for
regulation and public attention, influencing reputation learn-
ing. For example, when the social identity of audience members
or entire groups is at stake, perceived threats to that identity may
further intensify emotional responses.
These dynamics show that integral as well as incidental emo-
tions shape the processing and interpretation of reputational
information, influencing how audiences perceive and evaluate
agencies. Because emotions toward government agencies often
emerge through complex pathways that combine direct and me-
diated forms of engagement, the analytical boundary between
integral and incidental emotions can blur. For example, politi-
cally charged messaging about an agency like the Environmental
Protection Agency can activate emotions that are deeply tied to
perceptions of the agency's actions or symbolic role, even though
they do not stem from firsthand experience. These emotions are
related to the target stimulus and can be considered integral,
although they are not generated by direct interaction. While we
recognize that the conceptually useful distinction between in-
tegral and incidental emotions can be empirically messy, it still
retains analytic value. Specifically, for example, integral emo-
tions, tied to specific agency actions, may prompt audiences to
scrutinize agency decisions more closely or, conversely, be more
lenient. Incidental emotions, shaped by broader contextual fac-
tors, can alter the framing and reception of agency communica-
tions either favorably or unfavorably.
A key mechanism in Affect-­as-­Information Theory is mood con-
gruency, which suggests that emotional states influence the type
of information individuals focus on and how they interpret it
(Forgas 1995). For instance, individuals in a negative emotional
state, such as anger or frustration, are more likely to pay atten-
tion to negative information. In reputation learning, this can
result in selective processing of unfavorable agency actions, am-
plifying their salience while downplaying positive contributions.
Another key component of this theory is the affect heuristic,
where individuals rely on their emotional reactions as shortcuts
for judgments (Slovic et al. 2002). Positive emotions from prior
positive interactions can lead audiences to evaluate subsequent
information more favorably, while negative emotions, such as
resentment or frustration, can distort interpretations and cause
disproportionate reputational damage. Finucane et  al.  (2000)
observed that people often base judgments on their emotional
reactions, whether toward a product, activity, or regulation.
In this case, emotions serve as valuable information, influenc-
ing audience behavior (Clore and Palmer 2009). To make these
concepts more accessible, we provide examples that show how
emotions can shape reputation learning in bureaucratic con-
texts. Integral emotions, like anger, arise when agency actions
are perceived as conflicting with audience values or interests.
For example, public anger over perceived leniency in enforc-
ing environmental regulations can prompt people to focus
selectively on an agency's failures while downplaying its suc-
cesses—even when effective programs are in place. Incidental
emotions, unrelated to specific agency actions, were evident
during the COVID-­19 pandemic. Widespread health-­related
anxiety heightened scrutiny of government messaging, prompt-
ing audiences to question credibility, even when agencies acted
responsibly. These emotional dynamics are further influenced
by mood congruency, where frustration directs attention to neg-
ative performance aspects, while positive moods may highlight
achievements. The affect heuristic explains how optimism about
an agency's sustainability initiatives can lead to positive repu-
tational assessments, even when outcomes remain uncertain.
Lastly, AIT explains how emotions shape information process-
ing: anxiety triggers critical and vigilant processing, whereas
enthusiasm fosters reliance on established heuristics, as seen
when audiences scrutinize agencies during crises but support
familiar initiatives during stable times.

merely cognitive; they are also affectively informed. Positive

gratitude, preserving perceptions of competence despite lo-

inaction during a crisis may prompt audiences to scrutinize
agency performance more critically, causing reputational
damage. Similar dynamics have been evidenced by studies on
candidate evaluations and blame management (Huddy and

tions influence cognitive engagement: anxiety is expected to
trigger critical processing, whereas anger and enthusiasm are
expected to encourage heuristic processing that reinforces ex-
isting beliefs.
7   |   Theoretical Framework of Emotions in
Reputation Learning by Audience Networks
Audience members utilize new reputational information and di-
rect experiences with agencies to form quick, perception-­based
impressions of the agency and engage in reputation learning
over time. Therefore, it is critical for research to distinguish the
content and the processes of reputation learning across different
audience networks with varying emotional states. Drawing on
the theoretical framework outlined in Figure 1, we propose a
novel model of reputation learning that incorporates emotion-
ality as content and as process: first (Type 1), by acknowledging
that integral and incidental emotions are part of the content of
bureaucratic reputations shared among audiences; and second
(Type 2), by noting that different emotions generate affective
states within audience networks, influencing the learning pro-
cess and leading to divergent outcomes based on the original
emotional responses.
The model focuses on short-­lived or immediate emotions (e.g.,
Lerner et al. 2015; Loewenstein and Lerner 2003) that arise in
response to high-­intensity, concrete events experienced at the
moment of choice, as opposed to a longer-­term, low-­intensity af-
fective responses, such as sentiments or moods (Capelos 2013).
In this model, new agency-­relevant information, presented as
texts, signs, or symbols, as well as experiences with the agency,
interact with emotional components, shaping updated percep-
tions and evaluations of the agency, which result in reputation
learning.
The interaction between emotions and cognition creates a
feedback loop, where emotional responses shape cognitive
appraisals, which, in turn, influence subsequent emotional
reactions. For instance, repeated positive interactions with an
agency can enhance trust and foster positive emotions, creat-
ing a self-­reinforcing cycle of favorable reputation learning.

Against this conceptual background, we can deduce a set of
general expectations regarding the role of emotions in repu-

in reputation learning, future research could examine how
emotion-­based reputational information integral to an agency
and held by various audience networks (indirect reputational

that audiences' reported knowledge of an agency contains
several emotions—anger, hope, fear, enthusiasm, joy, worry,
concern, gratitude, surprise, disgust, happiness, sadness—as-
sociated with other elements of reputational information. In
addition, integral emotions are likely to be shared and circu-
lated among audience members, especially in contexts where
inter-­audience power relations come into play. In this sense,
integral emotions could constitute part of the reputational
capital of agencies (Capelos et al. 2016).
As outlined in our discussion above, we expect that reputational
information—whether communicated directly by the agency
or disseminated indirectly through audience networks—plays
a distinct role depending on the agency's proximity to the au-
dience. Information regarding the agency's regulatory rules,
processes, or outcomes often functions as emotion-­laden repu-
tational data, especially in contexts where direct information is
scarce. However, we propose that this reputational data affects
different audiences in unique ways, influenced by cognitive
needs, access to information, and reliance on emotional pro-
cessing. Specifically, we anticipate that (1) uncertainty and (2)
the multidimensional nature of the content are conditions under
which emotions become integral to reputational information.
Regarding uncertainty and information gaps, emotions serve as
a crucial source of information, particularly for audiences with
limited access to an agency's activities and outcomes. These
audiences, often further removed from the agency and less in-
formed, may rely on emotion-­based reputational cues, especially
when facing uncertainty due to scarce or indirect information
sources. As Carpenter and Krause  (2012) note, agencies are
perceived “through the glass but dimly”, highlighting how au-
diences, such as the general public lack a clear understanding
of the agency's precise outputs, processes, and behavior. This
limitation prompts them to form reputational judgments based
more on perceptions rather than direct observations of agency
behavior. Consequently, these distant audiences are especially
vulnerable to interpreting reputational information through an
emotional lens.
In contrast, audiences closer to the agency—such as private inter-
est groups or collaborators directly involved with its activities—
typically have access to more detailed and accurate information
about its functions, actions, and performance. This proximity
allows them to construct multidimensional assessments of the
agency's reputation, drawing on concrete information rather
than perceived cues (Christensen and Gornitzka 2019; Lee and
Van Ryzin  2020; Overman et  al.  2020). Research shows that
these closer audiences have a more comprehensive grasp of
the agency's organizational image (Overman et al. 2020), rely-
ing less on emotion-­based judgments and more on substantive
evaluations, leading to more accurate assessments of its multidi-
mensional reputation.
Taking the above into account, we propose that emotions serve
as an essential content of reputational information, especially
for audiences operating at a greater distance from the agency.
These audiences—typically unfamiliar with the agency's roles,
capacities, and conduct and lacking entrenched reputational bi-
ases (i.e., neutral reputational evaluations) (Luoma-­aho 2007)—
are more likely to rely on emotion-­driven interpretations to
guide their judgments. We posit that emotions function as con-
tent which helps fill information gaps, especially when audi-
ences must navigate uncertainty or have limited exposure to the
agency's actions.
In our model, we argue that neutral reputational evaluations
provide a key context in which emotions serve as primary con-
tent of information for reputation formation. When no prior pos-
itive or negative reputation exists, emotions such as concern or
hope may serve as prominent cues, shaping how audiences in-
terpret reputational information and guiding initial judgments
that shift neutrality toward either a positive or negative trajec-
tory. Importantly, our model suggests that neutrality creates rep-
utational dynamics in which emotions play an outsized role due
to the absence of strong evaluative anchors.
Second, we expect that reputational information across
Carpenter's multidimensional model—comprising technical,
performative, legal-­procedural, and moral dimensions—will
resonate differently among audiences and will be processed
as emotion-­based reputational information in distinct ways.
Agencies engage in a complex balancing act, tailoring their
communication to convey various aspects of their activities
(Carpenter  2010; Maor  2020). For example, they may high-
light the scientific foundations of their risk assessments (tech-
nical dimension), the legal and procedural transparency of
stakeholder consultations (legal-­procedural dimension), their
operational efficiency in resource allocation (performative
dimension), and the ethical principles guiding their regula-
tory actions (moral dimension) (Carpenter 2010; Busuioc and
Rimkutė  2020a; Rimkutė  2018, 2020a). We argue that each
of these dimensions contains emotion-­laden reputational
data based on audience focus and values. However, we expect
that reputational information concerning an agency's moral
conduct is more likely to be emotionally charged than other
dimensions. Moral reputation, often constructed around an
agency's ethical behavior, values, and commitment to public
welfare, elicits deep emotional responses because it engages
audiences' sense of justice, empathy, and trust (Maor  2016;
Christensen and Gornitzka  2019). As a result, moral

as these dimensions are perceived as procedural or expertise

than ethicality. Thus, we anticipate that emotional responses
will be particularly influential in shaping moral reputation,
influencing how audiences judge an agency's alignment with
social and ethical norms and expectations.
Regarding the downstream effects of emotions in information
processing (Type 2), the AIT model suggests that different
emotions can change the process by which audiences engage
with reputational information. Specifically, audiences experi-
encing anger would be less likely to engage in systematic in-
formation processing. Instead, they would be inclined to seek
and accept information consistent with their existing beliefs.
In such cases, cues offered by an agency are likely to reinforce
preexisting reputations. Similarly, enthusiastic audiences
would be less inclined to search for contradictory information
and are thus more likely to maintain positive impressions of
an agency, even in the face of negative media coverage. In con-
trast, individuals experiencing anxiety would engage in more
vigilant information searches to reduce uncertainty, making
them more likely to update their perceptions, particularly in
negative information environments. Thus, emotions deter-
mine process: while anger and enthusiasm are expected to re-
inforce existing views, anxiety is expected to prompt greater
openness to new information, increasing the likelihood of rep-
utational shifts.
8   |   Temporal Dynamics of Emotion and
Reputation Learning
We address the relationship between short-­lived emotional reac-
tions and enduring reputational outcomes by considering how
temporal dynamics shape reputation learning. While episodic
emotions are inherently transient, they can serve as catalysts
for initiating and sustaining reputational shifts, particularly in
response to high-­impact events. In our framework, short-­lived
emotional responses initiate these shifts, especially when pub-
lic resentment, frustration, or fear arises from an agency's ac-
tions—such as a controversial or unpopular policy decision or
a crisis mismanagement. When these emotions are collectively
experienced and shared across audience networks, they can
amplify reputational consequences, generating ripple effects
that persist beyond the initial event. The memory of such emo-
tionally charged events lingers, shaping future perceptions of
the agency. Political psychology studies support this dynamic,
showing how emotions evoked by singular events like wars, fi-
nancial crises, or scandals can have lasting effects on attitudes
and behaviors (Capelos et al. 2018; Marcus et al. 2000).
We further emphasize the critical role of repeated emotional
triggers in reinforcing reputations. While episodic emotional
reactions are short-­lived, their recurrence in response to similar
events or agency communications can lead to cumulative effects
on reputation. Repeated frustration over perceived agency inac-
tion or consistent enthusiasm for agency achievements can con-
tribute to the stabilization of reputational judgments over time.
Studies in public administration note that repeated emotional
responses reinforce audience perceptions by creating patterns
of experience that serve as markers of an agency's reliability or
competence (e.g., Moynihan 2008).
This reinforcement effect suggests that reputation learning is
not solely the result of isolated emotional episodes but could
be shaped by cumulative emotional experiences that audi-
ences recall and integrate into their broader assessments—
what Capelos (2000) describes as affective tallies. Furthermore,
psychology and neuroscience research show that emotion-
ally intense events are more likely to be encoded into long-­
term memory, shaping sustained evaluations (e.g., LaBar and
Cabeza 2006; Phelps 2004). Emotionally vivid and memorable
episodes, particularly those in high-­stake contexts, function as
“flashbulb memories”—events that are mentally and socially re-
hearsed, leading to confident assessments of the past event (e.g.,
McGaugh 2004). In this way, transient emotional experiences
serve a crystalizing function, solidifying agency impressions
into long-­term reputational shifts.
9   |   Towards a Novel Research Agenda on
Reputation Learning and Emotions
The theoretical framework advanced here opens several
promising avenues for advancing research in bureaucratic
politics. Understanding how multidimensional reputation is
acquired through audience networks (Bustos  2021; der Veer
and Arthur  2021), and the role emotions play in the reputa-
tion learning process, is crucial. This is especially important
given the significance of bureaucratic reputation for govern-
ment agency autonomy and independence (Carpenter  2001,
2010; Maor 2007), authority (Bertelli and Busuioc 2021), trust
(Lee and Van Ryzin 2020; Zhang et al. 2024), and legitimacy
(Rimkutė 2020a, 2025; Rimkutė and Mazepus 2025).
In this contribution, we urge future research to examine how in-
tegral and incidental audience emotions (e.g., fear, hope, worry,
anger, enthusiasm, joy, concern, gratitude, surprise, disgust,
happiness, and sadness) can serve as information content and
as process, shaping reputation learning across technical, per-
formative, legal-­procedural, and moral dimensions. Our under-
standing remains limited regarding whether specific emotions
influence how citizens and other relevant audiences process
various dimensions of reputational information. In particular,
it is unknown whether specific emotions affect the processing
of technical, performative, legal-­procedural, and moral aspects
of agency conduct in similar ways. Furthermore, we encourage
scholars to examine how integral and incidental emotions influ-
ence the processing and judgment of reputational information—
whether directly conveyed by the agency or disseminated within
complex audience networks defined by power dynamics. This
research could yield valuable insights into how reputations are

Earlier, we drew attention to the empirical complexity of how
integral and incidental emotions manifest in real-­world settings.
For the purposes of our model, we consider “integral” emotions
as those connected to the agency—whether directly or indi-
rectly—while “incidental” emotions refer to those unrelated to
the agency as a source or target of evaluation. Notwithstanding
the analytical usefulness of this conceptual distinction, integral
and incidental emotions can be understood along a continuum
of emotional proximity to the agency stimulus, rather than as
strictly dichotomous categories. Future studies could examine
how political messaging may act as a mediator, transforming
incidental emotions (such as partisan resentment or outgroup
hostility) into agency-­relevant perceptions that feel integral to
the observer—even in the absence of direct contact.
Another area that would benefit from future research is the
direct interaction between agencies and their audiences—spe-
cifically, how and why agencies strategically adopt certain
reputation management tactics, and how these are received by
different audiences. This is particularly relevant in volatile en-
vironments where emotionally charged audience reactions can
pose significant reputational threats. For instance, studying
communication styles and the tone of agency messaging—such
as information about the health tradeoffs of novel medicines—
could provide valuable insights into government agency repu-
tation management efforts. These research avenues enable a
comprehensive investigation of the internal and external dimen-
sions of reputation learning. Exploring the internal dimension
could reveal how agencies strategically tailor their communica-
tions to manage emotional responses across diverse audiences.
In addition, examining the external dimension could shed light
on how these tailored communications shape audience percep-
tions and influence reputation learning processes. Together,
these perspectives offer a richer understanding of the implica-
tions of emotions for effective and legitimate public governance.
Furthermore, while our model has focused on the downstream
effects of emotions on reputation learning, future studies could
examine the origins of both incidental and integral emotions.
Here we argue that integral and incidental emotions—whether
tied to direct experience or not—are consequential because
they consume cognitive and emotional bandwidth, reduce re-
ceptivity to alternative framings, and anchor beliefs about the
agency's reputation. In particular, negative emotions shaped by
appraisals promoted by political actors—who frame agencies as
threatening—are central to understanding how audiences form
perceptions of government agencies, even in the absence of di-
rect interaction. Studying incidental and integral emotions is
valuable because reputational dynamics are shaped not only by
agency processes, output, or behavior, but also by emotionally
charged cues from the broader political environment. Political
elites often have a strategic interest in portraying certain gov-
ernment agencies as threats, thereby activating incidental as
well as integral emotions that may become part of the affective
content of audience judgments. This line of research—focusing
on the origins of incidental and integral emotions—comple-
ments our focus toward a more comprehensive account of the
role of emotions in audience networks.
Our analysis has focused on the content and process function
of emotions but has underplayed their activating and/or deac-
tivating dimensions, which are also important in psycholog-
ical models of affect (see Russell  1980; Feldman Barrett and
Russell 1998). Future studies could generate hypotheses based
on the valence and arousal properties of integral and inciden-
tal emotions in the context of reputation learning. For example,
while emotions such as fear or anger typically stimulate mental
and behavioral arousal, emotions like sadness, despair, or calm-
ness may prompt withdrawal or passivity. Low-­arousal emotions
such as resignation or apathy may signal reputational disengage-
ment or audience fatigue.
A related hypothesis is that while such emotions may not drive
immediate reputation-­relevant actions (e.g., complaints, sup-
port, or protest), they could be linked to latent reputational
risks such as erosion of agency legitimacy or indifference. These
risks may be particularly damaging in domains where agency
legitimacy or active engagement is essential. In such cases, we
would expect deactivating emotions to lead to more neutral
or gradually deteriorating reputational outcomes, rather than
pronounced shifts, as with the high-­arousal emotions. Future
studies could also distinguish between affect valence and activa-
tion/arousal, and examine how motive-­consistent and motive-­
inconsistent emotions—regardless of whether they are positive
or negative—can be either activating or deactivating. While ac-
tivating emotions such as anger, fear, or enthusiasm are more
likely to trigger visible reputational shifts, deactivating emotions
such as sadness, boredom, or contentment may result in inac-
tion or resignation. These nuances matter because, although
the effects of such emotions are harder to detect, they can be
important for understanding reputational shifts. Future studies
could examine why audience reactions differ and how emotional
arousal—or its absence—influences the pace and direction of
reputational change.
Such contributions could make significant theoretical advance-
ments in understanding how emotions are elicited, manifest,
and consequential in bureaucratic politics. For example, studies
could examine whether emotional patterns align with psycho-
logical theories or exhibit distinct characteristics within bureau-
cratic politics. Moreover, they could clarify the role emotions
play in audience-­agency interactions, particularly in shaping
reputation learning processes and outcomes.
10   |   Conclusions
In this research agenda article, we present a novel theoretical
framework highlighting the downstream effects of emotions
in reputation learning. We encourage scholars to examine how
emotions—incidental and integral—influence audience behav-
ior in forming and updating perceptions of government agencies.
We move the research agenda forward in four significant ways.
First, we advocate for a shift from conventional rational models

in bureaucratic politics and rulemaking, aligning with behav-
ioral public administration (BPA) scholarship (e.g., Battaglio Jr.,
et al. 2019; Grimmelikhuijsen et al. 2017; Hallsworth 2023; Van
der Voet 2023), which incorporates psychological factors in its
frameworks. Building on this work, we emphasize the interplay
between cognition, emotion, and perception in shaping indi-
vidual and collective responses to agency communication. Our
study extends BPA literature by demonstrating how emotions
influence citizen-­state interactions (e.g., Hattke et  al.  2020)
and, more broadly, bureaucratic decision-­making and behavior
(e.g., Overman and Schillemans  2022; van der Heijden  2023;

This approach challenges the assumptions of standard rational
choice models in politics and economics (Von Neumann and
Morgenstern 1944), particularly those underlying bureaucratic
politics and principal-­agent frameworks. These models assume
that actors hold beliefs perfectly aligned with objective prob-
abilities—an assumption problematic for scholars prioritizing
empirical plausibility. Decision-­making is influenced by cogni-
tive biases (Kahneman and Tversky 1981), motivated reasoning,
and hot cognition (e.g., Braman and Nelson 2007; Kunda 1990;
Taber and Lodge 2006), making it crucial to consider how mo-
tivations shape beliefs. Additionally, emotions play a dual role
in decision-­making: they introduce biases similar to cognitive
biases while also serving as valuable informational signals
(Lerner et al. 2015). Interdisciplinary research in neurobiology
further illustrates the substantial overlap between cognitive
and affective domains, showing how emotions shape cognitive
processes (Engelmann et al. 2018). The documented influence
of emotions on the cognitive mechanisms involved in reputa-
tion learning underscores the need to move beyond traditional
P-­A models by integrating psychological insights (Bendor
et al. 2001; Simon 1997). We advocate for bottom-­up research
approaches that examine the downstream effects of emotions
on reputation learning and influence on audience-­agency in-
teractions, particularly beyond conventional principal–agent
settings.
Second, we extend the role of emotions within audience net-
works interacting with government agencies, enhancing the
empirical validity of future analytical frameworks on bureau-
cratic reputations. We build on Carpenter's (2010) concept of
“symbolic beliefs” which suggests that “[w]ithin and between
the audiences of government there flow opaque and symbolic
beliefs about the agency […]” (p. 34) by incorporating an emo-
tional layer. Integrating emotions in this framework deepens
our understanding of how reputational beliefs are formed,
transmitted, and interpreted within audience networks, of-
fering a more nuanced perspective on bureaucratic reputa-
tion dynamics. This contribution is crucial, as bureaucratic
reputation scholarship often treats audiences as a unified en-
tity, overlooking their cognitive and emotional diversity. By
highlighting how audience members process reputational in-
formation differently based on their emotional and cognitive
dispositions, we open new avenues for research on the role of
emotionally differentiated audiences in the politics of bureau-
cratic reputation.
Third, we highlight the significant role of emotions in shaping
fluctuations in bureaucratic reputations within the framework
of reputation learning. Emotions vary across audiences, time pe-
riods, and geographical contexts, influencing how reputational
information is processed and interpreted. We urge scholars to
examine how these emotional variations within audience net-
works shape reputation learning. While emotions are inherently
ambiguous, detailing their impact is essential despite the chal-
lenges posed by their nuanced nature. A deeper examination of
these dynamics will enrich our understanding of how emotions
interact with the broader processes of reputation acquisition and
change.
This article offers a nuanced exploration of the complex rela-
tionship between emotion and reputation learning, shedding
light on how government agencies can strategically manage
these dynamics to maintain positive public perceptions and
engage effectively with their stakeholders. Navigating these
relationships requires a strategic approach, akin to Wilson's
concept of viewing agency “types functionally” (Wilson 1989),
which includes coping, craft, procedural, and production
roles. Considering reputation and turf politics—especially in
the context of agencies' interactions with emotionally diverse
audiences—clarifies why agencies reassess their priorities
and adjust their scope across these functional categories. This
nuanced perspective highlights the importance of adaptabil-
ity and strategic decision-­making in fulfilling mandates while
maintaining positive stakeholder relationships.
In conclusion, our contributions establish a new research
agenda in bureaucratic reputation scholarship by examining
the significance and impact of emotions in bureaucratic pol-
itics. While existing research has largely focused on govern-
ment agency outputs, processes, and strategies for managing
reputational threats and fostering positive reputations (e.g.,
Boon et al. 2023; Capelos et al. 2016; Maor and Sulitzeanu-­
Kenan 2016; Maor et al. 2023; Rimkutė and van der Voet 2024),
it has comparatively overlooked audience decision-­making
and behavior (Bustos 2021; der Veer and Arthur 2021). This
gap is critical, as audiences shape and transmit government
agencies' reputations. To address this, we theorized how repu-
tation is learned by observing and judging audiences, empha-
sizing the downstream effects of emotions in this process. By
introducing a model that integrates emotions as content and
process into reputation learning, we provide a framework for
future scholarship to challenge fundamental assumptions in
bureaucratic reputation scholarship and other public admin-
istration domains. This approach deepens our understanding
of the interplay between emotion, audience perception, and
bureaucratic reputation, opening new avenues of inquiry in
the field.

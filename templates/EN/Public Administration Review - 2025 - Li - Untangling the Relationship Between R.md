ABSTRACT
Although red tape remains a significant policy concern and despite meta-­analytical research showing that it impacts employee
and performance outcomes, research elucidating why and under which conditions it does so remains scarce. Using social cogni-
tive theory, we first hypothesize that the relationship between red tape and job satisfaction is mediated by self-­efficacy. Second,
we argue that red tape is particularly harmful for job satisfaction in high-­individualistic cultures. Using a survey dataset of
110,746 teachers across 45 countries, we find that self-­efficacy is a statistically significant mediator in the red tape—job satisfac-
tion relationship, explaining about 8% of the relationship. Self-­efficacy does not offer a particularly potent mechanism explaining
the red tape—job satisfaction relationship, though it does matter. Importantly, we also corroborate assumptions about the role
of culture in red tape and public administration research, by finding that red tape is much more harmful for job satisfaction in
high-­individualistic cultures.
1   |   Introduction
Standardized procedures, rules, and regulations are a core el-
ement of the Weberian Bureaucracy, which is an oft-­adopted
model of organizing in government aimed at ensuring neutral-
ity, rule of law, and equal treatment (Bozeman and Rainey 1998).
However, rigidly adhering to these principles can result in un-
expected bureaucratic pathologies, of which red tape is one of
the most cited (Kaufman 1977). Bozeman (2012, 257) defines
red tape as a set of rules that “remain in force and entail a com-
pliance burden for designated stakeholders but whose contribu-
tion to [achieving] stakeholders' objectives or values is less than
the compliance and implementation resources expended on the
rule”. Red tape has been argued to be a major threat in public
organizations worldwide (Bozeman and Feeney 2011; Pandey,
Pandey, and Van Ryzin 2017; Van der Voet 2016), in part also due
to its association with corruption (Choe et al. 2013) and reduced
trust in government (Tummers et al. 2016).
Street-­level bureaucrats, like teachers and caregivers, have been
particularly affected by red tape (Steijn and Van der Voet 2019;
Murphy and Skillen 2015; Travers 2007). A study by Hanekamp,
Heesbeen, and Taks  (2020) found that long-­term care profes-
sionals in the Netherlands spent an average of 35% of their time
on administrative tasks. And 60% of them argued that these
tasks, and the compliance burden they entail, are increasing.
Similar evidence has popped up from the United Kingdom
(Haw and Kotterbova  2016) and the United States (Christino
et al. 2013). Teachers, the unit of analysis of this paper, are an-
other group of street-­level bureaucrats particularly affected by
red tape (Tiggelaar, Groeneveld, and George  2023; Jacobsen
and Jakobsen  2018). A survey done by the Organization for

Several empirical studies have supported the negative impact of
red tape, with a recent meta-­analysis demonstrating its negative
and significant impact on individual and organizational-­level out-
comes across studies (George et al. 2021). One such an outcome,
which is often included in red tape research, is job satisfaction
(Wright and Davis  2003; Steijn and Van der Voet  2019; Gerich
and Weber 2020). Job satisfaction has been linked to a range of
attitudes and behaviors of employees, such as job performance
(Willem, Buelens, and de Jonghe 2007), workers' health (Thielgen
et al. 2015), and turnover intentions (Lu et al. 2016). Yet, while
contemporary evidence thus suggests that red tape is harmful to
well-­being outcomes like job satisfaction, much heterogeneity can
be observed between effect sizes indicating that red tape is not
equally harmful in all settings (George et al. 2021). This heteroge-
neity raises an important research question: Why and under which
conditions does red tape negatively impact job satisfaction?
To answer this research question, we first draw on social cogni-
tive theory, which proposes that self-­efficacy can act as a cogni-
tive appraisal process that connects the external environment
with individual behavior (Bandura  1997). We hypothesize and
test whether red tape decreases teachers' job satisfaction through
the mechanism of self-­efficacy (why). Second, we investigate the
role of culture using one of the oft-­cited culture dimensions of
Hofstede (2011) namely individualism–collectivism. We hypothe-
size and test whether red tape is more harmful in high-­individualist
cultures (under which conditions). To test the hypotheses, we use
the OECD's Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS)
2018 dataset, including data from 110,746 teachers in 8402 schools
across 45 countries or economies. We perform Generalized
Structural Equation Modeling (GSEM) to test the mediating role of
self-­efficacy and Multilevel Modeling to test the moderating role of
individualism–collectivism.
In doing so, our study offers several contributions to public ad-
ministration theory and research. We bring both social cognition
and country-­level culture into theorizing about red tape by fo-
cusing on self-­efficacy and individualism–collectivism. Previous
theorizing about why and the conditions under which red tape
impacts job satisfaction remains scarce, and has mostly relied
on models such as the Job Demands—Resources Model (e.g.,
Muylaert et al. 2022; Borst 2018; Steijn and Van der Voet 2019).
While very useful, this research has typically not investigated
the role of country-­level culture nor social cognitive variables
like self-­efficacy. By theorizing and testing whether these vari-
ables mediate (in the case of self-­efficacy) or moderate (in the
case of individualism–collectivism) the relationship between
red tape and job satisfaction, we answer calls for a more fine-­
grained theorization of this relationship (George et al. 2021).
Similarly, most red tape research draws on evidence from one
specific country (though there are some exceptions, including
Kaufmann, Hooghiemstra, and Feeney  2018 and Tiggelaar,
Groeneveld, and George 2023). This paper is one of the first to
take a cross-­country perspective on red tape, using evidence from
45 different countries or economies. This perspective offers two
advantages, first, it allows the examination of contextual, macro-­
level variables such as culture. Second, it allows to add country-­
fixed effects in models thus helping to assess whether relationships
remain once all country-­level variables have been controlled for
(George, Van de Walle, and Hammerschmid 2019). Bringing in the
macro-­level, and connecting it to more micro-­level phenomena has
long been called for in public administration, and we answer that
call in this paper (Bryson, George, and Seo 2024; Moynihan 2018).
In conclusion, we also stipulate an agenda for future research es-
pecially on specific moderators or mediators that could be useful
to further “untangle” the red tape—job satisfaction relationship.
2   |   Theory and Hypotheses
Public administration scholars have developed numerous in-
sights related to the conceptualization and measurement of
red tape. Davis and Pandey (2023) provide a summation of two
main distinct approaches, namely the system-­centric approach
versus the individual-­centric approach. The system-­centric ap-
proach emphasizes the unified organizational reality of rule
dysfunctionality and evaluates red tape as ‘a technical exercise
entailing a rational analysis of the benefits and costs of rules’
(Campbell 2019, 1659). The individual-­centric approach draws
on psychological process theory, and interprets red tape as a so-
cially constructed reality that is derived from contextual infor-
mation available to individuals given their organizational role or
vantage point (Davis and Pandey 2023). In this approach, evalu-
ations of red tape thus rely on negative perceptual assessments of
compliance burdens imposed by an organization (Pandey 2021).
This study takes the individual-­centric approach and regards
red tape as negative emotional experiences with rules, and seeks
to unravel its complex relationship with job satisfaction.
The psychological process theory of red tape by Davis and
Pandey (2023) provides a well-­established three-­stage model
to unravel the process of the subjective assessment of red
tape: (1) primary appraisal of threat to well-­being, (2) sec-
ondary appraisal of coping resource, and (3) third appraisal

2.1   |   Red Tape and Job Satisfaction
The red tape–employee outcomes relationship is the subject
of more recent literature (Steijn and Van der Voet 2019; Van
der Voet 2016). Underlying this literature is the assumption
that work constraints caused by red tape limit employees'
discretion and enhance alienation. Job alienation is concep-
tualized as a general cognitive state of psychological discon-
nection from work, which can date back to Marx's arguments
(Kanungo  1979). We explain the theorized negative associ-
ation between red tape and job satisfaction, drawing on the
framework of job alienation.
Feelings of powerlessness and meaninglessness, described by
Seeman  (1959) as components of alienation, influence why
some administrative tasks are a negative and harmful expe-
rience for individuals in their daily work (DeHart-­Davis and
Pandey 2005; Hattke, Hensel, and Kalucza 2020). In particu-
lar, the appraisal process of red tape has posited that excessive
burden can evoke negative perceptual assessments by isolat-
ing employees from formal authority, reducing work freedom,
and diminishing the likelihood of finding fulfillment and
purpose in their roles (Davis and Pandey 2023). This appraisal
process results in the categorization of red tape as negative
emotional experiences within the workplace (Hattke, Hensel,
and Kalucza 2020). This, in turn, is harmful to numerous well-­
being outcomes, including job satisfaction (George et al. 2021).
Wright and Davis  (2003) reported that the dissatisfaction of
public sector workers could be the result of red tape as it hin-
ders them from pursuing their altruistic, other-­regarding mo-
tivations. Similar findings were uncovered in other settings
such as child welfare workers (Strand and Dore 2009).
Specifically applied to teachers, there is a growing body of evi-
dence suggesting that red tape encountered by school leaders and
teachers is increasing (Oberfield 2021; Muylaert, Decramer, and
Audenaert 2023). Schools are becoming more bureaucratic as a
result of accountability pressures (Viac and Fraser 2020), and
teachers are confronted with grueling demands that often trans-
late in additional administrative work (Tiggelaar, Groeneveld,
and George 2023). Professionals with high prosocial motivation,
such as teachers, are also found to be extra sensitive to experi-
ence the negative consequences of administrative tasks (Steijn
and Van der Voet 2019). Therefore, in keeping with contempo-
rary research on red tape, we propose the following hypothesis:
H1.  Red tape is negatively associated with teacher's job
satisfaction.
2.2   |   The Mediating Role of Self-­Efficacy
The second hypothesis concerns the indirect negative asso-
ciation between red tape and job satisfaction via employees'
self-­efficacy. Self-­efficacy is defined as “the belief in one's
capabilities to organize and execute the course of action re-
quired to produce given attainments” (Bandura 1997, 3). The
theoretical logic underlying this relationship can be derived
from social cognitive theory, which denotes self-­efficacy as a
crucial self-­appraisal that connects the external environment
with individual behavior (Bandura  1997). As a result, self-­
efficacy can influence how the work context is perceived by
having the power to alter perseverance and effort in the face
of task difficulties (Wright 2004). We expect that self-­efficacy
can act as a causal mechanism explaining the relationship be-
tween red tape and job satisfaction.
Existing evidence showed that self-­efficacy, as a general
cognitive appraisal of an encountered situation (Davis and
Pandey 2023), is built on employees' past experiences, vicarious
learning, verbal persuasion, and physiological and psychological
states (Tschannen-­Moran, Hoy, and Hoy 1998). We expect that
the experience of red tape can decrease self-­efficacy by increas-
ing emotional exhaustion, reducing personal autonomy, and de-
tracting energy from meaningful public services to unnecessary
procedures within the organization.
•	 First, red tape, as the hindrance stressor, can prompt employ-
ees to experience negative physical and emotional arousal
(Hattke, Hensel, and Kalucza  2020), resulting in cognitive
resource depletion and emotional exhaustion (Muylaert,
Decramer, and Audenaert 2023). The more negative an indi-
vidual is about attaining a certain job, the less the individual
would be motivated to execute it and experience decreased self-­
efficacy in succession (Rai, Patyal, and Maheshwari 2022).
•	 Second, red tape is regarded as “unmanageable factors
that unnecessarily thwart personal goals” (Dawson,
O'Brien, and Beehr 2016). Barriers caused by red tape can
threaten employees' capacity of influencing their work
environment and decrease their perceived autonomy.
Consequently, they may doubt their own abilities to nav-
igate and overcome these work obstacles, leading to a de-
cline in self-­efficacy.
•	 Finally, as red tape entails excessive constraints, employ-
ees can find it harder to focus on their main tasks and
fully engross in their work (Borst 2018). This can result
in role ambiguity, as it confuses employees regarding
their organizational role, job responsibilities, desired per-
formance, and contributions to the mission (Wright and
Davis 2003). Consequently, the ability of employees to vi-
sualize their performance is impaired, thereby reducing
employees' confidence in their ability to perform effec-
tively (Dasgupta 2012).
Previous research has provided empirical evidence that con-
strained work contexts can result in low self-­efficacy behaviors,
such as feelings of inferiority and withdrawal. Wright (2004)'s
survey on US state employees found that encountering red
tape resulted in a decrease in respondents' self-­efficacy via per-
ceived unclear performance objectives. Similar findings were

2.3   |   The Moderating Role of Individualism–
Collectivism
Culture is considered a critical variable to explain differences
in organizational behavior. One of the most known and oft-­
cited operationalizations of cultural dimensions is proposed
by Hofstede  (2011) with six dimensions: individualism–col-
lectivism, low-­high power distance, masculinity-­femininity,
uncertainty avoidance, long-­term orientation, and indulgence.
Individualism–collectivism is particularly relevant in the con-
text of red tape, which relates to the extent that members of a
society are supposed to look after themselves or are embed-
ded in groups (Hofstede 2011). Indeed, in one of the few cross-­
country studies on red tape, Kaufmann, Hooghiemstra, and
Feeney  (2018) examined the comparison of red tape percep-
tions in different cultural contexts, and found that people are
more likely to experience red tape in individualistic cultures.
Similarly, studies that sought to replicate experiments on red
tape originating from high-­individualistic cultures in more col-
lectivist cultures failed to replicate original findings—urging
the need for more research into the role of individualism–collec-
tivism as an important boundary condition in red tape research
(Campbell and Ahn 2023; Walker, Lee, and James 2017).
As suggested by the cognitive process model (Davis and
Pandey  2023), individualism–collectivism can influence red
tape's impact on employees outcome through shaping their role
expectations regarding the availability of coping resources and
the accountability for work demands. Specifically, individualists
highly value individual autonomy and hold self-­serving attri-
bution bias (i.e., a tendency to attribute positive events to one-
self and negative ones to external causes) (Mezulis et al. 2004;
Goodwin and Hernandez Plaza  2000). Collectivism, on the
other hand, seeks social harmony through group cooperation
and sees the “prevalence of hierarchies and adherence to rules”
as something essential for the completion of any task (Hui, Yee,
and Eastman 1995). Thus, we expect that:
•	 Red tape is easier to be regarded as constraints that under-
mine autonomy and availability of completion of working
tasks in high individualistic cultures implying stronger im-
pacts on job satisfaction whereas,
•	 In more collectivist cultures, employees may easier accept
the constraints on autonomy imposed by red tape as they
are more used to rules and hierarchy, implying a smaller
impact on job satisfaction (Lee and Peterson  2000). Our
third hypothesis reads:
H3.  In countries with high individualistic culture, the negative
association between red tape and job satisfaction is stronger.

3   |   Methods
3.1   |   Sampling and Response
This article relies on data from the 2018 OECD Teaching and
Learning International Survey (TALIS). The survey includes
items reflecting teachers' well-­being, daily workload, and evalu-
ation of the organization. To ensure the validity and reliability of
the survey, the development process of TALIS involved several
stages, including a pilot study conducted with focus groups, a
quantitative field trial, and rigorous psychometric analyses. The
selection of participants involved a two-­stage random sampling
procedure. Specifically, 200 schools were first randomly selected
in each country, followed by the random selection of 20 teachers
from these schools (OECD 2019). To mitigate potential bias caused
by nonresponse rates, each country was required to achieve a re-
sponse rate of at least 75% among the sampling schools, with a
minimum response rate of 50% per included school (OECD 2019).
Additionally, a minimum overall participation rate of 75% of
teachers was mandated for each country (OECD 2019).
Furthermore, to ensure comparability between countries/econ-
omies, the survey instruments underwent three verification
steps: national adaptation verification, translation verification,
and layout verification (OECD 2019). Finally, the survey instru-
ments were adapted, translated, and verified for 48 countries/
economies across all ISCED levels in 62 language versions. All
data collected through the TALIS survey, along with additional
clarification and technical reports, are publicly available for fur-
ther examination (OECD 2019).
We focus on the survey data coming from teachers in lower sec-
ondary education (labeled “BTGINTT3” by the OECD team).
While TALIS also has data from primary, higher secondary, and
vocational education, the lower secondary education dataset in-
cludes most countries and by focusing on this specific context we
aim for a more homogeneous sample to avoid having to control
for a range of “level of schooling”-­related variables (Tiggelaar,
Groeneveld, and George  2023). In total, our dataset contains
answers from 153,682 teachers in lower secondary education
clustered in 47 countries and economies. In our final analysis,
we only include respondents who do not have missing values on
one of the variables presented in our model, which reduced our

3.2   |   Variables and Measurements
3.2.1   |   Dependent Variable
Job satisfaction is defined as the “pleasurable or positive emo-
tional state resulting from the appraisal of one's job” (Locke,
King, and Cortina  1976, 1304). Teacher's job satisfaction in
TALIS is measured based on teachers' response to eight items
corresponding to two sub-­scales: job satisfaction with work envi-
ronment and profession. Participants responded on a four-­point
Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly
agree) (for more information, see Table 2). The Cronbach's Alpha
reliability values of the two scales were acceptable (0.79 and
0.82 or higher across all countries; see OECD 2019). A compos-
ite score for summarizing teacher's overall job satisfaction was
computed by taking a simple average of the corresponding stan-
dardized scores of these two sub-­scales based on Confirmatory
Factor Analysis (CFA) (OECD 2019).
3.2.2   |   Independent Variable
Compliance burden is the primitive concept across the definition
and measurement of red tape from both the system-­centric and
individual-­centric approaches (George et  al.  2021). However,
the meaning of compliance burden in these two perspectives
is different, with the former referring to the actual expenditure
of resources while the latter referring to the perceptual assess-
ment of individual experiences with rules (Pandey 2021). Given
the obstacles in accounting for factual resource expenditure on
rules (Pandey 2021), we take the individual-­centric view to use
a perceptual assessment of compliance burden as the measure-
ment of red tape.
In TALIS, teachers are asked to assign a specific percentage of
their time spent in the classroom on three activities: adminis-
trative tasks, keeping order in the classroom (maintaining disci-
pline), and actual teaching and learning. In this study, we focus on
“Administrative tasks” and teachers are given an example, namely
“recording attendance and handing out school information/
forms.” In the school and classroom context, these clerical duties
have been identified by global policy initiatives as common obsta-
cles that distract teachers from their core mandate, and thus need
to be reduced (e.g., Education Red Tape Reduction Act in China
in 2019 and the New South Wales Government in 2023). Teachers'
response to this item relies on the first-­person experience and
is found to hold a negative valence (Tiggelaar, Groeneveld, and
George 2023). As shown in Table 3, teachers spent on average 8%
of their time on this administrative task in the classroom, but the
high standard deviation implies much variation in responses.
Figure 2 presents country averages across our sample. Perceptual
assessment of compliance burden is highest in South Africa and
Saudi Arabia, and lowest in Estonia and Bulgaria. We add an
overall average line to identify which countries score above or
below the average. These findings give some insights into the
between-­country variation in red tape perceptions, further
demonstrating the earlier argued heterogeneity in red tape re-
search (George et al. 2021).
3.2.3   |   Mediating Variable
Self-­efficacy was first defined by Bandura  (1997) as belief in
one's capability to accomplish desired outcomes. In this study,
it is operationalized as a situation-­specific instead of a stable
personal trait, which changes over time as new information and
task experiences are obtained (Bandura 2006). In the school and
classroom context, teacher's self-­efficacy is aligned with specific
teaching practices and requirements to enhance student learn-
ing. In TALIS, it is measured based on teachers' response to 12
items corresponding to three scales: self-­efficacy in classroom
management, instruction, and student engagement (Gouëdard,
Kools, and George 2023). Participants responded on a four-­point
Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (a lot) (for more in-
formation, see Table 2). The Cronbach's Alpha reliability values
of the three scales were acceptable (0.84 for classroom man-
agement; 0.79 for instruction and 0.83 for student engagement;
see OECD 2019). Like with job satisfaction, a composite score
for summarizing teacher's overall self-­efficacy was computed

3.2.4   |   Moderating Variable
We include the individualism–collectivism index per country,
as recorded by Geert Hofstede using the Values Survey Module
Scale (VSM). Geert Hofstede made the data available for re-
searchers on his website (https://​www. hofst​edein​sights.​com/​
count​ry-­​compa​rison​/). The method underlying this index has
been developed and tested since 1967 and has been replicated
multiple times since 1990 (Hofstede 2011). This index explores
“the degree of interdependence a society maintains among its
members,” which ranges from 0 to 100. Individualistic societies
have loose ties that often only relate an individual to his/her im-
mediate family. Collectivism describes a society in which tightly
integrated relationships tie extended families and others into
in-­groups. We divided the countries into three groups, depend-
ing on low, middle, or high individualistic culture (respectively,
scores of 0–33, 34–66, and 67–100). The final group (67–100) is
considered the countries with a high-­individualistic culture and
is included as a country-­level dummy (0/1) in our multilevel re-
gression model. This final group holds 16 countries. Table 1 lists
the different countries in the dataset per group.
3.2.5   |   Controls
Several control variables are added to our models to account, as
much as possible, for spurious associations. First, a range of socio-­
demographic variables were added to account for nonresponse
patterns. These include gender, years of working experience as a
teacher, and the highest degree obtained (Tiggelaar, Groeneveld,
and George 2023). Our sample includes 69.5% female and 30.5%
male teachers, the average year of working experience as a teacher
is 16 years, only 1.64% did not obtain ISCED 2011 Level 3 education.
Second, student characteristics are included, namely the share of
students from socio-­economically disadvantaged homes and the
share of students with special needs because such students typi-
cally require additional attention from teachers, increasing teach-
ers' workload and thus decreasing their job satisfaction (Gesel,
Foreman-­Murray, and Gilmour 2022). Both variables are ordinal,
and teachers could select “None” (socially disadvantaged = 23.41%
of teachers, special needs = 29.15% of teachers), “1% to 10%” (so-
cially disadvantaged = 44.31% of teachers, special needs = 53.82%
of teachers), “11% to 30%” (socially disadvantaged = 19.67% of
teachers, special needs = 13.78% of teachers), “31% to 60%” (so-
cially disadvantaged = 8.64% of teachers, special needs = 2.74% of
teachers) and “More than 60%” (socially disadvantaged = 3.97% of
teachers, special needs = 0.51% of teachers). Finally, to account for
variation as a result of country-­level variables, we added country
dummies to our analysis thus, in effect, creating a country fixed-­
effects regression model (to test H3, these dummies are, however,
removed to build a multilevel regression model). For full transpar-
ency, all the key variable measurements and description are listed
in Tables 2 and 3, respectively.
3.3   |   Statistical Analysis
Seeing as the dependent, mediating, and independent vari-
ables of this study are all continuous (either composite scores or
a percentage), we use multilinear regression analysis to test our
hypotheses. Three core assumptions need to be reported when
using multilinear regression analysis (Lee, Benoit-­Bryan, and
Johnson 2012), namely: (1) multicollinearity, (2) heteroskedastic-
ity, and (3) independent observations. To test assumption (1), we
calculate the VIF-­values of each variable added into the model as
an independent variable. The highest VIF is 1.05, suggesting that
multicollinearity is no concern. For assumption (2), we include ro-
bust standard errors to deal with any potential heteroskedasticity
concerns. Assumption (3) is more problematic. Indeed, our obser-
vations (i.e., teachers) are not independent but, rather, nested in
schools and countries. To account for this nesting, we conduct two
interventions in our analyses. First, we cluster standard errors at
the school-­level to account for clustering in schools. Second, we
either add country dummies to account for country-­level variance
or we employ multilevel modeling (see below).
Path analysis is employed to test hypotheses 1 and 2. Path anal-
ysis allows us to simultaneously estimate direct, indirect, and
total associations. Structural equation modeling (SEM) is used
to conduct path analysis. Specifically, GSEM is adopted to allow
the addition of country dummies (Rabe-­Hesketh, Skrondal,
and Pickles  2004). Based on suggestions of Preacher and
Hayes (2004), 500 bootstrap resampling is also included to pro-
duce the bias-­corrected confidence intervals of the mediation
coefficient and standard errors.

offer some support as a psychological mechanism underlying the
association between red tape and teacher's overall job satisfac-

test the mediating role of self-­efficacy in different tasks. The
result shows that teacher's self-­efficacy in student engagement
does not have a significant mediating impact while teacher's self-­
efficacy in classroom management and instruction aligns with
our findings (see Appendix 1 for more details and discussion).

5   |   Discussion
This study started off by asking: Why and under which condi-
tions does red tape negatively impact job satisfaction? Based
on cross-­country data from the OECD Teaching and Learning
International Survey 2018, we first provided evidence for the
direct negative red tape—job satisfaction relationship. Then
we found that self-­efficacy does partially help to explain the
red tape—job satisfaction relationship, though only accounting
for about 8% of the total effect. Moreover, we uncovered that
a high-­individualistic culture significantly moderates the red
tape—job satisfaction relationship, with the negative associa-
tion between red tape and job satisfaction being stronger in a
high-­individualistic culture. These findings have clear implica-
tions for public administration theory, research, and practice.
5.1   |   Implication for Theory
First, our study provides empirical evidence for a direct, nega-
tive relationship between red tape and teacher's job satisfaction.
This finding is not a surprise and supports previous literature
which already demonstrated similar findings in for example
youth welfare organizations (Steijn and Van der Voet 2019), hos-
pitals (Muylaert et  al.  2022), and the military (Hattke, Vogel,
and Znane-­witz 2018). By testing this relationship in an educa-
tional context and including country-­fixed effects from 45 coun-
tries, our study aligns with and further corroborates George
et al. (2021)'s observation that red tape is harmful across sectors,
countries, and professions. Consequently, this study adds to the
perspective that red tape is a universal issue.
Second, we corroborate—though with some nuance—the theo-
retical proposition that red tape could have an indirect, negative
association with job satisfaction via its impact on self-­efficacy,
as argued based on social cognitive theory. More specifically,
in the context where the work environment is characterized
by procedural constraints, employee work attitude may suffer
as they perceive their performance goals as unachievable re-
gardless of their effort (Wright  2004). This finding addresses
the call by Pandey (2021) to unravel the psychological process
through which red tape affects individuals. By focusing on self-­
efficacy, we provide additional empirical evidence, highlighting
the mediating role of the cognitive appraisal process (Davis and
Pandey  2023). However, the mediation analysis showed that
self-­efficacy can only explain a small portion of the relationship
between red tape and teacher's job satisfaction (about 8% of the
total effect). Many other potential mediators could matter, mak-
ing self-­efficacy not the “magical bullet” through which to look
at the red tape-­job satisfaction relationship.
Finally, linking back to Hofstede's model of cultural values, our
research opens the black box of variations in consequences of
red tape across countries and shows that high-­individualistic
culture is an important moderating factor in the relationship
between red tape and job satisfaction. In high-­individualistic
cultural contexts, individuals perceive red tape as a constraint
on their freedom, independence, and autonomy, resulting in a
significant decrease in job satisfaction. However, in moderate
and low individualist cultural contexts, the greater value on
collectivist identity, conformity, and group harmony can reduce
the negative association between red tape and job satisfaction.
This research addresses the call by recent public administration
research that individual experiences cannot be separated from
their context and to investigate the embeddedness of red tape
in specific macro-­, meso-­, and micro-­level contexts (Bryson,
George, and Seo  2024; Moynihan  2018; George et  al.  2021).
Taking this mid-­range theory perspective, we suggest that red
tape is not equally bad all of the time, everywhere, and that cul-
ture serves as an important boundary condition.
5.2   |   Implication for Research
A number of recommendations for future research emerge from
this study. First, the negative relationship between red tape and
job satisfaction resonates with recent research on green tape
that correlates burdensome rules with lower employee morale
while arguing that “good” rules (e.g., consistent rule application
and optimal control) can enhance positive employee outcomes
(DeHart-­Davis, Davis, and Mohr  2015). We encourage future
research to account for specific attributes of organizational
rules—beyond the presence or absence of rules themselves—to
fully understand their effect on employee outcomes.

Second, as our results reveal a relatively small, negative indirect
relationship between red tape and job satisfaction via self-­efficacy,
we call for additional research into this complex relationship. As
advocated by Walker and Andrews (2015), more sophisticated the-
oretical models are needed to unravel this complex relationship,
taking moderation and mediation into account. For example, the
multidimensionality of self-­efficacy identified in this study can
support follow-­up research to establish moderated mediating
models, investigating how and under which conditions some di-
mensions of self-­efficacy can be activated when encountering red
tape. Incorporating insights from stress appraisal theory can cer-
tainly prove useful in this regard. Given the scattered evidence, fu-
ture research can consider including multiple mediations, which
can contribute to identifying the most important path of the over-
all relationship between red tape and employee outcomes, and
theorizing a more comprehensive understanding of the complex
dynamics at play (Vandersmissen, George, and Voets 2024).
Third, more work is required to bridge the micro–macro schism.
Understanding how organizational-­level characteristics (such
as organizational size and structure) and individual-­level char-
acteristics (such as personality traits and self-­efficacy) inter-
act with country-­level institutions in shaping responses to red
tape is an area that requires further exploration. Cross-­country
datasets such as the one used in this study are becoming widely
available and can provide the necessary data to test multilevel
theoretical frameworks. For example, Tiggelaar, Groeneveld, and
George (2023) combined TALIS survey with Hofstede's country-­
level dataset and found the importance of the cultural dimen-
sion of power distance in moderating the association between
time spent on administrative tasks and job stress. Analyzing the
impact of other cultural dimensions or administrative traditions
in the experience of red tape could be a follow-­up.
Fourth, future research should consider using multi-­source
data, as our results are based on single-­source perceptual data,
which could be susceptible to CSB (George and Pandey 2017).
Although our one-­factor model suggests that influential CSB is
absent, that CSB is not a universal inflator of the effect sizes of
red tape (George et al. 2021), and that interaction effects cannot
be an artifact of CSB (George and Pandey 2017), it might still
be interesting for future research to use multi-­source data when
investigating the impact of red tape.
Finally, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our
study, particularly regarding our unidimensional measure of
red tape, which cannot fully capture the nature of subjective
assessments of compliance burdens (i.e., information process-
ing and emotional consequences) as suggested by Davis and
Pandey (2023). As a result, we cannot draw robust causal con-
clusions from our findings, although to what extent this lim-
itation of measurement can influence or bias our findings is
still in doubt, given the insignificant differences between the
reported level of red tape concerning administrative delay, ad-
ministrative burden, and rule dysfunctionality (Hattke, Hensel,
and Kalucza 2020). To address these concerns, we recommend
that future research could capture the multidimensionality of
red tape, for instance by taking into account the rule dynamism
and emotional consequences (Bozeman  2012). Moreover, ex-
perimental methods or adopting a longitudinal research design
could help cope with endogeneity concerns.
5.3   |   Implications for Practice
Our research provides empirical evidence that red tape is det-
rimental to employee outcomes. Public managers should draw
on the concept of green tape to design and implement rules
in ways that enhance, or at least not damage, job satisfaction
(DeHart-­Davis 2009). Moreover, our findings help public man-
agers understand how and why red tape elicits undesirable em-
ployee outcomes. Managers must be aware that employees' lower
self-­efficacy could be a potential signal for their job dissatisfac-
tion when they perceive excessive red tape. However, targeting
self-­efficacy alone may not be the most productive strategy.
Additional strategies aimed at other causal mechanisms are
needed to tackle red tape's negative effects. Teachers are partic-
ularly affected by red tape. Public managers in education need
to carefully unravel the red tape—job satisfaction relationship
to understand why exactly it is that teachers experience reduced
satisfaction through red tape. Unraveling this relationship can
help public managers identify causal mechanisms that can be
targeted, especially when the amount of red tape remains a given
that cannot be reduced. Asking teachers why exactly they experi-
ence negative consequences linked to red tape, and how this can
be addressed according to them could be a clear starting point.
Our results suggest that red tape is a universal issue while
also being embedded within a cultural context. Its impact on
job satisfaction is more pronounced in high-­individualistic
countries. Public managers operating in such contexts should
be particularly aware of red tape concerns, making red tape
a clear pathology to address. In other less-­individualistic set-
tings, tackling red tape as “the” way of increasing public-­sector
workers' job satisfaction may not be an equally high priority,
red tape may be more of a nuisance than pathology. Apart from
public managers within these settings, our findings also have
implications for those working across contexts. This includes
international organizations such as the OECD, UNESCO or
UNICEF. To identify issues in education systems, a thorough
understanding of culture can matter as culture can influence
the extent to which specific concerns actually impact teachers'
job satisfaction.

We separately test the mediating role of self-­efficacy in classroom management, instruction, and student engagement. As shown in Table 1, the
indirect association via teacher's self-­efficacy in classroom management and instruction accounts for about 8% of the total association, which is
consistent with our initial findings of the mediating role of the overall self-­efficacy. This indicates that teacher's self-­efficacy in these two tasks has
a relatively small and partially mediating impact on the association between red tape and teacher's overall job satisfaction. However, teacher's self-­
efficacy in student engagement does not have a significant mediating impact and red tape is not significantly associated with teacher's self-­efficacy
in student engagement.
These results are aligned with multifaceted nature of self-­efficacy that some people might have high self-­efficacy for some tasks and, at the same
time, very low self-­efficacy for other tasks (Bandura 2006). To this regard, red tape may not influence job satisfaction through ruining their self-­
efficacy equally in all core tasks. In the school context, our results show that only the efficacy in classroom management and instruction are influ-
enced by the red tape, which in turn decreased the teacher's job satisfaction (Table A1).

ABSTRACT
A new mayor's transition period is widely regarded as important to their overall success, yet mayoral transitions have received
little research attention. This exploratory, mixed-­method study of 15 newly elected U.S. mayors combines primary survey data of
time use with two waves of mayoral interviews to illuminate the nature and purposes of mayoral activities during the transition
period. Analyses of the survey data suggest that mayors' activities vary significantly and are associated with a range of city and
mayor characteristics. Comparison of these data with mayors' ex ante forecast time use finds that mayors-­elect underestimate
demands for certain types of mayoral work during their first 100 days, such as short-­term policy work and being physically pres-
ent in city hall, while overestimating the time they will spend on strategic work and community presence. Drawing on interview
data, the paper conceptualizes mayoral transition work as a strategic investment in personal, relational, and organizational
resources.
1   |   Introduction
The job of a U.S. mayor typically encompasses broad responsibil-
ities including policymaking, public relations, fiscal administra-
tion, and community engagement (Boin et al. 2005; Svara 1990).
Yet mayors-­elect often lack adequate preparation for these di-
verse duties; indeed, many have never worked in government at
the time they take office. The transition period, conventionally
defined as the first 100 days of a new administration, is therefore
an acute period of adaptation. Though relatively brief, a may-
or's transition period is a singularly important window during
which they prepare for their new role while also performing it—
building relationships, translating campaign priorities to opera-
tional goals, and beginning the implementation of their agendas
(Feiock et al. 2003).
Despite their importance to mayoral success, mayoral transi-
tions have received surprisingly little research attention. While
practice-­oriented guides published by consultants and think
tanks offer advice to new mayors and their staffs (see, e.g.,
Dharasathy et al. 2018; NDI 2021), academic research typically
addresses transitions only within larger frameworks of mayoral
leadership and management, often without attending to distinc-
tive aspects of the transition period (Hambleton and Gross 2007;
Mouritzen and Svara  2002). Key questions therefore remain:
What is the nature of mayoral work during the first 100 days of
a new administration? How might mayors better navigate this
critical period and thus lay the groundwork for their long-­term
effectiveness?
This article first addresses these questions through an explor-
atory two-­wave time-­use survey of 15 newly elected mayors
of U.S. cities. Using time allocation as a lens for understand-
ing executive behavior (Coviello et  al.  2014; Porter and
Nohria 2018), we compare mayors' forecast time use for their
first 100 days in office (first wave, collected prior to entry into
office) and their retrospectively reported time use for the same
period (second wave, collected shortly after their first 100 days
in office). We find that reported time use during the transition
period varies considerably according to differences in mayors'
personal backgrounds and structural factors, such as the sys-
tem of city governance, that circumscribe the mayoral role in
each city. In addition, we find systematic differences between
ex ante forecast and ex post reported time use during the first

To further explore mayoral interpretations of and motivations

mayoral transitions, defining transition work as “an elected offi-

conceptualization helps to explain descriptive differences in the
activities and priorities that characterize different mayors' tran-
sition periods. It also offers a prescriptive framework for how
transitions may be used to build essential governing capacity.
The article proceeds by situating the study in the small literature
on mayoral transitions and research on executive transitions more
broadly. We then describe our methodology for collecting and ana-
lyzing data from 15 newly-­elected mayors. Results of the time-­use
survey are presented first, followed by the results of the interviews
and the development of a novel resource-­based conceptualization
of transition work. The article concludes with a brief discussion of
future research directions and implications for practice.
2   |   Background
Research on mayoral transitions remains surprisingly lim-
ited (Prusinski et al. 2015; Spicer and Graham 2016), typically
appearing as a secondary theme within larger historical analyses
of urban political development in individual cities (e.g., Stone 1989)
or comparative analyses (e.g., Trounstine 2008). Detailed discus-
sions of transition dynamics are found in some historical studies
of specific mayoral administrations (e.g., Biles 2018; Holmes and
Roper  2020; Koeneman  2013) and mayoral memoirs (see, e.g.,
Buttigieg 2019; Nutter 2018), but these are rarely conceptualized.
Scholarship on Black, Latino, and women mayors (e.g., Colburn
and Adler 2001; Orr and Morel 2018; Holman 2015) also addresses
political transitions but tends to emphasize the impact of broader
structural and contextual factors affecting leaders belonging to
these groups rather than dynamics specific to their transitions.
Other studies beyond mayors offer additional insights into
executive transitions. A substantial literature addresses the
transitions of private-­sector executives, particularly CEOs
(Sonnenfeld  1988; Berns and Klarner  2017). In the public
sector, studies of U.S. presidents examine practical and op-
erational issues during presidents' first few months in power
(Hess 2010; Kumar 2015), evaluate specific cases of transition
success (Burke 2001; Lovvorn and Walker 2012), and examine
legislative effectiveness during transitions (Bimes et al. 2023;
Dominguez and Knudsen 2005). A small body of research in-
vestigates government transitions in parliamentary systems
(‘t Hart and Uhr  2011), often emphasizing interactions be-
tween new governments and career civil servants (Althaus
and Vakil  2013; Bourgault and Dion  1990; Catterall  1997;
Richards 2009). Non-­academic publications by consultants and
NGOs address practical transition challenges, predominantly
at the national level (Halchin 2017; Larson 2001; Dharasathy
et al. 2018; NDI 2021; OECD 1998), but with limited focus on
local-­government transitions (LCLG 2006; Mayrl 2007). Such
publications generally propose best practices supported by an-
ecdotal evidence.
3   |   Methods
Given the limited research on mayoral transitions, we adopt
an exploratory, mixed-­method approach (Edmondson and
McManus 2007) that combines survey and interview methods.
Given the nascent state of research in this area, the aim of the
present study was not to identify or adjudicate between causal
mechanisms, but to provide initial descriptive evidence and con-
ceptualization to guide future, more fine-­grained investigations.
The present study used a survey of mayoral time use as an em-
pirical entry point to understand mayoral transitions, building on
previous studies of executive time allocation (Coviello et al. 2014;
Porter and Nohria 2018). Executive time is widely recognized as a
scarce resource essential to individual and organizational perfor-
mance (DeVoe and Pfeffer 2011). Mayors, as leaders of large and
complex organizations, face intense and wide-­ranging demands on
their time (Yates 1977). Time pressures are likely to be particularly
acute during the transition period, when mayoral responsibilities
are wide-­ranging and emergent. Studying how mayors choose to
allocate their time under these conditions therefore offers a unique
window into their priorities and behavior.

4   |   Sample and Data Collection
Our sample consisted of 15 newly elected U.S. mayors re-
cruited from participants in a November 2022 educational
program for mayors-­elect of U.S. cities with populations ex-
ceeding 75,000. Of 25 educational program participants, we
excluded three who had been elected in Spring 2022 and had
therefore already served more than 100 days in office. Among
the 22 remaining eligible mayors-­elect, five did not respond to
an initial survey invitation sent prior to the beginning of the
educational program, and two did not respond to our second
survey request after their first 100 days in office. All 15 mayors
who completed both surveys participated in an initial inter-
view at the time of the second survey, and 14 participated in a
follow-­up interview 1 year later.
All study-­related activities were carried out by researchers who
played no role in the educational program's design, organiza-
tion, or implementation. The content of the educational program
encouraged mayors-­elect to pay attention to their time use, but
did not refer to our study or any of the categories of time use
addressed by it.

4.2   |   Survey Data Collection
We collected time-­use data in two waves with identical ques-
tions. In the first wave, conducted via an on-­line survey plat-
form prior to the program, mayors provided ex ante forecasts of
how they would allocate their time during their first 100 days in
office. In the second wave, administered via virtual video con-
ference shortly after the end of their first 100 days in office (i.e.,
April 2023), mayors reported ex post on how they had actually
spent their time during their first 100 days in office. During
the second survey, questions were administered verbally with
response options displayed visually via a shared screen.
4.3   |   Interview Data Collection
Following completion of the second wave, two authors con-
ducted a semi-­structured interview with each mayor that
elicited their reflections both on the largest differences be-
tween their expected and reported time use and on the nature
of mayoral work during the transition period. All interviews
were video-­recorded and fully transcribed, and mayors were
promised anonymity as a condition of participation. Analysis
of interview transcripts took place after all interviews were
complete. A second wave of interviews was conducted with
14 of the 15 original participants approximately 1 year after
the first wave of interviews (i.e., in Spring and Summer 2024).
These interviews focused on mayors' time use during their
transition periods, the work that they carried out during their
first 100 days in office, and the role of their transitions in their
subsequent work as mayors.
5   |   Results
5.1   |   Reported Time Use
Basic description of mayors' ex post reporting of their transition
period time use reveals substantial variation between mayors
in different types of governance systems. Table 4 presents sum-
mary statistics for the overall sample and for subsets based on
the interaction of two variables identified by prior research as
relevant to time use: council-­manager versus strong-­mayor sys-
tem of city governance (Eskridge 2014; French and Folz 2004)
and full-­time versus part-­time mayoral role (French and
Folz 2004). Notably, the sample included no part-­time mayors
in strong-­mayor systems. Table 5 reports summary statistics for
additional subgroups.
5.1.1   |   Hours Worked
New mayors worked long hours, clocking 57 h per week on av-
erage. As expected, full-­time mayors worked substantially more
hours than part-­time mayors (63 h vs. 46 h weekly). In interviews,
nearly all mayors described working on weeknights and week-
ends, often attending council meetings and community events.
5.1.2   |   Urgent Work
New mayors reported spending nearly 40% of their work time
during their first 100 days on issues that they categorized as ur-
gent. Mayors in our sample experienced a wide range of emer-
gencies during their first 100 days, including floods, wildfires,
sinkholes, tornadoes, gas leaks, and mass violence.
5.1.3   |   Physical Location
New mayors were in city hall for about half their time overall on
average, and somewhat less for part-­time mayors. Mayors fre-
quently mentioned the importance of in-­person meetings for es-
tablishing or building relationships with staff and city managers
(in council-­manager systems), and they also frequently used city
hall for meetings with constituents. About one-­quarter of may-
ors' time was spent at events in community locations, such as
graduation ceremonies, school plays, groundbreaking ceremo-
nies, funerals, and meetings at local establishments, and about
10% was spent at home.
5.1.4   |   Time Alone
Mayors spent only about a quarter of their work time alone,
engaged in activities that included reading and responding to
emails, reading briefs, and preparing for meetings. Among the
sub-­groups, full-­time mayors in council-­manager systems spent
the largest proportion of their work time alone.

5.1.6   |   Type of Work
Two types of mayoral work—short-­term policy work and

Substantial differences exist in the share of time spent on ac-

strong mayors spending five times as much of their time on this

spent almost one-­third of their time on the power-­broker role,
compared with one-­fifth for mayors in strong-­mayor systems.

5.2.1   |   Hours Worked
The ex post reported work hours of new mayors during their
transitions consistently exceeded their ex ante expectations.
Multiple mayors attributed this to unanticipated pressure
from constituent groups to build relationships, while others
noted that they expected more work to be handled by council
members, with one remarking, “…what I would like to see is
for me to use my city council members more. I don't need to be
at everything” (Mayor #2). Yet the tendency to underestimate
working hours was not universal: one mayor who overesti-
mated the time they would spend working noted that they had
“bought into this hype… I thought I'd be sitting in the office,
and everything would be bombarding… that's what I was an-
ticipating” (Mayor #4).
5.2.2   |   Physical Location
In terms of physical location, new mayors generally underes-
timated how much time they would spend in city hall, while
overestimating the time they would spend at home and in the
community. Some mayors explained that scheduling back-­to-­
back meetings with constituent groups in city hall was more
efficient than traveling between multiple meeting locations in
the community. Yet one new mayor expressed surprise at their
sense of confinement from spending so much time in city hall,
remarking that “I feel like I'm locked in my office” (Mayor #9).
Another new mayor reflected that “it's gotten better at the tail
end, but at the beginning, I was spending all day, every day in
city hall. That was isolating” (Mayor #13). Other new mayors
noted unexpected advantages of remaining in city hall. Several
were surprised to find that constituents preferred to visit city
hall. One new mayor reflected on the sense of authority that
they gained by meeting in the mayor's office, perceiving it as
a “homefield advantage” (Mayor #1). Other new mayors de-
scribed how spending time in city hall allowed them to learn
about city processes and establish relationships with city staff.
Many, however, lamented that they had not spent more time in
the community during their transitions.
5.2.3   |   Co-­Working
New mayors generally overestimated the time they would spend
alone and with city council while underestimating the time they
would spend with others. Several acknowledged that meetings
often resulted from their own choices and the discovery of their
newfound access, as mayors, to city stakeholders. One remarked
that “if I ask for a meeting, it will happen… so many people to
meet with” (Mayor #7). Expressing a tendency to accept every
invitation to attend an event, another mayor shared that “my
chief of staff jokes that I have community FOMO [fear of miss-
ing out]” (Mayor #3). Yet this mayor and many others expressed
surprise and regret that they did not have more time during their
transitions for solitary work and reflection. One mentioned that
“I wish I had more uninterrupted time to read and think… for
deep work” (Mayor #11).
Mayors also generally overestimated the time they would spend
with city council during their transitions. Some attributed this
difference to the inexperience of councilmembers, noting that
many of the councilors in their cities were new to their roles and
had not yet begun to make direct demands on the mayor's time.
One suggested that councilors “really haven't figured out how to
pull the levers that they have. It's a slow start for them” (Mayor
#13). Other new mayors noted their surprise at the extent to
which councilors seemed preoccupied with other issues and un-
interested in city business. One reported, “I thought I'd spend a
lot of time with the council to build relationships… I try to en-
gage them, but the responses are short and dry” (Mayor #6).

6   |   Toward a Resource-­Based Conceptualization of
Mayoral Transition Work
The time-­use survey allowed us to document the activities that
make up mayors' transition periods and to identify individual
and structural correlates of large differences in these activities
across mayors. Comparison of ex ante forecast and ex post re-
ported time use further identified areas in which mayors-­elect
are well informed about the nature of this work. The time-­use
data can offer only limited insight, however, into how mayors
use the transition period strategically to lay the groundwork for
their tenures as mayor. In this section, we draw on two waves of
in-­depth mayoral interviews to develop a conceptual framework
of mayoral transition work.
We draw on our interview data to conceptualize the work of
new mayors during their transitions as strategic investments
in the development of resources anticipated to be important to
these mayors' future effectiveness. This perspective builds on
resource-­based theory from strategic management (Barney 1991;
Grant  1991) and specifically its application to public-­sector
organizations (Bryson et al. 2007; Lee and Whitford 2013). In
this context, resources are defined as “any assets that an or-
ganization might draw on to help it achieve its goals” (Bryson
et al. 2007, 704). Several studies have attributed the sustained
effectiveness of public-­sector organizations to deliberate re-
source cultivation aligned with organizational goals (e.g., Meier
and O'Toole Jr 2002, 2003; Mayne et al. 2020; for a review, see
Walker and Andrews 2015).
From this resource-­based perspective, we categorize the re-
sources addressed by mayors' transition activities according
to two main dimensions. First, we observed both investments
in specific resources and generic resources. Mayors invested in
specific resources to achieve goals that were defined prior to
the beginning of their term, often during their campaigns,
such as school performance, infrastructure projects, and ad-
ministrative reform. However, the usefulness of specific re-
source investments was often limited by their specificity. For
example, studying the details of residential zoning ordinances
might be essential for achieving a predetermined goal of con-
structing affordable housing, but is of limited use for achieving
other goals, including those that might emerge later in a may-
or's term. Thus, mayors also used their transitions to invest
in generic resources that were flexible and could be deployed
in response to emergent opportunities or exigencies. For ex-
ample, activities designed to build a strong culture among
employees in city hall were not intended to fulfill a specific
campaign promise, but rather to aid the city in pursuing a

6.1   |   Resource Endowments
A key part of the context of transition work is the resources al-
ready available to a new mayor at the time of their entry into office.
The resource endowments available to the mayors in our sample
differed widely. Many mayors derived personal resources, both
specific and general, from their previous roles working in city
government. For example, in referring to one of their main policy
goals, a mayor claimed that because of specific technical knowl-
edge acquired through many years of work for the city, “I know
how to solve the problem” (Mayor #6). Other personal resource
endowments were more general in nature. One mayor who was
previously a council member noted, “I was already going to coun-
cil meetings as an advocate on issues. So, I was very comfortable
with how to run a meeting. I was comfortable with, you know…
how the sausage is made” (Mayor #3). Similarly, a mayor with a
background in other forms of public office explained that from
their long career in public leadership, “I have a certain system
or a certain approach that I use which mostly involves listening
to as many sides as I can get in the time that I have to make a
decision and then make the call” (Mayor #9). Others mentioned
the value of their established reputations in the city. For example,
one mayor who previously led a prominent city department men-
tioned that “I was leading [several] hundred [employees]… that
just kinda has its own thing that comes with it, and people just
naturally see you as a leader” (Mayor #12).
Mayors also took office with various existing relational resource
endowments. Multiple mayors relied heavily on personal net-
works to hire their senior staff, with one reflecting that “little
did I know that I would need to rely on all those relationships
that were just organically built over my career, my lifetime…
I've had to call upon every single one of those for one reason
or another” (Mayor #9). Another new mayor, who previously
led a city department, noted that “because of my prior history
with the city, I was very, very familiar with the players, and I
could contact them directly” (Mayor #8). Another mayor in a
council-­manager system mentioned that “the first time I talked
to [the city manager] was when I was running for council [years
before], so I've known him for a while, and we worked well to-
gether the last [number of] years” (Mayor #7).
Mayors also were endowed with significant organizational re-
sources located within the mayors' offices and city halls they
entered. One mayor attributed their smooth entry into office
to “having a very supportive city manager and very supportive
staff who are really aligned on values. You know, I wasn't hav-
ing to fight battles with them. They were very like, ‘okay, we're
all gonna row in the same direction. How do you want to do
it?’ That was extremely helpful” (Mayor #7). Similarly, another
mayor highlighted the value of key personnel inherited from
the previous mayoral administration: “my chief of staff… he
was chief of staff to the prior Mayor. He has really helped [me]
with navigating things” (Mayor #11). In at least one case, a new
mayor's strong relationship with their predecessor helped them
to quickly assess and access existing organizational resources:
“you know, we are friends, and while [the previous mayor] was
transitioning out, I had a pretty good 3 to 6 months head start in
getting into office” (Mayor #4).
6.2   |   Transition Work
The new mayors in our study invariably perceived a gap be-
tween their resource endowments and the resources that
would be required for them to govern effectively throughout
their terms in office. Our interviews suggest that mayors
viewed their transitions as an opportunity to develop various
resources and thus to close this gap. We conceptualize tran-
sition work as: “an elected official's initial investments in the
personal, relational, and organizational resources that they
anticipate to be necessary for effective leadership and gover-
nance through their tenure in office.” Thus, transition work
is guided by a compensatory logic that makes new resource
investments to address perceived deficits in a mayor's initial
resource endowments.
6.2.1   |   Personal Transition Work
Many of the mayors in our sample reported investments in
personal resources specific to certain goals. For example, sev-
eral described developing deep policy knowledge in anticipa-
tion of implementing their agendas. As one described, during
their transition “I spent a lot of time… talking to other cities,
looking at what people have done across the country” (Mayor
#3) related to one of their main campaign issues. Mayors also
invested in learning about specific areas unrelated to their
personal interests, but within the scope of mayoral respon-
sibilities. As one mayor described it, they “knew there was
going to be some reading to do in advance” (Mayor #9). One
type of specific personal resource mentioned by multiple may-
ors as an important investment during their transitions was
knowledge of ongoing legal proceedings. One explained that
they “spent significant time working with our city attorney
on some of the legal actions against our city… I was not aware
of the time that I would want to spend… making sure that I
understand” (Mayor #1).
In addition to developing these specific resources, mayors also
invested during their transitions in general personal knowl-
edge and skills that were not specific to any particular agenda
or task, but which they nonetheless expected to be valuable.
One described being “very interested in understanding the
police department, the fire department, storm water, housing,
and so I spend a good amount of time meeting with staff in
those departments… I have the strong desire to really under-
stand how the city works, so that I can better explain things
to the public” (Mayor #10). Other mayors described general
learning about the mayoral role itself. For example, one re-
flected that “once you get elected, there's about 10 other jobs
that nobody ever told you that you were going to be doing…
we have an automatic board seat on a lot of different state and
national organizations… you go to everything, and then you
just have to figure out what's the best fit for your city” (Mayor
#3). Another mayor described the transition as being about
“understanding all the tools, all the leverage that you actually
have and using it strategically” (Mayor #5).

commenting that “my kids and my [spouse] were very involved
in my campaign… I think that was good prep for them. They
kind of knew what to expect” (Mayor #15). Another noted that
the transition period allowed them to recalibrate their reaction
to negative feedback: “when I receive an angry email, I now re-
alize it's one of hundreds that I will receive… it doesn't impact me
the same way” (Mayor #10).
In reflecting on their transitions, mayors identified a number of
areas in which they wished they had invested differently. One
noted that “I have to force myself to just take more personal
time… for my long-­term, you know, physical, mental, marital,
family health” (Mayor #15). Another expressed regret at not
having invested more in communication skills during the tran-
sition period: “Being a better communicator to the public and
with the media are things that I definitely have had to learn on
the fly… I would have put more time into that, and I would advise
all mayors on that going forward” (Mayor #9). Multiple mayors
described regret at being too tentative during their transitions,
one describing that “if there is one thing that I've learned out of
[early months as mayor] it is confidence” (Mayor #4). Another
reflected that “I wish I could have put impostor syndrome aside
a little sooner” (Mayor #12).
6.2.2   |   Relational Transition Work
A significant portion of mayors' transition work focused on de-
veloping relationships specific to the policies that they intended
to advance early in their terms. One mayor reported that they
“spent a lot of time just before I was elected knowing what poli-
cies that I really want… [now I am] working with my colleagues
to make sure that I get them there” (Mayor #5). Other mayors
used their transitions to identify potential allies by soliciting
feedback, and thus clarifying the boundaries of their coalitions
related to specific policy goals. For instance, one described how,
in an initial retreat with council during the transition, they
“threw up a test balloon about how I want to add another pri-
ority to the list, and that is [the mayor's signature initiative]… a
couple were positive and a couple were, you know… against it”
(Mayor #4).
While some relationship-­building was focused on specific
goals, mayors spent significant time during their transitions
building connections with certain powerful actors who they
expected to be important later in their terms. As one described
it, “it's too late in the moment to build relationships” (Mayor
#1). Another relayed that “to build relationships takes time,”
and that they spent much of their transition “inserting myself
into interests that don't have an agenda” (Mayor #8). While
many mayors described a long stream of incoming meeting
requests, some initiated these meetings proactively: as one
described, contrasting their own approach to that of their
predecessor, “90% was me initiating and saying ‘hey, I'd like
to talk with you.’… I did try to be really intentional… folks who
were influential in the community, or that interact with the
city on city issues a lot” (Mayor #7). One mayor visited 100
small businesses in their first 100 days, while another engaged
community leaders by setting up community hiring boards to
appoint senior staff (Mayor #5). One reflected that early rela-
tionship building made it “easier to put together coalitions in
the community,” despite spending less time on those relation-
ships later in their term (Mayor #3).
New mayors, especially those in council-­manager systems,
viewed relationship-­building with council as a central transi-
tion goal. One new mayor recalled that they “proactively did
things that I thought would help the Council have a better
relationship [with me]… one-­on-­one meetings monthly with
each of the council members, and just checking in with them”
(Mayor #10). Another mayor described “sharing the wealth”
with councilors by inviting them to make public appearances
in their place (Mayor #12). As another mayor put it, “the first
100 days is building a foundation… if you're going to imple-
ment something, and you haven't established the conversation
and collaboration with those on the council or the public… you
may get to where you want, but you may have less support
along the way” (Mayor #8).
Mayors also viewed their transitions as a key period for building
relationships with staff. One mayor described extensive meet-
ings with staff across their city as a way to build confidence
and identify allies and resources throughout city government.
Another mayor described this process as “trying to understand
the various roles within city government, and also the people
that I trusted” (Mayor #8). Another recalled that “for a while I
was really trying to invite a lot of people into city hall and into
my office. There were people like the police chief who had never
been in the Mayor's office. The Public Works Director had never
been in the Mayor's office” (Mayor #12). One mayor noted, for
example, that “if I do delegate, that it's going to get done… a lot
of these folks that you start with, I didn't know and now I have
relationships with them so that I can lean on people, or if I don't
understand something… I kind of know where to go to find the
answers” (Mayor #7). “Getting to know staff has been really
key,” another mayor described, “and I get to know them in a way
that I didn't as a council member, or know… a greater number
of staff… more of a depth on who does what, and that has been
really important” (Mayor #10).
Mayors also described the importance and challenges of using
the transition period to invest in building or deepening ties with
external government agencies, including state and federal of-
ficials. Sometimes, mayors sought to build these relationships
with specific campaign commitments and projects in mind. For
example, one mayor used transition time to pursue relationships
with state-­level education officials responsible for local educa-
tion funding, but noted that “navigating the complex person-
alities and bureaucracy of the state is far more difficult than I
had anticipated” (Mayor #13). Other mayors invested in state
and federal relationships based on the general expectation that
they might need to call on these relationships in the future.
One noted that these officials “don't have any connection to us,
but… have great control over us at some point” (Mayor #1). Some

some cases, these relationships were contentious and required
recalibration or repair during the transition. One new mayor,
formerly a council member, described their approach to mend-
ing relationships after a difficult campaign: “I just said, let's hit
the reset button. Everybody has a clean slate with me, and I will
judge you by the content of your character” (Mayor #8). Another
related that “my predecessor was very alienating” to other city

good mayor, but an introvert, and he deferred completely to the
[city manager], so much that it was said that the [city manager]
was the mayor… and that is not my personality” (Mayor #2).

day after my election, I would tell myself to fast track those re-

same mayor noted “nobody gave me a Rolodex, for if things
go bad, here are friends in [the state capitol]… and I am abso-

(Mayor #1). Another mayor noted that the diffuse structure of
the local business community made it difficult to build relation-
ships, as “there's a lot of different people running businesses…
there's some organizations, but they don't necessarily represent
everybody” (Mayor #7). Others criticized their predecessors for
failing to develop relationships on behalf of the mayor's office,
with one mayor stating, “my immediate predecessor, I mean she
never met with [city council]… I always said that if I ever got
there I would be totally different… that's what I'm trying to do”
(Mayor #4). Others expressed regret at having been too focused
on relationship building early on, in part due to the difficulty of
saying no to external requests. As one described this challenge,
“I didn't anticipate the pressure… to schedule meetings with or-
ganizations and individuals, and speaking at various events. I
think I envisioned more space in my calendar for much larger
thinking” (Mayor #10). Similarly, another reflected that it was
“difficult to structure my own time, because, fortunately or un-
fortunately, outside pressures kind of demand their own grav-
ity” (Mayor #1).
6.2.3   |   Organizational Transition Work
A final key component of transition work was investment in the
formal organization of the city itself. In some cases, mayors' or-
ganizational transition work involved the creation of new types
of staff or structures to accomplish specific policy goals. During
their transition, one mayor created committees of city staff and
community leaders focused on key social issues “where I most
want to see us move the needle in my first term” (Mayor #11).
Another mayor related that “my priority is housing and eco-
nomic development. So I established a deputy mayor of housing
and a deputy mayor of economic development, and eliminated
the deputy mayor of [other issue],” noting that their approach
was to give their staff “a lane to become experts” (Mayor #5).
Another mayor highlighted certain policy priorities by elevat-
ing certain senior staff working on those roles to independent
deputy mayors that reported directly to the mayor, suggesting
that “we needed a new structure… to move in a new direction”
(Mayor #15).
Most organizational transition work, however, involved in-
vestments in general organizational capacity. Many mayors
described pressure to hire staff, particularly at the senior level.
One recalled that “I was still writing my own press releases up
until 2 months ago, right? Like I think the [newly-­hired staff
position] will help to just serve as a clearing house for comms,
digital, scheduling, all of that which takes up at least 5 to 6 h a
day” (Mayor #2). Another mayor explained, “it's a lot of time in
developing a team, like hiring and interviewing people… that ad-
ministrative stuff that just has to be done” (Mayor #5). Multiple
mayors emphasized that investment in team capacity was essen-
tial to unlocking their own ability to focus on higher-­level issues:
“you've got to spend time with them to make sure that you know
things are carried out the right way,” one recalled (Mayor #5).
Another recounted that “the most significant thing that we've
done… is making sure that most of our [senior staff positions]
are permanent now, including the city manager… if we had not
had them in permanent positions, we would not be faring as well
as we are in many categories” (Mayor #1). In many cases, devel-
oping capacity required specific approvals for new positions or
new funds. One mayor focused on upgrading an executive as-
sistant position to a chief of staff who “gets my calendar better,
knows who the right people that I need to be meeting with are…
a city government mover,” as opposed to a “secretarial adminis-
trator” (Mayor #1). Still another “fought… to expand our budget,
to add those two deputies” (Mayor #5). One mayor described the
time spent building relationships with senior leadership, saying
that “deep and solid relationships with the staff, the senior lead-
ership, has paid off in a lot of ways for them, for me, for the city”
(Mayor #12).
A key theme among mayors was that organizational issues often
were treated as foundational and therefore required attention
before other, more specific policy issues could be addressed.
Reflecting on their transition, one mayor noted that “once we
made a lot of movement on [corruption and transparency mea-
sures], I could focus more… on my personal policy and agenda
work” (Mayor #3). Yet another mayor identified the need to re-
negotiate with the fire and police departments in order to over-
come their capacity challenges: “I led efforts to open contracts
with the unions where they couldn't recruit, and within 30 days
actually offered them a better contract” (Mayor #8). Others noted
the importance of spending time early on building or restoring a
desirable culture in city government, reporting significant time,
in the words of one mayor, “establishing norms… helping to de-
velop managers in the early days… it's worth it” (Mayor #13).
As with other types of transition work, mayors expressed some
regrets. Multiple mayors noted that pressure to hire new staff
sometimes led to hires who were not completely aligned with

7   |   Conclusion
While the importance of the mayoral transition period—the
first 100 days—is widely accepted, few studies have examined it
directly. This study is the first scholarly account, to our knowl-
edge, that focuses specifically on the topic of mayoral transitions,
thus adding depth to an area of research previously addressed
primarily by practitioners offering consulting and other prac-
tical guidance. This paper presents new, exploratory evidence
based on a mixed-­method study of 15 newly-­elected U.S. may-
ors. Through a two-­wave time-­use survey, we document the
types of work in which mayors engage during their transition
periods and identify structural and individual factors correlated
with differences in this work, noting discrepancies between
mayors' ex ante expected and ex post reported transition time
use. Two waves of interviews with mayors provide additional
insights into the motives and strategies of mayors during their
transitions. Based on these data, we propose a novel resource-­
based framework of mayoral transition work.
Our study offers several contributions to the literature. First, our
time-­use data offer new descriptive detail regarding the nature
of the mayoral role during transitions. Some of these insights—
for instance, that new mayors generally work long hours—may
be intuitive. Others, such as the finding that even part-­time
mayors are working more than full-­time hours, may be more
surprising to both academics and practitioners. Similarly, it
may be alarming to some observers that new mayors spend
relatively little time alone and on long-­term strategic work. A
recurrent pattern in our data is that the transition work of may-
ors differs fundamentally by municipal governance structure
(i.e., strong-­mayor versus council-­manager system) and other
features of the mayoral role (e.g., full-­time versus part-­time).
For example, while transition work in strong-­mayor systems
and by full-­time mayors may resemble that of a new business
CEO, a different model of transition work likely pertains to new
mayors in council-­manager systems, who researchers describe
as occupying more facilitative roles (Greasley and Stoker 2008,
724; see also Yates 1977; Svara 2003; Nelson and Svara 2012).
While our resource-­based perspective can be applied across
these types, future scholarship might seek to further describe
and theorize about these differences across mayoral roles and
the consequences of these differences for understanding may-
oral transitions.
A second contribution of this study is the identification of par-
ticular areas of mayoral transition work that were systematically
overestimated or underestimated by mayors-­elect. Professional
role transitions of all kinds are rife with uncertainty and expec-
tations based on often-­imperfect images of one's new role (e.g.,
Ibarra 1999). In most management roles, recalibrating expecta-
tions to match the reality of a new role is viewed as a normal
and acceptable process of socialization (e.g., Oberfield  2010).
However, for prominent public officials such as new mayors,
initial misunderstandings may diminish the effectiveness of
high-­stakes transition decisions, and thus future effectiveness.
Our data show that mayors-­elect systematically underestimate
certain types of mayoral work during their first 100 days, such as
short-­term policy work and being physically present in city hall,
while overestimating the time they will spend on strategic work
and community presence. Future research might examine the
cultural and relational sources of new mayors' inaccurate expec-
tations about their roles. Such research might further attempt to
develop evidence-­based interventions to help new mayors bet-
ter understand and anticipate the demands and nature of work
during the transition period.
Third, our study provides a novel resource-­based conceptual-
ization of mayoral transition work based on mayors' own ac-
counts of the goals and consequences of their transition periods.
While prior research has developed the notion of resources as
underlying effective public management (Bryson et  al.  2007),
our framework identifies the distinctive nature and importance
of transitions and transition work in the initial development of
specific and generic resources. Prior work on strategic resources
in public management, following the strategic management
tradition, has primarily conceptualized resources at the orga-
nizational level (see, e.g., Meier and O'Toole Jr 2002, 2003). In
addition to such organizational resources, our qualitative data
provide additional evidence of investments in personal and rela-
tional resources. It further highlights that resource development
during the transition period depends fundamentally on the re-
source endowments that new mayors carry from their previous
experiences and inherit from their predecessors.
This resource-­based conceptualization of mayoral transition
work raises additional questions that offer opportunities for
further research. Like other resource-­based models, it focal-
izes the agency of the mayor as a single strategic actor, yet
compelling evidence suggests that effective urban governance
requires the collective leadership of multiple actors within
and outside of government (Ospina 2017). While it is perhaps
unsurprising that mayors would focus on the development of
resources supporting their own agency, future studies could
explore how mayors' transition work sets the stage for effective
collaborative governance. Future research might also consider
the relative value of these investments for the realization of
mayoral goals, and perhaps how they relate to other differences
between public leaders, such as leadership style. Finally, while
our framework differentiates between personal, relational,
and organizational resources, it is likely that these types are
mutually reinforcing in the long run. For example, building
relationships with community leaders during the transition
(investment in a relational resource) is likely to provide mayors
with important information pertinent to effective governance
(a personal resource).

Our study was exploratory and its results should be interpreted
in the context of several limitations. First, results based on
a small sample of 15 mayors should be interpreted with some
caution (Folz 1996). Nonetheless, this sample is relatively large
compared with other studies on this relatively rare and difficult-­
to-­access period in public leadership and offers a significant im-
provement on previous evidence. Second, as in nearly all studies
of time use, the data in our study are self-­reported and poten-
tially vulnerable to social desirability and other biases. Future
studies might consider designs that rely on third parties to audit
and verify time use and other features of the transition period.
Third, the study's sample was conditional on self-­selection into
an educational program and into the study itself, and two may-
ors were dropped between the first and second waves due to
non-­response to a follow-­up request. Finally, it is important to
clarify that this study did not attempt to examine the success of
new mayors' transition work, such as its role in allowing mayors
to fulfill campaign promises. While we acknowledge the diffi-
culty of measuring the performance of elected officials, this is
one of many promising directions for future research on mayors'
first 100 days.

Appendix A
Reported Versus Forecast Time Use, by Mayoral Characteristics
This appendix provides a methodology and results for evaluating differ-
ences between mayors in the overall accuracy of their forecasts. Overall
accuracy was calculated through a multi-­step process. First, category-­
level distances were calculated by taking the square root of the sum of
squared differences between expected and forecasted time allocation,
in percentage terms, for all items within that category (category score).
Categories included (1) urgency of work, (2) location of work, (3) type of
work, (4) work with whom, (5) mayoral roles played. Second, category-­
level distances were normalized to set the maximum distance to 1 and
the minimum distance to 0. Third and finally, these five category-­level
distances were averaged to generate a single score for each mayor (over-
all score). As these scores capture the distance between forecasts and
retrospective reports, higher scores indicate lower accuracy. Appendix
Figure A1 summarizes these overall accuracy scores across subgroups.

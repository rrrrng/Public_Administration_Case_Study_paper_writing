Article
Judging the blame game: how do citizens react to blame
shifting in public service delivery?
Oscar Nowlan
,*
Department of Government, London School of Economics and Political Science, London, WC2A 2AE, United Kingdom

Abstract
This article examines blame shifting, where elected officials attempt to deflect blame for negative outcomes onto other actors. While prior research
suggests that citizens generally disapprove of this tactic, this study re-evaluates how contextual factors shape these reactions, focusing specifically
on cases of public service failure. In many areas of public management, service delivery is delegated or contracted out to public or private orga­
nizations, raising the question of whether such institutional arrangements make it easier for politicians to shift blame onto these agents. A survey
experiment (n = 955) was conducted in the United Kingdom involving a hypothetical public service failure. Information cues varied the response
strategy of local elected officials (shifting blame or accepting responsibility) and the service delivery model (public or private sector; high or low
delegation). The results from OLS regression analyses show that participants were generally less approving of blame shifting compared to accepting
responsibility. However, approval increased when the organization being blamed was viewed by participants as carrying more blame for failures
in service delivery than the official. Although delegation levels did not directly moderate the effect of blame shifting, further logistic regression
analysis shows that higher delegation made participants more likely to view the service provider as culpable, which in turn influenced how they
reacted to blame shifting tactics. These findings highlight the conditional nature of public reactions to blame avoidance behavior, showing that
citizens’ evaluations of tactics like blame shifting depend on their beliefs about who is responsible, which can be shaped by institutional context.
The study offers new insights into when blame shifting may appear more credible or justified and underscores the role of context in shaping the
effectiveness of political blame avoidance strategies.
Key words: blame games, delegation, contracting out, crises, public service failure
Introduction
In representative democracies, where elected officials are held to
account by citizens for their conduct in office, those officials are
not passive bystanders in this process but also intervene to shape
how citizens judge their performance (McGraw 1991). When it
comes to managing public perceptions, theories of blame avoid­
ance suggest that elected officials are particularly motivated to
avoid personal blame for negative outcomes (Hood 2011;
Weaver 1986), and studies in political communication have
focused on how political incumbents attempt to protect their
reputations through various strategies, particularly when they
become implicated in personal scandals (Hamrak et al. 2024;
Schiff, Schiff, and Bueno 2025), face corruption allegations
(Dulay and Lee 2024), or implement controversial or unpopular
policies (McGraw 1991; Robison 2022). Yet political incum­
bents are also held to account and blamed for poor government
performance more broadly, including for the quality of public
services (Boyne et al. 2009), where responsibilities are often
shared across levels of government or delegated to other actors.
Accountability in these contexts is often more complex but
remains no less important. How elected officials respond to poor
performance and failures in public services can shape how citi­
zens assign responsibility for these outcomes, as well as their
broader evaluations of, and trust in, the public institutions that
deliver them. Understanding public reactions to how elected
officials handle such situations is therefore important for assess­
ing how democratic accountability functions under modern
governance arrangements. A key part of this involves how citi­
zens respond when officials attempt to shift blame rather than
accept responsibility. This study focuses on that dynamic and
asks: how do citizens respond when elected officials try to deflect
responsibility for public service failures and shift blame onto the
organizations tasked with delivering them?
Existing experimental research has found that public audi­
ences generally disapprove of blame shifting and that this tactic
is less effective at placating public opinion than other types of
strategies (Miller and Reeves 2022). However, less attention
has been given to why people evaluate blame shifting in this
way or what factors might shape these evaluations. In many
areas of public management, responsibilities for service delivery
are increasingly delegated or contracted out to other service
delivery organizations, which can affect who individuals choose
to blame and hold accountable for service failures (James et al.
2016; Marvel and Girth 2016). Yet, experimental research on
the effects of blame shifting on public opinion has largely over­
looked how variation in these institutional arrangements might
condition how individuals respond to elected officials’ attempts
to publicly deflect blame onto service delivery organizations.
To address this gap, this article examines how citizens
respond to blame shifting tactics across different organizational

To understand circumstances in which public opinion

the institutional arrangements through which public services

how institutional arrangements shape how political blame

(Hinterleitner 2020), including the possibility that delegating
and contracting out responsibilities for public service delivery

preemptively shield themselves from future blame but also

Therefore, this article argues that, under such conditions,
higher levels of delegation and contracting out increase citizens’
perceptions of service providers’ responsibility for failures,
potentially making them easier and more credible scapegoats
should elected officials choose to deflect blame.
This article tests these ideas using a novel survey experiment
conducted with an online panel of UK residents (n = 955), in
which participants were presented with a hypothetical scenario
of public service failure in UK local government and asked to
evaluate different aspects. The experiment varied the information
cues participants received along three dimensions: the type of
organization responsible for delivering the service (public sector
or private sector), the extent to which responsibilities were del­
egated to that organization (high, low, or no information), and
how local elected officials responded to the crisis (blame shift,
blame claim (i.e., accept responsibility), or neither). Previous
experimental studies on blame attribution for public service fail­
ures have only examined institutional arrangements for service
delivery (James et al. 2016; Leland, Mohr, and Piatak 2021;
Marvel and Girth 2016; Mohr et al. 2023; Piatak, Mohr, and

experimental combination of these factors to examine how insti­
tutional arrangements, such as levels of delegation in public ser­
vices, not only influence the allocation of blame, but also shape
the effectiveness of scapegoating tactics such as blame shifting.
In line with other studies (Miller and Reeves 2022), the
results show that participants generally disapproved of elected
officials who shift blame compared to those who accept respon­
sibility. However, when the blamed organization was perceived
as more culpable than the official, disapproval of blame shifting
decreased. This is an important finding, which provides empir­
ical support for the idea that public acts of blaming by elected
officials receive greater public support if they align with how
public opinion views the relative culpability of the actor being
blamed. The results also show that, when delegation levels were
higher, participants were more likely to assign greater blame
to the service delivery organization than to the elected official,
consistent with expectations about how institutional arrange­
ments influence blame attribution. However, higher or lower
levels of delegation did not directly moderate the effects of
blame shifting as hypothesized, providing only indirect evi­
dence into how institutional arrangements conditions how
public opinion responds to blame shifting strategies. Overall,
the article’s findings provide new evidence into when blame
shifting may appear more credible or justified in ways that
underscore the role of context in shaping the impacts of polit­
ical blame avoidance strategies.
Defining and conceptualizing blame shifting
Blame shifting, sometimes also referred to as “blame deflect­
ing” (Miller and Reeves 2022), involves blaming other actors
for negative outcomes to minimize perceptions of one’s own
responsibility for those same outcomes. Attributing blame is a
cognitive process but can also constitute a social act of moral
criticism (Malle et  al. 2022). Internally, humans engage in
moral information processing to make judgments about other
humans and whether they are to blame for observable incidents
of norm violation. Humans may also express these judgments
to others, and the practice of blame shifting necessarily involves
the social expression of blame.
As a form of blame avoidance behavior, the assumed goal of
blame shifting is to protect one’s own reputation and limit
blame towards oneself by attempting to frame other actors as
more (or at least equally) culpable.1 The political logic of blame
avoidance makes elected officials strongly motivated to avoid
blame for unpopular and negative outcomes (Hood 2011;
Weaver 1986) and engage in “ways of trying to minimize,
evade, or transfer culpability for harm and loss perceived to
have been avoidable” (Hood 2014, 603). Blame shifting is
therefore a common tactic in political blame games between
actors who seek to avoid blame and ascribe blame to others
for policy failures or other controversies (Bach and Wegrich
2019; Hood 2011; Hood et al. 2009; Hood, Jennings, and
Copeland 2016; Hinterleitner 2020; Heinkelmann-Wild et al.
2023; Mortensen 2012). More broadly, it is an example of the
type of negative communication that often characterizes con­
flictual interactions between political actors (Geer 2006).
Blame shifting is a communicative form of blame avoidance
and a type of “presentational strategy” (Hood 2011). Presenta­
tional strategies can be differentiated from other strategies for
blame avoidance, such as “agency strategies” that avoid or limit
blame via organizational design, or “policy strategies” that
select policies or operating routines to limit institutional and
individual liability and discretion (Hood 2011). While agency
and policy strategies are most relevant during the “anticipatory”
phase of the blame game, as they aim either to prevent blame­
worthy events from arising or prepare for their fallout, they are
less viable in the “reactive” phase, since implementing these
measures on an ad hoc basis is challenging and their credibility

Blame shifting in public service delivery
This article chooses to examine how citizens evaluate elected
officials who engage in either blame shifting or blame claiming
(i.e., accepting responsibility) over instances of crisis or failure
in public service delivery. The principal-agent relationship in
public service delivery between elected political principals and
the organizations that have been delegated responsibilities for
delivering services provides a model blame game interaction
through which to study the effects of blame shifting and blame
claiming strategies on public opinion. In this context, delega­
tion is conceptualized in general terms, as “the (partial) transfer
of decision powers over a public task from an organization that
is directly controlled by a democratically elected official[…]to
another public or private organization” (Overman 2016,
1240), meaning the transfer of responsibilities over service
delivery to a public sector organization or via some contracting
agreement to a private sector organization can both be viewed
as related forms of delegation. This approach to delegating
responsibilities for service delivery to other organizational enti­
ties can create incentives and opportunities for political prin­
cipals to engage in either credit claiming or blame avoidance,
so that “credit for positive outcomes flow upwards to them
while blame for negative outcomes flows downwards to other
public managers” (Hood and Lodge 2008, 182). However,
when things go wrong in public service delivery, there is inev­
itably less credit to be claimed. Instead, in this blame game, it
is assumed that a political principal can either accept personal
responsibility when something goes wrong in public service
delivery or choose to shift the blame onto the organizational
agents tasked with delivering these services.
The following subsections address how citizens respond to
elected officials’ use of blame shifting versus blame claiming in
the context of public service failures. The discussion first
explores why accepting responsibility is generally expected to
generate greater public approval compared to shifting blame.
It then considers how citizens’ evaluations of blame shifting
depend on their perceptions of blameworthiness and the appro­
priateness of the blamer’s actions. Finally, the role of institu­
tional context is addressed, particularly how different delegation
arrangements shape the credibility and effectiveness of blame
shifting. Each subsection introduces a hypothesis that builds
on the previous discussion, addressing distinct but complemen­
tary aspects of how citizens evaluate blame management strat­
egies, rather than posing competing claims.
Blame shifting vs. blame claiming
Studies suggest that elected officials become more willing to
attribute blame and responsibility to public managers in
instances of low public service performance (Nielsen and

Moynihan 2016) and may try to exploit delegation structures
to shed blame onto other actors (Bach and Wegrich 2019). When
public service delivery has been delegated to some other delivery
organization, that organization (in theory) gains more control
over service delivery and is therefore assumed to have more
“causal responsibility” for service outcomes (James et al. 2016;
Jilke and Baekgaard 2020; Marvel and Girth 2016). Central to
attributions of blame and responsibility is the consideration of
causality, that is what an actor did and how it contributed to
some outcome (Alicke 2000; Hamilton 1978). Thus, if another
organization is causally responsible for public service delivery,
this may provide justification for an elected official to publicly
shift blame onto that organization for service failures.
However, even if delivery organizations have increasing
causal responsibility over outcomes in public service delivery,
elected officials are still perceived to maintain a large degree of
role-based responsibility (Hamilton 1978). A role-based model
of responsibility emphasizes the “functional responsibility” for
public services (and government performance more generally)
that is inherent to the offices of elected officials, meaning that,
“in their social role as elected politicians and political principals
to service delivering organizations, they remain responsible by
the duties that are attached to their position” (Jilke and Baek­
gaard 2020, 132). This functional responsibility limits their
ability to credibly shift blame for service failures. Instead, polit­
ical leaders at the top of the organizational hierarchy are
expected to engage in a ritual of responsibility-taking and
blame claiming as it projects leadership and quells further pub­
lic debate over some controversy (Thompson 1980).
Research to date suggests that elected officials are generally
punished for trying to shift blame onto others in these types of
scenarios. While exposure to blame attribution statements by
political executives and public managers can sometimes alter
public perceptions of who is to blame in government (Marvel
2014), blame shifting can be a risky tactic (Moynihan 2012)
and evidence from several experimental studies indicate ways
in which this tactic is potentially less successful at protecting
or fortifying politicians’ reputations when compared to other
presentational strategies. Most notably, Miller and Reeves
(2022) show that elected officials who are willing to admit
blame for a crisis are consistently punished less by voters com­
pared to those who deny responsibility and try to blame others.
Others have found that blame shifting can even backfire and
increase blame towards elected officials (Porumbescu et al.
2023). Compared to blaming others, Miller and Reeves (2022)
also show that, by accepting culpability for negative outcomes
and events, elected officials can enhance public perceptions of
their leadership and character, thereby fortifying their reputa­
tions and mitigating blame. However, when political executives
engage in the blaming of others, they risk damaging public
perceptions of their character, thereby undermining their rep­
utations, and increasing the likelihood of incurring reputational
losses. Similarly, McGraw (1991) claims the effectiveness of
different presentational tactics depends on their impact on per­
ceptions of politicians’ characters, and finds that
excuse-orientated tactics, such as blame shifting, fail to posi­
tively shape these perceptions. Hypothesis 1 therefore bases its
predictions on existing expectations in the literature:
H1: Individuals express higher approval of elected officials
who claim blame for public service failures rather than those
who attempt to blame service delivery organizations.

Can elected officials blame appropriately?
The notion that citizens may not approve when elected officials
try to shift blame onto others is consistent with the idea that
people make judgments about when expressing blame is appro­
priate. The question of when blaming someone is appropriate
or permissible is a prominent one in moral philosophy. Central
to normative accounts of when blame is appropriate is a con­
sideration of facts about the blamer as well as facts about the
person being blamed, to determine if the person being blamed
is blameworthy and if the person blaming is blamer-worthy
(Friedman 2013; Tognazzini and Coates 2021). Normative
theorists have engaged extensively over the types of criteria
that persons must meet to be deserving of blame or to respon­
sibly blame others, but this article does not have the space to
explore these debates in depth.3 Instead, this general literature
provides the basis for two key premises. First, it is not appro­
priate to blame someone for some transgression they are not
deserving of blame for. Second, it is not appropriate for some­
one to blame someone else for some transgression that they
themselves are also guilty of, as they would lack the standing
to make this moral criticism and would be acting hypocritically
(Friedman 2013; Roadevin 2018). Applied to a political prin­
cipal blaming a delivery organization, it becomes more appro­
priate for the principal to blame the organization if, (1) the
organization is deserving of blame for the failure, and (2) the
principal is not also guilty or culpable for the failure.
The idea that there is a “logic of appropriateness” (March
and Olsen 2011) that generates moral criteria for what consti­
tutes appropriate blaming shares parallels with approaches in
social psychology that treat blaming as a socially regulated act
of moral criticism (Malle et al. 2022; Malle, Guglielmo, and
Monroe 2014). In social groups, blaming can be an important
form of moral criticism that serves to correct a transgressor’s
behavior; however, it can also be a costly act to the blamer,
transgressor, and wider community (Malle et al. 2022). To
manage these costs, societies place normative demands on the
social expression of blame, including the expectation to provide
“warrant” for blame (Friedman 2013; Malle et al. 2022; Malle,
Guglielmo, and Monroe 2014). The burden of warrant requires
that the blamer must be able to provide, or at least be perceived
as having, reasonable justification for directing blame at
another agent. Otherwise, they risk violating social norms
around blame expression and may themselves become a target
for criticism. Consequently, the demand for warrant and the
social costs of blaming motivates prospective blamers to sys­
tematically process information before expressing judgment of
blame (Monroe and Malle 2019).
Whereas Hypothesis 1 assumed that citizens generally dis­
approve of blame shifting by elected officials because this
behavior violates social expectations of political leadership and
responsibility-taking in government, separate consideration
should also be given to how citizens’ evaluations of blame
shifting depend on their judgments about the blameworthiness
of the person being blamed and the blamer-worthiness of the
blamer, along with their interpretation of the facts that bear
relevance on these judgments. A political principal cannot cred­
ibly blame a service delivery organization for some failure in
public services if citizens believe that the organization bears
little responsibility and blame for what happened, or if they

In this situation, to try and blame the service delivery organi­

that a service delivery organization bears most of the blame
and responsibility for a failure. Under these conditions, citizens
are likely to be more tolerant of, and perhaps even approve of,

H2: Individuals express higher approval of elected officials

they believe those organizations are more to blame than the
officials themselves, and lower approval when they believe
the organizations are equally or less to blame.
Blame shifting and the role of institutional context
Building upon the idea that citizens evaluate the appropriateness
of blaming by elected officials, this section examines whether the
effectiveness of blame shifting as a tactic depends on the context
in which it is deployed, as different contexts generate different
facts upon which audiences base their evaluations of the credi­
bility and appropriateness of a blamer’s behavior and the relative
culpability of the entity being blamed. Political blame games are
conceived of as highly context-sensitive events, meaning that
systematic attention to contextual factors is important for the
study of these events and blame avoidance behavior more gen­
erally (Hinterleitner 2020; Hinterleitner and Sager 2015). For
instance, Porumbescu et al. (2023) describe how partisan moti­
vated reasoning in politically polarized contexts creates an envi­
ronment within which citizens can interpret the blame avoidance
strategies of politicians in different ways, depending on the par­
tisan identity of politicians. This type of partisan selective attri­
bution is characteristic of how citizens evaluate the crisis
responses of governments (Graham and Singh 2024). However,
beyond considering the role of partisanship, experimental studies
have paid less attention to how other contextual factors might
shape how citizens respond to elected officials’ adoption of reac­
tive blame avoidance strategies.
Given this article’s focus on political principals who choose
to publicly blame an organization that has been delegated
responsibilities for delivering public services, institutional fac­
tors are central to its analysis. Relevant institutional factors
include the rules, norms, and structures that shape political
interactions between incumbents and opponents, accountabil­
ity systems, and the organization of policymaking and imple­
mentation more generally (Hinterleitner 2020, 26–31). These
factors emit both incentives and constraints for what types of
strategies actors can (or cannot) effectively deploy during blame
game episodes, as well as allocating relevant responsibilities
and duties among actors that establish the “rules of the game”
which dictate what actors can (or cannot) be credibly drawn
into a blame game (Hinterleitner 2020, 26). Following this
logic, this article proposes that, in contexts where there are
higher levels of delegation to either public or private agents in
public service delivery, elected officials may find greater success
in blaming service delivery organizations for failures than in
situations with lower levels of delegation, or none whatsoever.
The delegation of public service provision is a central com­
ponent to public management reforms in many countries
(Overman 2016) and has led to service delivery becoming
increasingly distanced from direct political control. These new
arrangements pose challenges for political accountability

Several experimental studies have examined the effects these

find partial support for the idea that structural differences

generally, with experimental evidence suggesting that citizens
react more negatively to negative performance information
when this information concerns a public organization provid­
ing a public service than a private organization (Van Den
Bekerom, Van Der Voet, and Christensen 2021). Collectively,
these various experimental studies reveal how institutional
arrangements condition public responses to service failures and
shape blame attribution.
If institutional design shapes citizens’ attribution of blame
in these ways, it should also influence the credibility of subse­
quent blame shifting efforts by elected officials. The blame
avoidance literature assumes that an important temporal rela­
tionship exists between anticipatory agency strategies and reac­
tive presentational strategies for blame avoidance, whereby the
former not only structures government in ways that preemp­
tively allocate or diffuse blame risk elsewhere but also shapes
the dynamics of subsequent blame games by determining which
actors can credibly be drawn into them (Hinterleitner and Sager
2017, 597). However, the way these strategies interact, partic­
ularly in terms of their combined impacts on public opinion,
remains underexplored in previous experimental studies, which
have tended to examine agency strategies and presentational
strategies separately, and to focus either on how institutional
arrangements shape blame attributions for government failures
and crises (James et al. 2016; Leland, Mohr, and Piatak 2021;
Marvel and Girth 2016; Mohr et al. 2023; Piatak, Mohr, and
Leland 2017; Walker et al. 2024) or on how politicians’ com­
munication strategies influence public opinion over these issues
(Miller and Reeves 2022; Porumbescu et al. 2023). Therefore,
an important motivation behind the experimental research
design discussed in the following section is to bridge this gap
and examine how these two types of blame avoidance strategies
jointly affect public opinion.
Of course, many reasons other than blame avoidance moti­
vate politicians to delegate (Elgie 2006; Overman 2016). In the
context of public service management, these may include ideo­
logical commitments to contracting out services in order to
reduce the size of government, or beliefs that granting greater
autonomy to public managers will enhance service performance
and responsiveness. This article does not make claims about
the motivations behind such decisions. Instead, it argues that,
when such arrangements do exist and there are higher levels
of delegation in public service delivery, this can create more
plausible targets for elected officials to shift blame onto, should
they choose to do so. Specifically, it is assumed that, in high
delegation scenarios, service delivery organizations are more
likely to be seen as to blame for failures, while elected officials
may be perceived as less directly to blame. These changing
perceptions of each actor’s culpability influence whether they
are perceived to be blameworthy or blamer-worthy, which
informs how appropriate the act of blaming is believed to be.
Accordingly, the expectation is that when responsibilities for
public service delivery have been largely delegated to another
organization, elected officials will find it easier to credibly shift
blame to that organization than in cases where delegation is
minimal or absent. Hypothesis 3 is as follows:
H3: Individuals express higher approval of elected officials
who attempt to blame service delivery organizations when
levels of delegation to those organizations are high, and
lower approval when levels of delegation are low.
Methods and data
Survey experiment design
To test these hypotheses, data were collected from an online
survey experiment involving UK residents conducted in June
2024. The survey experiment’s design and hypotheses were
preregistered and underwent internal ethical review and
approval at the author’s institution.4 Participants took part in
a study where they read and answered questions about a hypo­
thetical failure in local waste collection services in “Nor­
maltown,” a fictional town or city that could be anywhere in
the UK. A 2 × 3 × 3 between-subjects factorial experiment mod­
ified the wording of information cues in the scenario (see the
Online Supplementary Information for the full survey
vignettes). Focusing on blame in relation to local government
and municipal-level services builds on similar scenarios
explored in other experimental studies (James et al. 2016; Jilke
et al. 2017; Marvel and Girth 2016; Piatak, Mohr, and Leland
2017; Walker et al. 2024). In fact, the hypothetical location of
Normaltown and some of the language about the hypothetical
public service failure is borrowed directly from experimental
vignettes originally developed for a survey experiment in the
UK by James et al. (2016), which has also been replicated in
studies by Jilke et al. (2017) and Walker et al. (2024). Although,
while it borrows some features, this article is not a direct rep­
lication of James et al.’s original design, and combines different
sets of experimental treatments.5

problems and delays in Normaltown’s waste collection services
and viewed images of uncollected rubbish on the streets. They

lations varied: (1) who delivers the service (a public sector unit
or a private company) and (2) the level of delegation or out­
sourcing (high, low, or no information [i.e., control]). Imme­
diately after, participants separately rated from 1–7 (where
1=“no blame” and 7=“a great deal of blame”) the extent to
which they believed the elected Leader of Normaltown’s local
government and the service delivery organization were to
blame for the crisis. Next, participants read about a statement
made by the Leader responding to the crisis, where the final
experimental manipulation was introduced, varying whether
the Leader’s statement engaged in blame claiming, blame shift­
ing, or neither (i.e., control). Participants were then asked to
rate their approval of the Leader’s response on a six-point scale,
ranging from “strongly approve” to “strongly disapprove.”
Case selection
UK local waste collection services are chosen as a case for this
experiment for several reasons. Compared to other types of
public service that are not as universally consumed or provided
(for example, libraries or employment services), there is gener­
ally greater homogeneity in public consumption of waste col­
lection services and less risk that differences in peoples’
exposures to this service might influence how they respond to
the survey experiment. In England, Scotland, Wales, and
Northern Ireland, designated local authorities have a legal duty
to arrange for the collection and disposal of household waste,
but are free to decide how these services are delivered.6 Many
local authorities have delegated and contracted out their waste
collection functions to other organizational entities, making
local waste collection in UK local government a realistic and
informative case for studying the effects of blame shifting across
different organizational contexts in public service delivery. The
poor performance of local waste collection services in the UK
is also a real-life source of public frustration and frequently
prompts political blame games at the local level.7

Manipulated variables
The experimental manipulations described above correspond
to the three manipulated variables in this study: “response”,
“sector”, and “delegation level”. “Response” is one of the
main explanatory variables in this study and represents the type
of presentational strategy the Leader adopts in their statement
responding to the crisis. This variable has three treatment lev­
els: blame shift, blame claim, and control statement. The con­
trol statement, in which no presentational strategy is provided,
mentions only that the Normaltown local government has a
plan of action to address the crisis. Mentioning that the council
has a plan of action is to mitigate the extent to which any
“action bias” (Olsen 2017; Patt and Zeckhauser 2000) might
distort how respondents evaluate the Leader’s response to the
crisis. The same references to a plan of action are also included
in the blame shift statement and blame claim statement. How­
ever, in these other treatment conditions, it is also reported that
the Leader of Normaltown’s local government has either pub­
licly criticized and blamed the service delivery organization for
the failure, or that the Leader has announced that they carry
personal responsibility for what happened. Although partici­
pants were randomly assigned to these treatment conditions,
the odds of assignment were not equal. Specifically, participants
were more likely to be assigned to the blame shift treatment
(1:2) compared to the blame claim treatment (1:4) and the
control statement (1:4).
The other two manipulated variables, “sector” and “delega­
tion level”, vary the institutional context in which blame shift­
ing or blame claiming takes place. The “sector” variable
represents what sector the organization responsible for deliv­
ering the service belongs to and has two treatment levels: public
sector (i.e., a unit in the local government called the “Cleaner
Streets Unit”), or private sector (i.e., a private company con­
tracted by the local government called “Cleaner Streets Ltd”).
While this study does not test a specific hypothesis about public
versus private service delivery, organization type is varied to
account for any unobserved effects of participants’ attitudes
toward, and perceptions of, service delivery by public or private
organizations. This matters because the interaction between
political and cognitive biases can influence how citizens
appraise poor public service performance. For example,
anti-public sector bias (Marvel 2015; Van Den Bekerom, Van
Der Voet, and Christensen 2021), or other forms of
anti-bureaucracy sentiment, may shape how individuals attri­
bute blame and react in different ways to attempts by elected
officials to shift blame for failing services onto public or private
delivery organizations.
The “delegation level” variable represents the degree to
which the service has been delegated to the service delivery
organization and is one of the other main explanatory variables
of interest in this study. This variable has three treatment levels:
high, low, and no information (i.e., the control condition).
High and low levels of delegation are conceptualized in terms

Sample
1049 adults in the UK were originally recruited to the study
via Prolific, an online research participant recruitment plat­
form. The participant sample was largely representative of the
UK adult population in terms of age, gender, ethnicity, and
location (see the Online Supplementary Information for more
details on the characteristics of the study sample used in the
analysis). Attention checks were embedded in the survey to
assess participants’ attention levels and response quality.
Responses that failed these checks, were insufficiently complete,
or timed out, were removed, resulting in a final analytical sam­
ple of 955 observations.

Hypothesis 1 predicts that citizens approve more of blame

Discussion
This article has examined how citizens respond to blame shift­
ing by connecting three questions: how do the effects of blame
shifting on public opinion compare to accepting responsibility,
how do citizens’ perceptions of culpability shape their reactions
to blame shifting, and how might these reactions change under
different institutional arrangements for service delivery? The
experimental design, which manipulated both institutional
arrangements and presentational strategies, has enabled an
examination of these relationships in ways that extend beyond
previous experimental research. Its findings yield several
important contributions and insights into how public audiences
respond to different blame avoidance strategies.
Firstly, this article contributes to research comparing how
blame shifting and other presentational strategies affect public
opinion in response to performance failures and crises in gov­
ernment. Expanding this research agenda to the context of local
waste collection services in the UK, its findings align with sim­
ilar experiments (Miller and Reeves 2022) in showing that
participants were less approving when elected officials attempt
to shift blame rather than accept responsibility. However,
unlike other studies, the results indicate that participants did
not uniformly disapprove of blame shifting. Instead, as Hypoth­
esis 2 predicts, their approval depends on their perceptions of
culpability: when elected officials tried to shift blame onto a
service delivery organization and participants also viewed the
organization as most to blame, approval became more likely.
Conversely, when participants thought elected officials were
equally or more to blame, disapproval became signifi­
cantly higher.
Second, by varying the institutional context in which differ­
ent presentational strategies are deployed, the experimental
design allowed an examination of how agency strategies for
anticipatory blame avoidance might shape the subsequent
impact of reactive presentational strategies. The fact that, when
levels of delegation were higher, participants were more likely
to assign greater blame to a service delivery organization than
an elected official, is consistent with the agency strategy logic
of anticipatory blame avoidance by which elected officials
shield themselves from blame and aligns with findings from
similar experimental studies focused on blame attribution in
public services (Leland, Mohr, and Piatak 2021; Mohr et al.
2023; Piatak, Mohr, and Leland 2017; Walker et al. 2024).
However, no direct evidence was found to show that partici­
pants were then more approving of subsequent blame shifting
onto the service delivery organization when delegation levels
were higher, which went against the predictions of Hypothesis
3. One possibility is that, even as public services become
increasingly delegated to other agents, citizens continue to view
elected officials as functionally responsible for service outcomes
and therefore expect them to take responsibility for failures,
which suggests these structural changes may not be altering
peoples’ broader normative expectations about how politicians
should behave or remain accountable in these scenarios, even
if they do shift perceptions of who, in relative terms, is most
to blame. Nonetheless, the fact that different delegation levels
did change who participants assigned greater blame to, and
that this assignment of blame had an important moderating
effect on whether participants approved or not when also
exposed to a blame shifting statement, still suggests that insti­
tutional context may matter, albeit indirectly, for how effec­
tively politicians can employ blaming strategies in reactive
blame avoidance contexts. Institutional context may, therefore,
influence public reactions to blame shifting in a more indirect
way than this experiment was designed to detect. Future
research should explore this question further, whether through
more targeted experiments or through observational and qual­
itative work.
Lastly, the results are consistent with this article’s novel
account as to how socially internalized beliefs about the appro­
priateness of blaming shape how citizens evaluate political
blame games and blame avoidance behavior. Blame avoidance
strategies are often conceptualized as interventions aimed at
shaping how citizens perceive events, form moral judgments,
and attribute blame. However, in the case of blame shifting,
the use of this tactic by elected officials is also evaluated against
broader normative expectations regarding when and how
blame should be assigned. The social expression of blame is
governed by rules and norms that define acceptable blame attri­
bution, and this article has argued that citizens tend to disap­
prove when elected officials shift blame onto another agent for
a failure they themselves are also perceived to be culpable for.
The results of this experiment support this and signify an
important contribution to understanding how citizens interpret
blaming strategies employed by political elites. Furthermore,
although this aspect of the article’s findings and arguments do
not relate directly to the relationship between anticipatory and
reactive blame avoidance strategies, they do offer broader
insights into another important temporal issue in the study of
blame avoidance behavior, namely how inherited beliefs,

Limitations
This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.
Like many survey experiments, it faces inferential challenges
related to external validity and the generalization of findings.
While the study draws on a sample that is broadly representa­
tive of the UK adult population in terms of age, gender, ethnic­
ity, and location, caution is needed when extrapolating the
results beyond this context. The findings, based on a hypothet­
ical scenario of service failure in local waste collection in UK
local government, may not necessarily extend to other settings
and it is helpful to reflect on why this might be.
First, although similar experiments involving government
failures also find that blame shifting tends to harm public opin­
ion more than strategies like blame claiming (Miller and Reeves
2022), the dynamics of these two strategies may be very differ­
ent when applied to other types of issues and controversies
beyond failures in local waste collection. In some public service
areas, it may be harder for citizens to assess performance, or
the delegation and contracting arrangements may be more
complex, making it more difficult to determine where to assign
blame. Equally, as long as elected officials signal a commitment
to resolving problems, it may be easier for them to accept sym­
bolic responsibility for failures in local waste collection, given
that it is a more technical service area and generally less subject
to ideological contestation (Petersen, Houlberg, and Chris­
tensen 2015). By contrast, in policy areas like welfare or immi­
gration, where partisan division and ideological conflict over
both the means and ends of policy are more pronounced, taking
responsibility for perceived failures may carry greater political
risks, as it could be seen to compromise a politician’s commit­
ment to specific policy positions. Finally, when it comes to more
individualized political scandals—such as sex scandals—some
studies have found that politicians who apologize and accept
responsibility can actually face increased punishment from
voters (Hamrak et al. 2024). It is important, therefore, to rec­
ognize that the effectiveness of blame claiming, blame shifting,
and other presentational strategies inevitably depends on the
nature of the controversy at hand, as well as when, how, and
by whom such strategies are deployed.
Second, it remains to be seen whether the findings can gen­
eralize across other political and cultural contexts. From a
cultural-theory perspective, responses to scandal, catastrophe,
and failure in public management “are likely to be a key test
of cultural bias” (Hood 2004, 25). Patterns of blame attribu­
tion and the acceptability of different blame avoidance tactics
likely vary across political and cultural contexts, which each
have their own norms and conventions relating to how account­
ability is understood and practiced, meaning that “what gets
you off the hook of blame from one cultural starting-point may
only dig you into a deeper accountability hole in another set­
ting” (Hood 2014, 614). Although this study has focused on
UK local government, the broader norms and conventions
associated with the Westminster system of politics and
administration—in particular, the convention of ministerial
responsibility and the expectation that ministers should assume
public responsibility for the actions of their departments
(Grube 2024)—may make presentational strategies such as
blame claiming a more expected and appropriate course of
action for elected incumbents in a country such as the UK. In
contrast, blame shifting may more clearly violate prevailing
political norms in this context. However, this may not be the
same in other political traditions. It also remains unclear how
these beliefs still matter in shaping evaluations of blame shifting
in more politically polarized environments, where partisan
reasoning may influence judgments and responses by citizens
to political communications and presentational strategies
(Porumbescu et al. 2023).
Third, to avoid dampening potential treatment effects, there
are limits to the levels of contextual detail and variation pro­
vided in the experimental stimuli in the study (Brutger et al.
2023). While this approach ensures a controlled design, it also
constrains the extent to which the scenario captures the com­
plexity of real-world blame games. In practice, elected officials
rarely rely on a single presentational strategy but instead mix
and adapt their approaches in response to public and media
attention as it intensifies or abates over time (Bach and Wegrich
2019; Hood et al. 2009; Hood, Jennings, and Copeland 2016).
Additional background information may also shape how citi­
zens assign blame in such contexts. For example, if elected
officials were previously supportive of, or directly involved in,
decisions to delegate or contract out services, these earlier
actions may undermine their ability to credibly shift blame later
without drawing criticism themselves. Lastly, the hypothetical
interaction between a political principal and a service delivery
organization in this experiment simplifies the strategic dynam­
ics compared to what might otherwise occur in real-life. This
study focuses solely on the blame avoidance behavior of the
political principal, though service delivery organizations also
have reputational concerns and may resist blame or even
attempt to shift it back onto those targeting them (Hood
2002, 27).
Conclusion
One might conclude that, as long as elected officials do not
violate established norms and expectations about appropriate
blame attribution, they may be able to shift blame onto other
agents without facing public disapproval. However, this does
not mean that blame shifting is necessarily an effective, or easy,
strategy in such situations. Across all conditions in this exper­
iment, the control statement and blame claim treatment con­
sistently generated higher mean approval levels than the blame
shift treatment. Therefore, rather than offering evidence that
blame shifting can be effective, the findings highlight how pub­
lic audiences’ beliefs about responsibility for service failures
shape their evaluations of political blame games and point to
the conditions under which blame shifting may be received
more favorably. Notably, these more permissive conditions
coincide with scenarios where elected officials already face less
pressure to avoid blame, as they are seen as less culpable than
the service delivery organization. Evidence from this experi­
ment shows how increased levels of delegation in public service
delivery can establish these conditions. Therefore, the greatest
value of delegation as an agency strategy for blame avoidance
may perhaps lie not in enabling future scapegoating opportu­
nities, but in reducing the initial blame directed at political
principals. By doing so, it lowers the pressure to engage in
reactive blame avoidance when things go wrong.

A final key implication of the findings for understanding
blame avoidance behavior is that, when elected officials attempt
to name, shame, and blame other actors for public service fail­
ures they might otherwise be held responsible for, the credibility
of that strategy hinges on their ability to gauge public attitudes
and anticipate likely reactions. This includes understanding
how citizens are likely to view the relative culpability of other
actors who might also be targeted for blame. Institutional con­
text is important in shaping how citizens view relative culpa­
bility in these situations, even if the evidence in this article only
indirectly demonstrates how this goes on to influence the effect
of blame shifting on public opinion. Still, the ability of elected
officials to read the public mood and adjust their strategies
accordingly may be even more decisive, as it can determine
whether attempted blame shifting is seen as justified or whether
it backfires. But not all politicians are experts on public opinion
(Walgrave et al. 2023) and understanding and anticipating the
types of judgments citizens form in relation to poor government
performance is not straightforward. These realities underscore
the “craft” nature of blame avoidance (Hood 2011, 14) and
the fact that some politicians will inevitably be more skilled
than others at deploying such strategies, or more willing to take
the risks involved. Ultimately, the successful use of these blame
avoidance strategies depends both on context and on the polit­
ical skill required to navigate uncertainty and manage the risks
of deflecting accountability.

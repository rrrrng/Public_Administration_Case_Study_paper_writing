ABSTRACT
Cross-­boundary collaboration is a common strategy used to address environmental issues, but scholars do not yet fully under-
stand what makes a difference in the outcomes of externally directed collaborations. To fill this gap, we examine whether a piece
of state legislation that authorizes collaborative watershed restoration projects improves water quality. Using Oregon data from
1980 to 2021 and a regression discontinuity in time design, we find a 5% improvement in water quality after the legislation was
enacted. Furthermore, our longitudinal analyses show that network properties and management within collaborative projects
are key factors underlying this improvement. In general, networks led by collaborative governance regimes and local govern-
ments are more effective than those led by citizen groups or state agencies. However, state agencies show the strongest capacity
to manage projects with multiple goals. These findings underscore the importance of institution design and network governance
in achieving successful environmental collaboration.
1   |   Introduction
Collaborative governance is recognized widely as an effective
tool for addressing complex environmental issues (Emerson and
Nabatchi 2015a; Koontz et al. 2020; McIver and Becker 2021).
One of the theoretical rationales for environmental collab-
oration is that the management of cross-­boundary natural
resources, such as rivers and forests, extends beyond the coor-
dination ability of a single political jurisdiction or organization
(Ostrom  1990). Although policy actors may join collaborative
networks for various reasons—such as risk aversion (Milward
and Provan 2000), path dependency (Raynor et al. 2018), power
acquisition for survival (Abrams 2019), or privatizing govern-
ment services (McCarthy 2005)—successful collaborations re-
quire actors across different sectors to establish “shared core
goals” and take actions that benefit the socio-­ecological system
(Bryson et al. 2016, 914).
In recognition of this reality, many governments (both national
and subnational) have passed legislation that mandates or in-
centivizes the use of collaboration to address cross-­boundary
environmental issues. Such externally directed collaborative
efforts (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015a), which represent a par-
ticular form of institutional design, are sometimes overseen by
a designated collaborative platform, an organization or program
dedicated to helping create and support multiple collaboratives
operating in the same policy field and working toward the same
(or similar) ends (Ansell and Gash 2018; Cochran et al. 2019).
Institutional design, often embedded in legislation, plays a crit-
ical role in reducing transaction costs, risks, and information

Despite a growing volume of research on environmental col-
laboration, scholars know little about this form of institutional
design, and even less about how the network characteristics
of externally directed efforts shape collaborative outcomes. In
part, this is due to stifled conversation between collaborative
governance and network governance scholars. For example,
while institutional design is a prominent theme in the collabora-
tive governance literature, it rarely appears in network studies,
and conversely, network governance findings seldom are ap-
plied to collaborative governance studies (Wang and Ran 2023).
Recognizing this critical gap, we integrate research from both
collaborative governance and network governance to address
two questions: (1) Does legislation supporting externally di-
rected cross-­boundary collaborations improve environmental
outcomes? (2) Do variations in network properties and man-
agement (i.e., collaboration form, lead organization, number
of goals) matter in the implementation of externally directed
collaborations?
We address these questions through an investigation of wa-
tershed management in Oregon and specifically conduct two
analyses using a novel 41-­year water quality monitoring data-
set. First, we use a regression discontinuity in time design to
test the overall impact of the 1997 Oregon Plan for Salmon and
Watersheds (OPSW) on water quality improvement. This state-­
level legislation authorizes and encourages cross-­sectoral policy
actors to collaborate on watershed restoration projects. Second,
we conduct a longitudinal analysis of 8853 collaborative water-
shed restoration projects to examine how variations in network
properties and management impact water quality. Specifically,
we examine the effects of different collaborative forms (CGR, ad
hoc) and project lead organizations (citizen group, local govern-
ment, state government) on water quality and explore whether
the effects vary with an increase in the number of project goals.
The study is organized as follows. First, we explore institutional
design for externally directed collaborations and hypothesize
about the impact of legislation on environmental outcomes.
Next, we use insights from the network and collaborative gov-
ernance literatures to hypothesize about the impacts of network
properties (collaborative form, lead organization) and manage-
ment (project goals) on longitudinal environmental outcomes.
Finally, we empirically test the hypotheses and discuss their
implications.
2   |   Institutional Design for Externally Directed
Collaborations
Three primary types of institutional design for cross-­boundary
collaboration exist: (1) “self-­initiated” through the voluntary
cooperation of stakeholders, (2) “independently convened” by
an autonomous third party, and (3) “externally directed” by
an entity with enough power and resources to predetermine
how collaboration works (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015a, 159).
Unlike the first two types, which create self-­governing network
activities, externally directed cross-­boundary collaborations
often require formal institutions for implementation, such as
legal mandates and/or financial incentives provided by gov-
ernments or other entities (Amsler and Vieilledent 2021; Mehdi
and Nabatchi  2023). Externally directed collaborations often
are chosen strategically to cover various costs of collaboration
through “governmental [and other] contributions in expertise,
technical information, manpower, institutional networks, or
other resources” (Koontz 2006, 15).
Existing legislation on collaborative governance is often general
and broad and may not include the ‘nitty-­gritty’ details of what
should be done, how, or by whom (Amsler 2016; Bingham 2009,
2010). We argue that the enactment of legislation and/or dis-
bursement of funding enables the creation of cross-­boundary
collaborations and initiates collaborative actions. Although
some scholars have examined legal mandates for collaborative
governance at the local (Amsler and Vieilledent  2021), state
(Kim et al., n.d.), national (Batory and Svensson 2020), and in-
ternational levels (Newig and Koontz 2014), or across multiple
levels (Amsler 2016), these studies are primarily normative or
descriptive. To our knowledge, no empirical research has exam-
ined the effects of legislation—whether local, state, federal, or
multi-­level—on collaborative outcomes using a large-­n dataset
and producing plausibly causal estimates.
Legislation may help with collaborative efforts through for-
malization of rules on participants and decision-­making
(Doberstein 2016) or “process design” for interactions (Edelenbos
and Klijn 2006). However, if too loose or too rigid, legislation
might hinder the collective action of participants and negatively
affect collaborative outcomes (Amsler 2016). For instance, state
legal mandates can constrain collaborative problem-­solving
approaches in local communities through “intrastate preemp-
tion” (i.e., limitations imposed by state laws on the actions of
local government and community members) (Amsler and
Vieilledent  2021), while weak institutional and legal frame-
works for collaboration may fail to overcome political tensions
and uncertainties and impede process and productivity perfor-
mance (Avoyan et al. 2017).
Nevertheless, as long as it is unrestrictive and binding for par-
ticipants, we expect that state legislation authorizing and en-
couraging collaboration will have a positive net effect on target
outcomes. Specifically, a legal mandate can raise the salience
and urgency of an environmental issue (Agnone  2007), for-
malize and sustain the implementation of collaborative actions
Summary
•	 A supportive legislative framework that authorizes
and funds cross-­sectoral collaborations can enhance
and sustain environmental outcomes.
•	 Cross-­boundary collaborations should prioritize clear,
manageable goals in their actions to ensure focus and
effectiveness.
•	 The leadership of local governments in environmental
collaboration can be beneficial for producing collabo-
rative outcomes.
•	 State governments display strengths in handling mul-
tiple goals within collaborative actions.

H1.  Legislation authorizing collaborative projects will improve

3   |   Network Property and Management
Legislation serves as a formal institutional design that shapes
and is shaped by the system context for cross-­boundary col-
laboration (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015a). According to Wang
and Ran  (2023), this institutional design is fundamental in
shaping network properties, including size, age, structure,
and relationships between members. It also plays a key role in
guiding network management, which is the deliberate process
of facilitating interactions among members. In this system
context, policy actors leverage local knowledge to structure
and manage collaborative networks, shaping dynamics and
actions critical for addressing complex environmental chal-
lenges (Emerson et al. 2012). The network governance liter-
ature confirms the joint importance of network properties
and management (Cristofoli and Macciò 2018). For instance,
Cristofoli et al. (2015) suggest an interaction effect between
network structure, mechanisms, and managerial abilities,
which collectively impact network performance. Likewise,
Steijn et al. (2011) argue that while public-­private partnerships
are important, the type and intensity of managerial strategies
often have a more significant impact on outcomes than struc-
tural arrangements alone.
Building on these theoretical insights, this study moves from
cross-­sectional analyses to explore longitudinal network dy-
namics (Siciliano et  al.  2021). In the following sections, we
combine the network and collaborative governance literatures
to hypothesize about how, within the same legislative context,
different network properties (i.e., collaborative forms and
types of lead organizations) and managerial strategies (i.e.,
multiplicity of goals) influence longitudinal environmental
outcomes.
3.1   |   Network Property: Collaboration Form
Cross-­boundary collaboration can take many forms. Two forms
are of particular interest in this study: ad hoc collaborations and
collaborative governance regimes (CGRs). An ad hoc collabora-
tion is a collaborative effort that “forms in response to an imme-
diate need or instrumental purpose and is temporary, existing
only for the duration of a well-­defined, narrow and bounded,
one-­off project” (Mehdi and Nabatchi 2023, 499). After a project
has been completed, an ad hoc collaboration disbands, although
a successful project may stimulate additional collaborative ef-
forts (Mehdi and Nabatchi 2023). Given its short duration, an
ad hoc collaborative is less likely to build strong social capital
among participants and otherwise leverage collaborative ad-
vantage (Bryson et al. 2015; Schneider et al. 2003). In contrast,
a CGR is a governing arrangement where cross-­boundary col-
laboration is the predominant mode of decision-­making and
activity among autonomous participants pursuing a shared
goal (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015b). A CGR typically addresses
complex, interconnected challenges that necessitate a theory of
change and involve multiple projects and sustained, long-­term
participant engagement (Mehdi and Nabatchi 2023).
Given their distinct characteristics, the collaborative actions or
projects undertaken by ad hoc collaborations and CGRs also are
likely to differ. For example, because an ad hoc collaborative
arises to address a relatively simple, distinct issue, its collab-
orative projects tend to be narrow, highly focused, and site-­
specific. In contrast, because a CGR arises to address multiple,
interrelated problems, it tends to juggle numerous projects that
emerge directly from its comprehensive strategy to address both
site-­specific and system-­wide issues. The long-­term nature of
CGRs allows them to accumulate and leverage diverse knowl-
edge, skills, social capital, and resources over time, which is
essential for sustained collaborative success (Guarneros-­Meza
et al. 2018).
Provan and Kenis (2008) similarly explain how different network
properties affect network competencies based on variations in
participant numbers, trust levels, and goal consensus. Empirical
studies support their argument, indicating that formalized co-
ordination mechanisms can improve network effectiveness (Liu
and Tan 2023; Macciò and Cristofoli 2017). Organizations cen-
trally embedded in networks tend to gain greater trustworthi-
ness, reputation, and influence over time, enhancing sustained
collaborative success (Cristofoli et  al.  2015; Markovic  2017;
Provan et  al.  2009). These findings align with the concept of
CGRs, which build strong social capital among participants to
leverage collaborative advantages over a more continuous du-
ration (Bryson et al. 2016). This leads to our second hypothesis:
H2.  Projects convened by CGRs will improve environmental
outcomes more than projects convened by ad hoc collaborations.
3.2   |   Network Property: Lead Organization
Next, we propose that lead organizations play a crucial role in
networks and in shaping their collaborative outcomes (Wang
and Ran 2023). In their foundational work on network gover-
nance, Provan and Kenis (2008) describe a lead organization as
a “network broker” responsible for bringing together stakehold-
ers to achieve a common objective, facilitating interactions, and
coordinating decision-­making within collaborative efforts. We
suggest that lead organizations impact the implementation of
collaboration and its results for two main reasons. First, they act
as network brokers, serving as a link between the legal author-
ities shaping policy at the macro level and the grassroots actors
involved in the day-­to-­day activities of cross-­boundary collabo-
ration (Lee and Liu 2024). Second, lead organizations structure
and sculpt the processes and activities of collaborative decision-­
making with network participants (Emerson et al. 2012). They
typically take the most responsibility for strategic and opera-
tional decisions, making them well positioned to set network-­
level goals for their collaborations (Provan and Kenis 2008).
A variety of organizations—such as state government agen-
cies, regional government councils, local governments, civic

organizations, and nonprofit organizations—can lead collabo-
rative networks. However, scholars have yet to systematically
compare their impacts on collaborative outcomes within a policy
arena (Koontz et al. 2004; Koontz and Newig 2014). This empiri-
cal issue is crucial for understanding how network properties af-
fect collaborative outcomes. Government agencies typically lead
collaborations through a top-­down approach, using their legal
authority and abundant resources to achieve policy goals (Span
et al. 2012). They can mobilize key stakeholders, enact policies
that align with collaboration objectives, and provide funding
and administrative support (Koontz 2006; Yi 2018). However,
this hierarchical structure often leads to power imbalances,
where government officials can dominate decision-­making,
reducing inclusivity and stifling participant engagement (Scott
and Thomas 2017). Such collaborations may push through deci-
sions without full consensus, limiting the effectiveness of input
from other participants (Provan and Kenis 2008).
In contrast, civic sector organizations, such as nonprofits and
citizen groups, tend to adopt a bottom-­up approach to collabora-
tion, which is more community-­driven. These organizations tend
to leverage local knowledge, foster inclusivity by building trust
and reciprocity, and promote active stakeholder engagement
(Koontz 2006; Van Slyke 2007). Although civic sector organiza-
tions often focus on collective ownership and responsiveness to
local needs, they may struggle with securing sufficient funding
and managing power dynamics among participants, particu-
larly when more dominant actors are involved (Cornforth 2015).
Despite these challenges, bottom-­up collaborations tend to fa-
cilitate deeper community involvement and long-­term commit-
ment from participants. While both government-­led, top-­down
and citizen-­led, bottom-­up collaboration approaches offer dis-
tinct advantages, no solid evidence suggests favoring one over
the other in all contexts. Given the strengths and limitations of
each approach, we propose the following competing hypothesis:
H3a.  Projects led by government agencies will improve environ-
mental outcomes more than projects led by citizen groups.
H3b.  Projects led citizen groups will improve environmental
outcomes more than projects led by government agencies.
3.3   |   Network Management: Number of Goals
Network management refers to the set of activities that enable
and support interactions among organizations within a network
(Wang and Ran 2023). These activities encompass a variety of
strategies, including goal convergence, organizational culture,
process management, and others (Kapucu and Demirhan 2019;
Klijn et al. 2010; Klijn and Koppenjan 2016), all of which are
critical for achieving desirable outcomes in collaborative ac-
tions (Emerson and Nabatchi  2015b). Extending this body of
work, this study examines how goal multiplicity influences col-
laborative outcomes. Goal setting is essential for establishing
purpose in collaboration and driving it forward (Agranoff and
McGuire 1998). Among other key dimensions of public sector
goals, such as goal ambiguity (e.g., Chun and Rainey 2005), goal
conflict (e.g., Resh and Pitts 2013), and goal displacement (e.g.,
Bohte and Meier 2000), goal multiplicity stands out as particu-
larly salient in matters of implementation (O'Toole 1989). This is
because public managers, whether they are collaborating across
various boundaries or working alone, “operate in complex po-
litical environments where numerous external actors attempt
to exert influence over administrative decision making, and
they face greater goal multiplicity than managers in the private
sector” (Fernandez 2004, 206–207). Goal multiplicity in the ac-
tions of cross-­boundary collaboration, or the existence of more
than one goal guiding collaborative outputs, is a “ubiquitous
part of the interorganizational setting” and even observed in
cases where the goals are readily understood, easily measured,
widely accepted, and hardly in conflict with each other (O'Toole
Jr.  1993, 241). The prevalence of goal multiplicity in collabo-
rative projects raises an important but understudied question
for scholars and practitioners—if we pursue more goals, do we
achieve better outcomes?
Previous studies offer mixed answers. On the one hand, more
goals could mean more complex and cumbersome implementa-
tion, especially when multiple decision points demand “clear-
ances” by multiple actors involved in the process and “too many
cooks spoil the broth” (Pressman and Wildavsky  1984). Even
when all the goals align well, the participants may lean toward
prioritizing goals that are relatively noticeable, tractable, or
quantifiable, while overlooking others that might be pivotal to
improving environmental outcomes (Boyne 2003). On the other
hand, an increase in the number of goals in collaborative proj-
ects may indicate a more comprehensive and holistic approach
to problem-­solving and thus have the potential to generate more
and stronger benefits (Siddiki et al. 2015). For example, inclu-
sion and integration of multiple goals across diverse policy areas
and actors can create synergistic effects on environmental sus-
tainability by addressing a broad range of interrelated factors
such as “ecological integrity, economic viability, and social and
cultural harmony” (Rogers and Weber 2010, 559).
Given these conflicting perspectives, we propose that the effect
of goal multiplicity is contingent on network properties in col-
laborative projects. First, we postulate that the collaboration
form will moderate the impact of goal multiplicity on environ-
mental outcomes. Specifically, CGRs may cope better with goal
multiplicity in collaborative projects than ad hoc collaborations
because of their more extensive and more frequent collaborative
experiences. Although collaborative projects convened by ad
hoc collaborations can be effective at addressing a small num-
ber of narrowly targeted objectives, they may incur higher mar-
ginal costs when handling additional goals than CGRs (Mehdi
and Nabatchi 2023). Second, we expect that sectoral differences
among lead organizations may moderate the relationship be-
tween goal multiplicity and outcomes. Government agencies
may better manage a greater number of project goals than citi-
zen groups due to their legal authority and resources to enforce
policy objectives (Span et al. 2012). Thus, we offer the final set
of hypotheses.
H4a.  As the number of goals increases, projects convened by
CGRs will achieve better environmental outcomes than those con-
vened by ad hoc collaborations.
H4b.  As the number of goals increases, projects led by govern-
ment agencies will achieve better environmental outcomes than
those led by citizen groups.

4   |   Empirical Strategies
4.1   |   Context
We test our hypotheses within the context of watershed man-
agement in Oregon. Located on the Pacific Coast in the north-
western part of the United States, Oregon sustains a thriving,
high-­tech fishing industry. However, by the 1990s, water-
sheds in Oregon had been severely degraded and polluted due
to decades of overfishing and industrial pollution (Oregon
Wild  2024). In 1997, the state legislature enacted the Oregon
Plan for Salmon and Watersheds (OPSW), which, among other
things, introduced two formal institutional designs promoting
cross-­boundary collaborations in watershed management.
First, the OPSW enabled the creation of watershed councils
that coordinate cross-­sectoral actors in long-­term, collaborative
efforts to “protect and enhance the quality of the watershed”
(Oregon Statute 541.910). Watershed councils “engage people
in their communities to participate in collaborative, voluntary
restoration of watersheds” (OWEB, n.d.-­a). Watershed councils
are CGRs—they are cross-­boundary governing arrangements
in which “collaboration represents the prevailing pattern of
behavior and activity” (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015a, 18), are
sustained over the long term, and are focused on developing,
executing, and evaluating multiple projects identified through
assessment or planning process and aimed at systematically
addressing issues at the watershed or eco-­system level.1 This
empirical setting allows us to examine whether legislation au-
thorizing externally directed CGRs for watershed management
has an effect on overall environmental outcomes, and particu-
larly on water quality.
Second, the OPSW authorized the Oregon Watershed
Enhancement Board (OWEB), a state agency, to provide grants
for collaborative restoration and recovery projects in Oregon
watersheds. Some of these projects are undertaken by CGRs
(i.e., watershed councils), while others are undertaken by ad
hoc collaborations led by governments, private firms, citizen
groups, and nonprofits. In both cases, cross-­boundary collabo-
rative networks manage projects aimed to “address the factors
that affect fish populations and watershed health,” with a strong
“focus on water quality, stream flows, and habitat restoration”
(OWEB,  n.d.-­b). Therefore, although these collaborative proj-
ects may have multiple goals, they all are externally directed by
the OPSW legislation and share a common objective: improving
water quality in Oregon's watersheds.
4.2   |   Data
We use data from three sources: (1) the Oregon Water Quality
Index (OWQI), provided by the Oregon Department of
Environmental Quality (ODEQ) to measure environmental
outcomes, (2) the Oregon Watershed Restoration Inventory
(OWRI) for data on collaborative restoration and recovery
projects (i.e., collaborative actions), and (3) geospatial infor-
mation about watersheds from the US Geological Survey's
Hydrologic Unit Code (HUC) to delineate watershed bound-
aries in Oregon.
4.2.1   |   Oregon Water Quality Index
The ODEQ developed the OWQI to evaluate water quality
across the state, maintaining a consistent measurement frame-
work over the past 40 years.2 Focused on freshwater rivers and
streams, the OWQI excludes groundwater and tidally influenced
or estuarine sites from its analyses (ODEQ 2016). It consolidates
eight indicators (water temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, bio-
logical oxygen demand, total solids, nitrogen, phosphorus, and
bacteria) into a single index using the following formula, in
which SIi refers to the i-­th subindex (e.g., nitrogen) and n denotes
the number of subindices:
The OWQI scale ranges from 10, indicating the worst water qual-
ity, to 100, representing the ideal. The ODEQ categorizes these
scores into five groups signifying a different level of water qual-
ity: 10–59 (very poor), 60–79 (poor), 80–84 (fair), 85–89 (good),
and 90–100 (excellent). Overall, water quality data collection is
irregular. The ODEQ retrieves the OWQI from 160 water quality
monitoring stations scattered across the state. Data were sampled
intermittently on a monthly basis at each station, with records
available from 1980 to 2021. Some water quality monitoring sta-
tions collected samples three to 4 months per year, while most sta-
tions collected data once every other month. Moreover, the specific
dates of sample collection within a given month were not fixed but
rather were distributed throughout the year. In total, our dataset
contains 29,797 monthly water quality observations over 42 years.
4.2.2   |   Oregon Watershed Restoration Inventory
The OWRI is a comprehensive dataset that encompasses in-
formation on all 19,396 watershed projects funded by OWEB
since 1997. It identifies over 150 different kinds of projects, all of
which focus on watershed restoration and recovery in Oregon.
The OWRI includes details such as project start and end dates,
activities, geographic coordinates, costs associated with proj-
ect activities, project goals, and land use. It documents project
participants and coordinators (the lead organization in our the-
oretical framework), categorized by OWEB as governmental or
nongovernmental (see Table A1), and notes if a CGR undertook
the project. Our analysis only focuses on 9359 OWEB-­funded
projects completed between 1997 and 2020, as they directly re-
sult from the enactment of OPSW.3

eight-­digit code. Although Oregon has 92 watersheds in total,
only 68 contain at least one water monitoring station. Therefore,
our study only includes collaborative projects and water quality

4.3   |   Methods
We conduct two analyses: First, we employ a regression discon-
tinuity in time (RDiT) design, with monthly records from mon-
itoring stations as the units of analysis, to estimate the overall
impacts of the OPSW legislation on water quality (H1). Second,
we estimate the effects of network characteristics in OWEB-­
funded collaborative projects on water quality, using watershed-­
year panel data with longitudinal models (H2–H4).
4.3.1   |   Regression Discontinuity in Time Design
RDiT is used widely in environmental studies to estimate policy
effects on environmental outcomes (e.g., Dang and Trinh 2021;
Greenstone et al. 2022; Lang and Siler 2013). It assumes that any
significant change in the outcome variable, occurring around a
specific cutoff date when an intervention is introduced, can be
attributed to this intervention (Hausman and Rapson 2018). It
proves particularly useful for inferring causal impacts in sce-
narios where random assignment or a natural control group is
unavailable, especially when policies are implemented across
the entire observed region. Given the universal impact of OPSW
on all Oregon watersheds, identifying a suitable control group
within the state is challenging. Furthermore, neighboring states
like Washington and California have distinct water quality
index indicators of their own, making direct comparisons with
Oregon's water quality problematic.4 Hence, RDiT is a suitable
method for our study.
Moreover, the monthly, rather than yearly, observations
of water quality at monitoring stations align with the data
structure requirements of RDiT. This method demands a
larger sample size, compared to other causal inference meth-
ods, because it designates a “small window” around the cut-
off, ensuring the treated and control units have comparable
observed and unobserved characteristics (Cattaneo and
Titiunik  2022). As previously mentioned, we have 29,797
monthly water quality observations distributed across ob-
served years. Therefore, our sample has sufficient statistical
power to select comparable observations “slightly before” and
“slightly after” the OPSW implementation cutoff, which serve
as our control and treatment groups.
We chose January 1, 1997, as our cutoff date, as this date marks
the implementation of OPSW, the start of OWEB's support for
collaborative actions, and the first collaborative project funded
by OWEB in the OWRI dataset.5 Figure 2 depicts the historical
trend of the OWQI, with a noticeable jump from 76.56 to 83.05
between 1996 and 1997, descriptively suggesting the validity of
our chosen cutoff date. Based on the collection time of each ob-
servation from the monitoring stations, we calculate the time
distance of each sample from the cutoff date. We then compute
a “relative time” variable and select an appropriate time win-
dow (also referred to as bandwidth) to determine the number
of observations to include, which is crucial for obtaining a valid
RDiT estimate (Hausman and Rapson 2018; Villamizar-­Villegas
et al. 2022). We select our optimal bandwidth through a data-­
driven process, leveraging a widely accepted method aimed
at minimizing the asymptotic mean squared error (Calonico
et al. 2014; Imbens and Kalyanaraman 2012).

4.4   |   Longitudinal Analysis
We conduct a longitudinal analysis to examine the relationship
between collaborative projects and water quality, using aggre-
gated data at the watershed-­year level. This approach is neces-
sary for several reasons. First, watersheds often span multiple
local jurisdictions, so analyzing data at the watershed scale pro-
vides a more comprehensive and accurate measure of the effec-
tiveness of cross-­boundary collaboration. Second, OWQI data
are collected irregularly, and retaining the monthly structure in
a longitudinal analysis would result in significant missing data
issues. To address this, we calculate annual water quality, our
dependent variable, by averaging all available monthly OWQI
measurements within each watershed for each year. Third,
there is a geographic mismatch between the locations of water
monitoring stations and collaborative projects (see Figure  1),
making it infeasible to directly link a specific project's efforts to
water quality data from a particular station. Therefore, we ag-
gregate project information to construct watershed-­level explan-
atory and control variables. For each project-­related variable, we
calculate the number of projects with a specific characteristic
(e.g., convened by CGRs, led by local governments) within a wa-
tershed for a given year. In the following section, we discuss the
variable measurements and model specification in detail.
Collaborative governance regime reflects the form of the collabo-
rative that undertakes each project within each watershed (i.e.,
whether a project is launched by a CGR or an ad hoc collabora-
tion). This variable measures the total number of collaborative
projects that CGRs (i.e., watershed councils) convene within
a watershed area in a given year. Therefore, the coefficient of
this variable indicates the degree to which one additional CGR-­
convened project affects water quality improvement in a given
watershed year.
Lead Organization variables indicate the number of projects
within a given watershed in a specific year led by citizen groups,
local governments, or state government.6 By counting the proj-
ects led by each type of organization, these variables measure
the influence of the lead organization on collaborative outcomes,
reflecting the impact of network property (Milward et al. 2010).
Citizen groups include conservation groups, extension services,
individual researchers, sporting groups, and volunteers. Local
governments include city and county governments, local agen-
cies, and soil and water conservation districts. State government
refers to state agencies. All projects led by a representative of
citizen groups, local governments, or the state government fall
under the umbrella of ad hoc collaboration. By comparing the
coefficients of these three lead organization variables with the
CGR variable, we can investigate whether projects launched
by CGRs generate better environmental outcomes than ad hoc
collaborations.

employing a binary weighting scheme to represent hydrological
connections. Regressions using an alternative distance-­based
spatial lag8 are presented in Table A6 as a robustness check.

5.1   |   The Effect of Legislation on Environmental
Outcomes
The RDiT estimate supports H1—legislation authorizing col-
laborative projects improves environmental outcomes. The op-
timal bandwidth selection indicates a time window of 1112 days
(approximately 3 years) before and after the implementation of
OPSW (January 1, 1997) and observations within this window
are suitable for inferring the causal effect. Figure 3 visualizes
this effect and shows a noticeable improvement in water quality
after the cutoff date.
We formally evaluate the OPSW effect using linear regres-
sions. As shown in Table 1, the OPSW effect, D, is significant
in models (1–3). With optimal bandwidth, OPSW is estimated
to yield a 3.91-­point improvement in water quality. Compared
to the baseline outcome mean, OPSW implementation enhances
water quality by 5%. Models (2) and (3) narrow the bandwidth to
one-­year and half-­year windows, respectively, for a more con-
servative estimation of the OPSW effect, thus minimizing po-
tential time-­varying confounders. Results in both models with
these shorter timeframes reveal even more substantial OPSW
effects. Within 2 years (one-­year bandwidth) and 1 year (half-­
year bandwidth), OPSW implementation respectively improves
water quality by 8.70 and 13.25 points (equivalent to 11% and
18% improvements). Next, we relax the causal assumption to an-
alyze the long-­term effect of OPSW by including the complete
data from 1980 to 2021 in model (4). In the long run, the imple-
mentation of OPSW is estimated to improve the water quality
by 2.75 points (equivalent to a 3% improvement). We offer the
visualization of RDiT estimates for models (1–4) in Figure A1.
Two tests confirm the robustness of our RDiT findings. First, the
placebo test shows that no discontinuity appears at the placebo
cutoff date (see Figure A2 and Table A5), thereby confirming
January 1, 1997, as the start date of the treatment intervention
(Hausman and Rapson  2018). Second, the density test results
(Figure  A3) show no statistically significant difference in the
number of observations before and after OPSW, suggesting no
density bias in the results.

5.3   |   Exploratory Analysis of Goal Multiplicity
and Alignment
The above results indicate that CGR-­convened and local
government-­led projects achieve stronger long-­term improve-
ments in water quality, while state agencies excel in managing
projects with multiple goals. These findings suggest that the di-
chotomy between top-­down governmental and bottom-­up non-
governmental governance models should be reconsidered using
more nuanced analyses of their goal management strategies,
which may serve as a potential confounder of network proper-
ties. Therefore, we calculated the alignment of goals with the
legislative purpose of watershed condition improvement (here-
after referred to as “goal alignment”) as the ratio of water quality
goals to total goals, and we compared it with goal multiplicity
using project-­level data. Table A7 provides a list of the specific
goals that are directly related to water quality improvement and
thus categorized as water quality goals.
Figure 4 illustrates these relationships. The upper panel shows
that projects undertaken by CGRs and local governments have
significantly higher ratios of water quality-­related goals com-
pared to those led by other actors. This pattern aligns with the
regression analysis in Table 2, which indicates that CGRs and
local governments outperform state governments and citizen
groups in improving water quality. This suggests a stronger
alignment between goals and outcomes in achieving “shared
core goals” (Bryson et al. 2016, 914).
The lower panel of Figure 4 focuses on goal multiplicity across
network properties. First, although state agencies demonstrate
a strong capacity to manage multiple goals (see Model 9 in
Table 2), their projects do not, on average, have more goals than
those led by citizen groups or local governments. In fact, citi-
zen groups, with the highest number of goals per project (lower
panel of Figure  4) but lower goal alignment (upper panel of
Figure 4), face significant challenges in multitasking environ-
ments. Second, while state government–and local government-­
led projects have similar total goals, state agencies exhibit lower
goal alignment. This suggests even stronger evidence for the
goal management capacity of state government agencies. That
is, under increasing demands for goal multiplicity, state gov-
ernment agencies are better able to translate diverse goals into
intended outcomes despite having a higher percentage of goals
that are indirectly related to water quality.
The varying degrees of goal alignment among projects with dif-
ferent network properties raise the question of whether network
properties themselves still matter in improving water quality, or
if most of their effects are explained by goal alignment. To ad-
dress this question and strengthen the robustness of our results,
we aggregated the project-­level measure into a watershed-­level
variable: the average ratio of water quality goals to total goals
for projects in a given watershed each year. This variable is in-
cluded in the longitudinal models presented in Table A8, which
shows that, while goal alignment has a statistically significant
and positive association with water quality improvement, the di-
rections and coefficients on CGR and lead organizations remain
unchanged after controlling for goal alignment. The coefficients
on interaction terms between goal multiplicity and network
property variables are robust as well. Taken together, these re-
sults suggest that goal alignment may have its own direct and
independent impact on water quality improvement, rather than
acting as a confounding variable for network properties. While

6   |   Discussion and Conclusion
This study integrates insights from collaborative governance
and network governance into research on environmental man-
agement. While most network studies rely on cross-­sectional
data, limiting their ability to capture the long-­term effects of net-
work governance (Siciliano et al. 2021), our approach combines
RDiT and longitudinal models. The RDiT analysis identifies the
causal impact of legislation—which represents a set of institu-
tional design choices for collaborative governance—on envi-
ronmental outcomes. The complementary longitudinal models
reveal that network properties and management within projects
are key mechanisms for sustaining successful environmental
management over the long term. We detail the findings and im-
plications of our four hypotheses below.
First, we hypothesized about the effect of legislation on en-
vironmental outcomes. By leveraging the RDiT technique,
we underscore the causal impacts of institutional design for
cross-­boundary collaborations, demonstrating that the effects
of supporting collaborative governance through legislation on
environmental improvement can be achieved in a relatively
short time and be sustained for over 20 years. This finding
addresses the ongoing challenge of identifying the causality
of collaborative governance, which is complicated by the in-
terdependent nature of network data (Siciliano et  al.  2021).
Interestingly, narrowing the time window to one or 2 years re-
veals a stronger legislative impact, contradicting the conven-
tional wisdom that collaborative environmental governance
requires significant time to yield results due to its demands
on communication, conflict management, and consensus
building (Koontz et al. 2004; McLaughlin et al. 2022; Newig
et al. 2018; Provan and Kenis 2008). The OPSW spurred, and
continues to push, this rapid effectiveness by incentivizing
projects managed by collaborative networks, which, on aver-
age, complete 314 projects per year.
Moreover, the legislation effect sustains over time. The time se-
ries pattern of OWQI in Figure 2 shows that water quality in
Oregon has scored above the “fair” category (OWQI ≥80) in most
years after 1997. However, a slight decrease in water quality over
time also is observed in the RDiT slope in the bottom-­right panel
in Figure A1, which gradually approaches the threshold score of
80. This aligns with the concept of “performance coping,” where
agents prioritize visible and achievable goals to demonstrate ef-
fectiveness (Tummers et al. 2015; Tummers 2017). Our findings
extend this idea, suggesting that this phenomenon applies not
only to individual workers but also to collaborative networks.

Second, we demonstrate that the CGR collaboration form is an
effective network property in improving water quality. This
is consistent with the theoretical expectation that CGRs tend
to have advantages in situations where collaborative actions
demand high levels of coordination, such as the manage-
ment of cross-­boundary natural resources. These advantages
come from formalized structures, repeated interactions be-
tween participants, and close oversight of network activities
(Emerson and Nabatchi  2015b; Macciò and Cristofoli  2017;
Margerum 2011; Markovic 2017). Aligned with empirical evi-
dence from other CGR studies (e.g., Mehdi and Nabatchi 2023;
Scott  2016), our findings provide a practical suggestion to
public managers: designing a structurally and institutionally
supported CGR is crucial for maintaining effective network
activities and managing relationships among network partic-
ipants over time. This is particularly important for governing
natural resources, as environmental improvement should be a
sustainable, long-­run effort.
In addition, our findings refine the traditional dichotomy
between top-­down and bottom-­up governance and advance
Provan and Kenis's (2008) classic network theory on the lead
organization model. Sectoral differences cannot fully account
for the heterogeneous effects of different lead organizations
on collaborative outcomes. Both state agencies and citizen
groups are less effective at improving environmental out-
comes than local governments. These findings are consistent
with literature suggesting that state government may have
stronger coordination capacity but often lacks the knowl-
edge to address place-­based goals (Doberstein 2016; Vangen
and Huxham 2012). Conversely, citizen groups with substan-
tial local knowledge often lack the coordination power to set
unified goals (Emerson and Nabatchi  2015a; Fung  2006).
Positioned in the middle, local governments can gather infor-
mation from both citizens and the state government, gener-
ating positive environmental outcomes comparable to those
of CGRs. This finding remains robust even after controlling
for goal alignment and goal multiplicity variables. We offer a
possible explanation: local governments are perceived to be
more representative and thus more legitimate than state gov-
ernment or citizen groups, while their clear authority com-
pared to citizen actors further enhances their capacity to lead
collaborative efforts (Koontz and Newig 2014; Tausanovitch
and Warshaw 2014). Therefore, we suggest that top-­down ap-
proaches to ad-­hoc collaborations can be quite effective, espe-
cially when local government serves as the lead organization.
We also recommend that local citizen groups work with other
governmental and nongovernmental organizations to estab-
lish CGRs, as this collaboration form may be more effective
than the bottom-­up approach led by citizen groups themselves.
Furthermore, the goal analysis offers a more nuanced under-
standing of network properties and management. Goal multi-
plicity does not negatively affect CGRs. However, collaborative
projects led by citizen groups are the most likely to have multi-
ple goals, which tends to limit their outcome achievement com-
pared to those led by other organizations. In contrast, projects
led by state governments are the most effective at managing goal
multiplicity. Goal alignment is beneficial for environmental out-
comes, but collaboration form and lead organization still mat-
ter even when goal alignment and multiplicity are accounted
for. These findings align with the suggestion that “different
structures require different mechanisms for network success”
(Cristofoli et al. 2015, 510). Therefore, we recommend that citi-
zen groups and local governments avoid overloading on project
goals, as this may hinder their effectiveness. In contrast, state
agencies should focus on improving goal alignment while care-
fully increasing the total number of project goals (if needed)
to improve resource use and achieve stronger collaborative
outcomes.
We conclude with four suggestions for future research. First,
scholars can delve deeper into the temporal dynamics of col-
laborative outcomes, asking whether, why, and how legislative
effects can be sustained over time and exploring factors that
contribute to improvements or declines in collaborative out-
comes. Second, scholars can further explore the impact of goal
complexity on collaborative outcomes. For example, in addi-
tion to the number of goals, they also might examine whether
and how different combinations of goal types affect outcomes.
Third, while this study focuses on collaborative governance
in Oregon watersheds, future studies could examine the gen-
eralizability of these findings in different locations or on a
scale larger than a single state. Fourth, scholars could explore
whether the relationships between legislation, collaborative
actions, and outcomes hold or differ across environmental
management issues (e.g., air quality, land management, bio-
diversity, forestry, waste management) as well as within other
policy domains (e.g., healthcare, education, economic devel-
opment, public safety). Challenging as it may be, integrating
and comparing findings within and across geographic and
policy contexts remains an important task for scholars. Only
through robust and rigorous examinations of collaborative
governance and its impacts will we be able to identify water-
shed moments in collaborative processes.

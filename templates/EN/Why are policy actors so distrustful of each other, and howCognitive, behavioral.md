Abstract
In the age of collaboration and shared governance, paradoxically, distrust manifests frequently in government and political institutions and is
seen as dysfunctional to democracy, making governing networks challenging. Yet, previous studies emphasize the significance of promoting
trust more than addressing distrust in networks. Distrust differs from the absence of trust. It involves relationships characterized by doubt, sus­
picion, or opportunism. Relatively little is known about why distrusting relationships occur and how they develop in adversarial interorganizational
governance networks. Using quantitative network surveys and qualitative interview data from organizations involved in an adversarial local
hydraulic fracturing governance network in New York, our mixed-method analyses fill this gap. We found evidence of cognitive distrust from
different policy beliefs and identity-based subgroups and two sources of behavioral distrust (competition and non-collaboration), as well as the
interactions between cognitive and behavioral sources of distrusting relationships. We further identified underexplored sources of endogenous
relational distrust: strong and negative reciprocity, non-transitivity, and Simmelian ties (meaning mutual third-party ties). These relational sources
suggest that the distrust networks mutually reinforce each other but are less clustered and more indirect. Our study advances network manage­
ment scholarship by showing why distrusting relationships occur and how they escalate within adversarial networks.
Keywords: governance network; distrust; network analysis; mixed-method analyses; hydraulic fracturing policy.
Introduction
In the age of collaboration and shared governance, paradox­
ically, distrust manifests frequently across the public, private,
and civic spheres. Distrust makes governing networks chal­
lenging. When distrust dominates networked relationships
among policy actors, building and rebuilding trust are
daunting tasks (Cheng and Sandfort 2023; Guo, Lumineau,
and Lewicki 2017; Karimo et al. 2023; Oomsels et al. 2019).
Thus, it becomes essential to identify why and how distrust
occurs and develops to improve democratic networked sys­
tems, increase social cohesion, and avoid governance failure.
Governance networks are composed of interdependent
and diverse actors such as advocacy organizations, nonprofit
service providers, private industries, and state and local gov­
ernment agencies jointly making public decisions in the de­
sign and implementation of policies (Lemaire et al. 2024;
Steelman et al. 2021; van den Oord et al. 2023). Scholarly
discussions describe distrust as a frequently observed feature
of governance networks, particularly adversarial governance
networks (Cheng and Sandfort 2023; Fischer et al. 2016;
Offer 2021; Verhoest et al. 2024). However, little is systemat­
ically understood about why and how distrust occurs in this
network. Scholars have yet to define the dynamics behind
distrust formation and development during policy processes,
even though they claim that distrust can cause severe
consequences on networking behaviors such as information
distortion, disbelief, or lack of cooperation. It can also affect
network structures such as avoidance of collaborative recip­
rocal interactions or weak ties associated with cross-coalition
bridges (Hu et al. 2023; Oomsels et al. 2019).
Distrust is often defined as an absence of trust. However,
recent scholarship in social psychology and management
distinguishes between trust and distrust on separate dimensions
(Guo, Lumineau, and Lewicki 2017; Kostis, Bengtsson, and
Näsholm 2022; Lee and Lee 2022; Verhoest et al. 2024). On
one hand, trust is the willingness to be vulnerable toward an­
other person, which evokes feelings of hope, vulnerability, or
assurance. On the other hand, distrust is a feeling of fear or
skepticism, discomfort or danger that engenders protective
behaviors (Lee and Dodge 2019; Lewicki et al. 2006; Lo et
al. 2022). While distrust may increase transaction costs of so­
cial interactions, it is not the same as opportunism, which
Williamson (1975) famously defined as “seeking self-interest
with guile.” Rather, opportunism can be a source of distrust
if it evokes fear, skepticism, or discomfort. As we will show
below, seeking self-interest is related to certain sources of dis­
trust, but is not distrust itself. Understanding why and how
distrusting relationships are formed in adversarial networks
can help develop strategies to alleviate risks and fear and
foster a supportive and transparent problem-solving environ­
ment in governance networks.

While trust is a widely researched concept within govern­
ance networks, distrust is less studied. Yet, there is widespread
recognition among public network scholars that distrust is
more likely to occur than trust and is a common and recur­
ring problem in coordinating interdependent relationships
(Fischer et al. 2016; Karimo et al. 2023; McLaughlin et al.
2021; Ulibarri 2024), particularly in adversarial networks.
Understanding the sources, levels, and development of dis­
trust can reveal fractures among networked actors, explain
suboptimal decisions, and unveil why networks might falter.
It can further aid in bridging divides, managing political po­
larization, or addressing public value co-destruction.
Thus, we examine why and how policy actors (organiza­
tions) distrust others in adversarial governance networks.
Specifically, we ask: (1) What are the sources of distrust be­
tween networked organizations? And (2) How do sources of
distrust relate to levels of distrust in relationships? To address
these questions, we review the literature on distrust and its
sources in governance networks, followed by our methods.
We then present our findings on the cognitive, behavioral,
and endogenous relational sources of distrust in adversarial
networks. We discuss our study’s theoretical and practical
contributions to managing governance networks.
Governance networks and distrust
The literature on public governance networks offers various
approaches to understanding network formation, drawing
from resource dependency, institutional, and social network
theories (Iborra et al. 2018; Lemaire et al. 2024; Raab et al.
2015; van den Oord 2023). In contrast to hierarchical con­
trol and market competition explanations, previous network
research underscores the positive role of trust among govern­
ment, business, and civil society actors, serving as the “social
glue” in collective policy processes. Trust is seen as a desirable
organizing principle guiding interorganizational interactions,
fostering transparency, facilitating risk acceptance, promoting
knowledge sharing, reducing monitoring costs, and stabilizing
networks (Lee and Esteve 2022; Sedgwick et al. 2022).
However, other lines of inquiry from social psychology,
sociology, business management, and public administration,
have raised questions about whether trust is inherently benefi­
cial in building governance networks. These studies explored
whether trust creates negative consequences, under what
situations institutions can function without trust, or how
levels of trust influence the formation and effectiveness of
interorganizational governance networks (Cook, Hardin, and
Levi 2005; Granovetter 1985; Lee and Lee 2022; Oomsels et
al. 2019). Excessive trust can lead to adverse consequences
such as overconfidence, inflexibility, malfeasance, and an ina­
bility to monitor opportunistic behaviors (Cook, Hardin, and
Levi 2005; Granovetter 1985; Lee and Dodge 2019; Oomsels
and Bouckaert 2014). These studies more recently prompted
research on the roles of distrust in networks.
Distrust refers to expectations of injurious actions and
concerns that an interlocutor may act to cause harm, not
care about one’s welfare, or be hostile (Guo, Lumineau,
and Lewicki 2017). Notions of vulnerability and uncer­
tainty are central to conceptualizing distrust in interpersonal,
intergroup, and interorganizational behaviors. While distrust
is often viewed negatively, it can promote behaviors that fa­
cilitate accountability, transparency, and rigorous debates,
ultimately enhancing decision quality (Kostis, Bengtsson, and
Näsholm 2022; Verhoest et al. 2025). In interdependent gov­
ernance networks, distrust can form communication ties that
encourage vigilance, risk assessment, exploration of alterna­
tive policy ideas, and consideration of different viewpoints.
Moreover, perceived levels of distrust among network ac­
tors can affect network structures. High levels of distrust
can support bridging behaviors and create communication
connections for risk management, while low levels of distrust
can lead to fewer reciprocated ties and closed substructures in
the network (Lee and Dodge 2019).
However, distrust can also hinder collaboration, inhibit
creativity, foster disengagement, exacerbate disagreement,
and result in lower satisfaction, commitment, and motivation
toward shared goals (Cheng and Sandfort 2023; Lewicki et
al. 2006). It can foster behaviors such as revenge, cynicism,
and paranoia, prompting interdependent actors to decrease
vulnerability and shield their interests and policy beliefs
(Lewicki et al. 2006), leading to suboptimal policy decisions
for all. This raises an important question about the inter­
play between distrust and opportunism from the transaction
cost perspective. Opportunism entails deliberately taking
actions to maximize personal gain at the expense of others
by increasing adaptation or negotiation costs and considering
trade-offs when building social relationships (Williamson
1975). In adversarial networks, actors might be perceived as
acting in adversarial ways out of pure self-interested oppor­
tunism. Such opportunistic behaviors, or assumptions about
others’ opportunistic intentions, can relate to certain sources
of distrust in networks (Lubell et al. 2020). Distrust, then, is a
result of self-interested opportunism.
Therefore, understanding why distrusting relationships
form among network actors and how sources of distrust link
to levels of distrust is crucial for averting network failure.
Also, cultivating trust and managing distrust are simultane­
ously required to de-escalate conflicts and achieve shared
goals in networks (Lo et al. 2022; Mclaughlin et al. 2021;
Oomsels et al. 2019; Verhoest et al. 2024, 2025). We re­
spond to Oomsels and Bouckaert’s (2014) call to examine the
sources and levels of distrust within networks.
Sources of distrust in governance networks
Currently, research rarely systematically reviews sources of
distrust—particularly in interorganizational networks. To
advance the field, we synthesize research on the Advocacy
Coalition Framework (ACF), collaborative governance, and
governance networks and categorize findings into cognitive
and behavioral sources of distrust. Also, drawing from so­
cial network analysis theory, we introduce an underexplored
category—endogenous relational sources of distrust. These
three categories collectively provide a comprehensive under­
standing of the mechanisms driving distrusting relationships
within interorganizational adversarial networks.
Cognitive sources of distrust.
Distrust in adversarial networks can arise from differences
in policy beliefs and group identities due to incompatible un­
derstanding of policy issues and in-group favoritism. While
the ACF does not directly address distrusting relationships, it
specifies how shared or divergent policy beliefs between two
actors filter resource exchange, shaping network structures.

Similarly, Bijlsma-Frankema, Sitkin, and Weibel (2015: 3)
noted, “If this expectation of harm raises the perceived vul­
nerability to a point where actors become unwilling to accept
it, distrust—the unwillingness to be vulnerable—arises.”
Next, social identity, group entitativity, and self-
categorization theories suggest that boundaries between
political allies and adversaries are often determined by
the similarity in policy actors’ group identities (Hornung,
Bandelow, and Vogeler 2019; Lee and Huang 2023). If ac­
tors belong to differently perceived groups, they tend to
emphasize differences due to group bias. Consequently,
networks consisting of actors with differing groups tend to
fragment into highly distrusted sub-coalitions (Qi and Ran
2023; Weible and Ingold 2018), characterized by cognitive
imbalance and a lack of cross-coalition brokers (Ingold and
Leifeld 2016). Lewicki (2006) coined the term “identification-
based distrust” to describe distrust derived from incongruent
identities. In governance networks, this type of distrust is ac­
tivated by perceived gaps between in-group and out-group
markers because a sense of different group membership can
prevent mutual understanding critical for coordinating net­
work goals or working relationships (Hornung and Bandelow
2025; Lee and Huang 2023). Thus, we hypothesize:
H1: Divergent policy beliefs between two actors posi­
tively relate to the perceived levels of distrust in adversarial
networks.
H2: Different subgroups between two actors positively
relate to the perceived levels of distrust in adversarial
networks.
Behavioral sources of distrust.
Research on ACF, collaborative governance, and governance
networks has focused on positive and context-dependent ob­
servable organizational behaviors, paying less attention to
the behavioral sources of distrusting relationships. However,
literature on interorganizational conflict highlights specific
behaviors, such as competition (active rivalry or strategic
responses) and non-collaboration (avoiding joint efforts or
passive disengagement), that foster distrusting relationships
(Kostis, Bengtsson, and Näsholm 2022; Ulibarri 2024; Lee
and Lee 2025). These observable behaviors can be intensified
by a zero-sum mentality and uncertainty in policy tools,
common in adversarial networks (Ki 2023; Lee and Esteve
2022; Qi and Ran 2023).
In a competitive environment, actors engage in a win–lose
game where deceptive, opportunistic, and strategic behaviors,
such as exploiting others for competitive advantage, are
common to protect against potential harm (Ki 2023; Kostis,
Bengtsson, and Näsholm 2022; Ulibarri 2024). Actors may
also suspect others of withholding important knowledge or
information or using privileged knowledge to gain advantage,
leading to defensive behaviors and distrustful relationships
(Ingold and Leifeld 2016). Additionally, concerns about fair­
ness and ethics emerge when competition incentivizes uneth­
ical strategic behavior or exacerbates disparities in resources
and opportunities (Ki 2023; Lee and Lee 2025), fueling
perceptions of injustice and undermining trust in competitors
and the competitive system itself. When these behaviors occur
amidst high levels of uncertainty in policy information, dis­
trust between actors may be elevated or more likely.
Concerning non-collaboration, the violation of collab­
orative practices and norms between actors in adversarial
networks—such as not keeping agreements or not upholding
responsibilities (Cheng and Sandfort 2023; Kostis, Bengtsson,
and Näsholm 2022)—further undermines positive social
relationships like trust, cooperation, and coordination. As ac­
tors break a sense of unity and are reluctant to collectively
achieve network goals, they may perpetuate a cycle of sus­
picion and disengagement. These observable behaviors can
be used to calculate the benefits of forming and maintaining
negative relationships, compared to the costs of ending them,
for example, which in turn can be used for self-protection in
response to the inherent uncertainties and transaction costs
associated with social exchanges in adversarial networks
(Bijlsma-Frankema, Sitkin, and Weibel 2015; Seo 2025).
Thus, we hypothesize:
H3: The presence of competition between two actors pos­
itively relates to the perceived levels of distrust in adver­
sarial networks.
H4: The presence of collaboration between two actors neg­
atively relates to the perceived levels of distrust in adver­
sarial networks.
Also, cognitive and behavioral sources of distrust can interact
in network relationships. Cognitive factors, policy beliefs,
and perceived subgroups influence behaviors in different
ways (Hornung and Bandelow 2025; Lubell, Mewhirter, and
Berardo 2020; Weible and Sabatier 2009). When actors hold
different cognitive evaluations—such as divergent policy
beliefs or subgroup interests—they may interpret the same
behaviors, like competition or collaboration, in different
ways. For instance, actors with divergent policy beliefs, or
those belonging to different subgroups within the adversarial
network, may view competition as a threat that intensifies dis­
trust. In contrast, actors sharing the same policy beliefs, or
within the same subgroup, may perceive competition as a less
threatening challenge or as a constructive opportunity that
sustains policy dialogue. Also, actors with divergent beliefs
or those from different subgroups may perceive collaboration
as more challenging, with greater potential for conflicting
interests or frictions, compared to actors with shared beliefs
or within the same subgroup. This dynamic can weaken the
negative relationship between collaboration and distrust
(Ambrose and Siddiki 2024; Guo, Lumineau, and Lewicki
2017; Lee and Huang 2023). Thus, we hypothesize:
H5ab: The positive relationships between competition and
the perceived levels of distrust in adversarial networks are

Endogenous relational sources of distrust.
Distrust can also stem from different types of network ties
(or connections). Certain types of ties in a network can be
configured into different micro-structures characterized
by, for example, reciprocity, where two actors are mutually
connected, or transitivity, where the tie between a first and
second actor, and between the second and third actor, dictates
the tie between the first and third actors. These micro-
structures are endogenous because they arise from within the
network itself. These are distinct from behaviors, referring to
specific actions between actors (Hu et al. 2023; Lemaire and
Provan 2018). Previous research has concentrated on micro-
structures in positive networks, involving collaboration and
knowledge sharing, while neglecting negative networks. We
center on adversarial networks characterized by distrust and
examine three well-recognized micro-structures: reciprocity,
transitivity, and Simmelian ties (meaning mutual third-party
ties) while considering tie strength (Hu et al. 2023). We aim
to elucidate how micro-structures serve as endogenous rela­
tional sources of distrust among organizations within govern­
ance networks.
Reciprocity.
Reciprocated ties (e.g., from A → B and from B → A) are im­
portant sources of endogenous relational distrust, representing
self-reinforcing exchanges between two parties (dyads). The
tendency to reinforce negative ties in dyadic relationships is
called the negative reciprocity principle (Bijlsma-Frankema,
Sitkin, and Weibel 2015). To illustrate, one actor’s distrust
can increase monitoring and surveillance of the other actor
based on a suspicion of ulterior motives and can, in turn,
result in holding back positive relational exchanges such as
collaboration, cooperation, and symmetrical communication.
This can, in turn, create hostility and deception (Karimo et
al. 2023; Ulibarri 2024) and hinder common understanding
of the problem, the development of shared interests, or
problem-solving (Karimo et al. 2023). Consequently, dis­
trust among dyadic relationships can intensify and foster po­
larization across the network. Reciprocal distrust in dyadic
relationships can have more severe negative consequences
when characterized by long-term or intense emotional ties
between parties (Krackhardt 1992; Offer 2021). Thus, we
hypothesize:
H7: Strong negative reciprocity positively relates to the
perceived levels of distrust in adversarial networks.
Strong nontransitive negative ties.
Transitive ties move beyond dyadic relationships to three
actors and are characterized by the principle that when a
relationship holds between two ties in a three-person net­
work, it will also hold between the third tie. For example,
when interactions between actors A, B, and C are positive
(e.g., friendship or alliance), transitivity follows the principle
of “a friend of a friend is my friend”—all three ties are de­
fined by friends (see Figure 1, left side). However, in negative
dynamics, the ties are nontransitive because the distrusting
relationships between actors A and B and between actors
B and C do not hold for the ties between actors A and C.
Instead, this triad reflects the principle of “an enemy of my
enemy is not my enemy,” which breaks the “chain reaction
of relationships.” This phenomenon is particularly notice­
able when actors maintain strong negative ties because open,
nontransitive ties (broken chains) are unlikely to evoke cog­
nitive dissonance, and actors will have limited motivation
to resolve these imbalances. Recent literature has generally
found that transitivity is weak or completely absent in neg­
ative relationship-based networks (Harrigan and Yap 2017;
McMillan et al. 2022).
Thus, we hypothesize:
H8: Strong transitive distrusting ties negatively relate to the
perceived levels of distrust in adversarial networks.

rect relationships that two actors share through mutual third
parties, which is mostly explored in positive relationships
(Lemaire and Provan 2018). They occur when two actors
are “reciprocally and strongly tied to each other and if they

and confirmation bias, where actors interpret information in
alignment with existing opposing viewpoints. These endog­

trust, making it difficult to rebuild trust and repair negative
relationships. Thus, we suggest:
H9: A higher number of strong distrusting Simmelian ties
positively relates to the perceived levels of distrust in adver­
sarial networks.
Methods
Given our aim, we collected quantitative and qualitative net­
work data on a local fracking policy network in New York
and conducted quantitative network and qualitative coding
analyses.
A local fracking policy network in New York
The case involves an adversarial network centered around
the fracking controversy in a mid-size city in New York.
This city was a manufacturing center in technology and de­
fense, but experienced economic decline since the end of the
Cold War in the 1990s. The prospect of natural gas develop­
ment in New York via hydraulic fracturing and horizontal
drilling emerged around mid-2007. By 2014, over a hundred
local governments had banned or imposed moratoriums on
fracking within the state. The controversy created divisions
between opponents—who raised concerns about environ­
mental degradation, adverse health effects, social and ec­
onomic repercussions, and the delay in transitioning to
renewable energy—and proponents who perceived economic
benefits for individuals and local governments (via taxation)
and energy independence (Lee and Dodge 2019).
Our case reflects four characteristics of adversarial
networks (Weible and Sabatier 2009). First, it features sci­
entific and technical knowledge disputes encompassing
health impacts, water quality, job creation, and local busi­
ness opportunities. This is evident through opposing “sci­
entific” analyses presented by coalitions of landowners and
environmental groups. Second, legal conflicts integral to the
case revolve around the authority of local governments over
fracking activities. After the city council and the local execu­
tive government jointly implemented a fracking moratorium,
a coalition of local landowners, oil and gas companies, and
small business owners united to file a lawsuit against a local
elected official. Third, key policy actors exhibited a “winner
takes all” mindset, centering discussions on whether local
governments should institute setback requirements or zoning
ordinances to permit or prohibit fracking activities. Fourth,
the network reveals coalitions with polarized policy beliefs
and fragmented authority. For example, landowner and envi­
ronmental coalitions diverge on local government control; the
former contending it results in regulatory confusion, and that
the latter insisting it protects residents from risks.
Data collection
Employing a three-step network specification method within
the “realist” tradition (Laumann, Marsden, and Prensky
1989), actors in the fracking network were identified based on
informant input rather than predefined criteria. We compiled
a list of thirty-seven potentially influential organizations from
newspaper articles, government documents, reports, and web
documents published between January and March 2014. We
then identified four informants from the list to assess the list,
resulting in the removal of eleven organizations deemed less
influential. Subsequently, during the interview, we asked for
recommendations on which organizations to include or ex­
clude. The county legislature was excluded due to its periph­
eral involvement. The final network comprises twenty-five
actors: landowner coalitions, media, oil and gas associations,
business groups, environmental organizations, city agencies,
the city council, county government agencies, and legal
entities (see Supplementary Appendix 1).
Between July and September 2014, we conducted semi-
structured interviews with eighteen of twenty-five organi­
zations involved in the network. The responding network
organizations included representatives of environmental and
civic organizations, oil and gas industry associations, land­
owner coalitions, city agencies, and the city council. The

Quantitative analysis
Quantitative network survey data were collected and analyzed
to test whether cognitive, behavioral, and endogenous rela­
tional sources of distrust relate to levels of distrust, consid­
ering the directionality in relationships. We aim to measure
how actors subjectively perceive their distrusting relationships
and the sources of distrust, as distrust is essentially based on
how actors think, act, or position themselves in a network.
First, our dependent variable, distrust, was operationalized
as “expectations of injurious action that is related to the
local fracking policy” (Guo, Lumineau, and Lewicki 2017).
Respondents were asked to subjectively report their perceived
level of distrust for every other network member on a scale
from one to five (1 = not distrust at all, 3 = moderately dis­
trust, 5 = extremely distrust). Utilizing this data, a valued, di­
rectional, perceived dyadic distrust network was established.
For example, when actor i indicates 2 (= slightly distrust) in
terms of its perceived levels of distrust toward actor j, 2 was
assigned to cell ij.
Second, we established three subjectively measured
categories for sources of distrust, rather than using objec­
tive measurements. One, we measured each actor’s policy
beliefs and perceived identity-based subgroups as cognitive
sources of distrust. An actor’s policy beliefs refer to norma­
tive ideas about how problems should be understood and/
or how responses should be structured (Weible and Sabatier
2009). We asked respondents to select one of three policy
beliefs (pro-, neutral-, and anti-fracking). The original vari­
able was compiled at the nodal level and then converted into
a dyadic measure based on divergence of policy beliefs. For
each pair of actors, the variable was coded as 1 if actors held
different beliefs and 0 if they shared the same beliefs. Our
model incorporated the network of divergent policy beliefs
and its interaction with two behavioral factors: collaboration
and competition. Identity-based subgroups were measured
by asking respondents to classify network actors based on
the following question: “Could you sort the listed organiza­
tions into groups according to how similar they are?” This
process shows how actors internalize subgroup prototypes
that describe other actors’ identities, beliefs, perceptions, and
behaviors in the network (Hornung, Bandelow, and Vogeler
2019) (see Supplementary Appendix 3). Based on respondents’
self-classification of subgroups, we created a network of dif­
ferent identity-based subgroups where a tie between two ac­
tors (1) represents membership in a different subgroup, while
the absence of a tie (0) indicates membership in the same
subgroup. Our model included identity-based subgroups and
their interactions with behavioral sources: collaboration and
competition.
Two, behavioral sources of distrust include competition and
non-collaboration among network actors (Kostis, Bengtsson,
and Näsholm 2022). Political competition is defined as
gaining the political ability to shape and control the content
and direction of a public policy (Ingold and Leifeld 2016).
To gauge political competition, we provided respondents with
the list of policy actors and asked them to indicate with whom
they competed during fracking policymaking to achieve their
policy goals in the past 12 months (1 = yes; 0 = no). This in­
formation was used to create a competition network, where a
tie (1) represents competition, whereas the absence of a tie (0)
represents no competition between actors. Collaboration is
defined as whether or not organizations informally work side-
by-side and actively pursue opportunities to work together
as a team. Respondents were asked if they have a collabora­
tive relationship with others in the network (1 = yes; 0 = no).
In a collaboration network, a tie (1) denotes collaboration
between two actors, while the absence of a tie (0) indicates
non-collaboration.

trust, we examined strong reciprocity, strong transitivity, and
strong Simmelian ties. For this analysis, we included only
those relationships that were defined by relatively strong
distrust. Our cutoff was relationships defined by moderate,
high, or extremely distrustful relationships.3 We created the

Qualitative analysis
To clarify cognitive and behavioral sources of distrust in ad­
versarial networks, verbatim responses from open-ended
questions were transcribed from digital recordings for
analysis. We used thematic, process, and causation coding
approaches coupled with iterative cycles of within-respondent
and across-respondents analysis. We aimed to identify recur­
ring ideas, concepts, themes, categories, and consistent or di­
vergent patterns across respondents or organizational types
(Miles and Huberman 1994).
We coded seventy-four statements or episodes containing
information about sources of distrust and interactions with
distrusted actors, using key terms and meanings (e.g., the main
subject of the statement/story or the obvious rhetoric em­
ployed by the interviewee). After iterative within-respondent
and across-respondent analyses, we inductively identified sev­
enteen recurring categories elucidating sources of distrust.
These seventeen codes were “fractured” and restructured for
maximum distinctiveness, enhancing thematic comparisons.
For example, initial codes included “profit motive,” “political
motive,” and “self-serving attitude or self-interests,” which
were collapsed into “self-serving attitude.” Also, “misrepre­
sentation,” “fabrication,” “lack of evidence,” and “lie” were
collapsed into “strategic misrepresentation.” Finally, the in­
itial seventeen codes were classified into cognitive and be­
havioral sources of distrust. This classification was guided
by prior literature concerning the formation of distrust in
organizational or group contexts. Because the qualitative
data did not reveal endogenous relational sources of distrust,
this aspect was addressed only through quantitative network
analysis. Category validity of the qualitative findings was not
established through inter-coder reliability checks but through
approaches aligned with inductive analyses (Miles and
Huberman 1994). We assessed the robustness of differences
and similarities of categories through repeated comparisons
across themes and with established concepts in the litera­
ture. This process allowed us to discern commonalities and
distinctions between existing concepts and our data. Lastly,
we present substantial evidence supporting these categories
(see Supplementary Appendix 6). The inductive approach
addresses the complexities behind the sources of distrust and
elevates the voices of network actors in generating findings
(Nowell and Albrecht 2019).

policy beliefs and different subgroups), behavioral (compe­

sources (reciprocity, transitivity, and Simmelian ties) relate to
the levels of distrust in relationships in the adversarial net­
work. We also investigated how interactions between cogni­
tive and behavioral factors associate with the levels of distrust
in the network. Our qualitative results specify why and how
cognitive and behavioral sources of distrust contribute to
distrusting relationships.

Our qualitative analysis revealed how and why diver­
gent beliefs on fracking policy and different subgroups con­
tribute to perceived levels of distrust within the network. It
thus provides insight into the causal connections between
cognitive sources of distrust and distrusting relationships,
moving beyond a simple linear understanding of their
associations. First, our qualitative analyses discovered that
divergent policy beliefs resulting in distrust are rooted in
closed-mindedness. Closed-mindedness breeds distrust when
actors hold hostile attitudes toward other actors with dif­
ferent values, beliefs, feelings, ideas, words, or actions. Key
terms identified include “culture of hatred and division,”
“no conceivable way,” “intolerance,” “closed-mindedness,”
“hostile attitudes,” and “extreme positions.” For some
respondents (Actors 4, 1, 3, 24, and 25 in Supplementary
Appendix 1), distrust arises when actors believe there is no
way to mitigate contentious arguments over fracking and
are closed-minded toward others with different ideas about
it. For instance, legal organizations and public agencies in­
dicated the closed-mindedness of civic organizations and
landowner coalitions as a source of distrust in the network.
Landowner coalitions and a media organization commented
that the anti-fracking organizations’ closed-mindedness
was a source of distrust and emotionally described them as
“crazy” and “lunatic.” As another example, a member of
the county government (Actor 24) commented “The thing
that made me distrust organizations is they always stick to
their position [on fracking] and never do what others do
under any circumstances. It tells me that they have a very
closed-mindedness [attitude] about it.” Interestingly, of the
seven organizations that noted closed-mindedness, only one
was a civic organization, whose member added, “If they
[pro-fracking organizations] [would] have more modulated
positions, I would be less distrustful of them.”
Secondly, distrusting relationships stemming from dif­
ferent subgroups reflect group-based self-serving attitudes.
Distrusting relationships arose when actors within the net­
work prioritized their own groups’ financial or political
interests over the collective network goals, suggesting the
link to perceived opportunistic attitudes in governance
networks (Ambrose and Siddiki 2024; Lubell et al. 2020).
The self-serving attitudes of different groups, characterized
by terms such as “profit motive,” “political motive,” “finan­
cial benefits,” “money,” “greed,” “rich,” “special interests,”
and “specific agenda,” often lead to generalizations about
the motives and objectivity of others, such as oil and gas
companies and public agencies involved in economic de­
velopment related to fracking. Recognizing the self-serving
attitudes of different groups relates to negative connotations
or assumptions about them, which in turn facilitates perceived
distrust. For instance, civic organizations (Actors 12 and 18),
landowner coalitions (Actors 1, 2, and 5), a legal organiza­
tion (Actor 4), and public agencies (Actors 19 and 24) all
indicated self-serving attitudes of other groups as a source of
distrust in the network. For example, a program manager of a
community development organization (Actor 18) noted that:
When I think of the biggest reason [why distrust arises], it
is [because of] …the profit and political motives. In other
words, you have politicians who take money from [special
interest groups]. These profit motives [come] from the gas.
They take money from industry.
Our findings on the cognitive sources of distrust suggest that
confirmation bias and negative stereotypes toward actors with
divergent policy beliefs or group memberships strengthen the
differentiation between “ourselves” and “others,” leading to
perceived distrust toward others in the adversarial network.
Behavioral sources
We hypothesized positive connections between compe­
tition and perceived levels of distrust (H3), and negative
relationships between collaboration and perceived levels of
distrust (H4). MRQAP analyses indicate a positive and sig­
nificant coefficient on competition at the P < .01 level in
Models 3 and 5, supporting H3. For instance, in Model 5,
competition is positively related to distrust in a relationship,
increasing it by 1.027 points in the adversarial network.
Also, the coefficients on collaboration were negative and sig­
nificant at the P < .01 level in Models 2 and 3, suggesting
that non-collaborative behaviors such as self-reliant or self-­
protective actions in networked situations can contribute to
higher levels of distrust in relationships within the network,
supporting H4.
Interaction between cognition and behaviors
Regarding H5ab, we observed that divergent beliefs and
subgroups have positive relationships with the levels of
distrust in a relationship between competitors (see posi­
tive coefficient on Divergent policy beliefs × Competition
P < .01 in Model 6 and the positive coefficient on Different
subgroups × Competition, P < .01 in Model 7), supporting
H5ab. This suggests that competitive behaviors used by actors
with divergent policy beliefs (rival groups), in which they ad­
vance their goals or protect themselves from potential harm in
the network, can fuel distrust in relationships. Also, regarding
H6ab, collaboration between different subgroups is negatively
associated with levels of distrust in relationships (significant,
negative coefficients on Different subgroups × Collaboration
from Models 4 to 7), not supporting H6ab. These findings
suggest that collaboration between different groups may offer
greater potential for mutual gain or increase interdependence,
leading to a more significant reduction in distrust compared to
collaboration within the same group. Conversely, violation of
collaborative practices or norms between different subgroups
prioritizing their own financial or political agenda over col­
lective goals can increase the levels of distrust in relationships,
affecting network goal consensus or failure.
Our qualitative findings paint more compelling pictures of
how and why competitive and non-collaborative behaviors
breed distrusting relationships and how behavioral and cog­
nitive sources of distrust interact.

Strategic misrepresentation plays a crucial role in fostering
distrust in relationships among competitive groups. In ad­
versarial networks, actors strategically shape policy debates
for their own benefit or interests by distorting “facts” or
manipulating evidence. Such behaviors suggest a link between
self-interests and competitive actions within groups. Key terms
identified include “fabrication,” “misrepresentation,” “exag­
geration,” “twisting the truth,” “falsehood,” “partial truth,”
“half-truth,” and “lie.” Planned, systematic distortion or mis­
statement of facts has been widely used in response to highly
competitive political and organizational pressure (Jones and
Euske 1991). For example, a landowner coalition described
its distrust toward an environmental group this way:
“The fact that they’re twisting the truth … They … show
they had this wonderful picture … of somebody being able
to light their water faucet with a match. Guess what? That
happened long before there was fracking. That was … a
known problem in that area … that methane had gotten
into the water table. And that, yes, you could light your
water faucet [on fire]. Yeah, they’re blaming it on fracking
and yes there’s methane in the water, but there’s no connec­
tion between the two.”
Landowner coalitions, civic organizations, public or­
ganizations, the media, legal organizations, and business
associations all commented that strategic misrepresentation
was a source of perceived distrust in relationships in the net­
work. Not surprisingly, the pro-fracking landowner coalitions
and the anti-fracking civic organizations both distrusted each
other because of strategic misrepresentation, confirming our
quantitative network analysis findings on the interplay be­
tween cognitive sources (different subgroups) and behavioral
sources (competition) of distrust.
The remaining three sources of distrust related to compe­
tition occur less frequently in the data but nonetheless con­
tribute to the overall understanding of how competition leads
to distrusting relationships and how competitive behaviors
and cognitive sources (group-based self-serving attitudes)
interact.
Disturbance can unsettle adversarial policy networks. A
prime example is when one organization protests against an­
other, a situation described by interviewees as “protest,” “dis­
ruptive,” and “made noise and yelled.” These disturbances
were based on divisive group-based self-serving attitudes. A
representative from an environmental organization (Actor
17) highlighted how such protest actions create distrust to­
ward industries and landowners groups:
“Well, the one thing that comes to my mind right off is
… [industries and landowner coalitions] sometimes can be
very disruptive at rallies that we are participating in. They
made noise and yelled. And the one guy who was known
to be paid by gas industries did do that. So, that’s all [a]
high level of distrust.”
Also, civic organizations, landowner coalitions, and business
associations all commented that intensely competitive dis­
turbing behaviors were reasons they distrusted others in the
network.
Fearmongering in the context of competition involves
using fear or anxiety to manipulate perceptions, influence
behaviors, or gain a competitive advantage over rivals.
Specifically, distrusting relationships from fearmongering are
formed when organizations induce fearful emotions in others
to achieve their purpose or interests, highlighting the links
with opportunistic, self-serving attitudes. Interview data re­
flect key terms including “intimidation,” “scare tactics,” and
“fearmongering.” A representative of the media organization
(Actor 6) commented that environmental or community or­
ganizations “do not deal with the facts, they deal with scare
tactics.” Also, a legal organization (Actor 25) commented:
“Certainly, there are conversations of important issues very
much characterized by scare tactics and fearmongering and
distortions.” In particular, anti-fracking civic organizations
were distrusted by landowners’ coalition, the media organiza­
tion, and public agencies for using fearmongering to promote
their policy agenda.
Deprivation stems from political power imbalances. In
adversarial contexts, zero-sum thinking prevails, where one
party’s gain is seen as another party’s loss. In this environ­
ment, actors compete to secure a larger share for them­
selves, which reflects self-serving attitudes and opportunistic
behaviors. This competition increases a sense of depriva­
tion. When actors feel deprived relative to others, they may
perceive the competitive process as unfair or biased against
them. This perceived unfairness can breed distrust in the net­
work or in the intentions of those perceived as benefiting un­
fairly from the competition. For instance, a president of the
steering committee of a landowner coalition (A5) expressed
distrust toward politically powerful actors who he believed
were depriving members of his organization of their rights
to develop fracked gas and enjoy its economic benefits. He
commented that
“[Organization] is playing with politics. [It] kept those
people from enjoying that vacation or maybe buying the
wife nice jewelry, or buying tractors, or improving their
houses. [It] has taken these [financial opportunities]
away from 40 people who died and so far, these [finan­
cial opportunities] have been taken away from the rest of
people too. It’s really sad.”
Only pro-fracking landowners’ coalitions noted deprivation
as a source of distrust toward the political appointee and anti-
fracking organizations.

behavior that can foster distrusting relationships by involving
passive engagement or the deliberate avoidance of joint efforts
in adversarial governance networks. This behavior includes
violating agreements and an unwillingness to share resources,
hindering shared network goals. Notably, non-collaborative
behaviors are linked to cognitive sources of distrust (group-
based self-protective attitudes) and opportunism.

non-collaboration, which stemmed from their own needs or
interests, which are seemingly incompatible with others in
the adversarial policy context. This supports our quantitative
network analysis findings on the interactions between cogni­
tive (different subgroups) and behavioral (non-collaboration)
sources of distrust and may suggest passive opportunistic
behaviors.
Endogenous relational sources
Even more intriguing are endogenous relational sources of
distrust that increase perceived levels of distrust between ac­
tors in the adversarial network. We found that strong negative
reciprocity positively relates to increasing perceived levels of
distrust in a relationship (see positive significant coefficient on
Strong negative reciprocity) from Models 2 to 7, supporting
H7. For instance, in Model 7, when a strongly distrusted rela­
tionship becomes reciprocated, the level of distrust in the rela­
tionship is expected to increase by 0.450 points. This finding
suggests that, similar to the “tit-for-tat” strategy, if one actor
strongly distrusts another, the other actor may retaliate with
equally strong distrust. In adversarial networks, two actors
are more likely to escalate perceived levels of distrust, which
may intensify the cycle of negativity and distrust across the
network.
Also, our results support H8 by showing significant neg­
ative associations between strong transitivity and perceived
levels of distrust from Models 2 to 7 (see negative, signifi­
cant coefficients on strong transitive distrusting ties). This
suggests a nontransitive principle in distrusting relationships,
encapsulated by the notion that “the enemy of my enemy
is not my enemy.” In adversarial networks, actors may not
feel obliged to address cognitive imbalances in distrusting
relationships or align their distrust uniformly across three
actors. This also implies that distrust may not necessarily be
spread among three actors. Thus, distrust networks display
more fragmented and dispersed relationships of distrust.
Finally, strong Simmelian distrusting ties showed positive
associations with distrusting relationships from Models 2 to 7
(see the positive, significant coefficients on Strong Simmelian
distrusting ties), which supports H9. This suggests that as
two actors share many common third-party distrusted ac­
tors, perceived levels of distrust in a relationship can increase.
Strong Simmelian distrusting ties contribute to establishing
durable and hostile structures through social influence
mechanisms. Also, shared indirect connections can act as
channels that facilitate the spread of distrust, thus increasing
the stability of distrust networks (Lemaire and Provan 2018).

visualizations of distrusting relationships among the twenty-

the network, thereby facilitating the diffusion of distrust.

Our visual and MRQAP network analyses provide a
nuanced understanding of endogenous relational factors in
distrust networks. They elucidate the mechanisms that rein­
force and spread perceived levels of distrust within the net­
work. This highlights the importance of effective mediation
or intervention by brokers capable of dismantling the cycle
of polarization, which seemed to be absent in this network.
Our results show that high distrusting relationships tend to
be reciprocated and nontransitive, and more shared third
parties in three-member cliques can reinforce and amplify
levels of distrust. These endogenous relational patterns align
with the concept of “negative reciprocity,” which involves
an “eye-for-eye” pattern of responding to perceived nega­
tive actions or harmful behaviors with a matched severity
level (Greco et al. 2019) and follows the notion that “the
enemy of my enemy is not my enemy.” Additionally, the ro­
bust and positive correlation between strong Simmelian ties
and perceived levels of distrust in a relationship may sug­
gest durable social influence mechanisms through higher-
order networks (Lemaire and Provan 2018), potentially
propagating or strengthening high levels of distrusting
relationships. This aligns with the notion of “cascading
effects,” where high levels of distrusting relationships ex­
tend conflict throughout the network, contributing to the
formation of larger clusters characterized by intense dis­
trust and lack of system knowledge and leading to value
co-destruction or governance failure.
Discussions and Conclusions
We currently live in an era characterized by the rapid
spread of distrust, misinformation, fake news, and out­
right lies. Unfortunately, this concerning trend has shaped
policy decisions driven by polarized political ideology, con­
flict, and partisan politics rather than collaboration and
trust (Mclaughlin et al. 2021). We aim to address this issue
by identifying cognitive, behavioral, and endogenous rela­
tional sources of distrusting relationships formed in policy
processes. Our findings advance governance network schol­
arship by showing why and how distrusting relationships
occur and develop within adversarial networks in theory and
practice.
First, expanding on research on trust and governance
networks, our quantitative and qualitative analyses found
cognitive sources of distrust in relationships, namely different
group-based identities reflecting self-serving attitudes related
to opportunism. When actors identify with a certain group,
they may devalue the interests of other groups and prioritize
their own. This can result in-group and confirmation biases
regarding the disparities between in-group and out-group
participants, leading to distrusting relationships (Lubell et al.
2020; Weible and Ingold 2018). The quantitative analysis in­
dicated that divergent policy beliefs were strongly related to
increasing levels of distrust in relationships, and the qualita­
tive analysis further suggested that perceived divergent policy
beliefs can facilitate distrust through the perception of others’
closed-mindedness.
Second, we specified two behavioral sources of distrust
in relationships in adversarial networks—competition and
non-collaboration. We further qualitatively uncovered six
distinctive behavioral sources of distrust closely linked to
unhealthy competitive behaviors and non-collaborative
actions: strategic misrepresentation, disturbance, fearmon­
gering, deprivation, violation, and reluctance to share. Four
of these—strategic misrepresentation, fearmongering, depri­
vation, and reluctance to share—demonstrate self-interested,
opportunistic behaviors as sources of distrust. These behav­
ioral sources of distrust and the related high levels of distrust
in relationships do not appear to have utility for adversarial
networks in terms of fostering collective action and collective
agreements (Lubell et al. 2020; Ulibarri 2024), although they

could argue these sources of distrust supported vigorous de­

away from the fact- or value-based disputes at issue and to­
ward assessing the groups themselves.
Moreover, we demonstrate how competitive and non-
collaborative behaviors are interpreted from the standpoint of
different group-based identities, contributing to the develop­
ment of perceived distrust in relationships. This nuanced view
on the interplay between cognitive and behavioral sources
of distrust suggests that competitive and non-collaborative
behaviors interact with divergent group-based interests
rooted in opportunistic behaviors in adversarial governance
networks. This, in turn, can lead to value co-destruction or
governance failure. While cognitive sources of distrust align
with the notion of the devil’s shift—a tendency to exaggerate
the power and evilness of policy opponents—occurring in
governance networks (Fischer et al. 2016), we propose that
adversaries not only cognitively portray their opponents
as more malevolent and powerful than they are but also
enact specific behaviors that contribute to feelings of help­
lessness and frustration, thus increasing highly distrusting
relationships. For example, perceiving an adversary in the
network as depriving one’s group of significant benefits
through their behaviors engenders strong distrust because, in
this case, it frustrates a long-held desire for economic pros­
perity in an area with declining economic prospects. Similarly,
with interference in political activities, distrust arises out of
frustrated actions meant to carry one’s point in the political
realm, thus generating powerlessness. Such interplay can be
seen as rooted in opportunistic behaviors that involve taking
advantage of circumstances over ethical principles. We under­
score the need for integrated strategies to promote behavioral
changes and address cognitively perceived destructive interest-
based rivalries to lower the levels of distrusting relationships
in governance networks.
Third, our findings provide empirical evidence of endogenous
relational configurations—strong and negative reciprocity,
non-transitivity, and Simmelian ties—that shape distrusting
relationships within adversarial governance networks. When
one actor forms distrusting relationships with another, it
is not solely the result of cognitive or negative behavioral
constructs but rather of exchanges within highly distrusting
relationships. Actors maintain their distrusting relationships
by following mutual norms and obligations of negative rec­
iprocity. They also abide by the principle that “an enemy of
my enemy is not my enemy” (non-transitivity), spreading
distrust across the adversarial network. Furthermore, the
growing number of distrusting relationships formed through
shared intermediaries (i.e., Simmelian ties) results in intense
interactions of distrust. These ties can restrict the actions of
policy actors and increase the likelihood of forming strong
negative connections through the amplification of negative
influence by shared adversaries (Lemaire and Provan 2018).
Also, these ties can create closed clusters characterized by
social capital, where most actors are acquainted with each
other and exchange redundant and cross-validated informa­
tion within closed groups (Henry, Lubell, and Mccoy 2010).
Combining these three relational configurations and network
visualization suggests that globally fractured and less clus­
tered subnetworks characterize the adversarial network.
Additionally, locally, indirect connections of distrust create
clustered groups where distrust flourishes, spreading through
shared connections and intensifying tensions within closely
knit cliques. Such relational dynamics foster both cohesion
and division across adversarial networks.
The findings on endogenous relational sources of distrusting
relationships contribute to our understanding of policy polar­
ization and negative relationships in adversarial governance
networks. We introduce the concept of a “polarization loop”
to explain negative feedback in negatively valenced networks.
This loop illustrates the reinforcing spiral that occurs be­
tween negative attitudes and behaviors among sub-coalitions
featuring differently perceived group-based interests, com­
petition, and non-collaboration, and that is structured by
reciprocal, nontransitive, and Simmelian negative ties. The
“polarization loop” stabilizes negatively valenced adversarial
networks by encouraging defensive behaviors, monitoring
members with potentially nefarious intentions, and facilitating
rigorous, possibly contentious debates. However, it suggests
that negative relationships can lead to unproductive levels of
polarization, which may hinder coordination between highly
fractured sub-coalitions, especially without effective brokers.
Entrapment in this loop could make it challenging to reverse
adversarialism.
While discussing our study’s implications, we acknowledge
its limitations and suggest potential areas for further research.
First, we relied on subjective and binary measures for the pri­
mary variables, such as competition and collaboration, rather
than objective and continuous measures. Future research
could incorporate observable manifestations and advanced
continuous measurements to explore distrust in governance
networks. For example, using a continuous measure of com­
petition could help identify potential curvilinear relationships
with distrust in these networks. Second, cross-sectional data
made it challenging to establish causality with the quantita­
tive results. However, the qualitative results suggest a causal
link between the cognitive and behavioral sources and levels
of distrust. Third, we only studied adversarial networks, so
our results are only generalizable to such networks, although
future research could test these results under other conditions.
Future analyses could also explore the evolving relationship
between cognitive and behavioral sources of distrust, and
the levels of distrust from a long-term perspective. This ap­
proach could reveal the underlying structures of distrust
among policy actors, contributing to developing effective
strategies for managing distrust and fostering collaborative
policymaking processes. Understanding the temporal dy­
namics of distrust and polarization can offer valuable insights
into the factors influencing distrust network structures and
guide policy-makers in implementing interventions to mitigate
polarization and promote productive collaboration within
adversarial governance networks. Fourth, future research
could explore the relationship between individual and organ­
izational perceptions of distrust within interorganizational
networks. In this study, respondents, acting as representatives
of their organizations, were asked to assess their distrust to­
ward other organizations, rather than expressing personal
distrust toward specific individuals. However, we recognize
that the distinction between individual and organizational
perceptions is often blurred. While individuals may express

organizational distrust, it may not always align with their
personal distrust, and the interaction between the two could
play a role in shaping interorganizational distrust.
The practical implications are twofold. First, brokerage
or leadership in governance networks could identify why,
how, and when distrust occurs, whether it is functional or
if it jeopardizes policy outcomes and could minimize unpro­
ductive distrusting relationships in the polarization loop. We
provide insights into how public and nonprofit managers
can implement practices to break this cycle. For instance,
they can set up knowledge-sharing platforms or policy fora
to provide inclusive data for evidence-based decision-making
or adjudicating values disputes, create independent advi­
sory bodies to provide impartial policy assessments and
recommendations, and use peer review and evaluation
mechanisms to assess proposals, decisions, and actions. These
practices can help address differences between group-based
interests, competitive and opportunistic behaviors, strategi­
cally intervene to weaken reciprocal distrusting relationships,
and break cycles of reinforcing distrustful relationships (Seo
2025; Whetsell et al. 2020).
Second, identifying sources of distrust and interrupting
the polarization loop at earlier stages of conflict develop­
ment within broader institutional policy contexts could be
useful. Our case represents a mature stage of a stabilized dis­
trust network where attempts to address distrustful attitudes,
behaviors, and ties are likely to be ineffective. Also, the
broader governance context, including political factors, needs
to be considered when designing governance networks. No
governance network arrangements exist in a vacuum outside
of politics (Siciliano et al. 2022). In our case, governmental
agencies lacked the necessary support from higher authorities
to effectively fulfill brokerage roles, given the high-stakes
nature of the fracking policy debate. Oil and gas lobbyists
in New York, and national and international corporations,
strongly advocated for fracking, aligning their interests
with the government’s need for revenue during an economic
crisis. Without the backing of the Governor at the time, it
would have been unrealistic to expect effective performance
in brokerage positions from state agencies. Furthermore, the
adversarial nature of New York’s political system poses sig­
nificant barriers to successful collaboration among involved
groups. These institutional features must be considered
when attempting to prevent unproductive negative attitudes,
behaviors, and ties when designing governance networks.
Our research uncovered an overlooked area in govern­
ance networks literature: why distrusting relationships occur
and how they develop in adversarial networks. The current
governance network literature primarily focuses on positive
dynamics in networks, such as collaboration, cooperation,
knowledge sharing, and trust, while paying little attention to
negative relationships. We advance the field by focusing on
sources and levels of distrust. Although our study is specific
to adversarial networks, it may provide insights useful to col­
laborative networks such as how perceived differences in sub­
group identities, unhealthy competition, non-collaboration,
and negatively reinforcing mutual interactions can lead to
adversarial transitions or distrusting relationships (Ulibarri
2024). These mechanisms warrant further investigation in
networks of all types, while their level of escalation may
vary across adversarial and collaborative networks. Effective
strategies can then be developed to manage and constructively
mitigate these dynamics before networks transition into ad­
versarial ones.

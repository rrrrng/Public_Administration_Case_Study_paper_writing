Abstract
Street-level organizations, which implement public policy on behalf of the state, often operate under unstable conditions. Workers routinely
face resource shortfalls, complex client interactions, and ever-changing rules, prompting them to develop coping strategies. These instabilities,
while disruptive, tend to be predictable, allowing those coping strategies to stabilize into routines that effectively constitute de facto, as distinct
from de jure, policy. But what happens when instability becomes unpredictable, such as during wars, disasters, or pandemics, where prior
experience offers little guidance? This paper explores two questions: (1) Do street-level workers develop different coping strategies under un­
predictable, as opposed to predictable, instability? (2) Can those strategies become routinized amid unpredictable flux? The second question
poses a conceptual challenge. If instability unfolds too rapidly and erratically for coping strategies to form, those strategies may never stabilize
into the kind of routines that matter—those that meaningfully shape policy in practice. To explore these questions, I modify the street-level bu­
reaucracy framework by incorporating concepts from the turbulence literature, particularly the notion of robustness: patterned responses that
enable systems to maintain core functions and values under conditions of unpredictable flux. Empirically, I draw from six-months of in-person
and virtual ethnographic fieldwork to examine how leaders from a single US-based street-level organization navigated the onset of the COVID-19
pandemic. Contrary to scholarship emphasizing innovation during the crisis, the leaders’ response was surprisingly ordinary, grounded in pre-
existing behaviors. Theoretically, these findings suggest that even amid unpredictable instability, street-level workers can still develop routines
that matter for policy-as-produced—not by inventing new coping strategies, but by reusing old ones.
Key words: street-level organization; turbulence; in-person and virtual fieldwork; crisis; street-level leadership.
Street-level organizations, or organizations that implement
public policies on behalf of the state, often operate amid
ongoing instabilities. Workers in these settings routinely
face shortfalls in resources, heavy caseloads, idiosyncratic
interactions with complex clients, changes to rules, policies,
and procedures, and frequent IT failures. In response, Lipsky
(2010) argues, street-level workers develop strategies, or be­
havioral adaptations such as bending and breaking rules,
or rationing resources, to cope (Tummers et al. 2015). Over
time, these coping strategies can solidify into routines—repet­
itive and recognizable patterns of action—which effectively
shape policy-as-produced, as opposed to policy-as-written
(Brodkin 2012, p. 942).
Instability is not the same as unpredictability, however.
Instability refers to frequent changes or disruptions in the en­
vironment. In contrast, unpredictability arises when a sense of
the future is unclear, and there is little or no past data to guide
decisions in the present. In many Western liberal democracies,
street-level organizations face instabilities that are, paradoxi­
cally, predictable (e.g., see Brunsson 1989). It is precisely this
predictability that allows street-level workers to routinize
their coping strategies—consistently matching general behav­
ioral rules to categories of situations—to manage the pressures
of their work (e.g., see Maynard-Moody and Musheno 2022).
The presence of unpredictable instability, such as amid un­
expected wars (Ferraro, Lotta, and Honchar 2025), sudden
government shutdowns or program cancellations (Peeters and
Campos 2023), erratic political leadership (Lotta, Piotrowska,
and Raaphorst 2024), and the spread of viruses like COVID-
19 (Gofen and Lotta 2021) raises two questions for street-
level scholarship. First, do street-level workers develop
different coping strategies under unpredictable, as opposed to
predictable, instability? Emerging studies suggest they often
do: established coping strategies often become ineffective in
unfamiliar and volatile environments, prompting workers to
develop new responses such as resisting, adapting, innovating,
defying, or even exiting their organization (Brodkin 2021;
Cox, Dickson, and Marier 2021; Ferraro, Lotta, and Honchar
2025).
Second, can these coping strategies become routinized amid
persistent unpredictability? This question is critical because,
as Brodkin (2012, p. 942) underscores, discretion, the space
where coping strategies emerge, gains analytical and practical
significance when discretionary acts are not random but struc­
tured. She writes, “It is these systematic, informal behaviors
that impart practical meaning to policy-as-produced.” Yet this
raises a conceptual challenge. If the environment shifts more
quickly and unpredictably than coping strategies can develop,
those discretionary strategies may never turn into the kind of
stable routine that substantively matter.
To better understand how street-level organizations re­
spond to unpredictability, I adapt the classical street-level
framework using insights from the turbulence literature

street-level scholarship. I argue that three commonly cited
challenges of street-level organizations—top-down directives,
rigid formal structures, and ambiguous goals—may, in fact,
facilitate robustness in times of unpredictability. Moreover,
by highlighting the third element of the organization's re­

stabilizing force, helping organizational members make sense
of disorienting change.
Street-level organizations amid (un)
predictable instability
Lipsky’s (2010) street-level framework begins with a core
question: how do workers such as teachers, police officers,
or social workers manage chaotic environments marked by
high demand, limited resources, complex clients, and ever-
changing rules and procedures? He offers a well-known an­
swer: the discretion inherent in these roles enables street-level
workers to develop coping strategies, such as bending and
breaking rules, rationing resources, or ritualizing tasks, to
bridge the gap between what they are expected to do, what
they can do, and what they want to do (Tummers et al. 2015).
From this, Lipsky makes his landmark proposition: policy
is not only made in legislatures or high-level government
offices, but also “on the streets” where street-level workers
exercise those coping strategies. Over time, those strategies
may solidify into informal routines that shape and constitute
policy-as-produced, which may diverge from formal, de jure
policy (Brodkin 2011, p. i255; 2012, p. 942).
This process of routinization assumes a context in which
instability is persistent, but predictable, however. To under­
stand why, consider the difference between predictable and
unpredictable instability. Predictable instability refers to
routine disruptions in the work environment, such as diffi­
cult client interactions, tensions between professional values
and bureaucratic rules, fiscal constraints, and operational
breakdowns (e.g., see Maynard-Moody and Musheno 2022;
Spitzmueller 2016; Watkins-Hayes 2011; Zacka 2017). These
disruptions occur so frequently that street-level workers come
to expect them, which is why they can develop a repertoire
of adaptive responses—or routinized coping strategies—to
manage them (Lipsky 2010 Part III).
But not all instabilities are predictable. Some erupt suddenly
and without precedent, such as the outbreak of war (Ferraro,
Lotta, and Honchar 2025), abrupt policy shifts driven by ca­
pricious political leaders (Lotta, Piotrowska, and Raaphorst
2024; Peeters and Campos 2023), or the emergence of a new
and poorly understood virus such as COVID-19 (Gofen and
Lotta 2021). Unlike more routine forms of instability, these
disruptions are so novel that street-level workers may lack
ready-made coping strategies to deal with them. In such cases,
pre-existing strategies may no longer apply, forcing workers
to improvise or even abandon familiar practices (Alirani et
al. 2025; Calò et al. 2024; Gofen, Lotta, and Marchesini da
Costa 2021; Phillips, Roehrich, and Kapletia 2023; Tang and
He 2023).
A context marked by unpredictable instability presents a
conceptual challenge for Lipsky’s street-level framework.
We might expect street-level workers to adjust their coping
strategies in response to shifting conditions. However, when
environmental shifts are both constant and unpredictable,
these new strategies may fail to  stabilize. As a result, the
shared, routinized behaviors that typically accumulate to
shape policy-as-produced may not form at all. What was
once collectively patterned and practiced can fracture into in­
consistent, fragmented action. This raises two questions: Do
coping strategies change when predictable instability gives
way to unpredictable instability? And does the implicit as­
sumption underpinning Lipsky’s core claim—that repeated
coping behaviors stabilize into routines that shape policy
on-the-ground—still hold under conditions of constant, un­
predictable flux?
Street-level organizations during COVID-19
To address those questions, we can turn to a burgeoning lit­
erature that explores how street-level organizations adapt
when confronted with unpredictable instability, where rules,
expectations, and operating conditions shift rapidly, often
without warning (e.g., see Brodkin 2021; Eiró and Lotta
2024; Ferraro, Lotta, and Honchar 2025; Lotta, Piotrowska,
and Raaphorst 2024; Peeters and Campos 2023). While still
emergent, this literature offers two key insights.
First, unpredictable instability disrupts and changes street-
level coping strategies, prompting workers to resist, impro­
vise, defy, or exit their organizations entirely (Brodkin 2021;
Cox, Dickson, and Marier 2021; Ferraro, Lotta, and Honchar
2025). During the early stages of COVID-19, for example,
the rapid proliferation of policies heightened ambiguity and

Within this altered discretionary space, many found their usual

improvising, “making do,” repairing gaps, redefining roles
and procedures, and fostering new relationships across or­
ganizational boundaries (Bentzen and Torfing 2023; Cancio
and Mustafá 2024; Gofen, Lotta, and Marchesini da Costa
2021; Tang and He 2023).
Yet a question remains: can these new and emergent
strategies become the kind of shared, repeated routines that
drive the proposition that street-level workers do not simply
take, but make, policy? After all, if the environment shifts be­
fore those new coping strategies solidify, those strategies may
remain fleeting, never quite becoming the kinds of routines
that substantively shape policy in practice.
Second, some studies show that pre-existing routines
endure, even amid significant disruption, insofar as they
are rooted in pre-existing occupational cultures, norms, or
governance patterns. For example, Brazilian police officers
refused to wear masks during COVID-19, seeing them as
“sissy” and incompatible with their masculinist organiza­
tional culture (Alcadipani et al. 2020, p. 399). In contrast,
police officers in Taiwan, drawing on an authoritarian-
era legacy of bureaucratic cooperation, enforced mask-
wearing with little resistance (Chen, Tsai, and Ho 2023).
Similarly, health workers in Thailand waited for govern­
ment direction before they acted, in line with hierarchical
norms. Meanwhile, their counterparts in Tanzania drew
on experience from past crises to independently spread
health messages using songs, loudspeakers, and radio
(Carlitz, Yamanis, and Mollel 2021; Sudhipongpracha and
Poocharoen 2021).
Taken together, this literature shows that unpredictable
instability both disrupts pre-existing routines and compels
workers to rely on them to navigate new conditions. Expressed
differently, street-level behavior in such contexts is neither
entirely new nor fully habitual. To capture this dynamic, we
need an analytic framework that accounts for both the per­
sistence of routinized behavior and the disruptions brought
forth by unpredictable environments—something the turbu­
lence analytic may help us do.
A modified street-level framework, amid
unpredictable instability
For public governance scholars, “turbulence” is a description
and analytic to study moments marked by “change [that] is
sudden, surprising and difficult to understand or track” be­
cause problems, solutions, causes, and effects are always on
the move (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023, p. 4). Drawing
on this analytic, I introduce three dimensions—shifting
parameters, temporal complexities, and robust response—to
offer a modified street-level framework to understand how
organizations manage unpredictable instability (Ansell and
Trondal 2018). These concepts clarify how organizations
are affected by unpredictable instability, and also theorize
how they sustain patterned behaviors or routines amid such
conditions.
Shifting parameters
In street-level organizations, parameters provide the foun­
dation for predictability in challenging environments. They
include formal structures—written rules, roles, and role
relationships that workers are expected to follow; informal
structures—emergent norms, power dynamics, and routines
shaped by day-to-day interactions; and the technologies, in­
frastructure, and inter-organizational relationships that sta­
bilize workers’ expectations and actions into predictability
(Scott and Davis 2015). When the environment shifts, these
parameters may rapidly adjust to accommodate new demands.
But under persistent, unpredictable fluctuation, attempts to
restabilize these parameters may be repeatedly disrupted. This
produces a cycle where organizations must constantly change
and adapt their parameters to cope with ongoing unpredict­
able instability. Such conditions complicate the formation of
routinized coping strategies.
Temporal complexities
Temporality involves several dimensions, such as timing
(the degree of coordination between elements), tempo (the
rate of change), sequence (the order in which events unfold),
and timeframe (bounded periods with a clear beginning and
end) (Howlett and Goetz 2014). In street-level organizations,
temporal complexities arise when different workers, each
operating within their own fiscal, political, organizational,
and inter-organizational schedules, must synchronize their
efforts to produce joint outcomes. Formal structures, such
as timetables and standard operating procedures, as well as
informal structures, such as the socialization into punctu­
ality, help synchronize these different temporalities. However,
during periods of unpredictable instability, these coordinated
temporalities may fall apart, creating unexpected disruptions
and chaotic misalignments. By foregrounding the work of
coordinating action over time, temporal complexities invite
scholars to trace not just how organizations react when un­
predictability first hits, but how they manage temporal com­
plexity as unpredictability fluctuates across hours, days,
weeks, and months.
Robust responses
Turbulence scholars study robust responses—patterned
sets of (re)actions to evolving change that result in “robust­
ness,” or “the ability of key features of a system to persist
across a range of different circumstances and across temporal
flux” (Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023, p. 8). Applied to
street-level organizations, robust responses involve actors
drawing on and modifying already-existing materials—
rules, resources, relationships, and routines—to uphold the
organization’s core functions and values (Ansell, Sørensen,
and Torfing 2021, p. 952). In this context, robustness is
assessed retrospectively: if the organization survives, it is
considered robust. This distinguishes it from resilience, which
implies a return to a prior stable state after disruption (Weick
and Sutcliffe 2015, p. 12).
Robust responses extend conventional street-level coping
strategies in three crucial ways. First, it expands the unit
of analysis: coping strategies focus on the individual’s ad­
aptation to everyday challenges, whereas robust responses
emphasize the system's adaptation to surprising and contin­
ually shifting conditions. Second, the analytical goal differs:
coping strategies are often used to theorize how street-level

one can trace how organizational behavior evolves or remains
the same amid shifting parameters and temporal complexities,
and how these patterns help maintain the organization’s
functions and values. Thus, robust responses do not neces­
sarily involve creating new practices. Instead, they may involve
the reapplication of familiar routines or coping strategies to
novel conditions (Ansell, Boin, and Farjoun 2015; Ansell,
Sørensen, and Torfing 2023, 2024).
Modifying the Street-level framework with
turbulence
This modified street-level framework enables scholars to ana­
lyze how organizations behave amid unpredictable instability
marked by shifting parameters and temporal complexities.
As shown in figures 1 and 2, it reframes the context, from
unstable, though predictable, to unstable and unpredict­
able. It also shifts the analytical focus from routinized
coping strategies that produce policy-as-produced to robust
responses, or organizational behaviors that preserve core
functions and values during prolonged periods of flux.
To preview the empirical portion of the paper, I primarily
focus on street-level leaders’ responses to COVID-19, both
for conceptual and practical reasons. Street-level leaders
are “responsible for the design, execution, and assessment
of street-level delivery arrangements, and held accountable
for its outputs and outcomes” (Gassner and Gofen 2018,
p. 555). They are central to the turbulence analytic because
they devise, communicate, and revise courses of action that
shape how organizations respond to volatility, making
them key agents of robust responses (Ansell, Sørensen, and
Torfing 2021, p. 955, 2023, p. 11). They are also critical to
street-level scholarship. By setting organizational missions
and strategies and translating policy between high-level
decision-makers and frontline workers, they shape the
conditions through which policy is made (Gassner and
Gofen 2018; Klemsdal, Andreassen, and Breit 2022; Møller
and Grøn 2024; Wong 2024). However, I ultimately focus
on street-level leaders for a more practical reason: I have the
most data on them.
Methods
This paper is based on fieldwork conducted at a network
of nonprofit health centers in Illinois, which I call the
“Organization.” The Organization is federally funded to pro­
vide primary health services to individuals regardless of in­
surance status or ability to pay. At the time of my fieldwork,
it employed over 700 employees and served over 80,000
patients. My data collection spanned six months, with two
months of in-person research (fig. 3a) and four months of vir­
tual research (fig. 3b). Although data for this paper are drawn
from these six months, it is contextualized within a larger
dataset collected from August 2019 to September 2020.
The data
The data for this paper draw on three sources:
Observations:
I observed over 79 in-person and virtual meetings with
healthcare leaders, managers, and administrative personnel.
These included COVID-19 clinical operations calls (n = 29),
health center operations meetings (n = 22), process improve­
ment meetings (n = 10), site management meetings (n = 5),
quality improvement meetings (n = 5), medical educational
lectures (n = 5) and all-site meetings (n = 3). After each
meeting, I wrote fieldnotes documenting key discussions and
observations.
Documents
I reviewed documents related to COVID-19 between
January and June 2020. These came from three sources: an on­
line folder accessible to all staff containing updated protocols,
a private folder for the COVID-19 committee containing
draft protocols and meeting minutes, and emails sent to all
staff about COVID-19. In total, I read 53 iterations of the
COVID-19 clinical protocols, 94 emails devoted to COVID-
19, and multiple PowerPoint presentations about COVID-19.
Formal interviews
Given the time and emotional burdens posed by the
evolving nature of the pandemic, I only conducted three
formal interviews about COVID-19 while in the virtual field:
with the Infectious Disease Officer (a leader), an Operations
Manager, and the Senior Manager of Process Improvement.
However, these interviews are part of a larger dataset of 51
interviews conducted with 30 organizational members.

My research on performance management abruptly
ended in March 2020. After the World Health Organization
declared COVID-19 a pandemic, I received a text message

The following week, Illinois Governor J.B. Pritzker mandated
nonessential workers to remain at home. As the Organization
scrambled to adapt to a changed landscape, which led to
the suspension of my ethnographic activities, I scrambled to
change my fieldwork. I quickly amended my ethics protocol
to include “collecting data about COVID-19” and committed
to no in-person interaction with staff.
Three weeks later, I resumed my research virtually, with a
new focus on COVID-19. This shift altered the types of data
I collected in three ways. First, instead of capturing informal,
emergent interactions such as hallway gossip or collective
eyerolls, my virtual fieldwork centered on observing online
meetings. Typically, these meetings were visually composed of
a screen, filled with a meeting agenda, relegating the meeting
participants, most with videos turned off and microphones
muted, into little black boxes stacked one upon the other on
the side of the screen. My fieldnotes subsequently thinned, be­
coming notes on what people said, who turned their cameras
on, unusual camera angles, muted and unmuted microphones,
silence as rejoinders, tech glitches, tech lags, and the weather.
Second, I conducted fewer interviews. Because my
interlocutors were saving lives while I safely stayed at home,
I refrained from imposing my research demands on them.
Ultimately, I only conducted three formal interviews during
the virtual phase, restricting the range of perspectives on the
Organization’s response to COVID-19; a limitation of this re­
search. Most other quotes from the virtual fieldwork come
from the goodwill of people I had built relationships with
during the in-person phase, and from what they shared via
Microsoft Teams, text messages, brief phone calls, and virtual
meetings.
Third, to compensate for my thinning virtual fieldnotes, I
relied more heavily on formal documents, such as protocols
and emails, edited by different organizational members and
finalized by the Senior Manager of Marketing who then
circulated those documents through all-staff emails.
Thus, the transition from in-person to virtual fieldwork
significantly shaped the types of data I collected which, in
turn, shaped how I analyzed and presented my findings (see
Appendix 1 for further discussion of the challenges posed by
this shift).
Data analysis
The analysis followed an abductive approach (van Hulst
and Visser 2024). It began with a theoretical surprise—the
Organization relied on protocols to drive change during
COVID-19—which challenged my initial expectation,
grounded in a Weberian reading of rational-legal bureauc­
racy, wherein formal structures like protocols tend to produce
rigidity (Kalberg 1980; Scott and Davis 2015; Weber 1947).
In response, I sought a framework that could make sense of
this unexpected use of formality.
I initially drafted a case study centered on the Organization’s
response to COVID-19, with protocols as the main protag­
onist. But this early version overlooked two important dy­
namics: the leaders role in shaping staffs’ orientation towards
both the virus and the protocols, and the strange temporal ex­
perience produced by the protocols' constant revisions. Thus,
I set the paper aside until three years later, I encountered the
analytic of turbulence, which offered a fresh vocabulary to in­
terpret these dynamics. The Organization’s continual change
resonated with turbulence’s “shifting parameters.” The pace
and chaotic coordination of those changes reflected its “tem­
poral complexities.” And the leaders’ reliance on conven­
tional tools and rhetoric to drive change revealed a dialectic
between continuity and change, captured in the concept of
“robustness.” I subsequently rewrote the case study through
the lens of turbulence (refer to Appendix 2 for details on why
I did not write this study in terms of “crisis”).

it in two stages. First, I traced   changes in federal and local

Limitations
For methodologists Schwartz-Shea and Yanow (2013), the
quality of research should be assessed based on its epistemo­
logical foundations, whether positivist or interpretivist. The
positivist’s quest for reliability, where the same measurement
procedure yields the same results irrespective of who does the
procedure, becomes the interpretivist’s task to be reflexive, to
elucidate what and how she sees is tied to her “positionality”
in the field. The positivist’s pursuit of validity, which describes
the measurement tool’s proximity in re-presenting the “real
world,” becomes the interpretivist’s demand for “thickness,”
or detailed rendering of what she has observed to stand in for
the “truthfulness” of her research.
This research is rooted in interpretive methodology.
I approached the protocols and all-staff emails as texts
through which the leaders gave sense to COVID-19 and its
impact on the Organization. The analysis was shaped by my
interactions with a select group of leaders and managers at a
large, nonprofit, community-based healthcare organization in
the US during the first wave of COVID-19. As such, the data
presented here cannot be considered “reliable” in the posi­
tivist sense; it represents a situated and partial interpretation,
shaped by my positionality as the researcher.
Furthermore, as an interpretive project, my writing
should be rich in detail, allowing readers to assess for them­
selves whether the findings resonate or fall flat with their
own experiences (Flyvbjerg 2006). Given the difficulties
of transitioning from in-person to virtual research, and the
limited number of interviews conducted while in the virtual
field, my descriptions may fall short of the “thickness” that
interpretivists typically demand. I therefore ask the reader to
appreciate the challenges of doing pandemic research.
Finally, due to space constraints, I had to compromise
what I could include in this text, including a richer discus­

Organization, after all, is in the United States, where the
murders of Black lives during lockdown—Ahmaud Arbery,
Breonna Taylor, Dion Johnson, George Floyd, Tony McDade,
and more—produced immense disruption and a prolonged
period of pain, which I could not include in this paper without
undermining its poignancy.
The leaders’ response, unfolding in four
phases
This section traces how the leaders of the Organization
responded to COVID-19, beginning with a description of
three key pre-existing organizational features that the leaders
would later use to navigate the uncertainties of the pandemic.
First, though the Organization is a nonprofit, community-
based organization that delivers services on behalf of the
state, it operates like Weber’s idealized rational-legal bureauc­
racy (Scott and Davis 2015; Weber 1947). It has a hierarchy,
with an established chain of communication and com­
mand. It has multiple departments, each with defined roles
and responsibilities. These details are governed by written
documents to ensure, at least in theory, that workers’ actions
and outcomes are standardized. The same documents also
ensure compliance with externally-imposed rules and perfor­
mance standards to secure government monies and special
legal statuses, habituating Organizational personnel to follow
imperatives from above.
Second, the Organization is made up of workers, many
who identify as professionals with specialized skills, and ad­
here to professional norms, such as a respect for science and
following evidence-based standards of care (Møller, Pedersen,
and Pors 2022). In fact, as fears about COVID-19 grew, the
Chief Executive Officer reminded his audience at an all-staff
meeting to be like Bob, a stick-figure man featured on a
PowerPoint slide, who “listens to science” and “doesn’t hoard
toilet paper.” “Please, be like Bob,” he urged, which elicited
laughter from the audience.
Third, the Organization is mission-driven. As highlighted in
its annual reports and marketing materials, it was borne from
the Civil Rights Movement with the goal of addressing racial
disparities in healthcare. A significant portion of its patient
population comprises young, Latinx, low-income women,
and one-third lacks health insurance in a country where
healthcare is both expensive and not universal.
With an appreciation of these features, we can now
turn to the leaders’ response to COVID-19. Rather than
organizing the empirical material around pre-defined ana­
lytical categories, I let the response unfold on its own terms,
allowing the three dimensions of turbulence—shifting
parameters, temporal complexity, and robust response—
recur as undercurrents. To help guide the reader, I have
separated the response into four phases: brewing anxiety;
movement to crisis; in the heat of COVID-19; and a gradual
drift to familiarity (fig. 4). Each phase reflects a shift in how
the leaders responded to the evolving pandemic. The leaders’
response, moreover, is separated into the formal and inter­
pretive. The formal involves the use of protocols and a com­
mittee structure; the interpretive involves the rationalization
of those changes in terms of the Organization’s shared
values. At the end of each phase, I offer a brief analytical re­
flection that connects the evolving response to the modified
street-level framework.

Phase 1: Brewing anxiety (January–February)
On December 31, 2019, a pneumonia-like virus sickening
dozens in Wuhan, China, made headlines. Three weeks
later, the virus traveled from Wuhan to Washington State.
Three days after that, it reached Chicago. On that day, the
Organization’s leaders issued their first official statement
through a one-page email. It opened with reassurance, “The
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) is closely
monitoring an outbreak of a new coronavirus strain (2019-
nCoV),” and concluded in bold text, “the risk in the com­
munity is extremely low,” with the addendum: “There is no
need to panic.”
The formal:
In response, the leaders followed a familiar script: they
produced a protocol to communicate and coordinate staff
around new public health guidelines. The protocol, called
the “Symptom Screening” protocol, comprised a single
bullet point with two sub-points addressing travel history
and symptoms, signalling to staff that the virus was serious
enough to warrant its own set of rules. Yet the protocol’s de­
livery, tucked into the text of an email, undermined its seri­
ousness, as staff could easily overlook or dismiss it amid the
daily deluge of emails.
The interpretive:
The virus’ low standing in the leader’s hierarchy of concerns
was echoed in their repeated reassurance that “there is no
need to panic.” This stance, justified by public health experts’
early risk assessments, aligned with the Organization’s core
values: follow the science and heed expert guidance. To il­
lustrate, each time the virus made a new, threatening, move,
leadership responded with an email urging calm, based on
expert advice. After the first US case of person-to-person
transmission, staff were informed with bold and underlined
text, “CDPH [Chicago Department of Public Health] has
communicated again that the risk to the general public is still
low” (31 January). With new evidence of community spread
in California, they reiterated: “Experts agree that the general
risk to those living in the US and Chicago remains low”
(February 27).
Alongside appeals to scientific evidence, the leaders
invoked the Organization’s commitment to racial justice. As
stories circulated media outlets about Chinese markets selling
sick animals and serving spoiled food (Human Rights Watch
2020), reviving long-standing tropes of immigrants with
unclean bodies that transmit infectious diseases (Anderson
1995), the Infectious Disease Officer coolly said in a med­
ical education lecture: “Myth. We all need to panic and blame
China for this emergency.” “Fact,” she refuted, “The panic
and hysteria that’s happening is fueled by racism and xeno­
phobia, not evidence or practicality.” In so doing, she affirmed
that for the Organization, combating xenophobia was just as
important as following the science.
These reassurances—rooted in both science and racial jus­
tice—may have initially helped allay staff concerns. But as
conflicting media narratives proliferated and patients with
suspected COVID-19 began entering the Organization, the
message that “there is no need to panic” began to fray. The
shift surfaced subtly at first. During the closing minutes of
a Site Management meeting, a highly regimented affair
governed by a chairperson who stops prolixity with the call
to “take the conversation offline,” and meeting participants
who adhere to the agenda because, as they often jest, a good
meeting is one that ends on time but a better one ends early,
a manager strayed from script. Hesitating, she asked: “Can
I ask a general question that I thought we’d bring up some
point during the morning? And I, I’m sorry to be the one
bringing it up, but it has to do with coronavirus.” Her col­
league sighed, but she persisted, saying “but you’re seeing
all this stuff coming out everywhere that CDC [the Centers
for Disease Control and Prevention] says that we should be
expecting a pandemic and it’s just a matter of time.”
Analytical reflection:
Initially, the level of shifting parameters and temporal com­
plexity posed no major disruption: their world remained
relatively predictable, and operations continued as usual.
Leaders responded to new public health information using
their standard approach—by developing a protocol to coor­
dinate and communicate change, grounded in appeals to sci­
entific authority and racial justice. But as the virus continued
to spread and public concern intensified, Organizational
members’ calm began to falter. Still, this initial move set a pat­
tern that would endure: leaders responded to the pandemic by
reusing familiar practices, grounded in shared values.
Phase 2: Movement to crisis (March 1–12)
The Organization’s response to COVID-19 accelerated fol­
lowing the first case of community transmission in Illinois
in March. Patients with known or likely COVID-19 were
redirected to emergency departments, while those with mild
illness were instructed to self-quarantine for 14 days.
The formal:
As new public health information about the virus poured
in, the leaders fell back on two familiar practices to cope.
First, they created a committee. Whereas the Infectious
Disease Officer had previously kept abreast of new COVID-
19 updates by dialing into weekly phone calls with Public
Health Departments, by Phase 2, those calls became daily. So,
the Infectious Disease Officer was joined by four other leaders
to form the “Coronavirus Committee,” tasked with absorbing
the deluge of public health updates and translating them into
operational guidance, primarily through revising the COVID-
19 protocol.
Second, they increased the frequency and urgency of
revising the protocol. As the leaders wrote in an email, “rap­
idly changing protocols will be a feature, not a bug, of our
response” [March 10]. Indeed, the protocol rapidly evolved,
starting as a single bullet point in an email, then growing

responded with gleeful gasps and head shakes. Chaos, once
again, brought on by a failure to read the protocol.
Analytical reflection:
The Organization’s accelerating response to COVID-19
corresponded with rising levels of shifting parameters and tem­
poral complexity. Yet, the leaders reproduced familiar routines
to manage the unfolding flux—forming a committee and
updating the protocol—which introduced their own temporal
challenges. Staff struggled to keep abreast with the rapid changes.
Meanwhile, the leaders continued to tie changes to a core organ­
izational value—trust in scientific authority—which may have
offered a sense of continuity in meaning and messaging even as
conditions destabilized. This meaning-making dynamic, sugges­
tive of robustness, would carry over into Phase 3.
Phase 3: In the heat of COVID-19 (March 13–April)
Phase 3 marked the height of unpredictability. On March
11th, the World Health Organization declared COVID-19
a pandemic. Two days later, the Organization implemented
sweeping changes: non-patient-facing staff were told to work
from home, appointments were canceled, and site hours
were reduced. Telehealth visits were quickly rolled out. The
Organization’s health centers were restructured into three
categories—“telehealth,” “well,” and “sick” sites—each with
distinct patient types and protocols. “Telehealth” sites pro­
hibited in-person encounters; “sick” sites served patients
with COVID-like symptoms; and “well sites” were for non-
symptomatic patients requiring in-person care. These changes
triggered a cascade of organizational adjustments: new
teams, schedules, room utilization rules, cleaning procedures,
policies on personal protective equipment, among numerous
other changes.
The formal:
To deal with this volatility, the leaders leaned on pre-existing
strategies. They continued to rely on committee-based coordi­
nation, but powered-up. The “Coronavirus Committee” was
restructured into the “COVID-19 Responder Committee,”
an incident command system composed of multiple “core
committees.” The core committees were deliberately small,
explained the Infectious Disease Officer, each focused on a
specific domain of the response. The committees were mod­
ular, emerging and dissolving in response to shifting public
health guidelines. Together, they functioned like an adaptive
machine, operating a communication and feedback circuit
structured around three steps (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967;
Sarta, Durand, and Vergne 2021). First, changes in one sliver
of the external environment prompted a committee to change
an analogous part of the Organization. Second, those changes
were codified in protocols, which were then delivered through
daily “COVID update” emails, rearticulated during nightly
leadership conference calls, and repeated to staff the following
day. Third, staff feedback was collected, synthesized, and used
to revise both protocols and organizational practices.
Second, they continued to rely on protocols, which rap­
idly proliferated. What began as a single “Symptoms
Screening” protocol soon expanded and split into three: the
“COVID-19 Screening, Testing, and Treatment Protocol,”
“COVID-19 Staff Monitoring and Exclusion Protocol,” and
“Extended use of Personal Protective Equipment Protocol.”
By the end of Phase 3, these protocols alone had been re­
vised 20 times. Meanwhile, new protocols were introduced
to manage emerging needs, including the “Medical Telehealth
Visit during COVID-19 Protocol,” “Door screener Protocol,”
“Work from home Protocol,” “Patient and Staff Resources
Protocol,” and more.
The interpretive:
Amid these changes, the leaders held fast to the same in­
terpretive framework: the Organization was following expert
guidance. As one manager summarized, “who we’re testing,
why we’re testing, what the [leaders] have done pretty well
is follow the Department of Public Health guidelines and use
that as the basis or rationale of changing the protocol.”
At the same time, the leaders grounded their decisions in
the Organization’s value of delivering quality care. In one
email, they wrote, “speed, strategy and doing what is safest
for our patients and staff is going to need to be prioritized
over convenience and predictability” [March 16]. Here,
quality was framed as agility in service of patient safety.

typically used to stabilize the spatial and temporal relationships
between people, tasks, and environments, could no longer keep
pace with the speed and scale of change. The world was moving
too fast for this alignment to occur, leaving the Organization in
disarray. Core committee members, working around the clock
with 8:00 a.m. and 6:00 p.m. daily meetings, reported feelings
of “going round and round in circles” and needing “about 40
hours a day” to cope. Their exhaustion echoed what many
frontline workers were beginning to report: feelings of “change
fatigue” and “information overload.” As one manager put it,
“they did the best they could with the information, but there
were pieces that got missed.”
Meanwhile, as media outlets reported an undersupply of
COVID-19 tests, overwhelmed hospitals, and rising case and
death counts, fear suffused the Organization. Patients with
COVID-like symptoms started entering the “well” sites, which
were meant to remain virus-free. “They’ve told us that they
don’t want to go to the sick site and catch COVID,” said a
manager. Staff also faced “a lot more screaming, yelling, name
calling,” recalled another, as patients resisted newly instituted
protocols like mandatory masking and temperature checks.
Some refused to wear masks on the grounds of personal lib­
erty; others resisted temperature checks, invoking conspiracy
theories about 5G and microchips.
At the same time, providers, now working in unfamiliar
teams, were unable to see their regular patients, which
created delays and compounding stress. They were “mad be­
cause they didn’t want to deal with COVID patients,” said
a manager. All the while, deescalating patients’ anxieties be­
came more difficult with protocols mandating staff to stand
at least six feet apart while speaking through surgical masks,
face shields, and gowns which, amid a national shortage of
personal protective equipment, must be worn until “visibly
soiled or damaged.”
On the other hand, time moved differently for those
working from home. With many routine activities suspended,
some staff, such as the entire dental assistant team, were
furloughed. Other workers began requesting extra tasks to
avoid this fate, including a risk officer who started releasing
yoga videos to help colleagues relax and ensure her work
remained worth her pay. This uneven experience of time—
overload for some, idleness for others—further fragmented
the Organization’s internal rhythm.
Analytical reflection:
Phase 3 marked the height of shifting parameters and temporal
complexity, driven by the scale, simultaneity, and multiplicity of
change. Protocols that typically provide stability—synchronizing
timelines, tasks, roles, and responsibilities—were all in motion
and out of sync. Amid the disorder, leaders turned to familiar
routines: revising and making protocols, committee-based coor­
dination, and appeals to organizational values. Yet the external
pace of change outstripped the Organization’s capacity to ab­
sorb and operationalize it, producing disorder, information over­
load, and change fatigue.
As formal structures strained under pressure, the leaders’
consistent interpretive framework may have offered a dif­
ferent kind of stability. By casting change as aligned with
shared values, specifically following the experts and quality,
they provided an interpretive throughline that may have
helped staff make sense of the upheaval—clarifying what was
happening and why (Besharov and Khurana 2015; Maitlis
and Sonenshein 2010). In this sense, robustness may not
have stemmed from aligning and adapting the Organization’s
structure with its environment, but from the leader’s ability
to sustain meaning, even as control slipped from their grasp.
Phase 4: A drift to familiarity (May–June)
As the endless spring of the pandemic gave way to summer,
the US death toll surpassed 100,000 (CDC 2020), and leaders
began to accept that “they’d just be exposed” because “danger
is the norm.” Amid this backdrop, the Organization began a
gradual return to a semblance of order. It expanded face-to-face
encounters, re-designated telehealth spaces for in-person visits,
and by late June, began moving providers back to their home
sites and allowing some administrative staff to return onsite.
The formal:
The leaders continued to rely on the COVID-19 Responder
Committee and protocols to coordinate organizational
change. But as public health guidelines began to slow down,
so too did internal changes. The tempo of COVID Update
emails subsequently decreased from daily, to three times per
week in April, to two times a week in early June, before being
decommissioned entirely by late June.
This deceleration gave leaders the capacity to communicate
organizational changes with more lead time. So, for the first
time since Phase 2, staff received two weeks’ notice before the
rollout of a new COVID-19 testing procedure. The pilot and
protocol were shared one week prior to implementation, and
the information was reiterated in emails, medical education
lectures, and meetings, both before and after the rollout.
The interpretive:
The leaders continued to stress the importance of reading
protocols, with the consistent refrain: “Please read this in­
formation and the COVID protocol carefully as this is a com­
plex change to our systems” [May 20, 22, 27, 29; June 2,
5, 9, 12]. With greater temporal slack, the leaders reiterated
familiar values—following the experts and providing quality
care—while also returning to their shared commitment to
racial justice. This was most evident in the expansion of
COVID-19 testing. Leaders framed this move as related to
their commitment to follow the experts for the sake of their
patients, writing: “In response to Chicago Department of
Public Health recommendations and the desire to provide the
highest quality care to our patients, the Organization is dras­
tically expanding COVID-19 testing” [May 20].
Yet this time, the change was given the added moral urgency
of racial justice. As the Infectious Disease Officer explained in
an all-staff meeting:
Our communities of color make up our essential workers.

Discussion
How did the street-level leaders respond to COVID-19?
And, returning to the conceptual questions that organize this
paper: did their coping strategies change as predictable in­
stability gave way to unpredictable instability, and can these
strategies become routinized under such conditions? Before
answering these questions, let us recap what transpired over
the phases (Table 1).
In each phase, the leaders employed a combination of
formal tools and interpretive frameworks.
•	 Phase 1: As scientific experts deemed COVID-19 low-
risk, the leaders delivered a protocol and told their staff
that “there is no need to panic,” reminding them to trust
the scientific facts, and to withstand the xenophobia that
fuels panic.
•	 Phase 2: As public health guidelines rapidly evolved,
the leaders formalized their efforts by creating a
“Coronavirus Committee,” tasked to update the pro­
tocol, and told their staff to “read read read” the pro­
tocol for the latest public health updates.
•	 Phase 3: With the declaration of a pandemic, the com­
mittee and protocol system scaled up. The interpretive
framework remained tied to following expert guidance,
grounded in the pursuit of quality care. However, the
pace of change induced feelings of “change fatigue” and
disorder.
•	 Phase 4: As the pace of change decelerated, leaders
continued to rely on protocols and a committee struc­
ture. And with greater temporal slack, the leaders used
an interpretive framework that linked organizational
decisions more explicitly to racial justice.
Across these phases, three elements remained consistent.
1.	 The leaders aligned organizational action with public
health guidelines. The tempo of organizational change
mirrored the pace of updates from public health
authorities: acceleration in Phases 2 and 3; deceleration
in Phase 4.
2.	 The leaders relied on formal structures. They used a com­
mittee structure that reached out and incorporated new
public health guidelines into the Organization. They also
used a communication structure, constituted and enacted
by the production of protocols, to coordinate temporal
and spatial action across different people, departments,
and sites.
3.	 The leaders offered a consistent interpretive framework.
They framed change as necessary to follow expert ad­
vice and ensure quality care. Yet, the content of their
interpretation varied across the phases. In more predict­
able phases (1 and 4), they offered a thicker interpreta­
tion, tying organizational changes to racial justice. But
in more turbulent phases (2 and 3), their interpretation
thinned, perhaps out of necessity. After all, when time
is short and change constant, technical details may have
taken precedence over symbolic interpretation because
interpretations, in and of themselves, cannot produce or­
ganizational change.
Taken together, these elements may be viewed as a type of
routinized coping in Lipsky’s sense—adaptive strategies that
street-level workers use to manage instability, which gradu­
ally solidify into routines that make policy-as-produced—but
with a twist. Here, the strategies were not enacted by frontline
workers but by leaders, and directed at the organizational
level to preserve organizational values and functions. In this
sense, they also qualify as robust responses. With this, we can
return to the conceptual questions guiding this study.
First, coping strategies did not radically shift in response to
unpredictable instability. Contrary to the common view that
crises spurs new and innovative responses (e.g., see Johnson
and Murray 2021), this study shows that the leaders’ response
remained consistent with pre-existing practices—aligned with
scientific guidelines, reliant on familiar formal structures,
and grounded in core values. In fact, when asked to compare
the Organization’s approach to change before and during
COVID-19, the Senior Manager of Process Improvement
replied, “It’s the same, it’s just expedited.”

Second, coping strategies can become routinized even under

may have helped produce policy from the ground up—
shaped not just by what staff did, but by how leaders framed,
communicated, and coordinated those actions.
On robustness
Having shown how routinized coping strategies can turn into
robust responses and persist under conditions of unpredictable
instability, I turn to a final conceptual concern: what made the
leaders’ response robust? Turbulence scholars, after all, are
practically oriented. They seek to identify specific actions that
enable systems to preserve their core functions and values
amid flux. In that spirit, I reflect on how three elements of
the leader’s COVID-19 response may have contributed to its
robustness, and then consider the implications for street-level
theory and practice.
Element 1 involved aligning the Organization’s actions
with evolving public health guidelines. While street-level
scholars often critique adherence to top-down policies as
fostering a “disciplinary regime” that prioritizes rule compli­
ance over client responsiveness (Brodkin 2011; Soss, Fording,
and Schram 2011; Spitzmueller 2016), this case suggests
such adherence may be beneficial under certain conditions.
Amid the profound uncertainty of COVID-19—when, as one
Operations Manager put it, “no one knew what the end game
was”—leaders created order by deferring to a familiar hier­
archical structure: leaders followed public health guidance,
while staff followed their leaders and internal protocols. This
affirms Weick’s (1993) assertion that returning to a familiar
role structure can avert organizational collapse by providing
a feeling of sameness amid disorder.
At the same time, deferring to external authority may have
permitted leaders to both perform and deflect responsibility.
By casting themselves as the mouthpiece, rather than the ven­
triloquist, through whom Public Health Departments spoke,
they could distance themselves from potential blame (Wong
2024). For, when someone else appears to be in control, that
someone else becomes available to take the fall.
Element 2 entailed the use of formal structures—especially
protocols and committees—to coordinate and communicate
rapid changes. Though often caricatured as rigid or “bureau­
cratic,” these structures proved productive. Protocols acted
as flexible instruments for producing action, organizing time
and space, and enabling responsiveness (Janssen and van der
Voort 2020; Moynihan 2008; Pedersen and du Gay 2021).
Yet, even the most adaptable formal structures have
limits. In Phase 3, the pace of external change exceeded the
Organization’s capacity to process and respond. The time re­
quired to conceive, communicate, and implement solutions
could not match the speed of disruption that COVID-19
produced.
Element 3 involved using an interpretive framework to an­
chor core values—quality care, adherence to the science, and
racial justice—as a rationale for organizational changes. While
such values can complicate performance measurement and
obscure the tangible outcomes of street-level work (Rainey
and Jung 2015), their ambiguity proved useful amid unpre­
dictability. Leaders used the same value to justify different
actions, preserving a rich variety of responses while signaling
consistency. For instance, the value of “quality care” justified
both the restriction (Phase 3) and expansion (Phase 4) of ac­
cess to COVID-19 tests. The ambiguity allowed the leaders
to construct messages that were both strict and loose—tied
to a shared value that few could contest, yet adaptable to the
moment (also see the turbulence concept of “polyvalence” in
Ansell, Sørensen, and Torfing 2023, p. 16).
Taken together, these elements may have made the leader’s
response robust. They grounded erratic external shifts in
stable internal values and familiar structures and routines,
which may have provided a form of continuity amid disorder.
This is what Ansell, Boin, and Farjoun 2015 call “dynamic
conservatism.”
Still, while the Organization’s response appears robust
in hindsight, such robustness is not guaranteed. The very
routines that support order in one moment may falter, or even
backfire, in another. Future research might examine when
and why routinized coping strategies sustain robustness, and
when they do not.
Implications for street-level organizations
Practical implications
Three practical implications for street-level organiza­
tions follow. First, leaders should communicate organiza­
tional values through interpretive frameworks that link
everyday practices to core commitments, especially during
crisis. Second, they should embed those values in formal
structures—such as rules, protocols, and hierarchies—to re­
inforce the consequentiality of routine, everyday work. Third,
they must manage change with care: aligning it with existing
values and pacing change to avoid overwhelming the organ­
ization. For, when change is too rapid or disjointed, it risks
fracturing the very robustness it seeks to secure.
Theoretical implications:
Three theoretical implications for street-level organizations
ensue. First, the research extends Lipsky’s framework by
showing how coping strategies can persist as routines not only
in predictably unstable moments but also amid unpredictable
ones. Second, three characteristics often seen as problems in
street-level settings—top-down directives and guidelines, the
use of formal structures, and ambiguous goals (elements 1, 2,
and 3 of the response)—may, under chaotic conditions, facili­
tate robustness. Third, the repeated invocation of shared values
(element 3) suggests that street-level organizations are more
than just Weberian rational-legal structures governed by rules
and hierarchies (Scott and Davis 2015; Weber 1947). They
are also ethical spaces where values are practiced, promoted,
and preserved. This aligns with scholarship advancing a
value-based interpretation of bureaucracy (Du Gay 2000;

Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022). As Møller, Pedersen, and
Pors (2022, p. 151) argue, street-level organizations cultivate
a “bureaucratic ethos” grounded in ethical conduct, profes­
sional norms, and institutional purpose. Extending this view,
this paper suggests that street-level leaders, not just frontline
workers, may act as promoters and protectors of the bureau­
cratic ethos by rearticulating shared values, stewarding col­
lective commitments, and aligning actions with those values.
Concluding reflections
In a world increasingly shaped by conflict and crisis, we need
better frameworks to understand how street-level organiza­
tions respond to unpredictable instability. To this end, I mod­
ified Lipsky’s street-level framework by integrating concepts
from the turbulence literature and asked two questions: Do
street-level workers develop distinct coping strategies under
unpredictable, as opposed to predictable, instability? And can
these strategies become routinized amid ongoing flux?
To explore these questions, I examined how leaders in one
US-based street-level organization responded to the onset of
COVID-19. Despite the extraordinary nature of the pandemic,
their response revealed robustness: patterned adaptations
that preserved core organizational values and functions amid
unpredictable flux. It consisted of three elements: (1) adher­
ence to evolving public health guidelines; (2) reliance on con­
ventional formal structures to communicate and coordinate
change; and (3) consistent invocation of core values through
a shared interpretive framework. Rather than inventing en­
tirely new approaches, the leaders adapted pre-existing ones,
suggesting that routinized coping can persist even amid pro­
found unpredictability.
These findings, however, are context-specific. As an extreme
case, they may only resonate with other cases under particular
conditions: during a time marked by exceptional unpredicta­
bility; in a liberal capitalist democracy where service delivery
is devolved to private organizations; and in a healthcare set­
ting where following evidence-based policies and protocols
is not only tied to one’s professional identity but also the
patient’s fate. Yet extreme cases, as Flyvbjerg (2006, p. 229)
reminds us, “often reveal more information because they acti­
vate more actors and more basic mechanisms.”
Beyond moments of crisis, this case study offers insights.
It reminds us that street-level organizations often serve as
institutional carriers of society’s highest ideals—promoting
welfare, protecting the vulnerable, enacting justice. That
work remains vital, especially as democratic institutions face
mounting threats: from erratic leadership and democratic
erosion to rising nationalism and polarization. Against this
backdrop, this study reminds us that robustness may lie not in
novelty, but in the careful use of familiar tools and practices,
especially those anchored in shared values. By promoting,
protecting, and reaffirming those values, street-level leaders
can offer themselves and their staff a source of meaning and
stability amid disorder. Or, more simply, help them carry on
through the daily grind of ordinary challenges.

ABSTRACT
Do no harm (DNH) represents a foundational moral framework for public administration ethics. Prohibitive moral principles,
rather than aspirational values, provide a more coherent and operational basis for ethical public service—even when confronting
demands for proactive intervention. Bernard Gert's theory of common morality reveals how rationality and impartiality under-
pin moral rules designed to prevent fundamental harms, while acknowledging challenges of defining and ranking competing
harms. This approach mirrors the U.S. Constitution and Bill of Rights, which constrain governmental action to protect individual
rights. We further develop the DNH framework by examining free speech issues in higher education and extend its relevance to
other controversial policy settings through a comparative ethical analysis that illustrates how institutions can navigate between
harmful action and harmful inaction. DNH offers morally grounded guidance for ethical decision-­making amid democratic
tensions and political pressures.
Public administration as a field has long struggled to articulate
a coherent ethical foundation capable of guiding bureaucratic
decision-­making in pluralistic democracies (Cooper  2004).
Dominant approaches—deontology, utilitarianism, and virtue
ethics—offer insights, but none provides a fully satisfying ac-
count of how public servants should navigate morally contested,
high-­stakes decisions within institutions charged with safeguard-
ing both democratic values and legal neutrality (Cooper 1991;
Garofalo and Gueras  1999; Pops  1999). Deontological frame-
works emphasize duties but often collapse into legal formal-
ism; utilitarianism encourages pragmatic benefit-­maximization
but lacks safeguards against rights violations; and virtue ethics
appeals to character and civic ideals without resolving con-
flicts among them (Chandler 1994; Hart 1994; Denhardt 1991).
Without a stable moral anchor, ethical codes tend to lean heav-
ily on aspirational values, such as “promoting the public good,”
without offering operational guidance for resolving ethical
tensions in practice (Frederickson and Ghere 2005; Lewis and
Gilman 2012; cf. Svara 2015).
Most existing moral frameworks are oriented toward “doing
good”—promoting justice, fairness, inclusion, or service—rather
than prioritizing the avoidance of morally unacceptable harms.
This imbalance is problematic in governance systems where
conflicting values routinely collide. The lack of a prohibitive eth-
ical structure leaves public administrators without a principled
method for ranking competing values or constraining institutional
overreach. This theoretical gap becomes acute when administra-
tors face dilemmas where any action might produce harm, and the
fundamental moral question is which harm must be avoided. In
such crises of institutional integrity, pragmatic responsiveness can
undermine core civic protections (Seibel 2020). Current theoreti-
cal treatments in public administration lack a universal, morality-­
based framework grounded in rationality, impartiality, and
prohibitive moral rules rather than expansive value promotion.
We propose a foundational ethic of public administration that is
grounded in a do no harm (DNH) approach. We argue that an
emphasis on prohibitive moral rules, derived from universally

1   |   Ethical Theory in Public Administration
The existing research on public administration ethics empha-
sizes the practical application of moral principles to specific is-
sues and decisions faced by public servants at the managerial
level. This research is often contextual, applied, and procedural.
In contrast, a foundational ethic of public administration refers
to a normative theory or philosophical framework that under-
girds the entire purpose and identity of the field. The debate over
the moral foundation of public administration continues and
has drawn upon most of the major traditional ethical theories
(Garofalo and Gueras 1999). These applications tend to be lay-
ered and fragmentary, resulting in a patchwork of theories, each
contributing value in some way. Our aim is not to accommodate
or synthesize ethical approaches but to make a preliminary case
for one particular perspective: a baseline ethic that precedes all
other considerations. Before doing so, we briefly review the tra-
ditional candidates for a foundational or normative ethic of pub-
lic administration: virtue ethics, utilitarianism, and deontology.
1.1   |   Virtue Ethics
The publication of Alisdair MacIntyre's landmark After Virtue
(MacIntyre 1981) sparked renewed interest in Aristotelian virtue
theory. Inspired by MacIntyre, Cooper's (1991) application to pub-
lic administration is an early and significant contribution. Cooper
identifies “self-­interest rightly understood” (151) as the paramount
virtue of citizenship. And since public administrators are citizens
first, this becomes the chief virtue of public servants as well. With
a broader brush, Hart (1994) considers the cardinal virtues of pru-
dence, justice, fortitude, and benevolence and their applicability
to the lives of public servants (see also Hart 1984; Frederickson
and Hart  1985; Dobel  1998). Such contributions make a com-
pelling case for virtue ethics as the moral foundation of public
administration. They tend to debate which set of virtues is most
ideal for street-­level bureaucrats. For example, Denhardt (1991,
109) claims that “all three imperatives—honor, benevolence, and
justice—make up the moral foundation of public administration.”
Since its genesis in the 1980s and early 1990s, research on
virtue ethics in public administration has continued to grow
and expand. Productive streams of research have been estab-
lished in areas such as public service motivation (Perry and
Wise 1990; Tang et al. 2024), public service values (Jørgensen
and Bozeman  2007; Witesman and Walters  2014), and public
service ethos (Carr 1999; Rayner et al. 2011), among others (see
also Witesman et al. 2024 for a clearer analysis of similarities
and distinctions). As helpful as these research streams can be at
the individual level, none provides ideal theoretical grounding
to the overall endeavor of public administration.
1.2   |   Utilitarian Ethics
Utilitarianism in public administration has been effectively ap-
plied at the policy and institutional levels. At the policy level, the
fundamental precept of the greatest good for the greatest num-
ber (among broader utilitarian considerations) provides an im-
portant perspective on the legislative and public policy process
(see examples by Goodin 1995, 1998 and Pattanaik 2009). At the
institutional level, scholarship on institutional consequentialism
has provided insights into the effective structure of institutions
and a focus on the aggregate and long-­term outcomes for society,
at least as far as they promote public value, justice, and demo-
cratic responsiveness (Ostrom 2008; Warren 2001; Miller 2010;
Miklós and Tanyi 2025).
Applications of utilitarian ethics to the practice of public admin-
istration are less common. Chandler (1994) argues that utilitar-
ian logic is particularly well-­suited to pragmatically dealing with
the moral pluralism needs of public administrators. In addition,
Sheeran (1993) acknowledges the value of utilitarianism. He sug-
gests that a utilitarian approach “may be the best and easiest to de-
termine what is right or wrong,” as a deontological ethic generates
too many rules and regulations for a public administrator to keep
in mind (152). Neither of these writers makes a foundational claim.
Their argument is simply that for day-­to-­day decision-­making, a
utilitarian approach seems more pragmatic and applicable.
The question is whether the moral foundation of public adminis-
tration consists of, or is inspired by, utilitarian ethics. Of course,
every nation seeks to secure a version of the “general welfare”
for its citizens. This aim is utilitarian, but it supplies only the
universal aspirations, not a solid groundwork for moral think-
ing. For that, deontology holds more promise.
Summary
•	 The practice of public administration lacks cohesive
ethical grounding. A do no harm (DNH) approach as a
foundational ethic begins by identifying and ranking
potential harms as opposed to promoting moral ideals.
•	 Some actions (e.g., deception, exclusion, coercion) are
always impermissible; others (e.g., compassion) may
be virtuous but are not ethically required.
•	 Administrators can use Gert's criteria to assess
whether policies are rational, acceptable to any rea-
sonable person, and are applied fairly across identity
and ideological lines.
•	 Clear, impartial prohibitions help prevent overreach
and selective enforcement, reduce perceptions of bias,
and strengthen institutional trust.

1.3   |   Deontological Ethics
Whereas utilitarianism tends to focus on outcomes, deontology,
in the context of public administration, focuses more on duty,
rules, and accountability. Some of the most compelling schol-
arship on a foundational ethic comes from this perspective. For
example, Rohr's (1989, 1998) research centers on the moral duty
of administrators to uphold the regime values enshrined in the
U.S. Constitution and to treat fidelity to those principles as a
binding ethical obligation rather than a matter of consequential-
ist calculation.
It is not a stretch to associate rules and regulations with gov-
ernment. This often simplistic or even cynical view does not do
justice to the moral safety produced through rule observance.
Pops (1999, 155) concludes that “the everyday world of the prac-
ticing public administrator is still built around the principles,
precepts, and regulations that deontological ethics encourage
and sometimes prescribe.” He argues that, despite its many
shortcomings, the focus on promoting unambiguous rules is
central to curbing public corruption. In what follows, we es-
tablish the necessity of certain rules in the moral core of public
administration.
2   |   The Moral Philosophy of “Do No Harm”
The duty not to harm is a hallmark of deontology. In every
prominent deontological theory, the duty of non-­harm has a
fundamental priority. This priority is central to Kant's (1964)
moral theory, including his designation of negative, or pro-
hibitive, duties as “narrow” or “perfect” in contrast to his
designation of positive duties (such as beneficence) as “wide”
or “imperfect ones.” The wide duties are less strict and allow
greater discretion. We find a similar asymmetry in the de-
ontological intuitionism of Ross (2002, 22), who claims that
“the duty of non-­maleficence [non-­harm] is … prima facie
more binding” than the duty of beneficence. He continues,
“We should not in general consider it justifiable to kill one
person in order to keep another alive, or to steal from one
in order to give alms to another” (22). This asymmetry be-
tween negative and positive duties is one of the primary ways
that deontologists distinguish their views from utilitarian-­
consequentialists. One of the more recent and well-­developed
deontological approaches to DNH is found in the scholarship
of Gert, who provides an extensive account of what he calls
“common morality.” In building a model of DNH as a foun-
dational ethic of public administration, we employ a simpli-
fied version of Gert's approach to develop a moral framework
that we believe is best suited to the context of public adminis-
tration. In what follows, we outline the basic tenets of Gert's
theory of common morality, which include rationality, impar-
tiality, and moral rules and ideals.
2.1   |   Rationality
An a priori assumption of common morality is rationality. Moral
actions are those that any rational person can accept. An ac-
tion is rational if it is not irrational. An action is irrational if it
brings harm to oneself or those for whom one is concerned. As
Gert (2005, 30, 31) states, “A person correctly appraises an action
as irrational when she correctly believes (1) it will cause, or sig-
nificantly increase the probability of, the agent suffering (avoid-
able) death, pain, disability, loss of freedom, or loss of pleasure,
and (2) there is no objectively adequate reason for the action.”
Objectively adequate reasons are justifiable to all rational persons
and make otherwise irrational actions rational. For example, al-
leviating an arguably greater long-­term harm is an objectively ad-
equate reason to allow the harm associated with surgery. In the
absence of adequate reasons, one would be considered irrational
for pursuing harm to oneself or those for whom one is concerned
(Gert 2004). While Gert begins from an egocentric perspective, his
analysis steadily leaves egoism behind. The key to moving beyond
the concern for oneself (and those for whom one is concerned)
comes in connecting rationality/irrationality to impartiality.
2.2   |   Impartiality
Impartiality is critical because it is not merely the absence of
bias but is a requirement to apply moral rules without privileg-
ing oneself or any particular individual except in unique and
justified circumstances. Moral rules that would be rational for
all persons to accept require impartiality. One must evaluate
moral rules not from a private or egocentric standpoint but as
a rational person committed to public moral reasoning. This
evaluation should be central to any ethic of public administra-
tion. Gert explicitly rejects any form of moral reasoning that
privileges oneself or one's close associates. He argues that “un-
less one has an impartial concern for all persons, a rational
person cannot advocate that her egocentric attitude be part of
a public system that applies to all rational persons. Each ratio-
nal person will demand that the attitude be modified at least
so as to require impartial obedience with regard to her and
those for whom she cares” (Gert 2005, 171). In other words, a
public system of morality necessarily entails universalizabil-
ity: what is permissible or obligatory for one must be so for all,
under similar circumstances. Furthermore, Gert links impar-
tiality to the concept of mutual moral expectations: “A rational
person also knows that all other rational persons want her and
her friends to obey the rules. Thus, insofar as the attitude ad-
opted must be one that it would not be irrational for all other
rational agents to accept, she must advocate that the rules be
obeyed by everyone, including herself and her friends” (171).
In short, impartiality is ensuring that moral norms are not
contingent on individual preferences or social location but
grounded in principles that all rational persons can reason-
ably be expected to accept and obey.
2.3   |   Moral Rules and Moral Ideals
A third essential consideration of common morality, especially
as it relates to public administration, is the distinction between
moral rules and moral ideals. Moral rules are a natural exten-
sion of impartiality. They are universal prohibitions or require-
ments that govern conduct affecting others. They are binding
on all rational agents and form the minimum standard of moral
behavior in a public moral system. The intent is to prevent be-
haviors that would cause unjustified harm. Gert identifies the
following moral rules:

•	 Do not kill.
•	 Do not cause pain.
•	 Do not disable.
•	 Do not deprive of freedom.
•	 Do not deprive of pleasure.
There are no “positive” prescriptions here—these are “negative”
duties. Gert adds a second set of moral rules to account for our
moral intuitions regarding behaviors that decrease the likeli-
hood that others will suffer genuine harms/evils:
•	 Do not deceive.
•	 Keep (do not break) promises.
•	 Do not cheat.
•	 Obey the law (do not steal, etc.).
•	 Do your duty.
Moral rules are prohibitive in nature and are the bedrock
of moral accountability in society. However, these rules are
not inviolable. In most cases, violations of these moral rules
are immoral, but there are justified exceptions. For example,
Gert (2004, 175) claims that “all people are to obey the rule ‘Do
not kill’ regarding everyone, except when a fully informed, ra-
tional person can publicly allow the violation.” Police officers
are justified in using deadly force to stop a criminal only when
rational people would agree that the action is a morally justified
violation of the principles outlined above.
Unlike moral rules, moral ideals are aspirational. Moral ideals
are not required in the same sense as moral rules; it is clearly
good to perform them, but not morally wrong to omit them.
Moral ideals reflect moral excellence rather than minimum
moral decency. Not all rational persons are morally required
to live up to them; rather, moral ideals represent aspirations
for moral growth. Moral ideals are essential for a flourishing
moral community because they promote virtues and positive
moral behaviors, but they do so without coercion or obliga-
tion. We do not suggest that public administrators bracket
or suppress their identities, experiences, or moral intuitions.
Rather, the DNH framework calls for these perspectives to be
filtered through a process of rational and impartial justifica-
tion. Personal positionality can inform ethical sensitivity, but
public justification must rest on principles all rational persons
can accept.
Moral rules are the baseline and principal focus of a DNH
approach. They are prohibitive by nature and are intended
to minimize harm. They provide a minimum threshold of
behavior and are required. Moral ideals, on the other hand,
consist of aspirational behaviors but are voluntary; failure to
live up to ideals does not invite condemnation or punishment.
Thus, actions should focus on prohibitions, and anything
else should be aspirational (Hennessey and Gert Hennessey
Jr. and Gert  1985). The real value of this approach is that it
avoids moral conflation by distinguishing what is morally re-
quired from what it morally admirable. So, using Gert's theory
of common morality as a foundation, we argue that there are
three major considerations to a DNH approach: rationality,
impartiality, and moral rules and ideals. The rest of the article
is devoted to applying this approach to the context of public
administration and arguing that it can serve as a robust and
pragmatic foundational ethic.
3   |   DNH as a Normative Foundation in Public
Administration
A DNH approach is a compelling possibility as a foundational
ethic of public administration. Ideally, such an ethic should
align with a country's founding documents or legislative man-
dates. The founding documents in the United States, including
the Constitution and the Bill of Rights, are reasonably repre-
sentative for the purposes of our discussion. We argue that one
reason for their enduring effectiveness is their focus on negative
duties or moral rules. Although our discussion focuses on U.S.
founding documents, we believe the DNH approach is equally
applicable in global contexts.
The U.S. Constitution is more than a document of political
founding values: it is a document whose roots are deeply em-
bedded in morality itself (Dworkin 1997). The Bill of Rights, the
ethical bedrock for public servants, is a collection of moral im-
peratives, all of which are substantive prohibitions. Take one of
the most famous—the 1st Amendment: “Congress shall make
no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting
the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or
of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and
to petition the government for a redress of grievances.” This is a
good example of how, in ethics, much is allowed but little is re-
quired, except by way of prohibition. This amendment gives sub-
stance to Gert's fourth moral rule, “Do not deprive of freedom,”
by specifying five freedoms that Congress prohibits suppressing.
These freedoms are moral rules based in prohibitions. To at-
tempt a positive listing (moral ideals) of all a free press should
be allowed to do, for example, would be cumbersome. It is far
easier to say, “Do not interfere with the press.” Of course, even
the press faces moral limitations. The “clear and present dan-
ger” doctrine is a line that it cannot cross. But this just means
that one moral rule ends where another begins. If a free press
were to threaten to incite riots and bring about loss of life, the
“clear and present danger” is a threat to the moral rule of not
killing or causing death. It is rational to expect the preven-
tion of some genuine harms to be superseded by the threat of
even greater harms. This is the case with all the constitutional
amendments.
The constitutional amendments reflect the broader principles
of a DNH approach: not only prohibitive in nature but also
grounded in rationality and impartiality. See Table 1 for an illus-
tration of these principles applied to several of the amendments.
All the harms, either directly or indirectly, align clearly with
those articulated by Gert. By contrast, if the Bill of Rights had
been informed by utilitarianism, it would look quite different.
For example, it would indicate opportunities for the govern-
ment to promote the greatest good for the greatest number while
not considering individual rights as directly. A DNH approach

We might say that the Bill of Rights and the other amendments
to the Constitution “legislate morality”—at least as morality is
defined by a DNH ethic. They do not go beyond that. That is why
an amendment against burning the American flag, for example,
would seem out of place: while some might find it offensive, it
violates none of Gert's moral rules. Similarly, it is unclear how
the 18th Amendment (prohibition) prevents a harm; this is an ex-
ample of the “legislating of morality” gone too far, and it was re-
pealed in the 21st Amendment. A DNH ethic spells out the moral
limits of government and builds reasonably clear fences around
the legislation of morality. Moral government is, in a sense, gov-
ernment that prevents harm. It might also redress or alleviate
harms, which would take moral priority over the provision of
goods and services. A police force is on a sounder moral foot-
ing than public museums and sports facilities. As Gert reminds
us, when moral prohibitions are turned on their heads and ex-
pressed as moral ideals, the moral ideals get confused with other
ideals, such as utilitarian, religious, or professional ideals, and
the moral foundation of public service risks becoming obscured.
4   |   A DNH Approach: An Illustrative Case
A prohibitive ethical framework grounded in DNH offers more
consistent guidance by asking not “what good should be pro-
moted?” but rather “what harm must be prevented?” This
framework provides administrators with practical tools for ethi-
cal decision-­making: rationality requires identifying objectively
harmful outcomes (death, pain, disability, loss of freedom, loss
of pleasure) that any reasonable person would seek to avoid; im-
partiality demands applying moral rules consistently regardless
of stakeholder identity or ideological position; and the distinc-
tion between moral rules and context-­dependent moral ideals
helps administrators prioritize competing claims. When admin-
istrators fail to apply these principles systematically—treating
subjective offense as equivalent to objective harm or applying
different standards based on political considerations—institu-
tional integrity suffers, and deeper harms often result.
Public administrators increasingly face complex ethical dilem-
mas amid growing political polarization, especially where con-
stitutional protections collide with competing values. Campus
free speech controversies illustrate the DNH framework in

4.1   |   Free Speech and First Amendment
Protections
Public administrators have struggled to navigate the increas-
ingly volatile landscape of free expression controversies, re-
vealing deepening institutional conflict and a troubling pattern
of administrative overreach. From campus protests over the
Israeli–Palestinian conflict to disputes about race, gender, and
political partisanship, administrators have faced mounting pres-
sure to prioritize emotional safety, reputational risk, or institu-
tional harmony over constitutional obligations. More than 1000
students and student groups were targeted or punished for pro-
tected expression between 2020 and 2024, with 203 incidents in
2023 alone, spanning ideological lines and driven increasingly
by administrative, rather than peer, action (Dougherty  2025).
Moral ideals—focused on promoting “safety,” “inclusion,” or
“campus harmony”—often lead administrators to abandon
constitutional principles when political pressure mounts. As
Mill  (1974, 76) warned, suppressing unpopular opinions does
not merely silence dissenters but impoverishes public reason:
“The peculiar evil of silencing the expression of an opinion is
that it is robbing the human race. … If the opinion is right, they
are deprived of the opportunity of exchanging error for truth.”
The pattern is troubling: institutions that should serve as guard-
ians of fundamental freedoms instead become venues where
those freedoms are systematically eroded through reactive, con-
textualized decision-­making—treating disagreement as harm
and moral restraint as expendable.
The constitutional foundation explored earlier becomes partic-
ularly relevant here. The 1st Amendment's prohibitive struc-
ture—“Congress shall make no law”—exemplifies DNH in
institutional design. However, when administrators lack system-
atic moral frameworks for implementing these constitutional
constraints, they often drift toward case-­by-­case determinations
that prioritize immediate political concerns over principled
consistency. This drift reflects the problem the DNH frame-
work addresses: without universally recognizable moral rules
to guide institutional action, organizations default to subjective
value judgments that lack rational justification and impartial
application. Alternative ethical approaches prove inadequate
in this context—utilitarian frameworks could possibly justify
restricting minority viewpoints to maximize aggregate comfort
(Baron 1995), while virtue-­based appeals to institutional char-
acter provide no clear guidance for ranking competing claims.
A DNH framework provides the systematic approach these situ-
ations require. Applying the rationality component reveals how
reactive governance systematically violates fundamental moral
rules. Ad hoc policy reversals and selective enforcement violate
the moral rule “Do not deprive of freedom.” No reasonable per-
son could rationally endorse a system where expression rights
depend on shifting political winds, where protest rules change
without notice based on content, or where identical behaviors
receive different treatment based on viewpoint. Rationality re-
quires that restrictions on speech be justified by clear, objective
harms such as violence, property damage, or major disruption.
Concerns about controversy, reputation, or stakeholder displea-
sure are less valid as reasons for restriction. When administra-
tors escalate enforcement responses and increase the probability
of serious harm without adequate moral justification, they risk
violating additional moral rules (“Do not cause pain” and “Do
not disable”).
Impartiality proves equally crucial for maintaining institu-
tional legitimacy. Constitutional law's viewpoint neutrality
requirement reflects the deeper moral truth that institutions
cannot rationally advocate for standards they would not ac-
cept if applied universally. Yet administrators frequently
succumb to pressure for content-­based restrictions, treating
identical behaviors differently based on political perspective,
stakeholder identity, or temporal convenience. Calls for cen-
sorship from across the political spectrum make impartial
application both necessary and feasible. When institutions
permit some protests while restricting others based on polit-
ical content rather than objective behavioral standards, they
violate the impartial consistency that legitimates their author-
ity. This violation often generates precisely the institutional
conflicts administrators sought to avoid, as affected parties
correctly perceive discriminatory treatment.
The distinction between moral rules and moral ideals provides
additional and essential clarity for prioritizing competing in-
stitutional values. The prohibition against depriving citizens
of freedom without adequate justification functions as a moral
rule—a universal constraint that cannot be overridden by aspi-
rational goals. Community support, emotional care, and insti-
tutional harmony represent highly valuable objectives, but they
function as moral ideals rather than binding constraints that
can justify violating fundamental prohibitions. This framework
acknowledges that certain forms of expression may cause genu-
ine distress, particularly to vulnerable populations. However, it
distinguishes institutional responses that provide support and
facilitate counter-­speech from those that suppress expression.
The reasonable administrator—operating within moral con-
straints while exercising practical judgment—would recognize
that subjective offense and emotional distress, while real expe-
riences deserving institutional response, do not justify violating
the foundational moral rule against depriving citizens of funda-
mental freedoms unless they clearly rise to the level of objective,
demonstrable harm. We acknowledge that public disagreement
often arises around what counts as a harm. The DNH frame-
work anchors ethical judgments in harms that are objectively
recognizable—those that diminish life, liberty, bodily integrity,
or autonomy—rather than treating subjective discomfort or of-
fense as sufficient grounds for prohibition.
A DNH approach ensures consistency through content-­neutral
policies established in advance, grounded in objective behavioral

crowd control based on safety needs rather than content, and
creating transparent processes demonstrating rational justi-

distinguish between preventing genuine harms and managing

such as requiring that you benefit some disadvantaged group.”
The DNH framework extends across contemporary challenges.
Table 2 shows how DNH reframes ethical reasoning, shifting
focus toward prohibitive harms, rational justifications, and im-

transforms decision-­making from value maximization to harm
prevention. In each domain, the framework identifies specific
deprivations violating moral rules, whether silencing speech,
excluding individuals, or using coercive force. The rational jus-
tification test asks whether any reasonable person would accept
such treatment if roles were reversed. This universalizability
principle anchors administrative action in moral rules rather
than political expedience. Notably, DNH does not prohibit pur-
suing positive goals like inclusion, but it requires respecting
fundamental boundaries. By asking first what harms must be
prevented rather than what goods should be promoted, admin-
istrators achieve consistency rather than deepening divisions
through selective enforcement.
5   |   Discussion, Implications, and Future Research
We revive Cooper's (2004) call to address the normative founda-
tions of public administration ethics as one of the “big questions”
facing the discipline. Current social and political challenges
make this question even more pressing. We offer DNH as a foun-
dational ethical framework grounded in Gert's theory of com-
mon morality, distinguishing between prohibitive moral rules
and aspirational ideals. While the field has long entertained
multiple ethical traditions, it has lacked a coherent method for
resolving value conflicts under political volatility. DNH ad-
dresses this need by providing an impartial, rational framework
prioritizing harm prevention as the minimum threshold for
moral legitimacy.
Moral disagreement need not undermine ethical coherence. A
shared framework can accommodate principled controversy
when rational persons agree on core moral rules while reaching
different conclusions about specific violations (Gert 2004, 28).
As Gert  (2004, 57) explains, “Common morality differs from
most philosophical descriptions of morality in this respect, be-
cause it holds that equally informed, impartial, rational persons
sometimes can disagree about whether a violation of a moral
rule should be allowed.” This distinction between consensus on
rules and disagreement in application proves essential in public
administration, where decisions involve competing interpre-
tations of harm. The presence of “a relatively small number of
unresolvable disagreements” should not overshadow the “over-
whelmingly greater agreement in moral judgments” undergird-
ing common morality (Gert 2005, 311). DNH's strength lies in
grounding dissent within shared principles of harm prevention,
rationality, and impartiality.
Our contribution clarifies that aspirational goals remain moral
ideals rather than binding obligations. DNH offers no retreat
from justice but keeps its disciplined pursuit within legitimate
constraints. By grounding ethics in universally accessible prin-
ciples, DNH helps avoid ethical overreach by providing stability
across political shifts while preserving institutional integrity.
DNH's principles reflect universal moral considerations. The
Bill of Rights' prohibitive focus demonstrates how democratic
institutions embody these principles. Gert's moral rules reflect
harms any rational person would avoid. DNH provides a stabiliz-
ing heuristic focusing on empirically demonstrable, publicly jus-
tifiable harms while acknowledging marginalized experiences.
The convergence with several major research streams validates
the DNH framework. Administrative burden scholarship demon-
strates how ostensibly neutral procedures impose disproportion-
ate costs on vulnerable populations, often through complexity,
compliance barriers, and discretionary enforcement (Herd and
Moynihan 2018, 2020). DNH offers normative criteria for when
such burdens cross the threshold into moral harm. Whereas bur-
den research describes patterns of exclusion, DNH prescribes
boundaries for morally legitimate administrative action, regard-
less of efficiency or intent. Not all burdens are unethical—some
prevent greater injuries—but DNH clarifies which forms of ex-
clusion, delay, or suffering violate prohibitive moral rules.
Emerging research on administrative harm, particularly
in healthcare, reinforces this urgency. Eighty-­one percent
of institutional leaders acknowledge participating in deci-
sions with “adverse consequences” (as reported by Burden
et al. 2024, 1015). In the broader context of public program
implementation, routine procedures may likewise evolve into
harm-­delivery systems when frontline workers internalize
“dispositions of enforcement” (McGann and Ball 2024, 443).
These recent studies reveal cascading effects: citizens suffer
exclusion, staff experience moral injury, and leaders com-
promise ethical commitments under performance pressure.
Unrecognized harm extends across policy and service areas,
in part because administrative systems can mask harmful
practices by shielding them through dehumanization and
moral inversion, allowing them to unfold without moral rec-
ognition (Adams and Balfour 1998). Studies of organizational
misconduct (Greve et  al.  2010; Lange and Washburn  2012)
and moral injury (Shay 2014) reveal how harm becomes nor-
malized through institutional routines, incentives, and ethi-
cal blind spots. These studies expose a widespread failure to
identify when administrative action crosses into moral imper-
missibility. DNH addresses this blind spot directly, offering
a principled, rule-­based framework rooted in rationality and
impartiality. It not only diagnoses institutional harm but de-
fines its moral boundaries, transforming descriptive findings
into actionable ethical guidance.

5.1   |   Implications for Practice
DNH requires shifting from benefit maximization to moral
risk assessment. Policy design must ask the following ques-
tions: Does this cause unjustified harm? Who bears burdens?
Are moral rules violated for aspirational goals? This approach
embeds harm-­prevention into deliberation and adjudication.
Organizations need systematic harm-­prevention beyond
cost–benefit analysis, including moral audits assessing rule
violations. Research reveals how “rigid rule following” en-
ables harm (McGann and Ball  2024; Tummers et  al.  2015).
Institutions need protocols examining the following ques-
tions: Do policies violate prohibitive rules? How do we protect
vulnerable populations? How might implementation am-
plify harms?
Professional education demands a comprehensive revision to in-
corporate DNH principles throughout curricula. Cooper (2012)
emphasizes that ethics cannot remain peripheral but must per-
meate administrative roles. Current programs typically focus
on procedural compliance or abstract values without equipping
practitioners to navigate conflicts between competing goods.
DNH training would develop capabilities to identify moral rules,
differentiate them from ideals, and apply principled reasoning
consistently. This is critically important in contexts where dis-
cretionary authority remains high and political expectations
shift rapidly. Hiring practices may prioritize candidates who
demonstrate moral reasoning over technical expertise alone
by articulating why certain actions remain impermissible re-
gardless of pressure. Decision-­making protocols would require
explicit harm assessments: identifying whose freedoms are
at stake, which populations bear disproportionate burdens,
and whether proposed benefits justify risking fundamental
violations.
Accountability mechanisms and performance measurement re-
quire fundamental reconceptualization. Conventional metrics
often obscure or ignore administrative harms, focusing instead
on outputs and efficiency. Organizations can evaluate whether
they genuinely protect individuals from coercive burdens, ar-
bitrary exclusions, and inconsistent rule application (Burden
et al. 2024). Such tools would monitor disparate treatment pat-
terns, assess institutional responsiveness to substantiated ver-
sus speculative harms, and measure enforcement consistency
across time and populations.

Our analysis of free speech and DEI implementation demon-

merit-­based systems—pursuing equity via harm prevention
rather than benefit redistribution.

distributions across populations, and frequencies of moral
rule violations. Organizations appear to “lack mechanisms
for identification, measurement, and feedback” (Burden
et al. 2024, 1017–1018), which underscores the need for em-
pirical tools that make harm visible and actionable. A DNH
metric could assess, for example, institutional capacity for
harm prevention, administrator orientation to prohibitive
ethics, organizational mechanisms that mask or reveal harm,
administrative burden levels across programs, rule consis-
tency over time, and long-­term outcomes of principled versus
reactive decision-­making (Greve et  al.  2010; Morrison and
Milliken 2000; Shay 2014). These tools would be particularly
valuable in evaluating outsourced or digitized services, where
harms are easily hidden or depersonalized, and in contexts
like contracting out public services, where harm is easily ex-
ternalized, and oversight fragmented. DNH's relationship to
distributive justice warrants longitudinal study. While DNH
promotes equity by preventing exclusion and structural harm,
comparative research could clarify whether harm prevention
functions primarily as a procedural constraint or as a pathway
to substantive justice. Such research should assess both pro-
cess integrity and outcome equity across populations.
Second, research should examine how DNH operates in spe-
cific policy domains with complex trade-­offs and risk exposure.
Healthcare administration offers a natural laboratory for DNH
principles, given medicine's longstanding commitment to “do
no harm.” The need to “more fully understand the true burden
of harm, the public health challenge it represents, and the im-
pacts of our interventions to reduce it” has been emphasized in
recent scholarship (Higgins et al. 2024, 800). Their commen-
tary focuses on clinical care but applies more broadly: harm
data are often fragmented, lagging, or shaped by institutional
reporting structures that obscure rather than illuminate pat-
terns of injury. The same limitations exist in public adminis-
tration, where harms frequently go unmeasured, whether in
the form of unjustified exclusions, burdensome compliance
regimes, or organizational practices that degrade trust or well-­
being. The call for timely, benchmarkable, and context-­sensitive
harm metrics echoes across public institutions, where decisions
made under uncertainty often produce diffuse or hidden inju-
ries. A DNH framework supplies the ethical architecture for
such measurement by distinguishing morally relevant harms
from mere inefficiencies or disagreements and by requiring
consistent standards of justification. Questions regarding how
administrative systems handle similar dilemmas could illu-
minate transferable practices across domains. Environmental
regulation, emergency management, social welfare systems,
public housing, and immigration enforcement present rich
cases to explore. DNH offers a framework not only for moral
evaluation but for organizational learning and reform.
Third, comparative studies should assess cross-­national applica-
bility. Many democracies feature prohibitive constraints through
constitutional charters, judicial review, or human rights instru-
ments. Examining how different systems operationalize harm
prevention and whether certain designs more effectively guard
against administrative overreach can yield valuable insights for
institutional reform.
Finally, understanding organizational dynamics that enable
moral masking, institutional complicity, and systematic silence
is critical for implementation success. Literature on organiza-
tional misconduct (Clinard and Yeager 1980; Thompson 1987)
and administrative evil (Farmer 2005) reveals how harm be-
comes routinized when efficiency logics and performance pres-
sures displace ethical considerations. DNH provides theoretical
resistance to these tendencies by anchoring accountability in
what administrators must categorically reject, regardless of or-
ganizational incentives or political inducements. Thus, a DNH
approach promotes exploring how organizations can foster cul-
tures that bring harm recognition to the surface instead of hid-
ing it, establishing safe ways for reporting ethical issues, and
rewarding moral bravery over mere compliance with silence.
As polarization deepens and institutional trust deteriorates,
public administration must safeguard its moral foundations
while maintaining legitimacy across ideological divides.
Harm often emerges not from individual malice but from sys-
temic features, what Adams and Balfour (1998) call the “ba-
nality of administrative evil.” Two decades ago, Cooper (2004)
called for revisiting the moral foundations of public adminis-
tration. We extend that call by arguing that the constitutional
structure of bureaucracy demands renewed scrutiny, focusing
not only on what public servants should pursue, but also on
what they must never permit. In a time of democratic fragility,
institutional strain, and intensifying polarization, DNH offers
a rare combination of ethical clarity and administrative real-
ism. It invites a disciplined moral governance, a commitment
to the quiet virtue of restraint in service to the dignity and
safety of those most easily harmed.
6   |   Conclusion
A common morality, strengthened by a constitutional architec-
ture, reveals that our democratic institutions already embody
the moral framework that public administration has needed.
The Bill of Rights does not promote aspirational goods—it
prohibits fundamental harms. When administrators grasp
that their core obligation is preventing harm rather than max-
imizing contested values, the entire ethical landscape shifts.
Suddenly, the impossible choices that paralyze institutions
(between inclusion and free expression, safety and liberty) be-
come navigable through clear moral priorities. While grounded
in United States constitutional analysis, these principles tran-
scend any single system. The harms we have applied—death,
pain, disability, loss of freedom, deception—are universally
recognized. Democratic systems worldwide employ similar
prohibitive architectures through charters, judicial review, and
human rights instruments. The DNH framework's power lies
in its universal accessibility: any rational person, regardless of
ideology, can recognize these fundamental harms.

When democratic institutions lose clarity about what they
must not do, they risk undermining the very legitimacy
they are meant to protect. When institutions abandon harm
prevention for value promotion, they do not just fail—they
become weapons against the very citizens they serve. For ex-
ample, in campus free speech controversies, administrators
who prioritize “emotional safety” over constitutional protec-
tion create deeper harms—eroding trust, enabling selective
enforcement, and ultimately delegitimizing the institution
itself. DNH breaks this destructive cycle by establishing in-
violable boundaries: prevent objective harm first and pursue
aspirational goods (or positive duties) only within those con-
straints. The framework's real value emerges in what it de-
mands of public servants. As Gert (2005, 311) reminds us, “It
is remarkable that the existence of a relatively small number
of unresolvable disagreements was taken as conclusive evi-
dence against a universal morality, while the existence of an
overwhelmingly greater agreement in moral judgments was
not taken as any evidence in favor of a universal morality.”
Recognizing that moral consensus exists beneath political
discord empowers administrators to resist partisan pressure.
They can ground their decisions not in ideological alignment
but in universal principles: “No one can simply opt out of it;
others will continue to judge a person morally regardless of
whether she claims she is above or outside of it” (12).
Such an approach cultivates leadership and decision-­making
that “doesn't excite or thrill” and “seems too careful, con-
trolled, and reserved” (Badaracco 2002, 178). Yet this is pre-
cisely the leadership democracy requires: public servants who
understand that their highest calling is not transformation
but preservation—maintaining the neutral institutional space
within which democratic disagreement can flourish. Because
prohibitive moral rules already undergird democratic institu-
tions, this framework transcends shallow notions of minimal-
ism. Where contemporary scholarship frames public service
as an engine of social transformation, DNH illuminates a
more profound truth: governance achieves its highest moral
purpose not through maximizing contested goods but through
disciplined prevention of universally recognized harms. Far
from diminishing administrative duty, this approach de-
mands the highest form of ethical discipline—a recognition
that in pluralistic democracies, legitimate disagreement de-
pends upon inviolable boundaries no majority may transgress.
Administrators serve not by choosing among competing goods
but by maintaining the moral boundaries within which such
choices can legitimately occur. In an era when institutional
legitimacy faces unprecedented challenges, DNH offers more
than a defensive posture; it provides a theoretical foundation
for public administration that commands moral authority pre-
cisely because it knows what it must never do.

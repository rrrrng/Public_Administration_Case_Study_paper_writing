ABSTRACT
Employing loyal external appointees has been identified as a key strategy used by incumbents to gain control over the state bureaucracy. This
phenomenon is known as politicization and has been associated with democratic backsliding. Frequently, career civil servants perceive these
appointees as illegitimate outsiders, leaders whose main objective is to ensure political compliance rather than advance the organization’s mis-
sion. In this study, we explore how civil servants interpret the presence of outside leaders in their organizations in the context of politicization
and how this influences their job attitudes and behaviors. We use a mixed methods exploratory sequential design to examine a recent case
of politicization in environmental agencies in Brazil. First, we interviewed 25 civil servants who reported an environment of abuse, fear, disen-
gagement, and alienation due to the actions of outside leaders. We identified silence (both defensive and acquiescent) as a key outcome of
this process. We extended and confirmed these findings with a quantitative study using a new sample of 255 civil servants. This second study
allowed us to test the relationship between the variables identified in the qualitative analysis. Our combined findings indicate that politicization
may reduce the effectiveness and capability of public agencies not only because some external appointees lack competence or a commitment
the organization’s mission (as suggested by previous studies), but also because their behaviors and decisions can have a significant negative
impact on civil servants’ job attitudes and behaviors.
Although the efforts of incumbent governments to gain con-
trol over public institutions and weaken the administrative
state are not new, recent trends indicate an increase in the
frequency and intensity of these attempts (Moynihan 2022a;
Moynihan and Roberts 2010). This process contributes to
democratic backsliding and has been associated with the rise
of authoritarian and populist regimes worldwide (Bauer et al.
2021; Levitsky and Ziblatt 2018; Peters and Pierre 2019;
Yesilkagit 2018). A key strategy to gain control over the state
bureaucracy is politicization, a process whereby politicians
fill executive management positions with loyal external
appointees (Ennser-Jedenastik 2016; Moynihan 2022b).
These appointees are chosen primarily because of their loy-
alty to incumbent politicians, rather than for their technical
competence (Moynihan 2022a). They are usually “largely
unfamiliar with the departments and agencies which they
will head, and with the systems, procedures, and ‘culture’ un-
der which they will operate” (Cohen 1996, 2). We call these
appointees outside leaders. That is, administrative leaders
perceived as “outsiders” by career civil servants1, whose pri-
mary objective is to politicize and control the bureaucracy.
Outside leaders in the context of politicization usually lack
legitimacy and expertise and focus essentially on serving the
interests of incumbent politicians (Durant 1990; O’Leary
2017). They can get in the way of effective policy design and
implementation and can divert public organizations from
their statutory missions (Moynihan 2022a). For example, in
Brazil, during the COVID-19 crisis, an army general with no
previous experience in health management was appointed to
the position of Minister of Health by President Bolsonaro,
a decision with serious consequences for the country (Greer
et al. 2022). Although the detrimental effects of politiciza-
tion for public value creation are evident (see Durant 1990;
O’Leary 2017), our knowledge of the leadership dynamics
triggered by the presence of such outsiders in public organi-
zations—and its consequences—is still very limited. Existing
leadership theories can help shed light on that.
Studies based on the social identity theory of leadership
(Hogg 2001; van Knippenberg 2011) demonstrate that outside
leadership can produce dysfunctional dynamics in work groups
and is associated with negative employee job attitudes and
behaviors (Steffens et al. 2021). When leaders are not perceived
to be “of the group” and “for the group,” their relationship with
followers can be impaired (Steffens et al. 2015). Outside leaders
are more likely to have a “deep-level dissimilarity” with their
followers (Tepper et al. 2011) and to behave in ways that are
antithetical to the group’s values and goals (van Knippenberg
2011). This is relevant given that public leaders who demon-
strate a strong commitment to public service values are more
likely to develop positive and inspiring forms of leadership
(Tavares et al. 2021). Leaders who embody the group’s values,
goals, and identity are more liked, trusted, and effective (van
Knippenberg and Hogg 2003). However, this is usually not the
case with outside leaders in the context of politicization.

Although previous public administration research has
focused on how politicization and outside leaders can
harm the administrative state and democracy in general
(Moynihan 2022a, 2022b), in this study we focus on the
intra-organizational behavioral dynamics triggered by their
presence in public agencies. More specifically, we aim to ex-
plore how civil servants interpret, feel, and respond to the ex-
perience of being led by someone they consider an illegitimate
outsider. Hence, we can more fully understand the spectrum
of harm caused by politicization on the public service.
We begin by drawing on a recent case of politicization in
Brazil’s federal civil service (encompassing organizations of
the Ministry of Environment) using an exploratory sequen-
tial mixed methods design (Hanson et al. 2005; Hendren,
Luo, and Pandey 2018; Richwine et al. 2022). That is, we
started with an exploratory qualitative study comprising 25
in-depth interviews with career civil servants to identify con-
vergent narratives, key constructs, and themes, rather than
anticipating specific outcomes. We then conducted a quan-
titative study aimed at examining and confirming the rela-
tionship between the variables suggested by the qualitative
study (see Mele and Belardinelli 2019). Based on the quali-
tative study and previous literature, we selected appropriate
variables to be tested, developed a theoretical model, and
proposed hypotheses. We tested the hypotheses using survey
data from a new sample of 255 civil servants. The qualitative
and quantitative studies provided convergent evidence that
both employee defensive and acquiescent silence (van Dyne,
Ang, and Botero 2003) are related to outside leadership,
mediated by abusive supervision (Tepper 2007) and employee
self-estrangement (Golden and Veiga 2018). We believe these
findings contribute to the public administration literature in
important ways.
First, although there is evidence that civil servants may
initially, proactively counteract politicization through voice
(e.g., whistleblowing; Schuster et al. 2022)—indeed our qual-
itative results confirm this—we found that in the long run,
despondency, fear, disengagement, and silence prevailed. This
implies that time (i.e., the length of time civil servants are
exposed to a politicized environment and outside leaders) is
a key variable in understanding the effect of politicization on
civil servants’ attitudes and behaviors.
Second, although previous studies have shown that politi-
cization can hurt agency performance (see Wood and Lewis
2017), we believe our findings may help explain the process
underlying this phenomenon. Politicization can negatively
affect the effectiveness of public agencies not only because
outside leaders lack competence and legitimacy (Lewis 2010;
Moynihan 2022a), but also because they may create a neg-
ative work environment that weakens the human capital of
the organization. In this vein, we also contribute to the dem-
ocratic backsliding literature (e.g., Bauer et al. 2021) since a
bureaucracy with strong capabilities, identity, and autonomy
to protect the institutions is a cornerstone of democracy (Du
Gay 2020). If civil servants are silent, disengaged, and unable
to counter democratic backsliding, they cannot preserve state
bureaucracy and protect the public interest and democracy.
Third, this article focuses on abusive supervision and neg-
ative forms of leadership in the public service, which has re-
ceived scant attention in public leadership research (Backhaus
and Vogel 2022; Hassan 2019). According to Backhaus and
Vogel (2022, 999), “as negative leadership may do more harm
than positive leadership does good, more research on the ‘dark
side’ of leadership is needed.” Showing that politicization and
outside leadership are related to abusive supervision is an im-
portant contribution to the public leadership literature, as
there are multiple studies illustrating how harmful abusive su-
pervision can be (see Mackey et al. 2017; Tepper 2007).
Lastly, our study sheds light on the phenomenon of polit-
icization in an important context, Brazil, a country that has
been affected by democratic backsliding (Peters and Pierre
2022). As a recent V-Dem report suggests (Boese et al. 2022),
Brazil is fourth on the list of the world’s top ten countries
undergoing a process of autocratization and in the top ten in
polarization. Brazil’s liberal democracy index dropped from
0.79 (2011) to 0.51 (2021). Moreover, the politicization of
the environmental agencies in Brazil can have global effects,
as these agencies are responsible for protecting the forests and
regulating the use of land in the Amazon region. This region
is one of the most biodiverse on earth and plays a key role
in climate regulation (Fearnside 2021). Therefore, the prac-
tical implications of this case extend beyond Brazil and have
global significance.
The article is organized as follows. First, we briefly review the
literature on politicization and outside leadership, elaborating
on some potential attitudinal and behavioral outcomes. Then
we present the study design (exploratory sequential mixed
methods) and its steps, followed by an overview of the case of
politicization in Brazil’s Ministry of Environment. Next, we
conduct a qualitative study and develop hypotheses based on
its results. We test these hypotheses in a subsequent quantita-
tive study. We conclude by discussing the implications of the
results for public administration theory and practice.
Politicization and Outside Leadership
Over the last decade, scholars have identified new waves of
democratic backsliding worldwide (Bauer and Becker 2020;
Bauer et al. 2021; Levitsky and Ziblatt 2018). Democratic
backsliding is defined as the gradual weakening of democratic
institutions by incumbent governments (Bermeo 2016). These
processes are enacted by authoritarian or populist politicians
who produce institutional reforms after coming to power
under electoral processes (Yesilkagit 2018) and use this new
position to enforce non-democratic changes (Bauer et al.
2021; Levitsky and Ziblatt 2018; Peters and Pierre 2019). To
follow through on this agenda, politicians usually begin by
trying to capture control of the state bureaucracy and civil
servants (Moynihan 2022a).
In fact, Bauer et al. (2021) suggest that politicians adopt
three different strategies to control civil servants: gaining their
loyalty (Hajnal and Boda 2021; Hajnal and Csengődi 2014),
sidelining them (Bauer and Becker 2020), or empowering
them (Peters and Pierre 2019). When it is difficult to gain their
loyalty or when empowering them may hurt their agenda,
politicians may try to eliminate bureaucratic resistance by
sidelining them (Stoker 2021). Politicization is a key method
to achieve this goal as many examples in history suggest: the
Nazi regime in Germany (Strobel and Veit 2021); Italy under
Berlusconi (Mascio, Natalini, and Ongaro, 2021); Hungary
(Hajnal and Boda 2021); the USA under Trump (Moynihan
2022a, O’Leary 2017) and under Reagan (Durant 1990);
Mexico under Obrador (Dussauge-Laguna 2022); Venezuela
(Muno and Briceño 2021); and Poland (Mazur 2021).

The literature indicates that career civil servants often re-
sist and counteract political interventions deemed illegit-
imate—at least initially—using strategies such as exit (e.g.,
leaving office), voice (e.g., whistleblowing), and sabotage
(e.g., boycotting) (Brehm and Gates 1997; Golden 1992;
Guedes-Neto and Peters 2021; Hirchman 1970; O’Leary
2017; Olsson 2016). However, in the case of politicization,
these strategies are unlikely to produce effective results. More
often than not, outside leaders remain in charge of public
agencies, following through with their politicization agenda,
despite civil servants’ initial resistance efforts (O’Leary 2017).
Therefore, the main question we raise in this study is: what
are the consequences of the continued presence of outside
leaders in public agencies on civil servants’ job attitudes and
behaviors? This is relevant given that negative employee
attitudes and behaviors (e.g., disengagement, low morale, low
productivity) can impair the performance of public agencies
(Kim 2004). Moreover, understanding this phenomenon will
shed new light on the detrimental effects of politicization.
We can anticipate a negative association between outside
leadership and civil servants’ job attitudes and behaviors
in the context of politicization based on the social identity
theory of leadership (Hogg 2001). According to this theory,
leadership is viewed as a group process influenced by social
categorization and social identity (Hogg 2001). Social iden-
tity is described as an individual’s sense of belongingness to a
social group (Tajfel 1972). Hogg (2001) discusses that leaders
will be seen as more effective by followers if they show pro-
totypical characteristics of their in-group; that is, if they are
perceived as embodying the group’s values, beliefs, goals, and
identity (van Knippenberg 2011). Group-prototypical leaders
are more liked and trusted to pursue the group’s best interests
(Giessner and van Knippenberg 2008; van Knippenberg
2011).
When leaders are perceived as outsiders, followers are more
prone to display negative attitudes toward them (Graf et al.
2012) and are less open to their influence (van Knippenberg
2011). Consequently, the relationship between leaders and
followers becomes rooted in conflict (Dulebohn et al. 2012).
Outside leaders are more likely to treat followers unfairly
and to be more autocratic (van Dijke and De Cremer 2010),
which further decreases the quality of their relationship
(Roch and Shanock 2006). Low leader-follower relationship
quality is a key predictor of several follower attitudinal and
behavioral outcomes, such as organizational commitment,
job satisfaction, organizational citizenship behavior, and job
performance (Dulebohn et al. 2012).
Moreover, how followers perceive the actions and decisions
taken by a leader play a role in shaping their perceptions of
the meaning of work and their sense of connection with the
organization and its mission (Jensen 2018; Wright, Moynihan,
and Pandey 2012). For instance, drawing on the social iden-
tity of leadership, Tavares et al. (2021) showed that when
public leaders display a strong commitment to public service
values, it reduces employee voluntary turnover. However,
since outside leaders usually display a “deep-level dissimi-
larity” (Tepper et al. 2011) with career civil servants, they are
more likely to make decisions that divert public agencies from
their statutory missions, weaken structural protections of civil
servants, and diminish organizational capacities (Moynihan
2022a). By doing so, they may end up negatively influencing
civil servants’ affective commitment to the organization
(Moldogaziev and Silvia 2015), perceived mission valence
(Wright and Pandey 2011), and perceived prosocial impact
(Grant 2008), all of them important predictors of employee
job attitudes and behaviors in the public service (Vigoda
2000). These arguments illustrate that there are several po-
tential outcomes of outside leadership. However, in this study,
we adopt a more exploratory, inductive approach to identify
the specific outcomes of outside leadership in public organi-
zations in the context of politicization (Hanson et al. 2005;
Hendren, Luo, and Pandey 2018).
Study Design
We followed an exploratory sequential mixed methods de-
sign (Hanson et al. 2005; Hendren, Luo, and Pandey 2018)
using Richwine et al.’s (2022) guidelines for mixed methods
research to address our research question. Thus, we began
by developing a qualitative study based on interviews aimed
at understanding civil servants’ perceptions of outside lead-
ership in the context of politicization. The interviews were
later coded to identify themes and variables. After conducting
a new literature review based on these emerging themes and
variables, we developed hypotheses and a theoretical model
(see Burch and Heinrich 2015; Mele and Belardinelli 2019)
and conducted a survey to test the hypotheses. In line with
existing guidelines, the survey was applied to a sample from
the same population (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). The
quantitative study helped us test and confirm the relationship
between the variables identified in the previous phase. Each
of these steps will be detailed below. Figure 1 summarizes the
study design. Data collection took place between the end of
2021 and the beginning of 2022.
Case Selection
This study focuses on Brazilian environmental organiza-
tions for four key reasons. First, Brazil is a country with a
potentially high level of politicization, as the coalitional
presidency (Abranches 1988) depends on negotiating polit-
ical appointments with the parties (Limongi and Figueredo
1998). Second, during Bolsonaro’s presidency, the country
faced a process of democratic backsliding (Peters and Pierre
2022) whereby the replacement of civil servants with external
political appointees was one of the key strategies to imple-
ment the institutional changes desired by the president (Peci
2021; Milhorance 2022). This is a typical case of politiciza-
tion (Seawright and Gerring 2008) that enabled us to effec-
tively analyze the impact of outside leaders on civil servants.
Third, the decision to study environmental organizations was
made because they were strongly impacted by Bolsonaro’s
agenda of dismantling, institutional changes, and attack on
bureaucrats (Milhorance 2022; Rajão et al. 2022). Lastly, as
previously mentioned, Brazil’s environmental policies have
global significance (Fearnside 2021).
Environmental Organizations in Brazil

Ministry of Environment and some smaller agencies under the
Ministry’s responsibility. Together, these organizations are re-
sponsible for policies regarding environmental preservation,
conservation, education, environmental inspection, and spe-
cial policies for sustainable development and climate change.

the negative interventions of outside leaders—such as the
strong ethos and identity of civil servants, the union, and

high degree of politicization in their organizations. For ex-

work is made unilaterally by their managers and the head of
the human resources department, all of whom are political
appointees. Moreover, if they have any problems with their
managers, they must contact the human resources department,
the ethical committee, or the internal affairs. However, all of

Exploratory Study
Qualitative Data Collection and Analysis
Interview Procedure.
To first approach the field, we interviewed two environ-
mental analysts (career civil servants) with whom we had
had previous contact. After the interview, we asked them
to indicate other interviewees. Using the snowball sam-
pling method given the sensitive context (Cohen and Arieli
2011), we recruited participants from different units of the
Ministry of Environment. In total, we interviewed 25 career
civil servants (all environmental analysis). The decision to end
the interviews was based on response saturation—meaning
that the interviewees started providing repetitive informa-
tion about the subjects being analyzed (Bernard 2012). Given
the threatening political context for these civil servants, all
respondents agreed to be interviewed anonymously and
without any identifiable information. Therefore, to ensure
their protection, we cannot divulge interviewee profiles here.
Our sample was diverse regarding gender, type of work, and
position in the organization. The interview guide was organ-
ized into four sections (Supplementary Material): (1) indi-
vidual profile and trajectory; (2) work routines; (3) changes
in work routines by the government; and (4) reactions to
these changes. The interviews lasted between 60 and 180
minutes and were conducted by one of the authors, who also
transcribed the recordings to protect the confidentiality of
the information. This research has been approved by FGV’s
Institutional Review Board (Protocol Number 088-2021).
Data Analysis.
We developed a thematic analysis in different phases fol-
lowing Braun and Clarke’s (2022) method. The first step
was the familiarization phase, in which we read all the
materials while trying to make sense of the data based on
our research question (“How do outside leaders affect civil
servants’ attitudes and work-related behaviors?”). Although
reading the interviews, we uncovered two macro-themes: ev-
idence about the politicization process (when civil servants
describe how they perceive changes in political appointments
or the arrival of outsiders) and the consequences of politici-
zation (when they narrate how they felt about these perceived
changes). We read the material again, coding the excerpts
under these two themes, which became macro-codes. At this
phase, the same excerpt could be associated with more than
one macro-code.
The second step involved reading all of the interviews a
second time and generating codes for each of the macro-codes
inductively through a process of open coding.
During the third step, we read the codes created in the
second step and proposed six themes based on the recogni-
tion of the central ideas stemming from the codes. Specifically,
we proposed two elements related to politicization and four
types of consequences. Regarding the first macro-code, politi-
cization, we found elements related to “perceptions of politici-
zation,” when interviewees mentioned how they perceived the
politicization process. We also found elements related to “out-
side leadership,” when interviewees mentioned that their new
leaders are outsiders with little legitimacy, not “one of them.”
Regarding the second macro-code, consequences of politici-
zation, we found four types of consequences that we named
in the coding process as follows: abusive supervision, aliena-
tion and disengagement, silence for self-protection, and silence
for resignation and disengagement. For example, whenever
interviewees spoke about cases of harassment and persecution
by managers, statements were coded as abusive supervision, as
in this example: “As soon as the new manager was appointed,
the persecution started.” When interviewees associated outside
leadership with alienation, disengagement, and a sense of de-
tachment from work, we coded it as alienation and disengage-
ment, as in this example: “They (the new managers) ended my
desire to work.” When interviewees reported being silent for
their own protection, we coded it as silence for self-protection,
as in this example: “I was afraid of retaliation. I don’t say an-
ything by email anymore.” Lastly, when interviewees reported
they silenced their voices because of hopelessness and disen-
gagement, we coded it as silence for resignation and disen-
gagement, as in this example: “People are apathetic. […] The
general feeling is: better to be quiet, not to engage.”

Results
Process of Politicization via Outside Leadership
All interviewees witnessed a process of politicization whereby
outsiders replaced civil servants with expertise. They re-
ported mainly two types of outsiders: those representing
the interests of agribusiness and military members. In both
cases, outsiders had no previous experience in the field and,
most of all represented interests aiming to dismantle environ-
mental policies. Many of them mentioned that the outside
leader replaced former leaders who knew about policies and
procedures. As one interviewee explains, “The first change I
saw with the government was the change of managers. They
replaced technicians who had worked in the area for many
years with soldiers [from the military] who didn’t understand
anything about the topic and who started to do administra-
tive work that hindered our mission” (I18).
Another common characteristic of these new appointees
is that they were loyal to the president’s agenda of policy
dismantling and lacked the required expertise to do the work.
Interviewees stated that the changes in political appointees
were based on the idea that the new appointees must be loyal
to the agenda, and bringing military members in was part
of the strategy. As one interviewee reported, “This new boss
dances to the music. She does what the government wants.
She is not a person trained in the environmental area, but she
does everything she is told to do” (I3). Another stated, “Here
we had a technical manager who did a great job. But he has
been replaced by a military member who does not have the
slightest qualification for the position. He doesn’t even know
how to write properly, he doesn’t know how to do anything,
and he’s violent with employees. He does everything they
(from the Ministry) asks him to do” (I8).
Consequences of Politicization: Environment of
Alienation, Disengagement, and Abuse
The civil servants interviewed reported that the new
outsiders were using their position abusively to enforce
many changes in their work. The first involved procedural
changes. Routines, processes, and work rules were changed
to reduce the scope of action of civil servants and their ability
to create barriers for the government. For example, one par-
ticipant pointed out that “we were excluded from many tech-
nical procedures. We are not invited to meetings, we cannot
make decisions, we cannot even access information on the
system” (I22). One example was the banning of the informa-
tion system that recorded all decisions made by civil servants.
Without this system, civil servants could not formalize their
decisions, which managers could change without formal
registration.
This situation increased feelings of alienation and disen-
gagement among civil servants. For instance, according to
interviewee I14, “Now in my sector it’s just apathy. People are
apathetic. Even people who were fiercer, like me.” Moreover,
outside leaders appeared to be engaging in abusive practices
of harassment and persecution that generated fear among
workers. As one interviewee said, “I am afraid of being
arrested, afraid of being dismissed” (I19).
Actual dismissal.
One week before submitting this article, this interviewee (I19)
reached out to one of the authors to report that he had been
dismissed. For two years, his manager (an outside leader) had
harassed him. He told he had not been given any duties, had
no place to work—no table or chair—and had been sidelined
even after asking for work. His manager made a case against
him for lack of work and managed to fire him. As a conse-
quence of the increase in harassment and abuse, civil servants
felt threatened, were afraid to speak up, let alone denounce
their situation since they didn’t know if they were being
watched, as noted by this interviewee, “They changed the
layout of the rooms: now there are no walls and managers
watch us all the time” (I22).
This continued presence of outside leaders reduced civil
servants’ motivation and engagement. As one interviewee
said, “I feel very unmotivated. It’s hard to keep motivated
with these managers. My initial revolt is turning into apathy.
What keeps me still at work is my commitment to the en-
vironmental agenda” (I6). And another one stated, “They
[the new managers] ended my desire to work. I’m passionate
about the environmental agenda and I’m getting more and
more apathetic. The work became a punishment” (I1).
From Alienation, Disengagement, and Abusive
Supervision to Silence
Like other civil servants working in the environmental field
worldwide (Abers 2019; Hysing and Olsson 2011; Olsson
and Hysing 2012), many (20) of our interviewees had a tra-
jectory of activism in which they engaged in environmental
movements before joining the public service. Twenty-three
of them said that they had already engaged in open resist-
ance activities prior to the Bolsonaro government, including
signing manifestos, participating in riots, giving information
to the press, and so on. However, the environment of alien-
ation and disengagement created by the current government
has clearly reduced these actions. According to them, open
resistance can only be done institutionally via unions. Even
sabotage has become dangerous since nobody knows who is
on the government’s side and could denounce you. Several
of them abandoned all social networks so as to no longer
be observed. As this interviewee noted, “People are terrified
because they built a strategy of individual persecution. Now
people are cornered and apathetic” (I19).
Consequently, a climate of silence began to take hold
among the civil servants. Based on the data, we found two
reasons for silence. The first is self-protection, as employees
were afraid of retaliation and harassment. As one interviewee
stated after witnessing leaders acting against the law, “I even
thought about blowing the whistle to the union, but I was
afraid of retaliation. I don’t say anything by email anymore, I
even avoid using the phone.” The second reason for remaining
silent is hopelessness and disengagement. Interviewees did not
feel that speaking up or complaining would solve anything in
this context. One interviewee said that “The general feeling is:

Confirmatory Quantitative Study
The second phase of our study is quantitative and confirm-
atory. Following Mele and Belardinelli’s (2019) recommen-
dation, the integration of the two phases was guaranteed
through the transformation of the emerging themes that came
from the qualitative study into questions and hypotheses that
were used in the design of the quantitative study (see table
1). The main objective of this phase is to test the relationship
between the variables identified in the qualitative study. First,
we present a brief review of the literature surrounding these
variables and how they relate to each other.
Silence
As seen in table 1, silence emerged as a key outcome. In the
literature, silence refers to when an employee withholds any
form of sincere opinion or information that might be useful
to the organization (Pinder and Harlos 2001). Silence is not
only a form of compliance, as previously thought, but also a
strategic choice (van Dyne, Ang, and Botero 2003). In other
words, individuals may choose not to speak. Three types of si-
lence have been proposed in the literature (Pinder and Harlos
2001; van Dyne, Ang, and Botero 2003): acquiescent silence
(reflects disengagement; e.g., when employees feel resigned
that no matter what they do or say, they will be unable to
make a difference); defensive silence (when employees de-
cide not to speak because they feel afraid or at personal risk
due to what they would voice); and proactive silence (when
employees are quiet in order to effectively collaborate with
others). Defensive and acquiescent silence are often dysfunc-
tional and have emerged as themes in our qualitative study
(see table 1).

The qualitative results show that outside leaders in the con-
text of politicization are not perceived to be “of the group”
nor “for the group” by career civil servants. In this situation,
the social identity theory of leadership predicts that the re-
lationship between leaders and followers will be impaired
(Steffens et al. 2015). Moreover, followers are less likely to
endorse outside leaders’ decisions and are less open to their
influence (van Knippenberg 2011). Consequently, outside
leaders become more likely to resort to authoritarian tac-
tics and punishment to maintain their power. Indeed, we
found that outside leaders are frequently perceived as abu-
sive by career civil servants (table 1). The abusive behaviors
mentioned in the interviews are similar to those described in

Self-Estrangement
As evidenced in the qualitative analysis, several participants
of the qualitative study defined themselves as environmental
activists, meaning that they are highly concerned about
making a meaningful difference in society through their work.
This evinces their high public service motivation (PSM) (Perry
and Wise 1990), their connection with the organization’s
mission (see Wright and Pandey 2011), and their interest in
making a prosocial impact through their work (Grant 2008).
To the extent that high-PSM civil servants fulfill their pro-
social motives and have a more meaningful work experience,
they demonstrate greater work engagement and organiza-
tional commitment (e.g., Bashir, Wright, and Hassan 2022).
Importantly, leaders play a key role in shaping employees’
perceptions of the meaning of work and their sense of con-
nection with the organization and its mission (Jensen 2018;
Wright, Moynihan, and Pandey 2012).
As noted above, the objectives of outside leaders are often
perceived as being misaligned with public organizations’—
and civil servants’—prosocial motives, values, and goals.
Such leaders are more likely to create role ambiguity for
subordinates insofar as they advance goals that go against
what the organization and its employees value. Therefore,
outside leaders in the context of democratic backsliding may
create an environment in which employees feel disengaged,
alienated, or disassociated from their work as they perceive
that they are asked to do things that are no longer aligned
with their self-image, their profession, and their personal
values. This sense of disconnection and alienation at work has
been defined as self-estrangement (Golden and Veiga 2018;
Sloan 2014) and was identified in the qualitative analysis.
Self-estrangement is associated with lower employee en-
gagement and job involvement (Golden and Veiga 2018),
which are root causes of acquiescent silence (van Dyne,
Ang, and Botero 2003). Differently from defensive silence,
which is an outcome of fear, acquiescent silence reflects
one’s psychological distancing from work and a belief that
one’s words and actions are unlikely to make any differ-
ence. As such, we expect that perceived outside leadership
in the context of democratic backsliding will be associated
with acquiescent silence through its impact on employee
self-estrangement:
H2a:
Perceived outside leadership is positively related to acqui-
escent silence.
H2b:
Self-estrangement will mediate the relationships between
perceived outside leadership and acquiescent silence.

Sample and Procedure
We contacted the interviewees of the qualitative phase and
asked them to distribute the survey link to their coworkers
who also worked in the Ministry headquarters (online
survey). We chose to prioritize them over regional offices be-
cause they are closer in proximity to the presidency, hold more
authority, and are more directly involved in decision-making
processes. These attributes make them more susceptible to
the impact of external leadership. Given the sensitive nature
of our research, we were unable to obtain official permis-
sion to distribute the survey and used only informal distribu-
tion channels. As a result, we could not estimate the number
of civil servants who received the survey link. We informed
participants that their participation was anonymous, volun-
tary, and free of risks. We obtained their informed consent.
We received 405 surveys back, but only 255 were complete.

Measures
Perceived Outside Leadership.
Perceived outside leadership was assessed using four items
from the supervisor’s organizational embodiment scale
(Eisenberger et al. 2010): “My supervisor is characteristic of
(name of the organization),” “My supervisor and (name of
the organization) have a lot in common,” “My supervisor is
representative of (name of the organization),” and “My su-
pervisor is typical of (name of the organization).” The items
were reverse-scored. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale is
.88. This measure captures the level of illegitimacy and “out-
groupness” of appointees without asking if they came from
outside the organization. Although in these organizations
many appointees (about 50%) are objectively outsiders, we
also wanted to allow some variance in perception since some
leaders could be more active in politicization than others.
At the end of the day, what matters is not only if the leader
comes from inside or outside (more objectively), but the ex-
tent to which they are perceived by followers to embody the
organization’s values and goals (van Knippenberg 2011).
In this sense, some appointees may come from outside but,
if they are perceived to have expertise, legitimacy, and do
not have an overt politicization agenda, they will probably
not trigger the negative organizational dynamics discussed
previously.
Abusive Supervision.
Abusive supervision was measured with a six-item scale used
by Harris, Harvey, and Kacmar (2011). Sample items in-
clude, “My supervisor makes negative comments about me
to others,” “My supervisor gives me the silent treatment,”
“My supervisor expresses anger at me when he/she is mad
for another reason,” and “My supervisor is rude to me.” The
Cronbach’s alpha for this scale is .94.
Defensive and Acquiescent Silence.
Defensive and Acquiescent silence were measured using the
employee silence scale (van Dyne, Ang, and Botero 2003).
Each scale has five items. Sample items for defensive silence
include, “I avoid giving suggestions to protect myself” and
“I retain suggestions or solutions to problems out of fear.”
The Cronbach’s alpha for this defensive silence is .93. Sample
items for acquiescent silence include, “I withhold my ideas,
based on resignation” and “I keep my ideas about solutions
to problems to myself.” The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale
is .88.
Self-estrangement.
Self-estrangement was assessed using four items developed
by Golden and Veiga (2018). The items include, “Time really
drags for me when I’m at work,” “When I’m working, I feel
like I’ve become just another part of a machine,” “Quite often
I feel like staying away from my workplace,” and “I used
to care more about my work unit, but not anymore.” The
Cronbach’s alpha for this scale is .80.
All items were scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging
from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Gender, age,
and race were included in all regressions as control variables.
We present the original items in Portuguese in Supplementary
Material.
Results
We first assessed the measurement model’s goodness-of-fit
indexes. We tested three different model configurations: a
one-factor model (all factors loading in the same latent vari-
able), a three-factor model in which we combined perceived
outside leadership with abusive supervision and defensive
silence with acquiescent silence, and a five-factor model (all
variables separated). The confirmatory factor analysis shows
that both the one-factor model (χ2(242) = 534.94; p < .001;
RMSEA = .06; SRMR = .05; CFI = .93; and TLI = .92)
and the three-factor model (χ2(249) = 1464.33; p < .001;
RMSEA = .14; SRMR = .12; CFI = .73; and TLI = .70) fit
the data poorly. The five-factor model fits the data very well
(Δχ2(242) = 534.94; p < .001; RMSEA = .06; SRMR = .05;
CFI = .93; and TLI = .92), which supports the discrimi-
nant validity between the variables and the adequacy of our
measures.

We tested the hypotheses using regression analysis (table 3).

leaders are seen as outsiders, employees are more likely to

likely to be perceived as abusive by subordinates, whereas
subordinates are more likely to experience a sense of dis-

side leaders. Both abusive supervision and self-estrangement

Specifically, we found that outside leaders—political
appointees who usually lack expertise and whose primary
objective is to politicize and control the bureaucracy—are
perceived as less legitimate and more abusive by career civil
servants and also create an environment of apprehension,
disengagement, and detachment from work. This ultimately
leads to employee silence, both defensive (related to fear)
and acquiescent (related to resignation). These findings do
not contradict previous studies showing that civil servants
initially respond to illegitimate political interventions with
voice (e.g., whistleblowing; Schuster et al. 2022). Indeed, the
qualitative results show that voice and confrontation may
be an initial reaction against outside leadership (albeit with
little impact). However, in the longer run—due to recurrent
instances of persecution, abuse, and misalignment between
outside leaders’ decisions and the organization’s values and
goals—silence prevails.
This voice-silencing effect of outside leadership deserves
attention, as many negative employee outcomes have been
associated with silence in organizations: stress, cynicism, dis-
engagement, dissatisfaction (Beer and Eisenstat 2000), failure
to detect serious problems, and lack of innovation (Morrison
2014). Although employee silence has received little attention
in public administration research (Hassan, DeHart-Davis,
and Jiang 2019), studies show it is an important variable in
the public-sector context, too. For instance, defensive silence
is associated with public employees avoiding raising questions
about policy proposals in their area of expertise in order to
avoid controversy (Hassan 2015). Similarly, Gambarotto and
Cammozzo (2010) have shown the negative effect of silence
on innovation in public services.
Our findings also contribute to the literature on demo-
cratic backsliding by examining how politicization enables an
increase in political control and can weaken state bureauc-
racy. Scholars have already suggested that politicization is one
strategy of political control that could eliminate bureaucratic
resistance (Bauer et al. 2021; Bauer and Becker 2020) and our
findings shed new light on why this happens. We show that
leaders who are perceived as outsiders are also more likely
to be perceived as abusive by civil servants. This affects their
capacity to react and impose resistance continuously, even in
the case of civil servants with previous experience in ­activism.
This takes the form of defensive silence. Similarly, acqui-
escent silence emerged from feelings of self-estrangement
driven by outside leadership. Employees who were previously
motivated and engaged began to disengage as they perceived
that whatever they did would make no difference since out-
side leaders had an anti-environment agenda and the power
to advance it. Along the same lines, previous studies have
found that civil servants may respond to illegitimate polit-
ical interventions with behaviors such as exit (Golden 1992)
and shirking (Brehm and Gates 1997). We expand the under-
standing of this phenomenon by investigating its mechanisms.
The article also contributes to the public leadership litera-
ture by focusing on abusive supervision and unethical forms
of leadership in public service. Public administration research
has focused almost exclusively on the “bright side” of lead-
ership, whereas overlooking the potential detrimental effects
of negative public leaders (Backhaus and Vogel 2022; Hassan
2019). Moreover, we add to the literature by showing that
politicization and outside leadership are key antecedents of
abusive supervision in the public service.
Limitations and Directions for Future Research
Despite the important contributions of this work, some lim-
itations must be acknowledged, especially because they offer
opportunities for future research. An important strength of
our study was the use of mixed methods to explore and con-
firm our findings. Nonetheless, we recognize that, in the quan-
titative phase, common method variance might have inflated
the correlations between the variables due to the cross-sec-
tional design (Podsakoff et al. 2003). Although we were able
to find support for the relationships suggested by the qualita-
tive phase, future studies may benefit from the use of multiple
data sources and longitudinal designs such as diary studies.
Ideally, a longitudinal study should encompass both the
period before and after elections in order to capture the entire
process of political appointments, changes in policy and in-
ternal procedures, and the resulting effects on public servants’
job attitudes and behaviors.
Another limitation involves the data collection process.
Since we contacted the interviewees from the qualita-
tive phase and asked them to distribute the survey link to
their coworkers, we could not calculate the exact response
rate. Moreover, considering the context of politicization
and democratic backsliding, some civil servants might have
been afraid to participate in the survey or to provide details
during the interviews. As this limitation may occur in any
context involving fear, we tried to handle it by ensuring an-
onymity and data protection. Moreover, if those who were
more afraid, disengaged, and silent were less likely to par-
ticipate in the survey, the effects we observed may have been
attenuated. Given the sensitive nature of our research topic,
we avoided collecting many demographic variables to pro-
tect participants’ identity and to make them feel more safe
in expressing their true perceptions and feelings about their
superiors. The downside is that some important control
variables, such as organizational tenure or rank, could not be
included in the regression models.
We also acknowledge that the case explored in this re-
search is atypical, as it represents an extreme situation of
dismantling of public environmental agencies, guided by ide-
ological and economic issues. This limits the generalizability
of our results. Although we would theoretically expect similar
effects in other contexts, we believe this extreme situation and
the ideological disputes around the environmental agenda
may have enhanced the negative effects of outside leadership.
Perhaps, in more ideologically neutral contexts or where po-
litical disputes are less salient, outside leaders will not trigger
such negative responses in civil servants. This is in line with
the social identity theory of leadership, which predicts that
intergroup conflict (e.g., right vs. left) makes social iden-
tity more salient (van Knippenberg 2011). In such cases,
out-group leaders are even more likely to produce negative
outcomes in the group (van Knippenberg and Hogg 2003).
It is important that future studies investigate which factors
could either prevent extreme cases of politicization or buffer
its negative effects in public organizations. For instance,
which bureaucratic and institutional capabilities (e.g., the
use of objective technical criteria to select public leaders)

bureaucratic resilience (Bauer et al. 2021)? Answering these
questions is crucial to confront the increasing cases of demo-
cratic backsliding in many countries (Boese et al. 2022).
Lastly, we believe future studies should investigate and pro-
pose strategies to recover the morale and motivation of public
servants in the wake of politicization. Are the negative effects
of politicization on employees’ job attitudes and behaviors
temporary or lasting? What have career civil servants learned
from these experiences and what are their recommendations?
Conclusion
In this article, we have examined how outside leadership in
the context of politicization negatively affects career civil
servants’ job attitudes and behaviors. We conclude that the
continued presence of illegitimate outside appointees in
positions of leadership can negatively impact the work en-
vironment in public organizations. We found evidence that
abusive supervision, employee disengagement, low morale,
and silence (both defensive and acquiescent) are associated
with outside leadership. By using an organizational behavior
lens, our study illustrates the previously unexplored negative
effects of politicization. By doing so, we advance the under-
standing of the potential harmful consequences of politici-
zation for the administrative state and democracy, as well
as devise strategies to protect both the civil service and the
public interest.
The analyses also have practical implications, especially re-
garding how to prevent or deal with democratic backsliding.
Considering the increase of autocratization worldwide—as
evidenced V-Dem data (Boese et al. 2022)—it is impor-
tant to understand how to strengthen public administration
using tools to resist this process. As the findings suggest, po-
liticization may be an effective tool for leaders who aim to
weaken democracy and the barriers imposed by bureaucracy.
Our case study shows the importance of creating rules and
procedures for a political appointment process that would
introduce legitimate leaders and avoid abusive supervision.
In addition, this research illustrates the importance of having
whistleblowing channels to strengthen civil service and guar-
antee voice—even when facing abusive supervision.

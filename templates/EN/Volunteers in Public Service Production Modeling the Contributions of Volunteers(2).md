Abstract
Volunteer use as an alternative service delivery arrangement entails public organizations directly incorporating volunteers in service produc-
tion through a quasi-employment relationship. However, research evaluating the contributions of volunteer labor to organizational performance
are relatively few. This article fills this gap by drawing from two theoretical insights. First, this study tests a linear model based on the tradi-
tional human resource management approach of interchangeability in which volunteers constitute a one-on-one substitute for paid employees.
Second, this study draws insight from critical mass theory to explore a nonlinear model based on the assumption that the productivity of one
volunteer may not be equal to that of one paid employee due to the systematic limitations of volunteer labor. Analyzing a five wave panel dataset
of 484 local law enforcement agencies in the United States that utilize sworn volunteers with general arrest powers, the findings indicate that
an increase in the number of sworn volunteers has a linear negative effect on clearing violent crimes in sheriffs offices. However, the results
also indicate evidence of curvilinear effects on clearing violent crimes in police departments. This article suggests the need to account for the
systematic limitations of volunteer labor when measuring performance and to explore multiple indicators. This study contributes to theory
building and testing by enabling a two-way understanding of the relationship between volunteer involvement and organizational performance
in the public sector.
Introduction
Different political, fiscal, administrative, and environmental
factors influence governmental decisions on how to pro-
duce services (Brown and Potoski 2003; Kettl 2000). Today’s
service environment is characterized by a wide variety of
hybridized arrangements such as partnerships, collaboration,
networks, and alliances with private sector entities (Agranoff
and McGuire 2003; Milward and Provan 2000). Depending
on the nature of the relationship, specific strategies and
programs vary on a continuum from lesser to higher degrees
of inter-organizational involvement. Volunteer use is a type of
arrangement in which public agencies incorporate lay citizens
into the organization through a quasi-employment relation-
ship and assign them with the same or similar tasks that full-
time employees assume (Alford and O’Flynn 2012; Brudney
1990; Thomsen and Jensen 2020).
As local governments are responsible for the provision
of public services (Walker and Andrews 2015), a key ques-
tion for public administration research is whether and how
service production choices serve as important determinants
of organizational outcomes (Brown and Potoski 2003; de
la Riva Agüero 2022; Lee and Hung 2022; Masten 1993;
Nederhand 2021). Surveys of service delivery alternatives in
the United States reveal that state and local governments have
involved volunteers in service production for many decades
and that their utilization is widespread across different
service areas (Brudney and Yoon 2021; Kang et al. 2020;
Nesbit and Brudney 2013). To date, research on public sector
volunteering have explored the advantages and disadvantages
of volunteer involvement (Brudney 1993; Gazley and Brudney
2005); factors that influence the use of volunteers (Brudney
and Kellough 2000; Brudney and Yoon 2021; Ferris 1988;
Kang 2021; Kang, Nesbit, and Brudney 2022); different
dimensions of volunteer involvement (Nesbit et al. 2018),
existing employees’ perspectives of volunteers (Dover 2010;
Thomsen and Jensen 2020); and the recruitment and man-
agement of volunteers (Choudhury 2010). However, aside
from a few exceptions (Brudney and Gazley 2002; Ivonchyk
2019; Kang 2019), one area that lacks attention is the in-
vestigation of how volunteer use as a service production ar-
rangement contributes to organizational performance. One
reason is due to the often informal and transitory nature of
volunteering which makes it difficult for organizations to
consider volunteers in formal evaluations of performance
(Brudney and Williamson 2000). This may have contributed
to the paucity of data collection on the numbers and work
hours, functions and tasks, budgets and expenditures, and
other critical information regarding public sector volunteers,
rendering it difficult to link such factors to performance.
To fill this gap, this article investigates the relationship be-
tween volunteer involvement and public organizational per-
formance by examining a two-pronged theoretical approach
to assessing volunteer performance. First, the goal attain-
ment and systems theory approaches enable the considera-
tion of volunteers as human resource inputs to the process
of producing organizational outputs or outcomes (Helmig et
al. 2014). The traditional approach of managing volunteers
is grounded in a “business-based” model that typically
prescribes a top–down, linear, predictable, and functional
mode of volunteer involvement (Macduff et al. 2009; Studer

Policing serves as the empirical context. This study draws

by the clearance rate. However, the results also indicate some
evidence of a curvilinear effect with clearing violent crimes in
police departments. By evaluating the notion of interchangea-

both a linear and nonlinear model of volunteer performance,
the results contribute to theory development by enabling a
more systematic assessment of the relationship between vol-

for practice are relevant in ways that enable a more thor-
ough evaluation of how volunteers may serve as a resource
or constraint in improving agency performance, as well as
prompting considerations of how volunteers can add value to
the overall process of producing public services.
Literature Review: Background and Theoretical
Perspectives
Among different production arrangements, volunteer use
comprises a strategic arrangement in which lay citizens
participate in public service provision as external, non-
organizational participants on an unpaid basis (Brudney
1993; Nesbit et al. 2018). A formal definition of a volunteer
is codified in federal regulations as the following: “[an indi-
vidual] who performs services for ‘civic, charitable, or hu-
manitarian reasons’ without expectation of compensation”
(29 C.F.R. § 553.101[a]). To understand the government–
volunteer relationship, it is important to review the roles of
volunteers and modes of coordination in the service produc-
tion process.
Service production involves a variety of activities and
occurs in different phases. For simplification, we may classify
according to two main activities of deciding and producing
(Alford and O’Flynn 2012). On one end of the spectrum, de-
ciding involves a set of activities that determine what is to be
produced, who produces it, and the provision of resources in
accomplishing that task. Deciding may be performed solely
by the government, by an external entity, or shared between
the two. On the other end, producing entails the set of activ-
ities that convert resources into results of value and may be
performed through full government production, full external
production, or shared arrangements. Volunteer involvement
is a type of arrangement in which government solely performs
the deciding role, whereas production is performed solely by
volunteers or in conjunction with government employees
(Alford and O’Flynn 2012).
The mode of coordination between the deciding party and
the producing party refers to the mechanisms required to en-
sure that the producing party’s actions are consistent with the
will of the deciding party (Alford and O’Flynn 2012). The
modes of coordination range on a continuum from compul-
sion on one end to collaboration on the other. For instance,
governments may rely heavily on compulsion and sanctions
for regulatory functions, although outsourcing to external
firms utilizes contracts and negotiations. Regarding vol-
unteer involvement, Alford and O’Flynn (2012, 24) define
government–volunteer relations as a “quasi-employment rela-
tionship” that combines different modes such as incentives,
informal sanctions, and collaborative elements such as trust
and mutual agreements.
Volunteer Involvement and Organizational
Performance: A Linear Approach
As volunteers assume the role of a producer in the service pro-
duction process, they may benefit public organizations in dif-
ferent ways (Brudney 1993). Agencies may involve volunteers
to save on labor costs by using volunteers to supplement or
complement the work of existing personnel. Volunteers may
contribute to improving service levels and quality by engaging
in either core or auxiliary functions, or both. Volunteers may
also enable agencies to strengthen community relations and
to better assess the needs of citizens and clients.
An important empirical question that has not received much
attention is whether and how volunteers contribute to public
organizational performance. The nonprofit literature provides
some insight into modeling the relationship between volunteers
and performance. Helmig et al. (2014) draw from the goal at-
tainment approach and the systems approach to model volun-
teer performance in the context of nonprofits. They identify four
dimensions of performance: inputs, outputs, transformation
processes, and the organizational environment. First, organiza-
tions must define outputs in terms of clearly stated and meas-
urable organizational goals. Next, they collect the necessary
resources or inputs to accomplish those goals. Organizations
then require the managerial capability to devise and implement
“efficient transformation processes” of converting inputs into
valuable outputs (Helmig et al. 2014, 1515). Finally, organiza-
tions must consider the environment by measuring success or
outcomes in terms of meeting internal and external stakeholder
demands as well as other long-term benefits.
Volunteer performance can be considered volunteering
activities, either direct or indirect and positive or negative,
that contribute to the attainment of an organization’s goals.
Volunteers are external human resources that comprise a type
of input and are often involved in ways that add value to
public agencies (Brudney and Meijs 2009). Effective manage-
ment and deployment processes are required to transform
volunteer activities into outputs. The production of outputs,
or public service provision, should contribute to enhancing
broader outcomes for society. Thus, the relationship between

The traditional HRM approach to volunteer management
generally prescribes a top–down, linear, predictable, and func-
tional model of volunteer input to an organization (Macduff
et al. 2009; Studer 2016). This approach centers on the notion
of interchangeability in which a volunteer is a one-on-one
substitute for one paid employee (Bowman 2009; Handy et
al. 2008). This suggests that the contributions of volunteers to
organizational performance may occur in a linear manner. To
test this assumption, the following estimation model regresses
some measure of performance, P, on volunteer involvement,
V, on a set of managerial variables, M, and on a vector of en-
vironmental influences, X, thought to influence performance
(Masten 1993; O’Toole and Meier 1999):
P = α + β1V + β2M + β3X + ε

The coefficient β1 is intended to capture the contributions of
volunteers to performance. If volunteers and paid staff are a
perfect one-on-one substitute, volunteer use should positively

H1: Volunteer involvement will be positively associated
with organizational performance in a linear manner.
However, there are challenges to the assumption of perfect
interchangeability stemming from certain systematic limita-
tions of volunteer labor (Brown 1999; Brudney and Gazley
2002; Handy et al. 2008). For example, volunteers are not
legally bound by formal labor contracts, compensation does
not serve as a means of control, and volunteers can withhold
their labor more readily compared to paid staff (Alford and
O’Flynn 2012). By nature, most volunteers lack the formal
training and experience of paid staff, do not work full time,
and may display higher levels of absenteeism, turnover, and
unreliability in fulfilling work commitments (Brudney 1993;
Handy and Srinivasan 2004).
Transaction costs are an important concern due to limited
information for predicting how external entities will behave
(Brown and Potoski 2003). Traditional production factors in-
clude the costs of fixed assets, labor, and capital, whereas trans-
action costs pertain to the managerial costs associated with
“planning, adapting, and monitoring task completion under
alternative governing structures” (Williamson 1985, 553).
The “quasi-employment” nature of volunteer involvement in
public agencies renders the mode of coordination to be consid-
erably weaker compared to other modes such as compulsion
or contracts. Consequently, many scholars have questioned the
perfect interchangeability of volunteers and paid staff (Brown
1999; Brudney and Gazley 2002; Handy et al. 2008). For or-
ganizations that consider volunteers as fully interchangeable
with paid employees, replacement cost models may overstate
the value of volunteers if the level of productivity for one vol-
unteer is not equal to that of one paid employee. Volunteer in-
volvement may generate contradictory effects if organizations
do not sufficiently take into account such limitations.
H2: Due to the systematic limitations of volunteer labor,
volunteer involvement will be negatively associated with
organizational performance in a linear manner.
Public choice theory suggests that a key reason for resorting
to external arrangements is to increase efficiency as well as de-
crease the costs of service production (Boyne 1998). However,
not only do organizations incur costs to train, equip and su-
pervise volunteers, but also volunteers negatively impacting
performance may raise serious concerns about the viability of
volunteer use. The logic of a functional and linear approach
would dictate that organizations discard this arrangement al-
together. Nonetheless, efficiency is not the only criterion, and
there are many different reasons for involving volunteers as
well as a multiplicity of ways that volunteers may add value
to the process of service provision. The next section turns
to modeling an alternative approach for evaluating how
volunteers may contribute positively to performance, whereas
accounting for their systematic limitations.
Systematic Limitations of Volunteer Labor: A
Nonlinear Approach
Although the disadvantages of volunteer labor consider-
ably weaken the assumption of perfect interchangeability,
volunteers may possess some degree of capacity to contribute
positively to performance. For example, many public safety
organizations require volunteer firefighters and sworn volun-
teer police officers to receive a minimum number of training
hours and be certified prior to deployment (Brunet et al. 2001;
Wolf et al. 2016). Managerial initiatives may also improve
the process of volunteer recruitment, training, and retention,
as well as employ volunteer risk management strategies and
techniques to enhance monitoring and control (Graff 2011).
A small body of work that draws insight from the notion
of critical mass provides some theoretical groundwork into
devising an alternative approach. Brudney and Duncombe
(1992) and Duncombe and Brudney (1995), although they do
not specifically mention critical mass, discuss how organizations
must determine an optimal level of paid and volunteer labor
mix to maximize cost-effectiveness. As paid staff may be more
productive due to their training and experience, the demand
for volunteers as substitutes for paid labor will not be perfectly
elastic, and the use of paid staff becomes more cost-effective at
some threshold. Kang (2019) builds upon this line of reasoning
to explore how a critical mass of volunteers is required to gain
momentum in contributing positively to performance.
In the social sciences, scholars have discussed critical mass
in terms of how the interdependent actions of individuals
amass into collective action (Oliver 2013). In public adminis-
tration, the concept has been explored in areas such as repre-
sentative bureaucracy and government–nonprofit relationships
(Andrews et al. 2014; Cheng 2019; Nicholson-Crotty et al.
2017). Regarding the provision of collective goods, Oliver et
al. discuss the different roles of a small group of deliberate ac-
tors in advancing social change (Marwell and Oliver 1993).
Central to the theory is the production function y = f(x), which
examines the relationship between outputs of the collective
good (y) and inputs of participation or contribution (x). The
form of the production function varies according to different
types of collective action situations with different dynamics,
as “there is no unitary phenomenon of ‘collective action’ that
can be described by a simple set of theoretical propositions”
(Marwell and Oliver 1993, 3). Depending on variations in
heterogeneity and group size, the production function can as-
sume different shapes such as linear, accelerating, decelerating,
general third-order, and step functions (Oliver et al. 1985, 527).

Empirical Context: Policing
In the United States, many law enforcement agencies involve
volunteer officers, often called reserve/auxiliary personnel, in
a variety of core and auxiliary functions (Dobrin and Wolf
2016; Pepper and Wolf 2015). Reasons for adopting volunteer
officer programs include cost savings, enhancing service levels,
and improving community relationships and local legitimacy
and accountability (Dobrin and Wolf 2016; Fredericksen and
Levin 2004; Wolf et al. 2015). However, there is no single def-
inition or category of “volunteer officers,” and scholars point
to the widespread variation in authorization and certification
of volunteers, statutory requirements, training, and utilization
of volunteer officers across the nation (Dobrin 2017; Malega
and Garner 2019; Wolf and Bryer 2020).1 Many states re-
quire volunteers to undergo rigorous training and certifica-
tion, which may often be the same as that of full-time sworn
(FTS) officers.2 Such volunteers may be sworn, certified, and
endowed with nearly the same level of authority to engage
in core duties on par with FTS officers. In other states and
jurisdictions; however, volunteers may be sworn but endowed
with limited authority, and others may not be sworn or cer-
tified at all and tasked primarily with complementary duties
(Wolf and Bryer 2020).
Such divergent definitions and characteristics of volunteer
officers generate issues of validity and reliability concerning
what constitutes a volunteer officer. This study narrows the
focus on the category of sworn volunteers who are authorized
to perform most, if not all, tasks and responsibilities of FTS
officers (Dobrin and Wolf 2016). The Department of Justice
(DOJ) Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS) has collected in-
formation on the number of unpaid reserve/auxiliary officers
in law enforcement agencies through multiple waves of the
Law Enforcement Management and Administrative Statistics
(LEMAS) surveys.3 Although different versions contain data
on different types of volunteer officers, unpaid sworn officers
with general arrest powers are the only measure consistently
available across multiple LEMAS waves.
Concerning performance measurement, if sworn volun-
teer officers work full time and are qualified on par with FTS
officers, a linear approach would suit the measurement of vol-
unteer performance. However, most sworn volunteers work
fewer hours, possess less training and experience, and display
higher turnover (Dobrin and Wolf 2016). For example, Level I
and II Reserve Officers in the LAPD Reserve Officer Program
who are unpaid and authorized to carry firearms do not serve
full time, but “work a minimum of 32 hours every two 28-day
deployment periods and attend mandatory monthly reserve
officer meetings” (Los Angeles Police Department, n.d.).
Sworn volunteer officers are not intended to substitute whole-
sale for FTS officers, which reinforces the need to supplement
the linear approach with a nonlinear estimation strategy.
Among different measures of performance, this study
examines two: the crime rate and the clearance rate (Bureau
of Justice Assistance 2008). The crime rate is one of the most
widely used, traditional measures of police performance and is
reflective of the common perception that reducing crime is a core
task of the police. However, the impact of police work on crime
rates is indirect and weaker, given that education levels, poverty
rates, age, drug use and mental health issues, and a host of other
socioeconomic and demographic circumstances influence crime.
This study notes that the analysis of crime rates may be more
exploratory in nature due to such associated weaknesses.
Second, the clearance rate reflects the “proportion of crimes
solved by a police jurisdiction in a given time period” (Maslov
2016, 10). Police work is considered to exert a more direct
effect through the ability of police officers to solve crimes
through arrests (Nicholson-Crotty and O’Toole 2004; Paré
et al. 2007). However, clearance rates suffer from their own
set of reliability and validity issues with regard to variations
in the definition and measurement of a “cleared” incident, as
well as the possibility of data manipulation to increase per-
formance in a statistical sense (Maguire 2007; Maslov 2016;
Paré et al. 2007). Also, smaller communities may display
lower crime rates, leading to fewer opportunities to clear
crimes, thereby displaying lower performance. Nonetheless,
the clearance rate is relatively more advantageous over the
crime rate in reflecting greater managerial influence over po-
lice performance (Nicholson-Crotty and O’Toole 2004).

Dependent Variables
The crime rate is measured as the number of crimes re-
ported divided by a population figure. This study calculates
the number of crimes reported per 1,000 persons. The
crime measures are separated into violent crimes (aggra-
vated assault, criminal homicide, forcible rape, and rob-
bery) and property crimes (arson, burglary, larceny-theft,
and motor vehicle theft) since the task environments vary
between the two. Next, the clearance rate is calculated as
the total number of crime cases within a given year that are
cleared by arrest and divided by the total number of actual
crimes within the year, and multiplied by 100.10 The model
estimates violent crime clearance and property crime clear-
ance separately.

Management
This model includes additional variables from the LEMAS
survey concerning the extent to which different managerial
and organizational factors serve to enhance or inhibit agency
performance (Eck and Maguire 2000; Nicholson-Crotty
and O’Toole 2004; Ouimet and Pare 2004; Paré et al. 2007;
Parker et al. 2005). For convenience, these comprise the
variables that are lumped into the category of management,
M, in equations (1) and (2).
The model includes a variable for the total number of FTS
officers per 1,000 general population. This signifies agency
size as well as resources available to an agency. Next, the
crime case workload is measured separately by the number
of violent and property crimes per sworn officer. A higher
workload is expected to be associated with lower perfor-
mance, since officers may have less time and resources avail-
able for handling a particular crime case (Paré et al. 2007).
Resource availability is measured using the percentage of
FTS officers engaged in duties other than patrol and investi-
gation such as administration, technical support, jail-related
duties, court-related duties, and other auxiliary activities.
Such functions may divert FTS personnel from core to pe-
ripheral activities, leading to lower performance (Nicholson-
Crotty and O’Toole 2004).
Technological initiatives may serve to enhance the capability
of agencies to clear crimes (Strom 2017). The model includes
a binary variable of whether officers in the field can transmit
criminal incident reports using in-car or mobile laptop or tablet
(1 = yes, 0 = no). The model also includes a factor score of
whether field/patrol officers have direct access to the following
types of information using in-field vehicle-mounted or mobile
computers: motor vehicle records, driving records, criminal
history records, warrants, protection orders, inter-agency in-
formation systems, address history, and crime statistics/map-
ping. As all the subitems are binary, a polychoric technique
was used to examine factor scores based on eigenvalues above
1, and factor loadings above 0.40 were retained.
Another constraint on both performance and volunteer use
may be the role of unions. Research on the influence of police
unions on performance is limited, and the findings are mixed
depending on the type of performance measure used (Byrne
et al. 1996; Lee 2020). In theory, police unions are generally
perceived to be barriers on police performance and innova-
tion, as they may exert considerable influence over the de-
ployment of officers, expansion of innovative programs, and
disciplinary actions (Morabito 2014; Walker 2008). Union
influence may also negatively affect volunteer involvement
if the latter are perceived as a threat by existing employees
(Kang 2021). The model includes a binary variable coded
one if the agency authorizes collective bargaining for sworn
officers, and this variable is interacted with volunteers to
examine whether union influence moderates the impact of
volunteers on police performance.
Control Variables
The model controls for several demographic and environ-
mental variables. Specifically, the model includes the per-
centage of minority population, percentage of households
living under the federal poverty level, population size, and
the number of nonprofits focusing on addressing crime pre-
vention. Regarding the latter, based on Sharkey et al.’s (2017)
analysis of the NTEE-CC classifications of community
nonprofits focusing on crime prevention, the model includes
the total number of 501(c)(3) tax-exempt charitable organ-
izations focused on crime prevention per 100,000 residents
registered between 1999 and 2015 in each city.
Control variables comprise the external environment in
which a law enforcement agency is located, and their effects
on police performance may vary. Minority populations tend
to reside in localities of lower socioeconomic status, which
may give rise to a more difficult policing environment.
However, police officers might make more arrests so that per-
formance seems higher in such areas. Larger agencies in more
urban and populated jurisdictions possess more resources,
indicating greater capacity to address crime. However, a more
difficult policing environment may pose greater challenges in
solving crimes through arrest. Meanwhile, nonprofits that ac-
tively engage in community efforts to address crime such as
developing youth programs, provision of private security, or
directly working against violence may have an effect on re-
ducing crimes (Sharkey et al. 2017). The descriptive statistics
for the panel data are presented in Tables 1 and 2, whereas the
correlation matrices are provided in Supplementary Material,
Appendix 3. The model analyzes police departments and
sheriffs offices separately as the legal requirements and tasks
of the latter somewhat vary between the two.11
Estimation Methods
This study uses two-way fixed effects (FE) and lagged dependent
variable (LDV) modeling to analyze the relationship between
volunteer involvement and multiple measures of police perfor-
mance. The Arellano–Bond generalized method of moments
(GMM) estimator is conducted as a final robustness check.
The latter is not used in the initial steps because of its design
in use for situations with a linear functional relationship. This
strategy is used for the following reasons. First, the Hausman
test indicates that fixed-effects are preferable over random
effects estimation, suggesting that unobserved, locality-specific,
time-invariant features may influence the dependent variables.
Second, prior studies suggest the importance of accounting
for the temporal dependence of past performance on current
performance (O’Toole and Meier 1999; Zhu 2012). The pres-
ence of serial correlation also provides statistical support that
the present depends on the past. However, combining FEs
and LDVs into a single model may generate a “Nickell bias”
in which the LDV is correlated with the disturbance (Nickell
1981; Zhu 2012). Although the GMM estimator can overcome
this challenge by generating a set of internal instruments for
endogenous variables based on their lags (Arellano and Bond
1991; Roodman 2009), this study estimates the FE and LDV
models separately to strengthen overall robustness (Cheng

The results for police departments in Table 3 display signif-
icant and curvilinear effects of volunteers on clearance rates,
providing some degree of support for Hypothesis 3. However,
the results vary by type of model specification. The FE model
for clearing violent crimes displays a U-shaped relationship
in which an increase in volunteers initially predicts a nega-
tive impact on performance, whereas the relationship turns
positive after a threshold. The LDV model displays an in-
verse U-shaped relationship in which volunteers contribute
positively to performance up to a threshold, but the impact
turns negative afterwards. For clearing property crimes, a
linear negative effect appears in the FE model, whereas the
LDV model displays an inverse U-shaped relationship be-
tween volunteers and performance. The discrepancies in the
shape of the curvilinear relationships may be attributable to
the highly sensitive nature of different model specifications
and their underlying assumptions. The FE model assumes that
each unit has a separate effect constant over time, whereas
the LDV model presumes that anything specific about a unit
is captured through the value of the dependent variable from
the previous period (Angrist and Pischke 2009). However, re-
call that using both the LDV and FE models have a bounding
effect on the coefficient of interest. Examined as a whole, the
FE and LDV estimations display a negative first derivative
and a negative second derivative, although some positive ex-
treme may exist between the two. This point is revisited in the
GMM estimations below.
Turning to sheriffs offices in Table 4, the FE and LDV models
produce more consistent findings, supporting Hypothesis 2.
For clearing violent crimes, the coefficients for the linear terms
are significant and negative in both the FE and LDV models,
whereas the squared terms are not significant. Substantively, an
increase in one sworn volunteer per 100 FTS personnel predicts
about a 0.2%–0.5% decrease in the violent crime clearance
rate. The substantially smaller coefficients from the LDV
estimates support the expected properties of using both the FE
and LDV models. For property crime clearance, the results are
not significant in the FE models, whereas the coefficient for the
linear term in the LDV model is significant and negative.
The GMM estimates in Table 5 serve as a robustness check
to overcome the limitations of estimating the FE and LDV
models separately and also to alleviate the issue of endogeneity
of prior performance influencing changes in volunteer use.
The Arellano–Bond tests for serial correlation did not display
second-order serial correlation in all models. The Hansen
overidentification tests of joint validity of GMM instruments
did not reject the null hypothesis of no overidentified
instruments. The difference-in-Hansen tests of exogeneity of
instruments also displayed insignificant p-values. All models
pass the assumptions of the system GMM estimator and dis-
play confidence that the GMM estimates are not driven by
weak instruments.
For police departments, the variable for volunteers is not
significant for clearing either violent or property crimes.
However, this does not necessarily mean that volunteers have
a null effect on performance. Rather, the findings reinforce
the long-term bounding effects of the curvilinear relationships
displayed in the FE and LDV models for police departments.
Concerning sheriffs’ offices, the findings for clearance rates
are also largely consistent with the FE and LDV estimates in
that volunteers display a significant and linear decreasing ef-
fect on clearing violent crimes. Substantively, an increase in
one sworn volunteer per 100 FTS personnel predicts approx-
imately a 0.4% decrease in the violent crime clearance rate,
lending support to the bounding effect of the FE and LDV
estimates. Another significant result is the decreasing effect of
volunteers on clearing property crimes, although the findings
from the FE and LDV models were not significant. Overall,
the most consistent and robust finding from the FE, LDV,
and GMM models regarding volunteer involvement is evi-
dent for clearing violent crimes in sheriffs offices which high-
light a linear negative relationship, supporting Hypothesis 2
when using the violent crime clearance rate as the measure of
performance.
The interaction of collective bargaining and volunteers
is briefly noted. Although there are no consistent findings
across the models, the interaction terms suggest a mitigating
effect of volunteer contributions to performance, particu-
larly for clearance rates. For example, in Table 3, the size of
the negative effect of volunteers on clearing violent crimes
increases from 0.443 to 0.651 for the FE quadratic model
that includes the interaction term. In the LDV models for
both violent crime and property crime clearance, the positive
linear coefficients are no longer significant in the quadratic
models that include an interaction term. Despite some limi-
tations with operationalization, the inclusion of the interac-
tion term provides some evidence of the challenges presented
by police unions concerning the use of volunteers in police
organizations.
Discussion
The comprehensive findings from the three model estimations
indicate that an increase in volunteers display a linear negative
effect on agency performance as measured by the clearance of
violent crimes in sheriffs offices. Substantively, from a linear
approach, volunteer involvement may produce contradictory
effects if public agencies do not sufficiently take into account
the systematic limitations of volunteer labor. However, the
nonlinear results from the FE and LDV estimations merit
further discussion, because the system GMM estimator is
designed for use in situations with a linear functional relation-
ship. For instance, the findings for police departments in Table
3 provide some evidence of a curvilinear relationship between
volunteers and clearing violent crimes, although the effects
varied by model specification. The non-significant findings of
the GMM estimations do not suggest that volunteers have a
null effect, but rather they support the statistical bounding
effect of the FE and LDV models. Substantively, from a non-
linear approach, the results lend evidence to the critical mass
and threshold effects of volunteers in contributing to agency
performance, particularly in police departments.
The results highlight another important implication re-
garding the differences in the findings between police
departments and sheriffs’ offices. On the one hand, the varia-
tions in the legal requirements and tasks between the two may

demands, although police departments operate in more urban
areas with higher service demands. Given that volunteer use
in sheriffs offices is already quite high and possibly beyond
a threshold, an even greater increase in volunteers from cur-
rent levels may be contributing to a negative impact on per-
formance. Conversely, in police departments, as the ratio of
volunteers is small to begin with, there is room for a crit-
ical mass of volunteer officers to positively contribute to
performance.

This study provides important implications for practice as
well. It is important to consider both linear and nonlinear
approaches to evaluating volunteer contributions to perfor-
mance, and organizations may benefit from more deliberate
planning and management of volunteers. Examining data
on service measurability and transaction costs (Brown and
Potoski 2003), a considerable amount of volunteer use occurs
in service areas in which the outcomes are difficult to measure
(Nesbit and Brudney 2013). Many public organizations may
involve volunteers without precise information about whether
volunteers are actually beneficial or not. There are variety of
ways that volunteers may add value to public agencies, but
the empirical question boils down to how and to what extent.
Agencies must look beyond normative assumptions about the
advantages of volunteer labor or be cautious of focusing on
limited aspects such as cost savings. Volunteers are not entirely
“free” labor, because many public organizations may devote
considerable budgets on training and education, equipping
and supervising volunteers (Brudney and Kellough 2000).
Moreover, if volunteers negatively contribute to performance,
this may raise doubts about the value of volunteers. Volunteer
management based on a linear and functional approach may
lead to considerations of discarding volunteer use altogether.
However, a flexible and nonlinear approach that is more stra-
tegic at weighing the advantages and disadvantages of volun-
teer labor can enable more effective and accurate volunteer
utilization and outcome assessments.
There are several limitations of this study. One issue
concerns the use of the crime rate as a performance indicator.
Although the measure has been and continues to be a popular
measure, analyses tend to be more exploratory in nature due
to the associated weaknesses of police work on crime rates. It
is difficult to isolate the direct impact of law enforcement ac-
tivities due to the extreme complexity of the policing environ-
ment as well as the myriad of external forces that are beyond
the control of police organizations.
Another issue concerns the empirical case, which is con-
fined to policing in the United States and may limit the gen-
eralizability of the findings. Whereas focusing attention on
a particular service realm enables us to better control for
many of the factors thought to influence volunteer use and
performance (Masten 1993), law enforcement organizations
are typically hierarchical and centralized. Volunteer use may
vary from other types of service organizations that are more
decentralized and flexible, and such variations may need to
be considered. Studies seeking to measure volunteer perfor-
mance may explore other service realms to test the modeling
strategy of this study and validate the findings.
Additional methodological limitations offer avenues
for future research. This study focuses on the number of
volunteers only, rendering it difficult to address in-depth
questions regarding why and how. Future data collection
efforts may focus on information such as the number of vol-
unteer hours, roles and functions, budgets and expenditures,
and other key information. Meanwhile, as different model
specifications produced conflicting statistical findings, qual-
itative research may aid in exploring the different roles and
functions that organizations assign to volunteers. Finally,
our sample is limited to large agencies, hindering the in-
vestigation of volunteer use in smaller agencies. Agency
size is an important factor associated with how volunteers
are utilized, especially given the disparities in resources be-
tween large and small organizations (Brudney and Kellough
2000; Nesbit et al. 2018). Scholars may explore volunteer
use in smaller agencies and employ methods that account
for measurement errors prevalent in data collected from
smaller jurisdictions. Future research may address these
limitations as data becomes available and utilize different
methodological techniques to enhance theoretical and sta-
tistical rigor.
Conclusion
Measuring performance is an important managerial and
practical agenda for local governments involved in the de-
livery of public services. As the adoption of externalized
service delivery arrangements has become widespread
and inseparable in today’s public service environment, an
important empirical question is how a specific produc-
tion mechanism functions as a critical determinant of or-
ganizational performance. Volunteer use is one alternative
in which public agencies incorporate lay citizens into the
organization through a quasi-employment relationship
and assign them with the same or similar tasks as those
of full-time employees. Volunteer use is widespread across
local governments, and numerous service domains utilize
volunteers in different ways. As few studies have empirically
explored the link between volunteer use and organizational
performance in the public sector, this study furnishes a theo-
retical and methodological contribution to the literature that
enables a more systematic assessment of the contributions
of volunteers. Future research may build upon this study to
explore performance in different service realms as well as il-
luminate the myriad ways that volunteers may add value to
the process of public service production.

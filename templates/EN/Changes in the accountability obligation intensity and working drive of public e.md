Abstract
Governments worldwide have established accountability as a key mechanism to promote public employees’ work behaviors. In this study,
we propose that changes in two critical aspects of accountability—intensity and obligation—have distinct effects on public employees’ work
drive. Increased accountability intensity, characterized by close supervision and strict sanctions, can reduce intrinsic motivation and decrease
public employees’ willingness to engage in their tasks proactively. Conversely, the imposition of an additional hierarchical accountability obli­
gation—the perceived duty to justify actions to higher-level administrative supervisors or political leaders—can either enhance or hinder work
drive, depending on whether public employees view it as an opportunity to gain benefits or as coercive pressure. To explore this, we conducted
a survey experiment with 1,221 public employees in China. Our results indicate that increased accountability intensity generally reduces public
employees’ work drive, although the impact of incremental accountability obligations on their work drive remains unclear. These findings deepen
our understanding of how changes in accountability influence the work drive of public employees.

Introduction
Accountability is widely acknowledged as a fundamental com­
ponent of contemporary democracies (Behn 2001; Bovens,
Schillemans, and Hart 2008; Brandsma and Schillemans 2013;
Busuioc and Lodge 2017; Han and Robertson 2021; Li and
Yu 2025a, 2025b; Li, Koppenjan, and Wang 2024; Overman,
Schillemans, and Grimmelikhuijsen 2020; Schillemans and
Busuioc 2015). Governments have implemented various ac­
countability mechanisms to foster positive work behavior
among public employees (Aleksovska, Schillemans, and
Grimmelikhuijsen 2022; Ali and Nicholson-Crotty 2021;
Bovens and Schillemans 2014; Kim and Lee 2010; Ossege
2012; Schillemans and Aleksovska 2024; Tu and Gong 2022;
Ye, Ma, and Li 2023; Zhou, Fu, and Hu 2025). However, a
growing body of literature has recognized the intricate rela­
tionship between accountability and diverse work behaviors
of public employees, including priority setting (Aleksovska,
Schillemans, and Grimmelikhuijsen 2022), prosocial behavior
and political behavior (Ossege 2012), efficiency, respon­
siveness to citizens, integrity (Han and Robertson 2021),
decision-making efforts, and impartiality (Aleksovska 2021).
A potential explanation for these observations is that account­
ability is a multidimensional concept characterized by var­
ious attributes, including intensity, focus, timing, probability
of sanctions, authority/expertise, orientation, and implemen­
tation styles (Aleksovska and Schillemans 2021; Aleksovska,
Schillemans, and Grimmelikhuijsen 2022; Li and Yu 2025b;
Schillemans 2016). This prompts a crucial inquiry into how
the distinct features of accountability mechanisms affect the
work behaviors of public employees.
In this contribution, our primary focus is on the re­
lationship between changes in two key features of ac­
countability mechanisms—accountability obligation and
accountability intensity—and one critical work behavior of
public employees, namely work drive (Lounsbury, Gibson,
and Hamrick 2003, 2004). These two changes relate to two

Analytic framework and hypotheses
Work drive of public employees
Work affords numerous advantages for public employees,
such as financial compensation, a sense of belonging, formal
relationships, and a defined identity (Ogbonnaya et al. 2022).
Nevertheless, these favorable aspects of employment do not
invariably compel public employees to engage in excessive
and compulsive work behaviors. The concept of a work drive
does not imply that individuals labor excessively with min­
imal enjoyment. Rather, it underscores an individual’s moti­
vation to dedicate energy, time, and effort to accomplishing
predetermined job-related objectives (Andreassen et al.
2012). This characteristic represents a psychological trait
related to the workplace that encompasses an individual’s
sustained motivation to invest time and effort in completing
work-related tasks, adhering to deadlines, and attaining suc­
cess (Lounsbury, Gibson, and Hamrick 2003, 2004).
In this study, we adhered to the definition provided by
Lounsbury, Gibson, and Hamrick (2004) definition, which
characterizes work drive as “an individual’s disposition to
work long hours and extend oneself, when needed, to meet
job demands and achieve job success” (p. 427). It is imper­
ative to acknowledge that in our study, we conceptualize
work drive predominantly as intrinsic motivation, reflecting
a profound, self-directed commitment to engaging in work-
related tasks. From a theoretical standpoint, this emphasis is
vital as it fosters a deeper comprehension of the underlying
mechanisms that drive public employees to excel in their roles,
independent of external rewards such as salary, promotions,
or recognition. From a practical perspective, work drive, un­
derstood as intrinsic motivation, demonstrates a significantly
greater value, as it signifies a substantial level of commit­
ment among public employees to fulfill their responsibilities.
Intrinsic motivation engenders various benefits unattain­
able through extrinsic motivation alone, including sustained
job satisfaction, enhanced retention rates, and improved
adaptability.
In our study, we investigate the relationship between
changes in two key accountability mechanism features—ac­
countability obligation and accountability intensity—and the
work drive of Chinese public employees. In what follows, we
elaborate on how changes in these two features relate to the
work drive of public employees.
Two changes in accountability and their influence
on the work drive of public employees
Accountability is widely recognized as an evergreen con­
cept in the field of public administration and has received
considerable scholarly attention (Behn 2001; Brandsma
and Schillemans 2013; Busuioc and Lodge 2017; Li 2024;
Romzek and Dubnick 1987; Schillemans and Busuioc 2015;
Yang 2012). Arguably, the benefits of accountability are mul­
tiple, including strengthening the perceived legitimacy of the
government and increasing citizens’ trust in the government
(Bovens 2010). However, the authors have defined it differ­
ently, which has resulted in its ever-expansion (Li, Koppenjan,
and Wang 2024; Mulgan 2000).
In recent years, an increasing number of scholars in the field of
public administration have begun to concentrate on individuals’
perceptions of accountability (Aleksovska and Schillemans
2021; Aleksovska, Schillemans, and Grimmelikhuijsen 2022;
Han and Robertson 2021; Lerner and Tetlock 1999; Li and Yu
2025a, 2025b; Tu and Gong 2022). As Overman, Schillemans,
and Grimmelikhuijsen (2020) have contended, “individual
perceptions are crucial to our understanding of when and
how formal accountability mechanisms ‘work’” (p. 1,749).
Felt accountability aligns with a phenomenological perspec­
tive, emphasizing that diverse account givers may develop
distinct perceptions of the same accountability mechanism
(Han and Robertson 2021). Furthermore, some scholars have
examined the sub-dimensions of felt accountability in greater
detail. For instance, Han and Perry (2019) identify five key
dimensions: observability (the presence of others), evaluability
(assessment by others based on rules), attributability (the cor­
relation between actions and self), answerability (the obliga­
tion to justify actions), and consequentiality (the rewards or
sanctions resulting from actions). These studies have enhanced
our understanding of the subjective nature of accountability,
contributing to the advancement of accountability research.
Furthermore, a significant distinction in the nature of ac­
countability resides in its interpretation as either virtue (in­
trinsic accountability) or mechanism (extrinsic accountability)
(Bovens 2007; Li and Yu 2025b). Intrinsic accountability
primarily pertains to the internalized sense of responsibility
among public employees, while extrinsic accountability
includes external stimuli such as sanctions, assessments, and
supervision (Bovens, Schillemans, and Hart 2008). This study
concentrates on extrinsic accountability, as its main objective
is to investigate how external accountability mechanisms such
as assessments, audits, supervision, and sanctions influence
public employees’ work drive (Aleksovska and Schillemans
2021). Accountability scholars have emphasized the signifi­
cance of cultivating effective and meaningful accountability
mechanisms. By focusing on extrinsic accountability, this
study identifies the mechanisms through which meaningful
accountability is established (Bovens, Schillemans, and Hart
2014; Schillemans and Aleksovska 2024).

The change in the obligation of accountability and work
drive of public employees
Accountability obligations fundamentally revolve around
the question of to whom account givers must justify their
actions (Behn 2001; Bovens and Schillemans 2014; Li and
Yu 2025b; Mulgan 2000; Thomann, Hupe, and Sager 2018).
Existing literature distinguishes between two primary forms
of accountability obligations: hierarchical and horizontal
(Busuioc and Lodge 2017; Li, Qin, and Koppenjan 2022;
Romzek and Dubnick 1987). In this study, hierarchical ob­
ligation is conceptualized as the expectations imposed by
superior authorities, particularly higher-level administra­
tive supervisors or political leaders (Bovens, Schillemans,
and Hart 2008; Brandsma and Schillemans 2013). This
obligation primarily aligns with principal–agent theory,
highlighting formality, authority, power, sanction, and obedi­
ence (Aleksovska, Schillemans, and Grimmelikhuijsen 2022;
Romzek and Dubnick 1987). Specifically, it seeks to introduce
stringent rule-based accountability requirements to manage
the behaviors of public employees (Li, Qin, and Koppenjan
2022; Tu and Gong 2022).
In Western democracies, hierarchical obligations prima­
rily pertain to administrative and political accountabilities
(Bovens 2010; Busuioc and Lodge 2017; Romzek and
Dubnick 1987). The former requires public employees to ad­
here to formal administrative rules and regulations, whereas
the latter predominantly concerns the electoral repercussions
of political actors’ unmet commitments (Schillemans 2016).
Administrative and political accountability have also been
recognized within the Chinese context, which places signifi­
cant emphasis on hierarchy, control, upward accountability,
and obedience (Li 2023; Li and Yu 2025b; Tu and Gong
2022). Lower-level public employees are predominantly ac­
countable to their higher-level supervisors or political leaders,
interpreting their compliance as a form of political correct­
ness that warrants serious consideration (Ye, Ma, and Li
2023; Zhou, Fu, and Hu 2025). In this discourse, hierarchical
obligation primarily refers to expectations established by
higher-level supervisors. Subsequently, the term hierarchical
obligation will be used in this context.
In contrast to hierarchical obligations, horizontal
obligations are defined as expectations imposed by var­
ious social actors, including citizens, media, and non-
governmental organizations (Bovens, Schillemans, and
Hart 2008). Rooted in the principles of affected rights
and interests, these obligations encapsulate societal
demands for accountability (Mulgan 2000; Schillemans
and Busuioc 2015). However, unlike formal authorities, so­
cial actors lack the institutional power necessary to impose
sanctions on public employees (Romzek and Dubnick 1987;
Schillemans 2016). Their influence is manifested through
informal mechanisms, primarily by shaping reputations
(Busuioc and Lodge 2017; Ye, Ma, and Li 2023). In both
Western democracies and China, various social actors can
utilize either formal channels (such as petitions or letters
and visits) or informal channels (including media engage­
ment) to articulate their opinions, concerns, and criticisms,
thereby influencing government accountability activities
(Bovens and Schillemans 2014; Li 2023). Although hori­
zontal obligations may encompass accountability expecta­
tions from various social actors, this discourse primarily
focuses on citizens (Li, Qin, and Koppenjan 2022; Tu and
Gong 2022). Henceforth, the term “horizontal obligation”
will primarily pertain to this demographic.
Concerning the relationship between the implementation
of an additional accountability obligation and the work drive
of public employees, two contradictory conclusions can be
derived from three theories, namely motivation crowding
theory (MCT), reputation management theory, and arousal
theory (Busuioc and Lodge 2017; Deci and Ryan 2012; Deci,
Olafsen, and Ryan 2017; Hanoch and Vitouch 2004; Lerner
and Tetlock 1999; Ye, Ma, and Li 2023). MCT posits that
external incentives and punitive measures may detract from
intrinsic motivation (Titmuss 1970). This phenomenon is
commonly referred to as the “crowding-out effect,” which has
been substantiated in numerous studies (Frey and Jegen 2001).
According to MCT, the enforcement of an excessively strin­
gent or punitive accountability mechanism, which is charac­
teristic of hierarchical accountability obligations, diminishes
the intrinsic motivation to exert effort (Deci, Olafsen, and
Ryan 2017). Compared to horizontal accountability, the in­
troduction of an add-on hierarchical obligation for public
employees may lead to increased nervousness, feelings of
exhaustion, and defensive behaviors aimed at delaying the
completion of job-related tasks (De Cremer 2003; Li, Qin,
and Koppenjan 2022). Furthermore, the implementation of
overly stringent accountability measures through hierarchical
accountability forums may convey negative signals to public
employees, suggesting heightened levels of scrutiny, thereby
culminating in uncertainty and anxiety regarding job security.
Ultimately, these factors may reduce public employees' work
drive (Cavanaugh et al. 2000).
Moreover, reputation management theory may further sub­
stantiate the assertion that horizontal obligation, rather than
hierarchical obligation, enhances public employees’ work
drive (Busuioc and Lodge 2017). According to reputation
management theory, public employees are likely to regard
their organizational reputation with great seriousness; in the
event of potential reputation losses, they are inclined to engage
in proactive measures, including a heightened work drive, to
address this issue (Bovens, Goodin, and Schillemans 2014;
Busuioc and Lodge 2017). We contend that although social
accountability instigated by citizens does not directly penalize
public employees, normative or reputational pressures, such
as expectations of responsiveness, moral obligation, or social
image, persist (Li and Yu 2025b; Zhou, Fu, and Hu 2025).
These pressures frequently imply significant normative rep­
utation pressures for public employees, motivating them to
exert efforts to fulfill the demands of horizontal accounta­
bility. Based on these arguments, we propose the following
hypothesis:
H1a (hierarchical obligation crowding-out hypothesis):
compared to the introduction of an incremental horizontal
obligation, a hierarchical one is more likely to diminish
public employees’ work drive.
Meanwhile, the literature suggests that arousal theory could
also provide some insights into the relationship between
imposing an incremental accountability obligation and the
work drive of public employees. According to this theory,
an individual’s level of arousal influences their motivation to
engage in certain behaviors, including work drive, which is
examined in this study due to its potential gains (Hanoch and
Vitouch 2004). Inspired by this, we assume that the presence

of others, notably referring to account holders, can effectively
motivate individuals to invest more effort and time in their
work (Tu and Gong 2022). Some studies have demonstrated
that public employees tend to engage in high-level group ac­
tivities when they expect their output to be identifiable (Lerner
and Tetlock 1999). In particular, engaging in an additional
hierarchical obligation in the Chinese context provides new
opportunities for public employees to show their abilities,
gain a good impression of hierarchical supervisors, and ob­

may invest more time and energy in fulfilling various hierar­
chical tasks (Laird et al. 2009).
By contrast, imposing an incremental horizontal obliga­
tion might not positively increase the work drive of public
employees because of its limited possibilities of arousal (Tu
and Gong 2022). The Yerkes-Dodson Law suggests that very
low levels of arousal can impair performance (Hanoch and
Vitouch 2004). Specifically, individuals may feel bored or dis­
engaged when arousal is low. In sum, compared with hor­
izontal accountability, hierarchical accountability can more
easily generate arousal, which encourages public employees
to effectively fulfill their job-related tasks (Lerner and Tetlock
1999). Based on these arguments, we propose an alternative
hypothesis to H1a:
H1b (hierarchical obligation opportunity hypothesis):
compared with imposing an incremental horizontal obli­
gation, a hierarchical one is more likely to increase public
employees’ work drive.
The change in the intensity of accountability and the work
drive of public employees
Accountability intensity is chiefly related to the frequency of
account-holding activities (Leidorf‐Tidå and De Boer 2023;
Tu and Gong 2022). It primarily refers to "the quantity of
account-holding activities. This includes how often forums
ask for reports or other types of performance information,
how often they ask (follow-up) questions and provide feed­
back about actor operations, how often they participate in
meetings or hearings, how often they voice praise or criti­
cism, or how often they use available control mechanisms"
(Leidorf‐Tidå and De Boer 2023, p. 217).
In this study, we draw on job demands–resources (JD-R)
theory to explain the relationship between changes in ac­
countability intensity and the work drive of public employees
(Bakker et al. 2007). A core premise of JD-R theory is that
public employees face two fundamental types of job char­
acteristics in their daily work: job demands, which require
effort and energy, and job resources, which provide moti­
vation and support (Bakker and Demerouti 2024). These
two components operate through distinct processes: job
demands are associated with exhaustion and burnout, while
job resources foster motivation and engagement (Bakker
and Demerouti 2017, 2024). When accountability demands
increase in frequency, implying high work demands, public
employees must constantly shift roles, adapt to the informa­
tion they provide, demonstrate an understanding of the diverse
interests and goals associated with accountability obligations,
continuously justify their decisions, and develop appropriate
responses (Cavanaugh et al. 2000). However, job resources—
such as autonomy, institutional support, or incentives—rarely
increase proportionally. Consequently, public employees are
more likely to experience stress, exhaustion, and reduced in­
trinsic motivation to invest efforts.
This assumption aligns with the broader literature, which
increasingly emphasizes that the effectiveness of account­
ability mechanisms depends not only on their quantity but
also on their quality (Halachimi 2014; Li and Yu 2025b). In
both accountability studies and practice, there is a growing
consensus that improving the quality of accountability is
more critical than simply increasing or decreasing the overall
burden. This approach necessitates a more careful look at
the design of accountability mechanisms and practices and
signals a shift in focus from demands for more (or less) ac­
countability to questions about what types of accountability
are relevant and the conditions and contexts in which they
are effective (Bovens and Schillemans 2014, p. 674). Many
public employees explicitly report that excessive account­
ability demands consume excessive time, making it diffi­
cult for them to focus on their core responsibilities (Pollitt
2003). Governors must design high-quality accountability
mechanisms to effectively and meaningfully enhance public
employees’ work drive. Based on these arguments, we pro­
pose the following hypothesis:
H2 (intensity hindrance hypothesis): an increase in ac­
countability intensity is negatively related to the work drive
of public employees.
The influence of interactions between changes in
accountability obligation and accountability intensity on
public employees’ work drive
As argued, the introduction of an additional hierarchical ob­
ligation is expected to influence public employees’ work drive
in multiple ways. Drawing on MCT, we posit that such an ob­
ligation, characterized by heightened control and compliance,
may lead to exhaustion and a decline in intrinsic motivation
(Deci, Olafsen, and Ryan 2017). Furthermore, an increase
in accountability intensity may exacerbate these negative
effects by imposing additional job demands and amplifying
the burden on public employees. Consequently, we argue that
heightened accountability intensity intensifies the detrimental
impact of introducing an additional hierarchical obligation
on employees’ work drive. Based on this reasoning, we pro­
pose the following hypothesis:
H3a (negative effect strengthening hypothesis): the nega­
tive impact of introducing an incremental hierarchical ob­
ligation on public employees’ work drive is exacerbated
when accountability intensity is increased.
However, arousal theory suggests that imposing an add-on
hierarchical obligation may create opportunities for public
employees to demonstrate their performance and capabilities
to various account holders, thereby stimulating their intrinsic
motivation to work harder (Hanoch and Vitouch 2004).
However, an increase in accountability intensity may weaken
this motivation due to excessive demand and insufficient re­
sources (Tu and Gong 2022). This is particularly relevant in
the Chinese context, where many public employees report
spending a significant amount of time navigating multiple
accountability mechanisms. The overwhelming burden of ac­
countability can diminish the arousal effects of hierarchical
obligations, ultimately undermining their positive impact (Ye,

Accountability of public employees in a
Chinese context
Similar to most Western democracies, the Chinese state
has established accountability as a fundamental approach
to safeguarding state legitimacy and good governance (Li
2023; Tu and Gong 2022; Ye, Ma, and Li 2023). In recent
decades, new frameworks, innovative mechanisms, and crea­
tive strategies have been developed to embed accountability
in political and administrative systems (Li and Yu 2025a;
Zhou, Fu, and Hu 2025). Undoubtedly, the institutionaliza­
tion of accountability within Chinese governance remains a
work in progress, rendering a comprehensive evaluation of
the accountability system challenging. However, some general
observations are made. On the one hand, the prevailing
trend indicates that public employees in China are increas­
ingly cognizant of diverse accountability expectations. They
acknowledge that multiple entities monitor their actions
and must, therefore, seriously consider these factors in their
policymaking and implementation processes (Tu and Gong
2022). Consequently, the responsiveness and answerability of
government employees have improved compared to previous
periods. Moreover, numerous local governments in China
have proactively developed initiatives, such as e-government,
one-stop government services, and online complaint resolu­
tion mechanisms, to better align with the expectations of var­
ious accountability platforms (Li, Qin, and Koppenjan 2022).
Collectively, these observations suggest that accountability
has increasingly emerged as a pivotal strategy adopted by the
state to guide public employees' conduct within the Chinese
context.
On the other hand, one may readily observe that the ex­
isting accountability framework in China continues to em­
phasize enhancing the capacity of hierarchical accountability
forums to regulate public employees better (Li 2023; Ye, Ma,
and Li 2023). In other words, internal control remains the pri­
mary strategy pursued by the central government to formu­
late nationwide accountability systems (Tu and Gong 2022).
The numerous new accountability mechanisms introduced by
the central government mainly concentrate on consolidating
and amplifying its oversight of local governments and public
employees. For example, the Chinese central government has
bolstered the disciplinary inspection system in recent years,
endowing the Discipline Inspection Bureau with substantial
authority to hold public employees accountable.
Simultaneously, it is important to note the presence of sev­
eral minor alterations worthy of attention. As previously
indicated, the Chinese accountability system emphasizes
the significance of hierarchical accountability mechanisms,
whereas internal control across the entire political framework
has been established as a pivotal strategy in the formulation
of accountability processes. An adverse outcome of this ap­
proach is that hierarchical accountability within the Chinese
context is disproportionately more pronounced than that
exercised by societal stakeholders (Tu and Gong 2022).
Authorities at higher levels implement various incentives and
deterrents, including cadre responsibility contracts, perfor­
mance assessments, and incentives for upward mobility, to
ensure accountability among public employees (Hu 2017).
Moreover, some innovative practices instituted by local
governments appear to convey additional signals. At the mu­
nicipal level, an increasing number of local governments are
emphasizing the significance of responsiveness and accounta­
bility to public concerns (Tu and Gong 2022). A pertinent ex­
ample is Nanjing municipality in China, which has permitted
citizens to assess the performance of public employees di­
rectly. Employees who receive low evaluation scores may be
subjected to severe formal sanctions imposed by the govern­
ment (Li, Qin, and Koppenjan 2022).
Data and method
Experimental design
This study adopts a large-N online survey experiment to test
our hypotheses raised previously. In particular, a survey exper­
iment was conducted to identify the causal effects of changes
in accountability obligation and accountability intensity on
public employees’ work drive. A key advantage of a survey
experiment is that it enables authors to precisely manipulate
experimental treatments and increase the internal validity
of the conclusions drawn (Halling and Petersen 2024). Our
study is designed as a vignette-based factorial survey, and a
between-subjects 2 × 2 factorial design has been applied (Li
and Yu 2025b). Before presenting our experimental treat­
ment, we developed a hypothetical situation related to the
implementation of a performance evaluation reform by local
governments. In the Chinese context, performance evaluation
is one of the most widely adopted accountability mechanisms
designed by local governments to steer the behavior of
public employees (Li, Qin, and Koppenjan 2022). All public
employees face different types of performance evaluations,
implying that our experiment instrument is highly realistic.
Our first part of the experimental manipulation is the changes
in accountability obligation: (1) adding a hierarchical obliga­
tion or (2) adding a horizontal obligation. The second part
relates to changes in accountability intensity: (1) increasing
the accountability frequency from half a year to a quarterly,
or (2) reducing the accountability frequency from a quarter
to half a year. Details of the four experimental vignettes are
listed in Table 1, and the original Chinese versions can be
found in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Subsequently, we employed the actual manipulation check
(FMC) questions, which evaluate respondents’ attentiveness
to and comprehension of the experimental manipulations
embedded within the scenarios by assessing their objective
responses to the factual information included in our ex­
periment (Kane and Barabas 2019). The FMC question re­
garding the changes in obligation of accountability was as
follows: “In the scenario you read above, which actor has
been newly engaged in the evaluation system: citizens, higher-
level government, or ‘I do not know’?” The FMC question
concerning the changes in intensity of accountability was as
follows: “In the scenario you read above, how does the eval­
uation frequency change: increased, decreased, or ‘I do not
know’?” Respondents who failed to answer these questions
did not pass the FMCs. In addition, although the majority
of MPA students work in the public sector, a small number
are employed by private enterprises and thus are not public
employees. Consequently, 56 respondents who indicated in
the workplace question that they work for private companies
were excluded. Finally, we obtained 1,221 valid questionnaires
for analysis, which exceeded the minimum of 1,056 indicated
by an a priori power analysis conducted in G*Power 3.1.9.7
with a small effect size of f = 0.10, α = 0.05, and power = 0.90.

Dependent variable
The dependent variable in this study is public employees’
perceived work drive. According to the literature, work drive
is essentially a multidimensional concept. We have followed
the study of Lounsbury, Gibson, and Hamrick (2003, 2004),
who have applied three items: “in the face of such evaluation
approach, I will spend more time in work; in the face of such
evaluation approach, I will spend more energy on work; and
in the face of such evaluation approach, I am willing to assume
more work responsibilities.” The respondents were asked to
score their degree of agreement with these statements, which
ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).
Regarding validity analysis, a confirmatory factor analysis
was conducted on the work drive scale. The standardized
factor loadings of all observed variables were found to be sig­
nificant (P = .000) and exhibited substantial values (for in­
stance, the factor loading was 0.862 for work time, 0.992 for
work energy, and 0.740 for work duty). Convergent validity
was supported by an average variance extracted (AVE) of
0.758, exceeding the 0.50 threshold, and composite reliability
(CR) was 0.902, surpassing the 0.70 criterion. Regarding reli­
ability, the McDonald’s omega for the three items was 0.903,
signifying a high level of internal consistency among the
items. Finally, the mean score of the three items was used to
gauge the work drive of public employees in this study.
In subsequent analyses, demographic variables such as
gender and age, as well as job-related variables including work
tenure and rank, along with commonly utilized variables in
public employment research such as public service motivation
(PSM) (Bao and Li 2016; Breaugh, Ritz, and Alfes 2018) and
policy meaningfulness (Tummers and Bekkers 2014), were in­
corporated as control variables. The wording of their items is
provided in Supplementary Appendix 2.

Analysis and results
First, we conducted a mean estimation of the work drive
perceptions of our respondents across four experimental
groups (Table 3 and Figure 2). The ANOVA results indicated
statistically significant group differences (F = 15.72; P = .000),
implying that the experimental manipulations differentially
influenced participants’ work drive perceptions.
Subsequently, regression analysis was conducted to iden­
tify the assumed factors shaping the perceived work drive of
public employees. Figure 3 illustrates the regression results
regarding how changes in accountability obligation and in­
tensity, along with their interaction effect, influence this per­
ception (for details, see Supplementary Appendix 2). Model
1 examines the impact of two experimental manipulations,
changes of accountability obligation and changes of account­
ability intensity, on public employees’ perceived work drive.
Model 2 expands this analysis by incorporating control
variables. Model 3 further includes an interaction term be­
tween changes in accountability obligation and intensity. Both
Model 1 (B = −0.111, P = .183) and Model 2 (B = −0.145, P
= .082) indicate that, compared to horizontal accountability
obligations, the imposition of an additional hierarchical ac­
countability obligation negatively impacts the perceived work
drive of public employees, although this effect fell well short
of the conventional 0.05 significance threshold. This finding
does not support H1a or H1b. A pivotal implication of this
conclusion is that it may be imprudent for governors to adopt
new accountability mechanisms, regardless of whether they
are horizontal or hierarchical, to influence the work be­
havior of public employees. As previously mentioned, tradi­
tional governance practices suggest that governors typically
design new accountability mechanisms to enhance the work
drive of public employees (Aleksovska 2021; Aleksovska and
Schillemans 2021). However, our study does not fully endorse
this conventional wisdom because the introduction of a new
accountability mechanism may complicate public employees’
ability to reconcile job tensions and strain, thereby impeding
their work drive.
Concerning the impact of accountability intensity, the
findings from Model 1 indicate that an increase in accounta­
bility intensity leads to a reduction in work drive among public
employees. This outcome corroborates our second hypothesis
that a heightened intensity of accountability adversely affects
the work drive of public employees (B = −0.558, P = .000).
This observation supports the crowding-out mechanism,
which posits that intensive accountability may convey a neg­
ative signal to public employees, thereby compromising their
intrinsic motivation to exert greater effort. In Model 2, upon
the inclusion of control variables in our analysis, the results
remained consistent (B = −0.538, P = .000).
Regarding the interaction effect, the results from Model 3
indicate that, although the coefficient of the interaction term
“Obligation×Intensity” is negative, which corresponds with
the anticipated direction of both hypotheses, it fails to attain
statistical significance (B = −0.080, P = .627). This implies
that we cannot statistically affirm whether the impact of im­
position of an additional hierarchical obligation on work
drive will be significantly enhanced or mitigated as the inten­
sity of accountability increases. Therefore, neither H3a nor
H3b receives support.
We have also reported the analysis results for three separate
variables: work time, work energy, and work duty. Figure 4
presents the results of our analysis (for detailed information,
see Supplementary Appendix 3). Model 4 examines the effects
of changes in accountability obligation and intensity on the
work time of public employees. Model 5 analyzes the effects
of changes in accountability obligation and intensity on the
work energy of public employees, and Model 6 examines the
effects of changes in accountability obligation and intensity on
the work duty of public employees. The results of the analysis
were consistent among the three models. Compared to hori­
zontal accountability, the introduction of a new hierarchical
accountability obligation reduces public employees’ willing­
ness to invest time (B = −0.035) and energy (B = -0.142), and
take on duty at work (B = −0.134). However, none of these
effects are statistically significant. Additionally, increasing ac­
countability intensity significantly weakens the work drive
of public employees, which is manifested by a significant de­
crease in the time (B = −0.288, P = .033) and energy they in­
vest in work (B = −0.500, P = .000) and a greater reluctance
to take on duties (B = −0.704, P = .000).

Discussion
The long-standing Friedrich-Finer debate in public admin­
istration has underscored the need to carefully balance bu­
reaucratic discretion with the control mechanisms embedded
in accountability structures (Schillemans and Busuioc
2015). To address this debate, most scholars have relied on
principal–agent theory as a conceptual lens (Behn 2001;
Brandsma and Schillemans 2013; Overman, Schillemans,
and Grimmelikhuijsen 2020). Much of the existing litera­
ture examines accountability primarily from the perspec­
tive of recalcitrant actors such as public employees and
independent accountability forums (Schillemans 2016). This
perspective assumes that account givers are inherently op­
portunistic and may prioritize their personal goals at the ex­
pense of their principals’ interests when misalignment occurs
(Frederickson and Smith 2003). Principal–agent relationships
have played a central role in shaping hierarchical account­
ability frameworks that emphasize performance-driven and
control-based management approaches (Williamson 1975).
Consequently, many accountability mechanisms have been
deliberately designed within this paradigm, focusing primarily
on monitoring and compliance (Aleksovska and Schillemans
2021; Schillemans 2016). However, an increasing number of
scholars have critically reassessed this approach, arguing that
accountability practices rooted in principal–agent theory may
not be the most effective means of resolving the inherent ten­
sion between autonomy and control (Bovens, Goodin, and
Schillemans 2014; Li and Yu 2025b). For instance, MCT
suggests that excessively stringent regulations imposed by
account holders can trigger crowding-out effects, thereby
diminishing account givers’ intrinsic motivation to exert effort
(Frey and Jegen 2001). This insight challenges the traditional
compliance- and control-centric view of principal–agent
dynamics, urging account holders to design accountability
mechanisms that are more closely aligned with account givers’
perceptions and motivations to achieve more effective and
meaningful accountability (Li and Yu 2025b; Li, Koppenjan,
and Wang 2024; Ye, Ma, and Li 2023).
Furthermore, as our research has demonstrated, the inten­
sive account-holding activities imposed by account holders
may adversely affect the intrinsic motivation of public
employees to exert effort. This finding further reinforces the
JD-R model, which posits that governors should carefully
consider the balance of demands and resources for public
employees to promote positive work behaviors, including
the work drive analyzed in this study (Bakker et al. 2007;
Bakker and Demerouti 2024). Additionally, this outcome is
consistent with the latest practices, theoretical advancements,
and evolving frameworks within the realm of accountability,
as noted by de Boer (2023): “new practices are often enacted
outside the hierarchical relationship that is associated with
the ‘ideal-typical’ unitary model of delegation and account­
ability (p. 1131).” These emerging frameworks increasingly
adopt a trust-based approach that correlates with stewardship
theory (Segal and Lehrer 2012). In contrast to principal–agent
theory, stewardship theory offers an alternative conceptual
framework that emphasizes cooperation and mutual trust
rather than hierarchical control (Van Slyke 2007). It asserts
that altruism can influence the behavior of account givers and
that public sector organizations can enhance performance by
harnessing intrinsic motivation and internal accountability
mechanisms, rather than relying solely on external controls
(Roberts 2002). This perspective provides significant insights
into account holders’ roles in promoting effective and mean­
ingful accountability.
In short, it is imperative to reconsider the dominance of
principal–agent theory in contemporary accountability studies
and integrate these alternative insights and frameworks
(Bovens, Goodin, and Schillemans 2014). Doing so would
facilitate a more nuanced understanding of accountability,
helping to reconcile better the relationship between control
and autonomy in accountability practices.
Conclusion
Governance and policy authors have widely regarded ac­
countability as a key mechanism in promoting the work

drive of public employees (Aleksovska 2021; Aleksovska and
Schillemans 2021; Li 2023; Ye, Ma, and Li 2023). Sometimes,
they intentionally adjust the design of accountability
mechanisms to achieve this goal. In this study, our primary in­
terest lies in how involving in new hierarchical or horizontal
accountability, as well as reducing and increasing account­
ability intensity, would influence the work drive of public
employees in the Chinese context. To answer our research
questions, we have adopted a survey experiment approach
to investigate 1,221 public employees in China. Our analysis
has found that an increase in accountability intensity reduces
the work drive of public employees, although the impact of
accountability obligations on work drive remains unclear.
Our study contributes to the current governance and policy
literature in three ways. First, the understanding of how ac­
countability mechanisms lead to positive work performance
remains limited, and there is a call for further research to
explore the conditions under which accountability may re­
sult in positive behavioral changes (Bovens, Goodin, and
Schillemans 2014; Schillemans 2011; Yang 2012). Although
some scholars have investigated the influence of account­
ability on the behavior of public employees—including
decision-making efforts (Aleksovska 2021) and responsive­
ness, efficiency, and integrity (Han and Robertson 2021)—to
the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to examine
the influence regarding the changes of accountability inten­
sity and accountability obligation on work drive of public
employees. It contributes to the existing accountability litera­
ture by elucidating the link between accountability and work
drive through an examination of two under-theorized aspects
of account-holding activities–changes in accountability ob­
ligation and intensity–and by investigating how they relate
to account givers’ work drive. The prevailing consensus in
Western democracies posits that, in contrast to hierarchical
obligations, horizontal obligations may enhance work drive
(Schillemans and Busuioc 2015). However, the results of
our study do not support this hypothesis. One plausible ex­
planation is that it is insufficient to exclusively examine the
impact of accountability obligations on the work drive of
public employees; it is imperative to consider the adminis­
trative system, administrative culture, and other institutional
frameworks in our investigation.
Second, building upon the above point, our study
contributes to the prevailing literature on accountability by
offering insights that more accurately represent real-world
contexts. Some scholars have underscored the necessity of
considering the actual environment in which accountability
systems are instantiated when conducting research in this
area (Bovens, Goodin, and Schillemans 2014; Schillemans
2016). A pivotal innovation of this study is its alignment with
real-world scenarios, particularly through recognition of the
inherent dynamism present in accountability mechanisms.
While several studies continue to examine accountability
mechanisms from a static viewpoint, investigating the
relationships between various features of accountability
and the behavior of public employees (Aleksovska 2021;
Aleksovska and Schillemans 2021; Li and Yu 2025a, 2025b;
Tu and Gong 2022), they frequently neglect the dynamic
character of accountability. Account holders continuously ad­
just accountability mechanisms to influence the conduct of
public employees. Disregarding this dynamism may constrain
researchers’ abilities to derive solid, actionable conclusions
about real-world accountability practices (Bovens 2014; Li,
Qin, and Koppenjan 2022). By acknowledging the dynamic
nature of accountability, our study illuminates how alterations
in accountability mechanisms affect public employees’ moti­
vational drivers. This enables us to align our findings with
the practical reality better, culminating in more reliable and
persuasive conclusions.
Third, it enhances our understanding of the functions of ac­
countability in non-Western contexts. Nowadays, most studies
on accountability are exclusively based on practices in the US
and Europe in pluralistic democratic systems with specific
legal traditions and civil liberties (Aleksovska and Schillemans
2021; Aleksovska, Schillemans, and Grimmelikhuijsen 2022;
Bovens 2007; Dubnick and Frederickson 2011; Han and
Robertson 2021). Our knowledge of accountability in a non-
Western context is limited (some exceptions see: Li 2023; Li
and Yu 2025a, 2025b; Li, Koppenjan, and Wang 2024; Ye,
Ma, and Li 2023). This study aims to address this imbal­
ance by examining the relationship between the changes in
accountability mechanisms and work drive among Chinese
public employees. It illustrates that intensive accountability
might not facilitate work drive, which is applicable in both
Western and Chinese contexts, further proving the generali­
zation of the conclusion that accountability does not always
necessarily facilitate the work drive of public employees.
This study has several practical implications. As our results
have shown, it might not be feasible for governors to solely
add new accountability mechanisms to promote public
employees’ work drive. Triggered by principal-agent theory,
many studies have highlighted the relevance of engaging in
more accountability obligations, arguably to improve public
employees’ work drive (Dubnick and Frederickson 2011;
Olsen 2013). However, our study does not support this ar­
gument; adding a horizontal or hierarchical obligation may
not help increase public employees’ work drive. Therefore,
a practical suggestion is that governors should be cautious
when imposing new accountability obligations. Moreover,
our study suggests that increasing the intensity of accounta­
bility negatively influences public employees’ work drive. One
practical suggestion for governors is to pragmatically reduce
the frequency of accountability, which would help increase
the work drive of public employees and promote organiza­
tional performance.
Finally, this study has several limitations. Our study uses
an experimental design that ensures high internal validity. A
randomized survey experiment facilitates a causal interpre­
tation. Nevertheless, we should bear in mind its potential
shortcomings. One key limitation is that it does not examine
public employees’ perceptions in real accountability cases
but instead relies on a hypothetical case in an online survey.
Our vignette-based survey experiment does not fully expose
respondents to real-world governance practices, which is a
common challenge in accountability research that utilizes
this methodology. As Schillemans and Aleksovska (2024) ob­
serve, “capturing the reality of the high-pressure accounta­
bility contexts in which decisions are made is not a simple
task. Participants may not perceived the setting in the way
that was intended by the researchers. Artificially generated
accountability pressures in an experimental scenario do not
recreate the feelings and mental states of decision makers
in a high-pressure political setting” (p. 401). Consequently,
vignette-based survey experiments may prove less impactful

ABSTRACT
This study investigates how administrative burden in college admissions affects individuals with criminal records, with atten-
tion to racial disparities. Grounded in administrative burden theory and the role of street-­level bureaucrats, it examines how
admissions representatives respond to applicants with disclosed criminal histories. Through a correspondence audit experiment,
fictitious applicants—Tyrone (presumed Black) and Christopher (presumed White)—sent inquiries to institutions of higher ed-
ucation (IHE) about how a criminal record might affect admissions. While most responses did not differ significantly in tone,
warmth, or encouragement, Tyrone faced greater administrative burden in two key areas: Tyrone was more likely to receive a list
of formal procedural requirements and to experience longer response times. These differences reflect psychological and compli-
ance costs that disproportionately affect minoritized applicants. Findings underscore the need for targeted policy reforms that
reduce discretionary barriers and promote more equitable pathways into IHEs for individuals with criminal justice involvement.
Scholars across disciplines have increasingly focused on admin-
istrative burden theory (Ray et al. 2023; Masood and Nisar 2021;
Campbell et al. 2023). This interest has led to several contribu-
tions where scholarship shows the impact of administrative bur-
den on how everyday people interact with the state (Halling and
Baekgaard 2024). For instance, Heinrich et al. (2021) describe
how nonprofit organizations navigated administrative burden to
support disadvantaged families. In other studies, scholars find
that administrative burden can have a negative impact on the
wellbeing of the public (Martin et al. 2024). An additional layer
of complexity is introduced when one considers that attempts
to alleviate burden may also introduce others (Linos et al. 2022;
Astudillo-­Rodas et al. 2024). Such complexity is evidenced as
Astudillo-­Rodas et al. (2024) find that attempts to reduce compli-
ance costs within the childcare subsidy system created psycho-
logical costs for recipients, described as tortuous in some cases.
Thus, the literature shows that administrative burden appears
in different domains and even during instances in which efforts
aim to mitigate its impact.
One area that is gaining attention is the role of administrative
burden in higher education (Gándara et al. 2024), with contri-
butions focusing on the critical issue of education access (Bell
and Smith 2022). Within this literature, there remains an oppor-
tunity to better understand how administrative burden impacts
access to institutions of higher education (IHEs) at the intersec-
tion of race and criminal background. This is particularly im-
portant when considering the racial identity of applicants who,
absent a criminal record, already face challenges to navigating
higher education admissions (Hurtado et al. 1997) as observed
in affirmative-­action debates that underscore persistent racial
disparities in college admissions (Iceland et al. 2023; Ford 2023).
And, although the U.S. Supreme Court's 2023 decision addresses
how race can be used in final selection, the ruling highlights

1   |   Theory and Literature
1.1   |   Administrative Burden and Higher
Education
Theoretical views of administrative burden have been import-
ant for understanding the ways in which citizens are tasked with
navigating various bureaucratic structures linked to the state
(Herd et al. 2013; Heinrich 2016; Fox et al. 2023) and encounters
(Burden et al. 2012) that help understand whether the state is
working for its citizens. For the public, engaging the state in a
variety of ways is a condition of basic citizenship necessitating
the need to understand the impact of administrative burden.
To understand administrative burden, this article follows
Moynihan et al. (2015) stipulation that administrative burden is
the residual effects of state regulation of public services sought
by individuals, with three categories that provide a framework
for understanding the impact of administrative burden: learn-
ing, compliance, and psychological costs. Learning costs can
be understood as the process that citizens undergo to acquire
information about public services and their relevance to individ-
ual situations. Psychological costs include an intrinsic feeling of
inadequacy and powerlessness that citizens feel when pursuing
public services. Compliance costs represent the social and ma-
terial costs incurred by citizens when trying to meet adminis-
trative requirements. An important dimension of these costs is
that burden is differentially construed by individuals because of
previous experiences, which ultimately shape their perspective
of the state.
This view of administrative burden may also be extended to
thinking about the bureaucratic structure that defines the ad-
missions practices of IHEs (Woelert 2023). As entities provid-
ing public services, like education, it is appropriate and useful
to operationalize higher education as comprising institutions
that deliver a public good (e.g., Levesque-­Bristol and Cornelius-­
White  2012)—and are domains for administrative burden to
occur. The existence of a bureaucratic structure governing the
admissions process is accompanied by a variety of potential
learning costs associated with understanding application pro-
cedures, financial aid details, and institutional requirements
(Scott-­Clayton 2013). There are also psychological costs in
admission such as with students experiencing imposter syn-
drome, which influences their navigation of the admissions
process (Breeze et al. 2022). Compliance costs have been com-
mon in admissions as they relate to navigating the application
fee structure and financial process (Sturm et al. 2011; Bettinger
et al. 2012). While the Common Application has mitigated some
barriers to access, including uncertainty in financial aid awards
(e.g., Dynarski et  al.  2021), other challenges remain (Knight
and Schiff 2022). One such barrier is navigating the admissions
process with a criminal background and its influence on admis-
sions decisions (Evans, Blount-­Hill, and Cubellis 2019, Evans,
Szkola, and St John 2019). This is particularly true in navigating
IHE policies around criminal background checks, including the
specific kinds of materials that are required.

on two primary outcomes: the use of criminal background in-
formation in admissions decisions and the extent to which crim-
inal records hinder applicants' chances of admission. Pierce
et al. (2014) found that institutions that collected criminal back-
ground information were less likely to offer admission to such
applicants. Similarly, studies by Evans and colleagues (Evans,

on racial discrimination in other domains, such as employment
(Pager 2003) and housing (Pager and Shepherd 2008), where race
is predictive of procedural burden placed on applicants (Bell and
Jilke 2024; Aiken et al. 2023). The finding also aligns with the
history of race and the U.S. education system, such as it being
illegal for Black people to read and write (see Cunningham 2023
on the Negro Act of 1740 in South Carolina, the 1831 Virginia
Anti-­Literacy Laws, and more anti-­literacy laws); or educa-
tional segregation in the mid-­1900s (Warren 1954)—a practice
linked to disparities in access to quality education in modern
day (Frankel and Volij  2011). Together, the research and his-
torical policies of race and access to education in the U.S. bol-
ster the need for the exploration of IHE administrative burden
across race. This dovetails into research that makes clear that
burdens affect citizens differently (Bell et al. 2023; Döring and
Jonas  2022)—including racialized burden, which is a novel
and relevant theoretical subtype (Ray et  al.  2023; Gándara
et  al.  2024). Racialized burden is the psychological, learning,
and compliance costs that are experienced differently by vary-
ing racial groups and that contribute to racial inequities (Ray
et al. 2023). This viewpoint particularly highlights how admin-
istrative burden acts as a mechanism for worsening and repro-
ducing disparities, and it often occurs alongside other forms of
racial bias.
Given the existing racial disparities in accessing IHEs, there is
potential for the presence of racial differences across burden.
At the intersection of race and criminal history, the structur-
ally embedded nature of race might exacerbate the challenges
applicants with criminal records face within an administrative
system that is not historically race-­neutral (Ramaswamy 2015;
Blume  2023). While the extant literature has established that
criminal background and race can negatively affect college ad-
mission prospects, the matter is more complex than whether
someone with a criminal record gains admission. Understanding
aspects of the process associated with navigating college admis-
sions—namely the burden differences that may exist in modern
times—illuminates the nuances of how administrative burden
occurs in this context. This study fills in an empirical void in
the understanding of the administrative burden associated with
having a criminal record and college admissions and how this
experience varies across racial groups in the U.S.
1.3   |   Street-­Level Bureaucrat in College
Admissions
A key actor in the facilitation of administrative burden is the
street-­level bureaucrat (SLB) as a public-­facing representative
of government and policy (Lipsky  1980). Since SLBs possess
discretion during interactions with members of the public,
they may alleviate or amplify clients' experiences of burden
(Lipsky 1980; Maynard-­Moody and Musheno 2000; Tummers
et al. 2015). Therefore, exploring SLBs as potential facilitators
of administrative burden in this context is appropriate because
admissions professionals occupy a vital role in engaging pro-
spective students and are tasked with conveying valuable in-
formation about institutional policies including admissions
(Sturm et al. 2011; Stewart and Uggen 2020). As public-­facing
representatives, admissions professionals at IHEs function as
SLBs. They are responsible for correctly interpreting and ad-
ministering institutional and public policy, thus granting them
discretion (Bell et  al.  2021; Bell and Smith  2022). Intuitively,
this discretion is present in admissions' interactions with the
public—prospective students and their families—as they may
choose to communicate as they see fit. Further, admission rep-
resentatives are often the first point of contact for prospective
students, highlighting their role as representatives on behalf of
an IHE. Consistent with scholarship on administrative burden
and SLBs, admissions representatives may mitigate or augment
prospective students' experiences of burden in higher education
admissions (Bell et al. 2021; Bell and Smith 2022). As working
professionals, admissions representatives experience time con-
straints, so they rely on cognitive heuristics and cues for mak-
ing decisions on how to interact with people. When prospective
students contact an admissions department, the representatives
assigned to respond often rely on cues about the student or their
inquiry in choosing how to respond. Cues may reflect biases that
in turn facilitate or perpetuate costs incurred by the student, po-
tentially varying based on race because of inherent racial bias.
Thus, admission representatives function as mechanisms for fa-
cilitating administrative burden, and it is beneficial to examine
their roles in promoting or discouraging access to IHEs for pro-
spective students with a criminal history across racial groups.
This study captures two key administrative burden elements:
the actions of the SLB or state action and the experiences of bur-
den (Baekgaard and Tankink 2022) that are placed on college
applicants.
To be sure, admissions representatives may not always fit the
classic definition of SLBs who primarily engage in face-­to-­face
interactions. Admission representatives that typically interact
with prospective students via email can be considered screen-­
level bureaucrats—a modern form of SLBs who interact with
citizens primarily through digital interfaces with a substantial
level of discretion (Bovens and Zouridis 2002; Buffat 2015).
2   |   Research Questions and Hypotheses
The salience of administrative burden, discriminatory treat-
ment in higher education by race, and the centrality of admis-
sions representatives as SLBs prompt a guiding question in this
article: What impact does the perceived race of an applicant with
a criminal record have on the administrative burden placed on
an applicant by a SLB? The associated hypothesis is that a Black
applicant with a criminal record compared to a White applicant
with a criminal record will endure significantly higher learn-
ing, psychological, and compliance costs. Moreover, the scholars
expect nuances in how these costs manifest. A critical insight
from SLB theory is how SLBs manage the pressures of their

work environment, such as high caseloads, limited time, and
informational uncertainty (Lipsky 1980; Maynard-­Moody and
Musheno  2000; Tummers et  al.  2015). SLBs develop routines
and cognitive shortcuts to manage their workflow efficiently—
sometimes by “moving away from clients” or even “moving
against clients” (Tummers et al. 2015, 1104). While these rou-
tines are often necessary, they may also be a mechanism through
which administrative burden is created and distributed un-
evenly. Crucially, these discretionary judgments are susceptible
to implicit biases. Cues like an applicant's name can trigger un-
conscious stereotypes that influence an SLB's decision-­making.
This is unlikely to manifest as overt discrimination, such as a
hostile tone, which is professionally and socially sanctioned as
part of an SLB's social frame (Knox and Arshed 2024). Instead,
bias is more likely to emerge in more subtle, procedural aspects
of the interaction that can be justified as routine case manage-
ment. For example, an SLB might delay a response to a perceived
difficult case as a coping mechanism. This study, therefore, uses
SLB theory not only to identify admissions representatives as
SLBs but to conjecture that the specific administrative burden
observed, or not observed, is the result of SLBs using discretion
to manage their workload under pressure, a process that is sub-
tly but significantly shaped by the applicant's perceived race.
3   |   Design and Methods
Public administration scholars have called for an uptick in the
use of mixed methods (Hendren et al. 2018, 2023; Raimondo and
Newcomer 2017; Mele and Belardinelli 2019). This study uses a
mixed method design, the data transformation model, a variant
of the triangulation research design (Creswell and Plano Clark
2006), to analyze qualitative data and to identify the important
variables that will be used in a quantitative data analysis. For
this study, the process included the fielding of the Dear Tyrone
correspondence audit experiment, a content analysis of the de-
rived textual data, quantification of this content (transforma-
tion), and a series of regression analyses.
3.1   |   Sample and Dear Tyrone Experiment
This investigation uses an experimental approach to capture
open-­ended responses from IHE SLBs about the admissions
process. The scholars created a stratified random sample of
colleges across the U.S. to reflect various IHE types, locations,
and levels of selectivity. Cappex (www.​cappex.​com), a website
that provides information on IHEs in each state, served as the
sampling frame. It included all 2-­ and 4-­year public and 4-­year
private IHEs. The scholars excluded 2-­year private and all for-­
profit IHEs due to inconsistent degree offerings. The scholars
then drew a simple random sample within each state stratum.
In the experiment, Dear Tyrone, e-­mails were sent from two fic-
titious applicants, Tyrone Thomas and Christopher Thomas, to
admission representatives at each sampled IHE between March
2018 and March 2020. The sending of emails was staggered to
avoid detection or suspicion by SLBs, and the first emailer of
each IHE was randomly selected. The primary goal of this corre-
spondence audit was to investigate how IHEs consider criminal
history in admissions. Tyrone (a Black-­identifying name) and
Christopher (a White-­identifying name) sent nearly identical
email inquiries asking whether applications asked about crim-
inal history and how felony convictions affected admissions. In
this experiment, race served as the treatment effect, with the
names signaling racial identity—a strategy common in similar
research (Elder and Hayes 2023; Crabtree et al. 2023), based on
evidence that U.S. names often indicate race (Comenetz 2016).
Both applicants' emails disclosed a felony conviction and inquired
whether it would affect admission chances. If an IHE did not re-
spond within a week, a follow-­up referencing the original message
was sent. In instances of automatic admissions messages, the
scholars treated any auto-­reply that was not followed by a human
response as unusable. Most automated messages either confirmed
receipt or directed applicants to generic admission forms without
substantive information. In cases where no response was received
after three attempts, the scholars excluded the institution from the
sample and replaced it with another randomly selected IHE. In
alignment with audit-­study practice (Pager 2007; Olsen et al. 2022;
Carnes and Holbein 2019), the scholars randomly selected a new
institution from the sampling frame, which allowed the scholars
to: (a) retain statistical power for more precise estimates; and (b)
reduce biasing the sample away from the state-­stratified design so
that it remains a closer proportional representation of each state
across the U.S. In the end, 423 institutions, 104 being replace-
ments, replied to both applicants. The emails that were sent under

The unit of analysis is SLBs' email responses to both appli-
cants, which is appropriate because the scholars sought to
understand how administrative burden was encountered by
applicants during an admissions inquiry. The email responses
of SLBs contained information about the policies and pro-
cedures that each applicant would be subjected to and the
potential administrative burden that might accompany the
admissions process for those with a criminal background.
Second, the content analysis relied on a stratified random
sample of email responses from SLBs that outlined informa-
tion about admissions procedures for those with a criminal
background. Third, as represented in Appendix S1, the schol-
ars engaged in an abductive coding process to develop a con-
ceptual understanding of administrative burden that allowed
data to be systematically organized and analyzed. In addition
to identifying patterns in data, an abductive approach allowed
the scholars to draw upon existing research and extend an un-
derstanding of administrative burden in the IHE context (e.g.,
Bell and Smith 2022).
Finally, and importantly, rather than simply presenting find-
ings from the content analysis, the scholars engaged in a
process of quantification that provided structured represen-
tations of administrative burden. This approach aligns with
Creswell's (2022) assertion that mixed-­methods research can-
not be reduced to the mere combination of qualitative and
quantitative techniques. Instead, the team integrated both
forms of data to generate a more comprehensive understanding
of administrative burden—one that neither method could pro-
duce alone. Central to this process was the use of an abductive
coding schema grounded exclusively in administrative burden
theory. As shown in Appendix S1, this schema organized in-
ductive codes—emerging directly from the qualitative data—
under the theory's three core tenets: learning, psychological,
and compliance costs. Each code was described, exemplified
with data, and interpreted through the lens of administrative
burden theory, ensuring conceptual consistency throughout
the analysis. This framework enabled the transformation of
qualitative insights into quantifiable variables (e.g., binary
or trinary codes), which were then used in statistical analy-
sis. For instance, admissions responses that demonstrated a
clear and encouraging tone were coded as a 1, while responses
lacking clarity or providing discouragement were coded as a
0. Similarly, compliance costs, such as additional formal or in-
formal requirements, were numerically coded based on their
presence or absence. This transformation allowed for system-
atic comparisons across institutions and enabled regression
analyses to explore the broader implications of administrative
burden in the admissions process.
3.3   |   Interrater Reliability
To ensure the reliability and accuracy of the coding process, a ro-
bust interrater reliability assessment was conducted. The scholars
initially assigned cases to coders randomly. Subsequently, a second
team member double coded a subsample of these e-­mails to evalu-
ate the level of agreement. The team achieved an agreement score
of 90%. During instances of disagreement, the coders engaged
in discussions to reconcile the case. If a consensus could not be
reached, a third coder was introduced as a tiebreaker. Additionally,
for variables with high levels of disagreement of 15% or more, all
observations assigned to a particular coder underwent a complete
recoding to ensure consistency. Lastly, the research team flagged
which coders were randomly assigned cases, allowing for a robust-
ness check for any potential coder effects as well.
3.4   |   Sample Statistics of Tyrone and Christopher
After the completion of the coding process, the sample con-
sisted of 846 email responses from admission representa-
tives across 423 IHEs. In terms of learning costs, 22.67% of
responses to Christopher and 22.70% of responses to Tyrone
prompted follow-­up questions due to unclear information.
Psychological costs were evident with 73.12% of responses to
Christopher and 71.57% of responses to Tyrone containing a
greeting, and 24.69% of responses to Christopher and 20.81%
of responses to Tyrone encouraging the applicant to apply.
The communication tone was categorized as encouraging in
18.64% of responses to Christopher and 15.99% of responses to
Tyrone, with an average response time of 4.45 business days
between initial outreach and responses for Christopher and
5.42 business days for Tyrone. Compliance costs were assessed
by identifying that 50.63% of responses to Christopher and
56.35% of responses to Tyrone introduced formal additional
steps, while 18.73% of responses to Christopher and 15.05% of

3.5   |   Analytical Approach
To investigate whether differences exist in the administrative
experiences of Tyrone and Christopher, a series of multivari-
ate regression analyses were conducted. The study outcomes
span three data types, so the research team applied the regres-
sion family best suited to each outcome measure. In general,
binary study outcomes (yes/no measures) were analyzed with
mixed-­effects logistic models, capturing how the log-­odds of
the outcome shift from Christopher to Tyrone while allowing
each college its own baseline propensity (random intercept).
Specifically, logistic regression analyses were used for the two-­
category outcome variables (Fitzgerald and Fitzgerald 2014),
including the variables of Formal Requirements and Informal
Requirements that have two response values (e.g., whether
an applicant was given formal requirements or not). Ordinal
outcomes (rank-­order measures) were estimated with mixed-­
effects cumulative logit models. These models assess whether
Tyrone's messages are more likely to fall into specific ranks

gression is appropriate for outcomes with more than two or-

and Fitzgerald 2014). For example, the tone of the SLB's re-
sponse—discouraging, neutral, or encouraging—was treated
as an ordinal outcome—measuring the probability that an
SLB's tone is at or below a given level. The study count out-
comes (0 and up) were over-­dispersed, so mixed-­effects
negative-­binomial models were employed. For example, the
days to response variable was treated this way (see negative-­
binomial models in Gardner et al. 1995) because its variance
was larger than its mean.
Notably, the scholars use multi-­level modeling throughout given
the nested structure of the data. That is, each IHE received one
email signed by Christopher and one signed by Tyrone, so the
observations cluster within colleges rather than being fully inde-
pendent. Institutional response styles may naturally vary, so ig-
noring this clustering could misattribute these IHE differences
to the applicant names. The analysis uses a multilevel frame-
work where level 1 contrasts the two messages within the same
IHE, and level 2 assigns each IHE its own random intercept that
captures its average IHE response style. Alternatively, treating
clustered data as independent would shrink standard errors
and increase the risk of a Type I error (Gelman and Hill 2007).
Moreover, the subsequent results will show an intraclass cor-
relations ranging from 0.16 to 0.28 across outcomes, which in-
dicates that 16%–28% of the total variance lies at the IHE level,
supporting the use of the multilevel model. Ultimately, these
analytical specifications provide more accurate effect estimates
and standard errors than a single-­level approach.

4.1   |   Regression Results Without Coder Effects
Two models revealed a significant difference in burden encoun-
tered by Tyrone and Christopher. Specifically, the nested model
examining the psychological cost measured in length of days
revealed that Tyrone received responses at 1.36 times the rate

To enhance analytical robustness, analyses were conducted

bias results. This process illuminated that when coder effects
were controlled for, the findings observed earlier remain con-

5   |   Discussion
This study contributes to the growing body of research on ad-
ministrative burden theory, providing empirical insights into
how perceived race and criminal history jointly shape the ad-
ministrative burden applicants encounter in IHE admissions.
The results show that Black applicants with a disclosed criminal
record, represented here by the name Tyrone, face significant dif-
ferences in some administrative burdens relative to their White
counterparts, represented by the name Christopher. Specifically,
for psychological costs, Tyrone faced longer delays in response
time. Furthermore, compliance costs also varied, with Tyrone
having a greater likelihood of receiving formal requirements rel-
ative to Christopher. Notably, the null findings are also promis-
ing—indicating that other costs like differences in encouraging
statements, tone, greetings, informal requirements, or a need for
clarity are not present. Together, these results illuminate how
administrative burden operates within IHEs to reinforce access
disparities tied to race and criminal background.
Overall, the findings are consistent with the initial hypotheses.
The pattern of results may be explained using SLB theory about
norms, routine, discretion, and coping strategies (Knox and
Arshed 2024; Lipsky 1980; Maynard-­Moody and Musheno 2000;
Tummers et al. 2015). The absence of bias in tone and encour-
agement is as theoretically instructive as the presence of bias
elsewhere. Admission representatives are likely aware of in-
stitutional and societal norms against overt discrimination as
part of their social frame (Knox and Arshed 2024). Sending a
discouraging message is a clear transgression, so they are likely
to manage such explicit communication aspects to ensure a pro-
fessional and neutral-­to-­positive tone for all applicants. As such,
these communication aspects are less subject to discretion and
more a reflection of standardized professional practice.
In contrast, the significant differences in response time and for-
mal requirements may be areas where the biases appear in the
more subtle, procedural dimensions. These are areas where dis-
cretion is higher, and actions can be more easily justified as rou-
tine workload management. The longer response time for Tyrone
likely reflects a common SLB coping mechanism: “moving away
from clients” by de-­prioritizing or postponing tasks perceived
as more complex or demanding (Tummers et  al.  2015, 1104).
An SLB, perhaps unconsciously influenced by racial bias, may
view an application from a Black person with a felony record as
a more complicated case and thus place it lower in their queue
while they manage easier inquiries. Meanwhile, the finding that
Tyrone was more likely to receive formal requirements can be in-
terpreted as a form of bureaucratic distancing, with SLBs “mov-
ing away” or even “against clients” through rigid rule-­following
(Tummers et al. 2015, 1104). Providing a formal list of rules may
be an efficient way for a busy SLB to close a case, reflecting a
form of resistance to deeper engagement with a complex case
(Knox and Arshed 2024). It requires less personal investment
than engaging in a more personalized dialogue (associated with
compassion or relational motives) (Knox and Arshed 2024). It
may also represent a shift from a customer-­service orientation
to a formal, regulatory one, a choice perhaps made easier in a
screen-­level interaction (Buffat 2015). This aligns with research
on racialized burden (Ray et al. 2023), where processes may not
be overtly discriminatory but still create higher barriers for cer-
tain racial groups. Thus, the burden experienced by Tyrone is
not necessarily the result of SLBs intending to be discrimina-
tory, but possibly of them applying routine coping strategies un-
evenly when faced with a case they perceive as more complex—a
perception amplified by racial cues.
5.1   |   Implications for Higher Education Policy
and Practice
Focusing on the differences documented in this study as areas
of improvement, there are some implications for policy and prac-
tice in higher education worth discussing. As institutions con-
front the need to reduce barriers to college access, these findings
reveal that admissions representatives play a significant role in
perpetuating or alleviating burden. In line with scholarship on
racialized burden (Ray et  al.  2023; Gándara et  al.  2024), the
results provide partial support that underscores how admin-
istrative burden can function as a mechanism for reproducing
structural inequalities within higher education. Specifically, the
elevated psychological and compliance costs faced by Tyrone
mirror a pattern of historical inequities in education access that
have disadvantaged Black people in the U.S. (Cunningham 2023;
Blume 2023). The higher costs Tyrone faced, such as often wait-
ing a longer period to receive a response from SLBs, could cre-
ate a more anxious experience and dejected feelings relative to
Christopher.

undergo formal reviews reflects the kind of discretionary prac-
tices that, while not overtly discriminatory, can have com-

applicant who is provided with detailed information about for-
mal processes triggered by their criminal record may perceive
the process as initially more burdensome or challenging com-
pared to an applicant who does not receive such information.
This may occur by way of cognitive overload as well as analysis
paralysis or a state of inaction due to too much information to
consider (see Kurien et al. 2014). On the other hand, some re-
search suggests that clear communication about an institution's
programs can bolster a prospective student's decision to matric-
ulate (e.g., Moogan 2011). The applicant with more detailed in-
formation might ultimately be better equipped to navigate the
application process. Regardless of how this dynamic is scruti-
nized, someone is left at a disadvantage.
The potential downstream effects are problematic as well, with
research showing that participants who lose access to public
goods and services have lower levels of trust in government,
often along racial lines (Bell et al. 2024). Thus, policy interven-
tions aimed at standardizing admissions processes and training
admissions personnel in bias mitigation could serve as effec-
tive tools to reduce inequitable burden and improve access to
higher education across the board. This study's findings point
to specific areas where institutional policies and practices can
be refined to promote equitable access. The nuanced nature of
these results suggests that interventions should be targeted to
the observed disparities. First, given the differential provision
of formal requirements observed, institutions should implement
standardized guidelines for initial admissions communications.
These guidelines should ensure that all applicants, including
those with a criminal background, making similar inquiries
receive consistent and clear information. Further, the finding
that Tyrone experienced longer response times highlights an-
other burden. It is also worth noting that several admissions
representatives sent automated receipts acknowledging Tyrone's
or Christopher's e-­mail but provided no timeline for a substan-
tive reply. Arguably, expectation-­setting auto-­messages that
specify when an applicant can expect a human follow-­up may
reduce burden and improve an applicant's experience. A low-­
cost intervention would be for admissions offices to reconfigure
their auto-­replies to include a clear response window, thereby
lowering applicants' psychological and learning costs without
increasing staff workload. These university-­level interventions
may further complement legislative efforts to promote fairness
and transparency while addressing the specific challenges as-
sociated with interpersonal communication in admissions.
Together, they represent a holistic approach to reducing inequi-
ties and making education more accessible to all.
6   |   Limitations and Future Research Directions
This study offers valuable insights but, like all empirical re-
search, has limitations and opportunities for future research—
several of which we address below. One key limitation concerns
the external validity of correspondence audits. While effective
in controlling experimental conditions, they may not fully cap-
ture the complexity of real-­world admissions processes or reflect
burden at other stages (Xu and Lee 2024). Future research could
incorporate longitudinal tracking or interviews with admissions
personnel to address this.
A second limitation involves the audit design. Emails from
Christopher and Tyrone were sent at separate times to avoid de-
tection, introducing potential temporal bias. Although analyti-
cal checks for time showed no impact on results, future studies
should better coordinate timing or consider randomized control
trials. Furthermore, slight wording differences between emails,
used to reduce detection, may also introduce bias. Randomizing
message versions could improve methodological rigor in future
studies.
A third limitation concerns the use of replacement sampling
(e.g., substituting non-­respondent schools), which may have
introduced bias, as these replacements might not reflect the
characteristics of original non-­respondents. This limits general-
izability and prevents analysis of outright nonresponse. An in-
crease in initial sample sizes could help address this issue.
A fourth limitation is tied to the content analysis, which was
conducted by academically trained coders, whose expertise
likely made estimates conservative. However, none had lived
experience navigating admissions with a felony record, which
may affect interpretation. Despite safeguards like double coding
and coder fixed effects (Nowell and Albrecht 2019), future work
should consider including coders with relevant lived experience.
A fifth limitation is that names may signal more than race,
including class or education. While Tyrone is strongly associ-
ated with Black identity (Elder and Hayes 2023; Gaddis 2017),
intersecting perceptions may influence responses. Future re-
search should explore how applicants with multiple marginal-
ized identities navigate burden, building on work like Bell and
Jilke (2024).
Sixth, the cumulative effects of burden also merit attention.
Delays and complex instructions may create compound disad-
vantages. Future studies could examine how institutional struc-
tures, such as admissions office placement and funding, shape
burden. Additionally, as AI tools become more common in ad-
missions (Lira et al. 2023), their role in shaping administrative
burden warrants investigation.
Seventh, the study draws on SLB theory to interpret nuanced bias
as a coping mechanism under workplace pressures. However,
it does not directly measure causal mechanisms like workload
or implicit bias. Combining audits with surveys or interviews
could clarify how SLBs make decisions and experience con-
straints. Finally, while this study treats time delays as a “psycho-
logical cost” (Ray 2019; Lipsky 1980), the field debates how best
to classify such costs (Baekgaard and Tankink 2022; Petersen
et al. 2024). Future work should refine how time-­related burden
is conceptualized.
7   |   Conclusion
This article extends administrative burden theory to higher ed-
ucation, illustrating that the interactions of race and criminal
history significantly influence applicants' experiences of some
costs. By positioning admissions representatives as SLBs, this
research underscores the importance of equitable practices in
mitigating racialized burden and fostering fair access to higher

Biographies
Victor J. St. John is an Assistant Professor at The Ohio State
University's John Glenn College of Public Affairs. His scholarship ex-
amines how criminal justice systems intersect with public policy and
administration to shape outcomes across education, health, and place.
Using multi-­method approaches, he investigates systemic patterns, bu-
reaucratic responses, and spatial dynamics to inform evidence-­based
policy and strengthen justice system interventions that foster fair and
effective outcomes for the public.
Gregory D. Wilson is an Assistant Professor in the John Glenn College
of Public Affairs at The Ohio State University. His research is at the inter-
section of race and nonprofit organizations. In particular, he is interested
in how the nonprofit sector is racialized and how this process impacts
the work of organizations led by people of color, especially those led by
African Americans.
Long Tran is an Assistant Professor at The Ohio State University's John
Glenn College of Public Affairs. His research focuses on public and non-
profit management, especially questions pertaining to organizational
collaboration or individual citizen engagement.
Lydia Applin is a PhD Candidate in the Public Policy and Management
program at The Ohio State University's John Glenn College of Public
Affairs. Her research is focused on criminal justice policy, particularly
concerning disparities found in policing and judicial settings.
Douglas Evans is a Professor of Criminology and Criminal Justice who
researches criminal stigmatization and barriers to reentry, the familial
and public health consequences and mass incarceration, and higher ed-
ucation programs in prisons. His recent work focuses on notification
around Clean Slate laws that clear eligible criminal records.

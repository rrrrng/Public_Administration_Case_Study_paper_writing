ABSTRACT
This study examines how revenue sources influence nonprofit policy engagement. Drawing on resource dependence theory and
nonprofit-­government relationship perspectives, we categorize 19 forms of engagement into three types: (1) Complementary
Partnerships, (2) Supplementary Contributions, and (3) Adversarial Influence on Government. Results show that the share of
government funding is positively associated with these three forms of policy engagement. Also, we distinguish between non-­
lobbying advocacy and legislative lobbying, revealing distinct funding patterns. Our expanded models further highlight that
access to key funding sources, especially government funding, matters more for advocacy than the extent of reliance on them.
These findings emphasize the need for more nuanced measures of nonprofit policy engagement to deepen our understanding of
how funding structures shape nonprofit-­government interactions.
1   |   Introduction
Nonprofit advocacy amplifies diverse voter interests in public
policy (Bass et al. 2007). Broadly defined as “the attempt to influ-
ence public policy, directly or indirectly” (Pekkanen et al. 2014,
3), nonprofit advocacy ranges from targeted lobbying on specific
legislation to broader activities such as public education on pol-
icy issues. These strategies enable nonprofits to represent client
and sector interests through interactions with government, even
though not all interactions necessarily qualify as advocacy.
Nonprofits engage in policy activities for several reasons.
They offer frontline expertise to inform decisions (Guo and
Saxton 2010), such as environmental groups supplying data for
sustainable practices. They also amplify marginalized voices
(Mosley and Grogan 2013; Kim and Mason 2018) and help en-
sure initiatives meet community needs (Young 2000); for exam-
ple, nonprofits serving low-­income families may work with local
authorities on housing policies. In addition, by monitoring gov-
ernment actions—like health organizations tracking interven-
tions and advocating improvements—they help identify service
gaps and promote responsive governance (Mosley 2010).
Several factors influence nonprofit policy engagement, including
government receptivity (MacIndoe and Beaton  2019), organiza-
tional expertise (LeRoux and Goerdel 2009), and resource avail-
ability (Bass et al. 2007; Berry and Arons 2003). Revenue sources
shape nonprofits' engagement in policy venues and adoption of ad-
vocacy tactics (Buffardi et al. 2015). Advocacy requires specialized
resources and appeals to funders in different ways. For example,

This study advances nonprofit advocacy research in three ways.
First, it examines 19 specific forms of nonprofit policy engage-
ment—broadly defined as advocacy activities—and through factor
analysis, identifies three theory-­driven categories of policy en-
gagement. Second, the study clarifies how different forms of policy
engagement relate to various revenue streams. Whereas prior re-
search has typically focused on one or two revenue sources (often
emphasizing government funding), our work investigates more
complex links with government funding, individual donations,
foundation grants, earned income, and membership dues. Finally,
by employing the first nationally representative survey of US non-
profit advocacy in two decades, this study overcomes earlier re-
search's geographic and sectoral limitations. The data support a
generalizable theoretical framework for nonprofit-­government
relationships, addressing the following questions: (1) Do nonprof-
its' advocacy activities follow generalizable patterns? (2) How are
these patterns linked to funding sources? and (3) Why are some
nonprofits more active in certain advocacy forms?
2   |   Moving Beyond a Single Concept of Nonprofit
Advocacy
Nonprofits engage in public policy through educational and in-
formational activities, such as hosting forums, publishing policy
briefs, and conducting outreach. Their engagement spans legis-
lative, administrative, grassroots, judicial, electoral, and media
domains. Berry and Arons  (2003) classify these as aggressive
legislative actions (e.g., lobbying, testifying, mobilizing mem-
bers) and less aggressive administrative actions (e.g., meeting
officials, advisory participation). Others distinguish between
insider strategies, such as direct policymaker engagement, and
outsider strategies, like media campaigns and protests, align-
ing broadly with Berry and Arons' typology (Almog-­Bar 2017;
Mosley 2011; Verschuere and De Corte 2015).
Another distinction exists between elite advocacy, which relies
on professionalized strategies like lobbying and policy advising
(Schlozman et al. 2012; Walker 2014), and participatory advocacy,
which mobilizes grassroots efforts through demonstrations and pe-
titions to amplify marginalized voices (Fung 2006; Mosley 2011).
Some argue that participatory advocacy fosters public support for
government actions and ensures that policies reflect the diverse
needs of the community (Guo and Saxton 2010).
Advocacy typologies often overlap, as discussed in the above
comparisons of insider–outsider and aggressive–non-­aggressive
frameworks. Some scholars propose multi-­dimensional models
that classify advocacy based on tactics (e.g., insider–outsider),
motivation (e.g., instrumental–expressive), and goals (e.g., polit-
ical–social) (Mosley et al. 2023; MacIndoe et al. 2025). Others
combine insider–outsider (similar to elite vs. participatory) with
direct–indirect strategies to refine how nonprofits engage with
policymakers (Pekkanen et al. 2014).
Research shows that nonprofit managers often prefer insider
tactics to build collaborative relationships with policymak-
ers and secure stable government funding (Almog-­Bar  2017).
Privatization and outsourcing reinforce this interdependence,
as governments rely on nonprofits for service delivery and non-
profits depend on government support (Piatak and Jensen 2024;
Salamon  2003). As a result, administrative advocacy—such
as meetings and correspondence with officials—is more com-
mon than confrontational tactics like protests (Bass et al. 2007;
Buffardi et  al.  2017; Donaldson  2007; Schmid et  al.  2008;
Verschuere and De Corte 2015).
While complex frameworks describe nonprofit advocacy as
multidimensional, much of the literature still relies on binary
classifications like insider–outsider approaches, which simplify
analyses but overlook the complexity of nonprofit–government
interactions (Ward et al. 2023). Although these typologies clarify
key trade-­offs, empirical testing of more nuanced frameworks
remains limited, and theory development is still evolving. This
study adopts a broader, theory-­driven perspective to explain
variations in nonprofit advocacy and offer practical insights into
policy engagement.
3   |   A General Framework for Nonprofit Advocacy
This study takes a theoretical approach to nonprofit–govern-
ment relationships to explain variations in advocacy tactics.
Specifically, it builds on Young (2000), categorizing nonprofit–
government relationships as supplementary, complementary,
and adversarial.
In the supplementary model, nonprofits provide services that
government cannot, addressing unmet needs and serving
populations overlooked by public policies. This aligns with
Weisbrod's (1975) public goods theory, which argues that gov-
ernments struggle to meet diverse citizen preferences due to
budget constraints, majority rule, and challenges in serving
minority populations. Nonprofits voluntarily step in to fill these
gaps, often supplementing or extending government-­funded
programs and services (Brecher and Wise 2008).
The complementary model describes collaboration between
nonprofits and government to deliver publicly funded services.
Rooted in Salamon's (1987) interdependence theory, this model
emphasizes mutual reliance: nonprofits depend on government
funding for sustainability and expansion, while governments
leverage nonprofits' community ties to deliver more effective
services. Since they are closer to local needs, nonprofits help
bridge representation gaps and align public services with di-
verse taxpayer priorities (MacIndoe et al. 2025).
Summary
•	 Nonprofits' revenue sources reflect the kinds of rela-
tionships they have with funding partners, such as
government agencies, donors, or customers.
•	 Funding from government is associated with nonprof-
its interacting more broadly with government, includ-
ing greater involvement in policy-­related activities.
•	 Nonprofits that rely more heavily on individual dona-
tions or income from selling goods and services tend
to be less active in policy engagement.

In contrast, the adversarial model frames nonprofits as advo-
cates for policy change, government accountability, and systemic
reform. This includes both confrontational tactics, such as pro-
tests, and collaborative pressure strategies, like coalition-­building
(Young 2000). At the same time, governments regulate nonprofit
activities to ensure accountability, creating a dynamic tension that
fosters policy innovation and institutional change.
Young's (2000) framework provides a strong theoretical foun-
dation for understanding nonprofit behavior, particularly
in public policy engagement. Building on broader theories
of nonprofits and government, it helps explain variations in
advocacy strategies based on specific nonprofit–government
relationships. For instance, nonprofits may (1) complement
government by partnering in policy implementation and reg-
ulation, (2) supplement government efforts by offering ex-
pertise to inform policy development, or (3) exert adversarial
pressure to influence policymaking. Ultimately, nonprofits'
policy interactions with government reflect the broader dy-
namics shaping their relationship with the public sector.
Using this framework, we expect nonprofits to align advocacy
strategies with their goals, resources, and contexts by adopting
complementary, supplementary, or adversarial roles. For instance,
they may supplement government by offering expertise, comple-
ment it through collaboration, or oppose it by mobilizing grass-
roots action. These roles build on Berry and Arons' (2003) typology
of aggressive and less aggressive tactics, offering a more nuanced
perspective on elite versus participatory advocacy. Elite advocacy
aligns with complementary and supplementary roles, such as ad-
vising or drafting policy, whereas participatory advocacy aligns
with adversarial actions, including protests and media campaigns.
Applying Young's framework moves beyond binary classifica-
tions like insider–outsider tactics, offering deeper insight into
how advocacy reflects nonprofits' broader relationships with
government. It also informs our second research question on
how advocacy types relate to revenue sources.
4   |   Funding Sources and Nonprofit Advocacy
Resource dependence theory argues that organizational sur-
vival depends on securing key resources—funding, expertise,
and information (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). In uncertain en-
vironments, nonprofits manage risk by building reserves (Sloan
et  al.  2016) and diversifying revenue streams (Young  2018),
sometimes through advocacy for greater government support.
Advocacy enhances reputation, legitimacy, and information ac-
cess, but is often shaped by funding constraints and competition
(Lu 2018). Still, findings on how funding sources influence ad-
vocacy are mixed and sometimes contradictory.
4.1   |   Government Funding
Government funding often comprises a substantial share of
nonprofit budgets, particularly for those in human services
(Garrow  2011). Some studies suggest that nonprofits receiv-
ing government funding may limit advocacy and lobbying to
protect financial stability and tax-­exempt status (Bloodgood
and Tremblay-­Boire  2017; Nicholson-­Crotty  2011; Suárez and
Hwang 2008). However, other research presents mixed findings,
indicating that the relationship between government funding
and advocacy may depend on local government involvement in
policy areas such as environmental protection or immigration
(Calderon et al. 2021; Donaldson 2007; Schmid et al. 2008).
Conversely, several studies argue that government funding encour-
ages nonprofit advocacy (Donaldson 2007; Lee et al. 2018; LeRoux
and Goerdel  2009; MacIndoe and Whalen  2013; Mosley  2011;
Onyx et al. 2008; Pekkanen et al. 2014). First, nonprofits may en-
gage in advocacy to manage relationships with elected officials, se-
cure existing funding, and attract additional support (LeRoux and
Goerdel  2009; Mosley  2010, 2011). Second, advocacy highlights
mission alignment with public interests, strengthening nonprof-
its' case for funding. Third, repeated interactions with government
agencies help build trust and legitimacy, increasing nonprofits'
role in policymaking (Fyall 2016; Van Slyke 2007).
From a government relationships perspective, reliance on govern-
ment funding strongly indicates a nonprofit's close connection to
government. Translating Young's (2000) framework to advocacy
activities, government funding would facilitate complementary
and supplementary types of advocacy actions. For instance, non-
profits receiving government funding would be more likely to re-
spond to requests for information (complementary) or testify at
administrative hearings based on their expertise (supplementary).
In contrast, adversarial relationships between nonprofits and
government could show mixed patterns. For example, using
technology to educate the public or publishing research reports
on policy issues can strengthen funding relationships. However,
nonprofits heavily dependent on government funding may avoid
confrontational activities like organizing public campaigns or
lobbying for or against legislation, fearing these actions might
alienate funders.
H1a.  Government funding will be positively associated with
complementary and supplementary forms of nonprofit policy
engagement.
H1b.  Government funding will have a mixed or no relationship
with adversarial forms of nonprofit policy engagement.
The distinction between advocacy and lobbying is critical
(MacIndoe 2011). Lobbying involves direct efforts to influence leg-
islative outcomes by engaging policymakers on specific proposals
(Donaldson 2007; Mosley 2011). Advocacy includes broader activ-
ities like public education, awareness campaigns, research, and
coalition building, which may not target legislation directly (Berry
and Arons 2003; Bass et al. 2007). While lobbying is a subset of
advocacy, not all advocacy constitutes lobbying.
Nonprofits reliant on government funding face constraints
in lobbying due to IRS regulations that limit lobbying by tax-­
exempt organizations (Internal Revenue Service [IRS]  2024a,
2024b) and concerns about conflicts of interest or the misuse of
public funds. These risks may lead to accusations of improper
use of taxpayer funds, which could threaten funding and reputa-
tions. To maintain neutrality and preserve government relation-
ships, such nonprofits often avoid lobbying. Also, funders may

4.2   |   Individual Donations
Nonprofits heavily reliant on individual donations often avoid
public policy discussions to prevent being perceived as partisan.
Advocacy and lobbying pose risks, as they may alienate donors
with differing political views. Research indicates a negative re-
lationship between private giving and advocacy engagement,
suggesting that reliance on individual donations may con-
strain policy-­related activities (Fyall and Allard 2017; Guo and
Saxton 2010).
While Young (2000) acknowledges that private funding can sup-
port advocacy, individual donations rarely provide the flexible,
long-­term funding needed for sustained efforts. Such contribu-
tions often fail to cover key advocacy costs like staff training,
coalition building, and lobbying (Bass et  al.  2007). Without
diversified funding, nonprofits struggle to navigate advocacy
complexities.
Thus, we expect organizations heavily dependent on individual
donations to be less likely to engage in policy activities. Their
funding model prioritizes donor satisfaction and discourages ac-
tions that might jeopardize support, creating structural barriers
that limit policy influence.
H2.  Individual donations will be negatively associated with all
forms of nonprofit policy engagement.
4.3   |   Foundation and Corporate Grants
Distinct from individual philanthropy, foundations can play a
crucial role in building nonprofit capacity, fostering innovation,
and supporting advocacy and lobbying (Jaskyte et  al.  2018).
Social justice-­oriented foundations explicitly endorse these
activities (Suárez  2012), and nonprofits receiving advocacy-­
specific grants are significantly more likely to lobby (Fyall and
McGuire 2015; Fyall and Allard 2017). These grants provide re-
sources to expand policy engagement, strengthen capacity, and
access high-­status networks that enhance nonprofits' influence
(Faulk et al. 2017).
Corporate funding adds complexity. While corporations support
nonprofits for social responsibility (Petrenko et al. 2016), some
nonprofits avoid taking policy stances in order to retain corpo-
rate funding. Still other corporate foundations bolster advocacy
(Austin et al. 2019; Lim and Young 2021). However, foundation
funding can come with restrictions on advocacy activities, lim-
iting nonprofits' ability to use grants for lobbying due to internal
policies or IRS regulations (Bass et al. 2007). Thus, while private
grants can enhance advocacy capacity, they may also impose
funding conditions to limit that activity.
From Young's (2000) typology, private grants may help nonprof-
its engage with government through building capacity and net-
works to advocate for services and policies aligned with their
missions. Private grants can also support complementary and
supplementary roles, such as fostering collaboration. However,
while grants may build advocacy capacity, foundations' priori-
ties or restrictions can suppress adversarial tactics. Given these
dynamics, we hypothesize that:
H3a.  Private grants will be positively associated with comple-
mentary, supplementary, and general advocacy forms of non-
profit policy engagement.
H3b.  Private grants will be negatively associated with adver-
sarial and lobbying forms of nonprofit policy engagement.
4.4   |   Earned Revenue
Earned income enhances financial autonomy, reducing reli-
ance on government and philanthropic funding (Young 2018).
Resource dependence theory (Pfeffer and Salancik  1978) sug-
gests that generating earned revenue fosters independence and
flexible resource allocation, including for advocacy engagement.
However, prioritizing market-­oriented activities can shift focus
toward clients and specific programs, potentially sidelining
broader community goals (Eikenberry and Kluver 2004).
Nonprofits with higher levels of earned revenue often adopt busi-
ness strategies to enhance sustainability (Dees 1998; Choi 2014;
Dong et al. 2022). While this improves financial stability, it may
shift focus toward performance over systemic or public issues
(Bailis et al. 2009; Eikenberry and Kluver 2004; Park et al. 2022).
The pressure to maintain revenue-­generating activities can
crowd out advocacy efforts like coalition building and com-
munity organizing. Although earned income is typically unre-
stricted and could support advocacy, its market-­driven nature
often creates competing priorities, leading nonprofits to favor
fee-­based services over systemic change (Suykens et al.  2025).
As a result, earned income may ultimately limit engagement in
advocacy and lobbying. Based on these considerations, we hy-
pothesize that:
H4.  Earned income will be negatively associated with all forms
of nonprofit policy engagement.

advocacy efforts may be limited.

5   |   Data
The data for this study come from a large-­N, nationally repre-
sentative survey of US nonprofit organizations investigating
nonprofit policy engagement, the Public Engagement Nonprofit
Survey (PENS) project. This is the first of its kind since the
Strengthening Nonprofit Advocacy Project (SNAP), a major
survey conducted in 2000 by OMB Watch, Tufts University, and
Charity Lobbying in the Public Interest. The survey was fielded
in 2022 to a stratified random sample of 501(c)(3) nonprofits
with revenues of at least $50,000, excluding hospitals and higher
education institutions (stratified by organizational size, subsec-
tor, and state). E-­surveys were addressed to Executive Directors
or equivalent positions to collect data on advocacy, lobbying,
and organizational characteristics. The survey responses were
merged with the 2018 IRS Form 990 core data files from the
National Center for Charitable Statistics, the most recent avail-
able at the time.
Given online surveys' typically low response rates, further im-
pacted by the COVID-­19 pandemic, we drew an initial sample
of over 20,000 organizations. The survey ran from June 29,
2022, to January 2, 2023. During this period, average email
and website survey response rates were reported at 6% and 8%,
respectively (Chung, n.d.). Our survey yielded 2282 completed
responses from 21,267 organizations with verifiable email ad-
dresses (10.7% response rate). To address potential nonresponse
bias, we analyzed organizational variables from the 990 core
files and community characteristics from the 2018 American
Community Survey 5-­year estimates.
The analysis found no significant differences between the sur-
vey and the full sample, except for a slight over-­representation
of arts organizations (15% vs. 11%), under-­representation of
organizations with annual expenses above $10 million (2% vs.
4%), and over-­representation of Midwest organizations (26% vs.
22%). Final data were weighted for eligibility and nonresponse
to ensure the representativeness of the US nonprofit sector, ex-
cluding hospitals and higher education (Bass et al. 2007). The
final dataset includes 2225 observations with complete data for
all variables in the analytic models.
6   |   Analytic Approach
Our analysis involves three stages. First, we apply principal
component analysis to identify three distinct, theory-­based
types of nonprofit advocacy activities, which serve as dependent
variables. Second, we use ordinary least squares (OLS) regres-
sion to examine how funding sources relate to these advocacy
patterns. Finally, we compare these results with logistic regres-
sion findings, analyzing separate, dichotomous measures of lob-
bying and advocacy.
7   |   Key Independent Variables
Survey participants were asked, “For 2021, approximately what
percentage of your organization's annual revenue came from the
following sources?” They were instructed to enter “0” for inap-
plicable categories and informed that estimates were acceptable.
Based on their responses, the analysis incorporates variables
capturing the revenue share from (1) government grants or con-
tracts, (2) individual donations, (3) corporate/foundation grants,
(4) earned income, (5) membership dues, and (6) other sources,
which participants identified as endowment interest, special
events, or non-­mission-­based retail activities such as book sales,
gift shops, or thrift stores.
Survey measures capture revenue shares from each source and
offer finer categorization than IRS Form 990 data. They distin-
guish individual donations from foundation/corporate grants
and separate government funding from other public support and
earned income. These distinctions address common limitations
in prior studies, where NCCS Form 990 data often combine gov-
ernment grants, individual donations, and foundation grants
into a single “contributions” variable, and government contracts
are frequently lumped with earned income.
8   |   Control Variables
Research shows that legal knowledge of advocacy regulations
significantly influences nonprofit engagement (Calderon
et al. 2021; Bass et al. 2007). To address advocacy challenges,
nonprofits rely on partnerships, training, and knowledge shar-
ing (Zhang and Guo 2012) and collaborate through associations
to build skills and offset financial constraints (Mosley  2011;
Raeymaeckers and Van Puyvelde 2021). The models control for
legal knowledge, collaboration, organizational capacity, politi-
cal and funding environment perceptions, and organizational
characteristics such as size, revenue, age, and subsector. Table 1

for analysis, indicating whether an organization engages in

assisting in drafting legislation, developing regulations, testi-

The Complementary Partnership activities align with insider
tactics identified in prior research, while Adversarial Influence

mutual interests, while Adversarial tactics are often more con-
frontational (Buffardi et  al.  2017; Donaldson  2007; Schmid
et al. 2008; Verschuere and De Corte 2015). The Supplementary
Contributions category stands out, as it blends insider and out-
sider tactics, incorporating both collaborative and confronta-
tional strategies.
Nonprofits supplement government by providing expertise and
resources to fill policy gaps. They assist in drafting legislation,
shaping regulations, and testifying at hearings, ensuring laws
are practical and responsive to community needs. Nonprofits'
collaboration with public agencies strengthens regulatory
frameworks through technical expertise and data-­driven in-
sights, while their testimony amplifies community voices, bridg-
ing policy design and real-­world challenges.
Nonprofit involvement in litigation highlights an interesting

file suits and legal briefs to challenge laws and inform the courts

9.2   |   Advocacy Versus Lobbying
To differentiate general advocacy from legislative lobbying, ad-
ditional models specifically analyzed responses to two survey
questions: “Did your organization engage in any lobbying ef-
forts on behalf of your constituents or your organization?” and
“Did your organization engage in any policy advocacy other
than lobbying?” Participants were asked based on the following
IRS-­based definitions: “Lobbying involves taking a position on
specific legislation and communicating it to legislators or their
staff, directly or indirectly. Policy advocacy includes broader ef-
forts to influence government policy, such as sponsoring public
awareness events, conducting research, educating the public, or
participating in coalitions.”
Responses were recorded as dichotomous variables (yes/no) and
placed toward the end of the survey to avoid biasing earlier re-
sponses about specific policy engagement activities (see Table 2).
Around 27.7% of respondents reported engagement in advocacy,
while 12.7% engaged in lobbying. The correlation matrix in
Table 4 indicates moderate correlations among the five depen-
dent variables (0.42–0.67), suggesting that participation in one
activity may be associated with participation in others.
9.3   |   Revenue Streams and Policy Engagement
To address the research question, “How are revenue streams
associated with nonprofit public policy engagement patterns?”
we employed OLS regression using three composite mea-
sures as dependent variables: Complementary Partnerships,
Supplementary Contributions to Government, and Adversarial
Influence of Government.
Table  5 shows that the share of government funding is posi-
tively associated with complementary and supplementary en-
gagement types. Although statistical significance is weaker for
supplementary engagement, the results support Hypothesis 1a.
Government funding is also positively linked to adversarial
engagement, contradicting Hypothesis  1b. The share of indi-
vidual donations is negatively associated with all three engage-
ment types, supporting Hypothesis 2. In contrast, the share of
private grants shows no significant effects, failing to support
Hypotheses 3a and 3b. Earned income share is negatively as-
sociated with all advocacy types, aligning with Hypothesis 4.
The share of membership dues shows no significant effect on
complementary engagement, supporting Hypothesis 5a, but it is
positively linked to supplementary and adversarial engagement,
supporting Hypothesis 5b.
Table  6 presents logistic regression results for lobbying and
advocacy. The main model shows that a larger share of gov-
ernment funding and a larger share of foundation grants are
each positively associated with general issue advocacy. The
results suggest that greater reliance on these revenue sources
increases advocacy engagement, supporting Hypothesis 1c and
partially supporting Hypothesis 3a. Government funding does
not appear to be significantly related to lobbying, aligning with
Hypothesis 1d. That is, government funding may encourage ad-
vocacy but does not directly drive lobbying.
A larger share of individual donations is negatively associ-
ated with lobbying but not with advocacy, partially supporting
Hypothesis 2. The share of earned revenue has no significant
effect on advocacy or lobbying, failing to support Hypothesis 4.
Membership dues do not show a significant relationship with
general advocacy but are positively associated with lobbying,
consistent with Hypotheses 5a and 5b. These findings highlight
the diverse ways revenue sources influence nonprofit engage-
ment in public policy.
9.4   |   Other Factors
The findings in Tables 5 and 6 show that legal knowledge, advo-
cacy collaboration, and organizational capacity positively influ-
ence nonprofit engagement across various advocacy forms. The
policy environment and organizational size are also important.
The policy environment supports complementary and sup-
plementary advocacy but negatively affects general advocacy,
with no significant link to lobbying or adversarial engagement.
Organizational size is positively associated with complementary
and general advocacy but has little impact on other policy activ-
ities. Although organizational age does not significantly affect
specific advocacy types, it is positively linked to general advo-
cacy and lobbying. Additionally, subsector differences highlight
the need to consider organizational context in nonprofit pol-
icy engagement. These results reinforce that internal capacity
and external conditions collectively shape nonprofit advocacy
strategies.
10   |   Robustness Checks
Not all organizations receive revenue from every source. In our
sample, 46.6% had government funding, 85.2% received individ-
ual donations, 65.3% secured foundation or corporate grants,
50.8% earned income, and 20.4% collected membership dues.
To ensure the robustness of our results, the analytic results in
Table 7 incorporated dummy variables for the presence of each
revenue source, following Yanagi et al. (2021). These indicators
account for structural differences between organizations with
and without specific revenue streams, addressing potential bias
from zero-­revenue cases.
For example, the variable Gov_Funding_Presence is coded as 1
if an organization receives government funding and 0 otherwise,
while the variable Gov_Funding_Size represents the proportion of
government funding in total revenue. Although the correlation be-
tween presence and share variables is generally low, government
funding (0.665) and private grants (0.538) show moderate correla-
tions. However, the variance inflation factor (VIF) tests indicate no
significant multicollinearity concerns, confirming model stability.

11   |   Discussion
This study analyzes nationally representative data on US non-
profit advocacy to examine how revenue sources shape differ-
ent types of policy engagement. Using factor analysis on 19
advocacy activities, we identify three types of engagement—
supplementary, complementary, and adversarial—that can be
categorized within Young's (2000) typology of nonprofit–gov-
ernment relationships, more generally. This approach con-
tributes a more general, theory-­based framework to explain
differences in the various advocacy tactics nonprofits can en-
gage in.
Our findings show that government funding is positively associ-
ated with most advocacy forms of engagement, except legislative
lobbying. As prior research suggests (Berry and Arons 2003),
nonprofits strategically cultivate government relationships,
often through funding ties, to align with policymakers and to
advance shared priorities, reinforcing advocacy efforts. These
results highlight the importance of distinguishing between
legislative lobbying and general advocacy, which differ in ap-
proach, legal constraints, and policy impact. While government
funding does not significantly relate to lobbying, it positively
corresponds with general advocacy and complementary, sup-
plementary, and adversarial policy engagement activities more
generally, underscoring the importance of government funding
relationships for nonprofit policy engagement.
Interestingly, reliance on individual donations is negatively
associated with all types of policy engagement. In politically
charged environments, nonprofits may avoid advocacy to pre-
vent alienating donors with diverse views (Fyall and Allard 2017;
Guo and Saxton 2010). Additionally, nonprofits often prioritize
fundraising over policy activities, since maintaining donor re-
lationships depends on outreach and engagement rather than
advocacy (Sargeant et  al.  2012). These results reinforce prior
studies showing nonprofits' reluctance to engage in contentious
policy debates, suggesting a cautious approach driven by donor
preferences.
Prior research suggests that nonprofits heavily reliant on
earned income adopt market-­oriented approaches, prioritiz-
ing financial sustainability over advocacy (Bailis et al. 2009;
Eikenberry and Kluver 2004; Park et al. 2022). Our findings
indicate that these organizations engage less in complemen-
tary, supplementary, and adversarial policy activities, likely
because their focus on program quality and revenue generation
limits capacity for grassroots advocacy and public education.
However, the absence of a significant relationship between
earned revenue and general advocacy or legislative lobbying
suggests that these broader or more targeted advocacy forms
may require resources or motivations that extend beyond
market-­driven constraints. This, combined with the nuances
from our supplementary analysis, calls for further research on
the different pathways through which earned income shapes
nonprofits' strategic advocacy priorities.
While our findings align with previous research on nonprofit
advocacy (Berry and Arons  2003; Mosley  2010, 2011), this
study expands the scope by incorporating a complementary,
supplementary, and adversarial engagement framework, reflect-
ing the evolving nature of advocacy, particularly in the digital
age. For example, social media-­driven policy education, catego-
rized as adversarial engagement, demonstrates how nonprofits
use digital platforms to mobilize support, raise awareness, and
amplify their policy influence.
This study also underscores the complex landscape nonprofits
navigate to advance their missions through different advocacy
approaches. The results emphasize the need for public adminis-
trators and policymakers to recognize the diverse forms of non-
profit policy engagement and develop funding and regulatory
frameworks that support their evolving roles. By examining the
influence of different revenue sources on advocacy, this study
provides practical insights for nonprofit leaders and policymak-
ers in shaping effective funding strategies.
Nonprofit–government relationships can take many forms.
Although our study focuses on financial ties, such as revenue
dependence, future research should examine other relational
factors that may influence advocacy strategies. These include
the extent of nonprofit involvement in service delivery, the for-
malization of partnerships (e.g., contracts or MOUs), frequency
and quality of communication, perceived government recep-
tivity to advocacy, and alignment of policy goals. For example,
Carré et al. (2025) show that service roles and perceptions of
government support can shape whether nonprofits pursue coop-
erative or confrontational tactics. Examining these dimensions
would deepen our understanding of how advocacy emerges
from broader nonprofit–government dynamics.
Some limitations highlight areas for further investigation. Our
research is a cross-­sectional look at nonprofit advocacy absent
consideration of specific policy contexts. Future studies could
examine how advocacy typologies function across policy areas
and explore alternative categorizations beyond Young's typology.
Additionally, future research could investigate how nonprofits
balance revenue strategies specifically for increased policy en-
gagement, including whether certain funding sources undermine
or limit policy engagement ambitions. Further studies could also
analyze the interaction between funding sources, regulatory en-
vironments, and venues of local, state, and federal policy engage-
ment. Finally, because our survey does not capture details about
specific lawsuits or legal action, future research could examine
the prevalence and types of litigation across nonprofit subsectors
to better understand this dimension of advocacy.
12   |   Conclusion
This study highlights the complex relationships between rev-
enue sources and advocacy. It offers practical insights that
nonprofits can strategically align their income streams with
advocacy goals. For example, a healthcare-­focused organization
might seek government grants or foundation partnerships to
support its policy efforts. Funding sources also shape advocacy
tactics and fundraising strategies, emphasizing the need to align
financial and advocacy priorities. By aligning funding strategies
with organizational goals, nonprofit leaders can better support
targeted policy activities.

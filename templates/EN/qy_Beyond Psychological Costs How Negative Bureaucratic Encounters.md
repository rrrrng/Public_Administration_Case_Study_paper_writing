ABSTRACT
The administrative burden framework suggests that citizens experience psychological costs when interacting with the state.
Previous research has primarily focused on context-­specific psychological costs, such as stress and loss of autonomy related to
particular policies or encounters. This study expands the framework by integrating it with the relational model of authority and
the stress and coping theory, and proposes that negative bureaucratic encounters have an adverse impact on citizens' general
psychological well-­being, with cognitive human capital serving as a mitigating factor. Using a large-­scale representative longi-
tudinal survey dataset from China, we test relevant hypotheses. The empirical analysis demonstrates that exposure to negative
bureaucratic encounters is associated with poorer mental health, while formal education helps mitigate the impact. These find-
ings underscore the significant influence of bureaucratic encounters on citizens' well-­being.
1   |   Introduction
Max Weber's seminal work on bureaucracy highlights the in-
herent power imbalance between state actors and citizens
(Weber 1978). This imbalance is not only a structural feature
of governance but a potential source of profound psychological
strain. Public health literature has already reported negative
correlations between individuals' mental health and various un-
favorable bureaucratic encounters, including aggressive policing
(Geller et al. 2014; Jackson et al. 2019), traffic restrictions during
pandemics (Arendt et  al.  2020), unfair treatment in prison
(Beijersbergen et al. 2014; Fitzalan-­Howard and Wakeling 2020),
and discrimination against immigrants and refugees (Silove
et  al.  2000). However, a theoretical gap persists: while these
studies confirm the psychological damage, they often treat the
negative encounter as a generic stressor, limited in explaining
how specific administrative processes generate these impacts.
Recent public administration scholarship has also begun to ex-
amine the psychological dimensions of administrative burden
(Baekgaard et al. 2021; Bell et al. 2023; Döring and Madsen 2022;
Hattke et al. 2020). However, advancing this inquiry requires a
shift from merely identifying immediate and context-­specific
outcomes to understanding how the procedural nature of bu-
reaucratic power erodes individual psychological well-­being in
the long run. Consequently, this study seeks to establish theo-
retical and empirical linkages between bureaucratic encounters
and the mental health of citizens.
While studies of bureaucratic encounters traditionally fo-
cused more on state actors (Andersen and Guul 2019; Jilke and
Tummers 2018; Lipsky 1980), researchers have recently begun
to investigate the impact of these encounters on citizens' percep-
tions of or attitudes toward bureaucrats or government agencies,
such as political trust, perceived legitimacy, and satisfaction
(Barnes and Henly  2018; Bell et  al.  2024; Döring et  al.  2025;
Du and Zhu 2024; Kang 2022; Nielsen et al. 2021; Ruder and
Woods 2020; Song et al. 2021; Van Ryzin 2004). Although these
variables are reported by citizens, they still reflect evaluations of

the state actors, rather than citizens' own well-­being. From the
perspective of public value (Bryson et al. 2014), a good govern-
ment should prioritize its citizens' well-­being over their evalua-
tions of public organizations. However, research on the impact
of bureaucratic encounters on citizens' general well-­being is
relatively rare. Our study suggests that negative bureaucratic
encounters can affect citizens' psychological well-­being (i.e.,
mental health), thereby generating more substantive influences.
The concept of administrative burden, defined as an individ-
ual's experience of policy implementation as onerous (Burden
et al. 2012), provides an effective approach to investigating the
effect of bureaucratic encounters on citizens' well-­being. This
concept places citizens at the forefront, positing that when cit-
izens interact with the state, three types of costs emerge: learn-
ing costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs (Moynihan
et al. 2015). We focus on the psychological costs, which include
the stigma of applying for or participating in a program with
negative perceptions, a sense of loss of autonomy in interactions
with the state, or the stresses of dealing with administrative
processes (Baekgaard et al. 2021; Hattke et al. 2020; Moynihan
et al. 2015). Psychological costs are important, not only for the
people who experience them, but also for society overall. When
state actions lead to poor psychological well-­being among certain
groups, it can undermine the success of programs. Additionally,
state actions that are psychologically distressing can also damage
democratic institutions by isolating these groups from the rest of
society (Baekgaard et al. 2021). Previous research confirms the
psychological costs associated with administrative burdens, but
it primarily emphasizes immediate or contextual psychological
outcomes, such as the loss of autonomy and the stress related
to a specific policy or encounter (Baekgaard et al. 2021; Döring
and Madsen 2022). Learning from the relational model of au-
thority (Tyler and Lind 1992) and the stress and coping theory
(Lazarus and Folkman 1984), our study proposes that negative
bureaucratic encounters can lead to perceived injustice that acts
as significant stressors, broadly affecting individuals' mental
health in general (McEwen 2012). Additionally, we argue that
formal education, as a type of cognitive human capital and
coping resources, can mitigate the adverse impact of negative
bureaucratic encounters. Based on a panel dataset from a large-­
scale representative longitudinal survey in China, we provide
empirical evidence supporting these hypotheses.
This study contributes to the literature in the following aspects.
First, it speaks to the literature of bureaucratic encounters by
providing a more comprehensive analysis of how negative en-
counters influence citizens' general well-­being, responding
to the recent appeal for citizen-­centered research (Jakobsen
et al. 2019). Second, this study contributes to the literature on
administrative burdens by advancing the concept of psycholog-
ical costs a step further, shifting from contextual perceptions of
a policy to broader impacts on general psychological well-­being
(Baekgaard et al. 2021; Döring and Madsen 2022). Third, this
study provides empirical evidence on psychological outcomes
of bureaucratic encounters in a non-­Western context. In fact,
outside of Western democracies, the larger power disparity be-
tween the state and citizens makes the consequences of negative
bureaucratic encounters even more significant (Gat 2007). Our
findings address the gap in the literature that predominantly fo-
cuses on Western contexts.
2   |   Literature Review
2.1   |   Bureaucratic Encounters
and Administrative Burden
Bureaucratic encounters refer to the interactions between cit-
izens and public officials within the context of bureaucracy.
These encounters are a critical area of public administration
research because they represent the point of contact between
the public and the state, where administrative policies and pro-
cedures are directly experienced by individuals (Bartels 2013).
Traditionally, public administration scholars regard bureau-
cratic encounters as an inherent problem of public authority in
democracies (Bartels 2009). By using Max Weber's ideal-­type of
bureaucracy as the reference point, earlier studies investigate
how the characteristics of formal bureaucratic organizations,
such as concentration of power, depersonalized communication
and decision-­making, and restricted discretion, functionally or
dysfunctionally influence the social environment (Bartels 2013;
Merton 1952; Mouzelis 1967). The practical behaviors of front-
line professionals, particularly the discretion of street-­level bu-
reaucrats, are the focal points of such studies (Goodsell 1981;
Lipsky 1980).
In recent years, scholars have increasingly shifted their focus
toward studying bureaucratic encounters from the perspective
of citizens. One stream of literature emphasizes the immediate
and contextual reactions of citizens to bureaucratic encounters.
Relevant studies often employ qualitative research methods to
explore citizens' perceptions, attributions, preparation, and cop-
ing strategies in relation to bureaucratic encounters (Barnes and
Henly 2018; Döring et al. 2025; Masood and Azfar-­Nisar 2021;
Nielsen et  al.  2021). Another stream of literature focuses on
the effects of bureaucratic encounters on citizens' attitudes or
perceptions outside the encounters. These studies rely more
on quantitative analyses of survey data to reveal the relation-
ships between bureaucratic encounters and political trust, per-
ceived legitimacy, or satisfaction with government performance
Evidence for Practice

The aforementioned shift toward a citizen-­centered research
interest is closely related to the recently emerging concept of
administrative burden (Burden et al. 2012), which makes a key
distinction between state actions and individual experiences
(Halling and Baekgaard 2024), and therefore offers a novel ap-
proach to recenter the citizen in public administration research
(Jakobsen et al. 2019). Administrative burden is defined as the
individual's experience of policy implementation as onerous
(Burden et  al.  2012; Herd and Moynihan  2019). The burden
is caused by state actions and can manifest as learning costs,
where individuals must acquire necessary information about
services or compliance requirements; compliance costs, which
encompass the effort and resources expended in adhering to
governmental processes; and psychological costs, which involve
the stress and emotional impact resulting from these interac-
tions (Moynihan et al. 2015).
The concept of cost offers significant explanatory power for
understanding the impacts of bureaucratic encounters on cit-
izens, with advantages evident in two key areas. First, cost is
an abstract and general concept not confined solely to specific
encounters, but potentially relating to various daily-­life ac-
tivities (Christensen et al. 2020; Halling and Baekgaard 2024;
Heinrich  2016; Martin et  al.  2024). Therefore, it provides a
theoretical basis, allowing researchers to extend their analysis
beyond the direct citizen-­state interactions to other attitudes,
behaviors, and well-­being of individuals. Second, costs can be
cumulative, potentially leading to general and noncontextual
impacts (McFarlane  2010). This expansion of focus enables
researchers to look beyond the immediate moments of bu-
reaucratic encounters and explore the sustained effects of ad-
ministrative burdens on the general welfare of individuals.
2.2   |   Negative Bureaucratic Encounters
and Psychological Costs
The core concept of this study is negative bureaucratic encoun-
ters, which are defined from the citizens' perspective as their
negative experiences encountered during interactions with bu-
reaucratic institutions (Nielsen et al. 2021). This definition in-
volves citizens' judgments and thus emphasizes their subjective
perceptions. Common examples of negative bureaucratic en-
counters include experiencing unfair treatment by bureaucrats,
engaging in conflicts with them, and facing unreasonable delays
or charges. These experiences are closely linked to administra-
tive burden and result in three types of costs. We particularly
focus on the psychological cost, as it describes a more ultimate
or downstream effect of administrative burden on citizens. In
other words, the costs that citizens put into learning and comply-
ing during bureaucratic encounters can ultimately affect their
emotional or psychological status (Hattke et al. 2020). Here, psy-
chological costs are conceptualized as a generic term for psycho-
logical outcomes of bureaucratic encounters, including but not
limited to autonomy loss and stress (Herd and Moynihan 2019).
Several studies have investigated how burdensome bureaucratic
encounters induce psychological costs. For instance, Baekgaard
et al. (2021) use experimental and quasi-­experimental designs to
reveal the positive effect of compliance demands on stress and
loss of autonomy. Döring and Madsen (2022) employ structural
equation modeling to study how administrative literacy and so-
cial capital mitigate stress and loss of autonomy related to unem-
ployment insurance claims. Based on a laboratory experiment,
Hattke et al. (2020) find that administrative burden evokes sig-
nificant negative emotional responses, especially confusion,
frustration, and anger. These studies have mostly identified
short-­term or policy-­specific psychological outcomes. However,
as suggested by Herd and Moynihan (2019), psychological costs
are not one-­off negative experiences caused by discrete encoun-
ters. Instead, these costs accumulate over time, leading to sus-
tained stress related to administrative burdens.
To understand how these costs accumulate, we build a concep-
tual bridge between the abstract nature of administrative bur-
den and the lived reality of bureaucratic encounters. Drawing
on the relational model of authority (Tyler and Lind 1992), we
argue that specific interactional dynamics within a negative
encounter can be the key drivers of these psychological costs.
When citizens interact with the state, they are not merely pro-
cessing transactions but are also receiving signals about their
status. Negative encounters are perceived as injustice (Du and
Zhu 2024), leading to a denial of standing and neutrality. These
interactional failures constitute the primary psychological cost
of the burden, transforming a bureaucratic procedure into a
profound experience of relational devaluation. Given the well-­
documented trend toward an expanding scope of government
functions globally (Afonso et al. 2023), driven by new societal
challenges and citizen expectations, understanding these dy-
namics is of considerable importance.
3   |   Hypotheses
3.1   |   Negative Bureaucratic Encounters
and Mental Health
Mental health is defined as “a state of well-­being in which every
individual realizes his or her own potential, can cope with the
normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and
is able to make a contribution to his or her community” (World
Health Organization  2004). The focus on mental health ad-
vances the concept of psychological costs a step further, empha-
sizing more general effects beyond the contextual perceptions of
specific encounters or policies. Prior research has reported that
the factors influencing individual mental health are complex,
including physiological factors, psychological factors, and en-
vironmental factors (World Health Organization 2022). Several
studies have also emphasized the negative impact of external
shocks on mental health (Charlson et al. 2019; Makwana 2019;
Frasquilho et al. 2015). To provide a theoretical basis for how
mental health is influenced by negative bureaucratic encoun-
ters, we embed the process within a framework integrating
the relational model of authority (Tyler and Lind 1992) and the
stress and coping theory (Lazarus and Folkman 1984).
As indicated above, the relational model of authority suggests
that individuals evaluate authorities not just by outcomes, but
fundamentally by the fairness of the procedures used and the

interpersonal treatment received (Tyler and Lind 1992). Negative
bureaucratic encounters, such as experiencing unfair treatment,
engaging in conflicts with officials, and facing unreasonable
delays or charges, can directly embody violations of these jus-
tice principles (Bartels 2015; Tyler 2006), generating perceived
injustice among citizens (Du and Zhu 2024). Specifically, these
interactional dynamics can signal a profound lack of standing
(e.g., being treated impolitely, disrespectfully, or as if one's rights
are not considered), a lack of neutrality (e.g., perceiving bias or
arbitrary decision-­making), and a breach of trust (e.g., doubting
the authority's ethicality or benevolent intentions) (Tyler and
Lind 1992).
These experiences of injustice, particularly when emanat-
ing from the state, an entity holding significant power and
societal importance, can be deeply threatening. Drawing on
the stress and coping theory (Lazarus and Folkman  1984),
we theorize that negative bureaucratic encounters function
as significant stressors through the process of cognitive ap-
praisal. They are not merely seen as administrative trans-
action costs, but are appraised as a threat to one's social
standing and control (Tyler and Lind  1992; Tyler  2006; Van
Prooijen 2009). Furthermore, given the often-­limited avenues
for redress in bureaucratic settings, these appraised stressors
are difficult to resolve and can become cumulative (Döring
and Madsen 2022). According to the stress process paradigm
(Pearlin et al. 1981; Sugie and Turney 2017), such persistent,
unresolved strain contributes to the deterioration of general
mental health over time (McEwen 2012). Therefore, we pro-
pose Hypothesis 1.
Hypothesis 1.  Negative bureaucratic encounters have a neg-
ative impact on citizens' mental health.
3.2   |   The Moderating Role of Formal Education
The stress and coping theory (Lazarus and Folkman  1984)
posits that individuals' responses to stressors are significantly
shaped by their available coping resources. In coping with
stress from negative bureaucratic encounters, citizens' re-
sources, particularly human capital, are crucial (Christensen
et al. 2020). In economics, human capital is traditionally de-
fined as the imbedded resources in people which can increase
future real income (Becker 1962). In the broader social science
literature, it is defined as the resources that individuals can
use to manage tasks and challenges (Vaaramo et  al.  2024).
Research on administrative burden also considers human
capital, especially the cognitive aspects of human capital, as
an important factor (Bell et al. 2024; Christensen et al. 2020;
Masood and Azfar-­Nisar 2021). A body of literature highlights
the role of general cognitive resources, especially executive
capacity for daily routines, in shaping citizens' perceptions
of state actions (Christensen et  al.  2020). Empirical evi-
dence has shown that citizens with fewer general cognitive
resources tend to perceive greater administrative burdens
due to weak executive functioning (Bell et al. 2023). Another
body of literature introduces concepts of administrative liter-
acy and administrative capital to emphasize the specialized
knowledge and skills in the administrative domain (Döring
and Madsen 2022; Masood and Azfar-­Nisar 2021). Empirical
studies have also demonstrated that administrative literacy
is negatively related to individuals' perceived administrative
burdens (Döring and Madsen 2022).
The correlations between cognitive human capital and admin-
istrative burden found in previous cross-­sectional studies are
highly insightful. However, given that we distinguish between
state actions and the costs experienced by citizens, treating
the former as external stimuli and the latter as individual re-
sponses, cognitive human capital should act as a moderator in
this process (Christensen et al. 2020). Specifically, when similar
bureaucratic encounters occur, individuals with different levels
of cognitive human capital experience varying degrees of stress,
leading to diverse mental health outcomes.
While other coping resources like social capital and spe-
cific administrative literacy are also crucial (Döring and
Madsen  2022), we particularly focus on formal education
(years of schooling), as it is the most common proxy for
human capital with explicit policy implications (Becker 1962;
Vaaramo et  al.  2024) and is highly correlated with general
cognitive resources and administrative literacy (Carlsson
et al. 2015; Persson 2015). These attributes are essential when
dealing with bureaucratic procedures, as they equip individ-
uals with the necessary skills to understand administrative
requirements and devise effective strategies to address them.
Based on stress and coping theory, individuals' psychological
outcomes are shaped by the coping resources they can use
in stress-­inducing situations. The cognitive resources (both
general and administrative) gained through formal education
serve as coping resources against the stressor of bureaucratic
encounters. With more years of formal education, negative bu-
reaucratic encounters can cause less damage to mental health.
Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 2.
Hypothesis 2.  Formal education mitigates the adverse im-
pact of negative bureaucratic encounters on citizens' mental
health.

4   |   Data and Method
4.1   |   Data
In order to analyze the effect of negative bureaucratic encounters
on citizens' mental health, we utilized a panel dataset sourced
from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), initiated in 2010
by the Institution of Social Science Survey at Peking University
(Xie and Hu 2014). The CFPS survey employed a stringent mul-
tistage probability sampling approach, covering over 40,000 in-
dividuals in 162 counties across 25 provinces, which collectively
represent around 95% of China's total population. Following the
baseline survey of 14,960 households in 2010, all original fam-
ily members and their future biological/adopted children were
defined as permanent genetic members to be tracked in all sub-
sequent waves (every 2 years) (Xie and Hu 2014). This rigorous
tracking design allowed us to observe the same individuals over
multiple time points.

We restricted our empirical analysis to data from the 2012, 2014,
and 2016 waves for two primary reasons. First, this period cor-
responded to a phase of rapid urbanization and economic de-
velopment in China prior to the nationwide rollout of digital
government services, when face-­to-­face interactions remained
the dominant mode of state-­citizen contact. This context allows
us to examine the psychological impacts of traditional bureau-
cratic encounters and also enhances the broader relevance of
our findings, as similar institutional conditions persist in many
developing countries where e-­government remains limited.
Second, survey questions on negative bureaucratic encounters
were discontinued in later waves, making this the most compre-
hensive panel available for our interest, while we acknowledge
that this data is not the most recent. We constructed an unbal-
anced panel by tracking individuals across waves. To account
for potential sample attrition bias, we confirmed our results
using a balanced panel that includes only individuals present
in all three waves (see Supporting Information Appendix  B,
Table B5).
4.1.1   |   Dependent Variable
Our core dependent variable is respondent's mental health,
which was measured by two scales. In the 2012 and 2016
waves, respondent's mental health was measured by the
Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CESD) scale
(Radloff 1977), which has 20 items and can generate a total
score ranging from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating
more severe mental health issues. In the 2014 wave, respon-
dent's mental health was measured by the Kessler-­6 (K6) scale
(Kessler et al. 2003), which has six items and can generate a
total score ranging from 0 to 24, also with higher scores in-
dicating more severe issues. Details of the items in these
two scales are given in Supporting Information Appendix A,
Tables A1 and A2. Both scales exhibit strong internal consis-
tency and reliability in epidemiological investigations (Kessler
et al. 2010; Van Dam and Earleywine 2011). Then, we stan-
dardized the original CESD and K6 scales to be between 0 and
10. As this variable is a reverse indicator of mental health, we
relabeled it as “Mental issues.”
It is important to note that although CESD and K6 are two dis-
tinct scales, both are reliable and have been tested in the Chinese
context (Chen et al. 2020; Zhang and Li 2022). Therefore, the
measures based on the two scales are comparable after stan-
dardization. Moreover, our empirical strategy is based on a two-­
way fixed effects model, which captures any potential systematic
differences between the two scales through year fixed effects,
and this potential difference should be irrelevant to the inde-
pendent variables. Therefore, we can use data from these three
waves simultaneously for empirical analysis (using data from
only 2012 and 2016 waves does not change the main results,
as shown in Supporting Information Appendix  B, Table  B4).
For mechanism analysis, we also incorporated trust in local
government (Trust in gov, an 11-­point scale) and self-­reported
confidence in future life (Future confidence, a 5-­point scale), as
proxies for citizens' perceptions of state justice and their broader
outlook.1
4.1.2   |   Independent Variable
Our core independent variable is negative bureaucratic en-
counters. The concept itself is multifaceted because citizens
may encounter a variety of bureaucratic situations, making it
difficult for them to recall every specific detail during a sur-
vey. Therefore, similar to Du and Zhu (2024), we adopted a
more generalized definition of negative bureaucratic encoun-
ters by asking whether a citizen has had several kinds of neg-
ative experiences in the past year. Specifically, in the 2012,
2014, and 2016 waves of CFPS, respondents were asked the
following four questions. (1) In the past year, have you expe-
rienced unfair treatment from government officials? (2) In
the past year, have you had any conflicts with government
officials? (3) In the past year, have you faced unreasonable
delays and stalling at a government agency? (4) In the past
year, have you encountered unreasonable charges paid to a
government agency? These questions were identically worded
and administered in the three waves, ensuring consistency in
measurement across the panel. For each question, we created
a dummy variable to code the respondents' answers, coding
one for “yes” and zero for “no.” Then, we used the mean value
of answers to these four questions as the main independent
variable, as these four items can comprehensively reflect citi-
zens' experience of negative bureaucratic encounters. We ac-
knowledge that these measurements have a certain degree of
subjectivity, but this is consistent with our definition of nega-
tive bureaucratic encounters.
Specifically, in developing countries, unreasonable charges
and delays in bureaucratic encounters are common and often
occur together. They are likely to lead to conflicts with officials
and perceptions of unfair treatment. The prevalence of these
encounters in our sample is noteworthy: 10.0% of observations
include reports of unfair treatment, 3.8% involve conflicts

4.1.3   |   Control Variables
We used control variables on two levels. The first level consisted of
city-­level socioeconomic variables that vary over time, including
gross domestic product (GDP), population, and fiscal expenditure,
sourced from the China City Yearbook. For each wave of the CFPS
survey, we considered these economic statistics of the correspond-
ing cities for the previous year, because these lagged variables
may be confounders influencing both bureaucratic encounters
and mental health. Additionally, we controlled for time-­varying
personal characteristics of the respondents, such as age, urban or
rural residence, education level, marital status, number of chil-
dren, employment status, and household income. These variables
are potential confounders, as they capture significant life changes
that may affect both the independent and dependent variables. In
addition, we also used education level (years of schooling) to con-
duct moderation analysis.
Finally, we dropped observations that have missing values and
established a panel with 84,737 observations from 39,845 unique
individuals. Table 1 shows the summary statistics. The gender
ratio is nearly balanced, the average age is 46.5 years old, and
86.1% of respondents are married. The average years of school-
ing are 7.7, and the average household net income is about 47,600
Chinese Yuan. These statistics are close to the demographic
characteristics of the Chinese population.
4.2   |   Model Specification
We used a two-­way fixed effects model to estimate the effect
of negative bureaucratic encounters on citizens' mental health.
The regression model is shown in Equation (1).
In Equation (1), the dependent variable is Mental issuesikt, indi-
cating the level of mental health issues of respondent i in city k
at year t. The core independent variable is Negative bureaucratic
encountersikt, indicating the reported experience of negative bu-
reaucratic encounters of respondent i in city k at year t. Xikt is a
set of individual characteristics, including age, urban residence,
years of schooling, employment status, household income, mar-
ital status, and number of children, for respondent i in city k at
year t. As changes in these variables can represent significant
life events that may influence both bureaucratic encounters and
mental health, they were controlled for as potential confound-
ers. Ykt is a set of city socioeconomic characteristics, including
GDP, population, and fiscal expenditure, for city k at year t − 1.
These variables capture changes in the external social environ-
ment that could also affect both the independent and dependent
variables, and were therefore included as controls. For potential
confounders arising from unobserved and time-­invariant in-
dividual features, we controlled for individual fixed effects δi.
These effects can include stable physiological predispositions
(e.g., genetic factors) and long-­standing psychological traits (e.g.,
baseline personality), also influencing both independent and de-
pendent variables. We also added year fixed effects λt to account
for the effect of shocks on all respondents in a particular year.
The error term, εikt, is allowed to be correlated across different
waves for an individual, and standard errors are clustered at the
individual level. We expect the coefficient β to be significantly
positive, indicating that negative bureaucratic encounters dam-
age mental health, thereby supporting Hypothesis 1.
Additionally, we added formal education into Equation (1) as a
moderator for the main effect. If the coefficients for the interac-
tion terms are significantly negative, we can verify that formal
education mitigates the adverse impact of negative bureau-
cratic encounters on citizens' mental health, thereby supporting
Hypothesis 2.

We first estimated how negative bureaucratic encounters influ-

Nevertheless, reverse causality could be a significant imped-
iment to causal inference, as citizens' mental health may also
influence their likelihood of having negative bureaucratic
encounters. Another issue is the measurement error of the
independent variable. Some may concern that our indepen-
dent variable measures trust in government or perception of
procedural justice of state actions (Beijersbergen et al. 2014;
Fitzalan-­Howard and Wakeling 2020), rather than the expe-
rience of negative bureaucratic encounters. To address these
two issues and strengthen causal identification, we pro-
posed an instrumental variable strategy, which exploits an
e-­government policy shock (as a natural experiment) and its
asymmetric impact on people with different levels of educa-
tion. The instrumental variable is defined as the interaction
between the e-­government policy shock and a dummy indi-
cating whether the respondent's education level exceeds the
sample median.
Specifically, in the latter half of 2014, the Chinese central
government selected 80 cities to implement the “Information
Benefiting the People” initiative. This policy aimed to enhance
information service capabilities of the public sector and to
promote equitable access to public services. The central gov-
ernment supported these 80 pilot cities in building unified, city-­
level “Internet + Government Services” platforms. The policy
was rigorously enforced, with the central government providing
standardized technical guidelines, financial support, and con-
ducting annual evaluations. Details of the policy are provided
in Supporting Information Appendix D. This policy interven-
tion enhanced e-­government capabilities in the pilot cities, po-
tentially reducing citizens' likelihood of experiencing negative
bureaucratic encounters. Additionally, due to the digital divide,
the benefits of this policy were not universally accessible (Pérez-­
Morote et al. 2020). During the period from 2012 to 2016, mobile
internet access was still limited in China, and individuals with
relatively higher levels of education were more likely to benefit
from the pilot policy. Consequently, we used the interaction be-
tween the pilot policy and respondents' education level (above
the median or not) as our instrumental variable.
The e-­government pilot policy is represented as a dummy vari-
able (E-­government), taking a value of one if the respondent is
located in one of the 80 pilot cities and the year is 2016; other-
wise, it is set to zero. Median Education is a dummy variable
that takes the value of one if the respondent's years of school-
ing is no fewer than the sample median (9 years, also the years
of compulsory education in China), and zero otherwise. We
acknowledge that finding a completely exogenous instrumen-
tal variable is challenging. However, we demonstrated that
time-­varying socioeconomic factors at the city level, such as
GDP, population, and fiscal expenditure, do not significantly
influence a city's likelihood of being selected for the pilot pro-
gram within our sample (Supporting Information Appendix D,
Table D2). The interaction term between the pilot policy and
education level is less likely to affect mental health through al-
ternative channels other than reducing negative bureaucratic
encounters, conditional on our extensive individual-­level and
city-­level controls. Although the exclusion restriction cannot
be fully tested, our instrument is logically valid. Supporting
Information Appendix E provides additional discussion of the
instrument as well as details of an alternative instrumental
variable strategy.2
The estimation results based on the instrumental variable strat-
egy are presented in Table  3. The first stage F-­statistics are
greater than 10, indicating that our instrumental variable does
not suffer from the weak instrument problem. Moreover, the co-
efficients for Negative bureaucratic encounters, as estimated by
the instrumental variable approach, are significantly positive
across models with different controls, further supporting the ro-
bustness of our main finding.
It should be noted that the IV estimate exceeds the baseline
result. Following Lal et  al.  (2024), we suggest that this likely
reflects a local average treatment effect. The compliers in our
context may be particularly sensitive to bureaucratic friction.3
Thus, while the IV confirms the causal direction, the magnitude
may represent the effect on this specific subpopulation rather
than the general public.
5.3   |   The Moderating Effect of Formal Education
Then, we examined how respondents' formal education moder-
ates the adverse impact of negative bureaucratic encounters on
mental health. Specifically, we utilized two variables as the mod-
erator. The first variable is Education, representing the number
of years of schooling. The second variable is Median Education,
a dummy variable indicating the respondent's years of schooling
are no fewer than the sample median. Models 4 and 5 in Table 2
present the moderation analysis results.
As shown in the two moderation models, the coefficients for
Negative bureaucratic encounters × Education (Model 4) and
Negative bureaucratic encounters × Education Median (Model
5) are significantly negative at the 1% level. This indicates that
more educated respondents are less susceptible to the adverse
impact of negative bureaucratic encounters on mental health.
Since we explicitly controlled for socioeconomic variables, in-
cluding income and employment, these results highlight the
cognitive benefits of education, distinct from its economic as-
pects. These results support Hypothesis 2.
5.4   |   Further Analysis on Trust in Government
and Confidence in Future Life
A core mechanism underlying the aforementioned theoretical
argument is the perceived injustice of the state. While our study
does not directly measure this specific variable, we utilized two
variables, including trust in local government (Trust in govern-
ment) and self-­reported confidence in future life (Future con-
fidence), as proxies for citizens' perceptions of state justice and
their broader outlook. The rationale for using these proxies is
intuitive: if citizens perceive significant injustice from the state,
their trust in government is likely to diminish. Furthermore, the
profound lack of standing and neutrality discussed earlier (Tyler
and Lind 1992) can erode an individual's sense of security and
efficacy, potentially leading to a decline in their confidence in
future life.

6   |   Discussion
Drawing on the literature of administrative burden, the rela-
tional model of authority and the stress and coping theory, this
study proposes that citizens' experiences of negative bureau-
cratic encounters damage their psychological well-­being and
cognitive human capital can mitigate this adverse effect. Based
on a representative longitudinal panel dataset, this study pro-
vides empirical evidence supporting relevant hypotheses. The
contributions of this study are in several aspects.
First, this study contributes to the literature of bureaucratic
encounters and especially responds to the recent appeal for
citizen-­centered research (Jakobsen et al. 2019). Our quantita-
tive analysis complements previous qualitative studies which
have mainly focused on citizens' perceptions of bureaucrats
or the bureaucracy and have found that such perceptions are
largely negative (Barnes and Henly 2018; Nielsen et al. 2021;
Watkins-­Hayes 2011). Our research extends the understanding
of bureaucratic encounters by examining not only the negative
perceptions of specific events but also noncontextual impacts
on general well-­being. We quantitatively measure negative bu-
reaucratic encounters and mental health, and employ rigorous
statistical methods to identify how these negative encounters
influence citizens' psychological well-­being. While acknowl-
edging the multifaceted nature of mental health, which is
influenced by a wide range of individual and environmental
factors, our research isolates the unique and significant im-
pact of a key aspect of governance: the interactions between
citizens and the state. We find that experiences of unfair treat-
ment by government officials, conflicts with government offi-
cials, and unreasonable delays or charges within the past year
all damage mental health. The impact of negative bureaucratic
encounters extends beyond satisfaction with or trust in the
government reported in previous studies (Du and Zhu 2024;
Kang 2022).
Second, this study contributes to the literature of administrative
burden by providing a nuanced and comprehensive extension
of psychological costs. By integrating Tyler and Lind's (1992) re-
lational model of authority and Lazarus and Folkman's (1984)
stress and coping theory with the administrative burden frame-
work, our analysis advances the concept of psychological costs
a crucial step further. We demonstrate that these costs are not
merely immediate or context-­specific negative perceptions
(Baekgaard et  al.  2021; Döring and Madsen  2022), but rather

research, our study also highlights the significant role of cogni-

effects of bureaucratic encounters (Christensen et  al.  2020;
Döring and Madsen 2022). The identified moderating effect re-
veals an externality of formal education in helping citizens cope
with administrative burdens. This finding is policy-­relevant, jus-
tifying investments in education while highlighting the need for
targeted administrative reforms rather than “one-­size-­fits-­all”
approaches. Specifically, interventions should prioritize cogni-
tive accessibility for citizens with lower educational attainment,
complemented by specific training for street-­level bureaucrats
in patience and de-­escalation strategies to mitigate the unequal
psychological toll on vulnerable groups.
From the government's perspective, administrative burden
can be seen as a policy tool (Moynihan et al. 2015). Some costs
are deliberately imposed as an “ordeal mechanism” (screen-
ing tool) to target benefits to the most needy (Nichols and
Zeckhauser  1982). The logic behind this “ordeal mechanism”
is that, based on rational calculation of utility, only those who
still benefit after enduring the complex processes are the ones
most deserving of policy support. Prior research has revealed
how state-­imposed burdens in policy design and implementa-
tion cause psychological costs among applicants (Baekgaard
et  al.  2021). However, our research indicates that the actual
costs of this tool may far exceed expectations. It does not only
cause immediate stress, loss of autonomy, and emotional reac-
tions (Baekgaard et al. 2021; Döring and Madsen 2022; Hattke
et al. 2020), but also significantly damages the health of those
affected. These results suggest that a more cautious approach
is needed when considering the distribution of administrative
burdens between the state and citizens.
Finally, as our data come from a non-­Western context, the gen-
eralizability of our findings should be further discussed. The
data from China may lead to more pronounced estimates. This
is because, compared to Western countries, the power disparity
between the state and citizens in China is greater, potentially
making the consequences of negative bureaucratic encoun-
ters more significant (Gat 2007). Therefore, we cannot directly
generalize the absolute values of our estimated coefficients to
broader contexts. However, we must point out that because our
theoretical framework and hypotheses are context-­independent
and given the substantial magnitude and significance of our es-
timated coefficients, our empirical results provide a reference
for future studies focusing on Western countries. Moreover, it
is precisely because bureaucratic encounters in non-­Western
contexts can cause significant welfare losses that we should pay
more attention to this phenomenon.
Our study has some limitations. First, our analysis uses data from
2012 to 2016, which is not the most recent one. Since then, the
expansion of “Internet + Government Services” in China may
have altered the nature of bureaucratic encounters. While this
could reduce traditional burdens like delays for some, it might
also create new ones, such as a “digital divide” for others. We
contend that while the form of negative encounters may evolve,
the fundamental psychological mechanism linking them to poor
mental health remains highly relevant. Our study provides a
key baseline for understanding this enduring relationship. The
second limitation comes from the measurement of the indepen-
dent variable. Our binary, retrospective measures cannot distin-
guish between single and repeated encounters, nor fully isolate
face-­to-­face from automated interactions. Individuals can also
have different understandings of unfair treatment, conflict, un-
reasonable delays, and unreasonable charges. This could bias
our estimation. Third, our causal inference is not perfect. We
have tried our best to control for potential confounding factors
through our fixed-­effects design and extensive control variables.
However, it is not possible to account for all dynamic influences
on mental health in a single study. Although our instrumental
variable strategy has addressed this issue to some extent, we
must still exercise caution when claiming causality. Future stud-
ies should use more granular data to examine how the intensity
and modality of encounters differentially impact mental health,
and employ precisely controlled field experiments to enhance
the power of causal inference.

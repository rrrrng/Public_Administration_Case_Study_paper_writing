Abstract
Legitimacy is a central concern for independent agencies tasked with shaping poli-
cies. While expertise-based and reputation-sourced authority bases are assumed
to be relevant for agency legitimacy, their individual and joint effects on citizens’
perceptions lack comprehensive examination. To address this gap, the study inte-
grates insights from bureaucratic politics, bureaucratic reputation, and cognitive
psychology. Our survey experiment with Dutch citizens, focusing on the European
Food Safety Authority, suggests that expertise-based authority positively affects
perceived agency legitimacy, while a negative reputation has detrimental effects.
Furthermore, expertise-based authority moderates the impact of reputation, ampli-
fying positive effects and mitigating negative ones. This implies that agencies are
more susceptible to reputational threats when they lack expertise-based authority.
The study advances the theoretical tenets of bureaucratic reputation theory and
offers effective strategies for agencies to strengthen their legitimacy among the
most critical audience in democratic political systems—citizens.
Evidence for practice
• Both expertise- and reputation-based authority bases are crucial in shaping the
perceived legitimacy of government agencies among citizens.
• Government agencies can enhance their legitimacy among citizens by empha-
sizing the scientific foundations of their regulatory policies and processes gov-
erning policy creation.
• The impact of agency reputation on citizens’ perceptions of legitimacy is asym-
metric: A positive bureaucratic reputation does not notably affect legitimacy,
while a negative reputation undermines it significantly. This suggests that gov-
ernment agencies should prioritize managing reputational threats to prevent
legitimacy loss in the eyes of citizens.
• Government agencies can mitigate the effect of a negative reputation on their
legitimacy by emphasizing the scientific foundations of their regulatory policies
and processes.

THEORETICAL EXPECTATIONS
The theoretical framework elaborates on how different
bureaucratic authority bases influence the perceived
legitimacy of independent government agencies. We out-
line key concepts and present theoretical expectations for
each authority base individually. Additionally, we develop
tentative expectations about the interactive dynamics
between expertise-based and reputation-sourced author-
ity in shaping the perceived legitimacy of agencies.
Before presenting the core hypotheses of this study,
we first define the concept of legitimacy. We adopt the
concept of empirical legitimacy, which has been devel-
oped and utilized in psychological theory and practice.
Empirical legitimacy is defined as “a belief or perception
among the public that decision-makers have the right to
make decisions and that these decisions should be
accepted” (de Fine Licht et al., 2022, 185; de Fine
Licht, 2014; Tyler, 2006). Specifically, it refers to the public’s
acceptance of public policies, the processes governing policy
creation, and the institutions responsible for policy formula-
tion. Empirical legitimacy is argued to be crucial for the
effective functioning of political systems, as it influences
voluntary adherence to rules and regulations (Levi, 1997;
Tyler, 2006). In this definition, trust is considered an inte-
gral element of legitimacy (Jackson & Gau, 2016; Levi
et al., 2009).3 Consequently, for an institution to be
viewed as legitimate by citizens, they must (a) trust that
those in power will exercise their authority appropriately,
with citizens’ interests in mind, and (b) feel a sense of
obligation to follow its policies and regulations.
EXPERTISE-BASED AUTHORITY
Bureaucratic politics scholars emphasize the relevance of
bureaucrats’ expertise-based authority and their strategic
acquisition of endogenous expertise as core sources of
legitimation
vis-à-vis
political
authorities
(Bertelli
&
Lewis, 2013; Boushey & McGrath, 2017; Carpenter &
Krause, 2015; Gailmard & Patty, 2007, 2012; Krause, 2013).
The delegation of bureaucratic discretion to government
agencies is driven by expert-related competences, and
this discretion is influenced by the investment in exper-
tise
and
capacity
by
bureaucrats
(Andersen
&
Moynihan, 2016; Gailmard & Patty, 2007). Thus, the
bureaucracy’s ability to systematically acquire and culti-
vate the perception among various audiences (beyond
political authorities) that it possesses expertise is consid-
ered the essential foundation of bureaucratic legitimacy
for its functions and role within democratic policymaking
processes. As stated by Carpenter and Krause (2015, 8),
“[f]or bureaucratic agents to possess and maintain legiti-
macy in the eyes of political authorities, they must pos-
sess
sufficient
expertise
to
inspire
respect
and
confidence.” Bureaucratic legitimacy, therefore, rests on
the premise that bureaucratic tasks have a technical

REPUTATION-SOURCED AUTHORITY
Reputation-sourced authority is considered a valuable
organizational asset, reflecting the assessment of an orga-
nization’s conduct across various dimensions over time
by diverse audiences (Bertelli & Busuioc, 2020). While
expertise-based authority refers to the authority derived
from a bureaucracy’s possession of specialized knowledge
and its ability to provide relevant expertise (e.g., policy
expertise) to address a specific policy issue (Gailmard &
Patty, 2007), reputation-sourced authority is a multidi-
mensional concept derived from an organization’s accu-
mulated reputation, encompassing its past performance
and behavior across various reputational dimensions rele-
vant to independent agencies (technical, performative,
legal-procedural, moral). Reputation-sourced authority, as
opposed to expertise-based authority, is “the outcome of
the history and the sum of stories told about the organi-
zation among its stakeholders” (Luoma-aho, 2007, 128)
resulting from “consistent information signals over time”
(Dentchev & Heene, 2004, 57).
Bureaucratic reputation scholarship consistently posits
that acquiring a positive reputation is linked to an
increase
in
bureaucratic
legitimacy
(Busuioc
&
Rimkutė,
2020a;
Carpenter,
2010;
Carpenter
&
Krause,

Huang
&
Provan,
2007;
Krause
&
Douglas, 2005; Lee & Whitford, 2013; Provan et al., 2009;
Rimkutė, 2018, 2020b; Van der Veer, 2021). Holding a
positive reputation in the eyes of relevant audiences is
argued to drive “legitimated vigor” for bureaucratic agen-
cies (Carpenter, 2010, 752). Conversely, when discerning
audiences perceive agencies as inferior, the conse-
quences for their legitimacy can be profound and far-
reaching. For example, unsuccessful management of the
expectations and demands of pertinent audiences can
lead to disputes, serious harm to agency legitimacy, and
even
threaten
organizational
survival
(Busuioc
&
Rimkutė, 2020b; Carpenter & Krause, 2012; Maor, 2015).
The assumed causal link between reputation and
legitimacy has led scholars to attribute bureaucratic agen-
cies’ inclination to proactively manage their reputations
with the aim of enhancing their overall bureaucratic legit-
imacy. For example, to strengthen their legitimacy, agen-
cies are argued to have strong incentives to strategically
cultivate positive reputations by employing a range of
multifaceted strategies. These strategies include refining
their communication tactics (Busuioc & Rimkutė, 2020a;
Chen et al., 2023; Christensen & Lodge, 2018; Gilad
et al., 2015; Rimkutė, 2020a; Salomonsen et al., 2021),
responding to multidimensional external demands (Bach
et al., 2021; Maor et al., 2013; Rimkutė, 2020b; Rimkutė &
Van der Voet, 2023), enhancing their operational perfor-
mance
(Krause

Douglas,

Krause

Kevin

and

Interactive dynamics of expertise-based and
reputation-sourced authority
Given some existing ambiguities in the literature on
bureaucratic legitimacy, particularly concerning the over-
lap between reputation, legitimacy, and expertise, we
explore a range of theoretical relationships among these
concepts. Our goal is to clarify these distinctions and
develop preliminary hypotheses. We introduce two sets
of alternative hypotheses that propose different explana-
tions for the interaction effect between expertise-based
and
reputation-sourced
authority
on
perceptions
of
agency legitimacy.
First,
we
expect
that
information
emphasizing
agency’s positive multidimensional reputation, reflecting
an excellent track record across various organizational
activities over the years, will have a greater impact on
perceived legitimacy compared to information about the
agency’s expertise on a specific matter (H5). Additionally,
we expect that reputation moderates the effect of exper-
tise on agency legitimacy: amplifying/diminishing the
effect of expertise on the perceived legitimacy when
the agency’s reputation is positive/negative (H6). These
expectations stem from bureaucratic reputation scholar-
ship, which emphasizes the far-reaching implications of
high reputation-sourced authority and the significance
of perceived agency qualities over actual capacity in
uncertain contexts. Successful efforts to cultivate strong
bureaucratic reputations lead to high reputation-sourced
authority, influencing relevant audiences’ perceptions
and actions (Bertelli & Busuioc, 2020). For example, high
reputation-sourced authority is argued to have far-
reaching implications, such as tying “the hands of legiti-
mate political principals, to deflect oversight of agency
action, and to
disguise—at least
for
some time—
regulatory failures” (Bertelli & Busuioc, 2020, 8). We
extend this argument and posit that information effec-
tively communicating multiple facets of an agency’s over-
all bureaucratic reputation (i.e., observed overall capacity)
is more likely to foster positive legitimacy perceptions
than cues solely focusing
on
expertise (i.e.,
actual
capacity).
Specifically, bureaucratic reputation is argued to be
the “observed capacity” (Carpenter, 2001, 125) of an
agency, as opposed to its “actual capacity.” This observed
capacity is shaped by the agency’s communication efforts
regarding its true capabilities, which in turn influences
external audiences’ perceptions. Bureaucracies are seen
“through the glass but dimly’ by their manifold audi-
ences” (Carpenter & Krause, 2012, 27), illustrating the
challenge of understanding their true capabilities. Conse-
quently, agencies that successfully cultivate a positive
reputation among relevant audiences reduce the motiva-
tion of political principals and citizens to closely monitor
their actual activities (e.g., provision of relevant expertise)
(Alon-Barkat & Gilad, 2017; Bertelli & Busuioc, 2020;
Carpenter & Krause, 2012, 2015). We argue that, from the
perspective of citizens, the overall observed reputation of
independent agencies—reflecting a track record of profi-
cient performance across various bureaucratic goals—is
of utmost importance. This argument is supported by
research indicating that agency communication and fram-
ing, rather than actual agency capacities, significantly

METHODOLOGY
Research setting and sample characteristics
The study’s hypotheses are empirically examined through
a pre-registered survey experiment conducted with a
population-based sample of 1476 Dutch citizens. Prior to
data collection, the experiment received favorable ethical
review (see Supplementary Materials S2). We investigate
citizens’ perceptions of the bureaucratic legitimacy of the
EFSA and its regulatory mandate to conduct scientific risk
assessments based on available scientific data. EFSA
exemplifies a typical EU regulatory agency with technical
and scientific responsibilities, operating across various
regulatory domains such as the European Chemicals
Agency,
the
European
Medicines
Agency,
and
the
European Aviation Safety Authority. Although these agen-
cies function as EU-level regulators with formally dele-
gated scientific responsibilities, they face significant
legitimacy issues due to their lack of direct accountability
to national political authorities. Therefore, EFSA, as a typi-
cal case of EU-level independent agencies, serves as a
good case for examining how bureaucratic authority
bases contribute to its legitimacy, under what conditions,
and in what ways.
Our focus is on EFSA’s recent scientific risk assessment
of the carcinogenic risks associated with glyphosate, the
most widely used pesticide for weed control. The selec-
tion of this specific case as the empirical focus is moti-
vated by its political salience, contentious nature, and
public visibility, despite the common observation that
regulatory decisions at the EU level tend to render such

The survey experiment was conducted from June 14th

Operationalization of the outcome variable:
Perceived legitimacy of EFSA
We rely on the literature stream defining agency legiti-
macy as the public’s acceptance of independent agencies
as rightful institutions for policymaking, encompassing
both their processes and decisions made by bureaucrats
within the agency (de Fine Licht et al., 2022; Ruder &
Woods, 2020). We measured our outcome variable using
three survey questions that correspond to the three
aspects of legitimacy proposed by de Fine Licht et al.
(2022): (1) acceptance of the decision-making process;
(2) acceptance of the decision; and (3) trust in the
decision-making agency. These three aspects of legiti-
macy are mutually exclusive analytically, as each focuses
on either the process, the decision itself, or the decision-
maker. To measure perceptions of legitimacy and capture
the three aspects, we adapted the items suggested by de
Fine Licht et al. (2022) and de Fine Licht (2014) for “proce-
dure acceptance” (Q1: I feel that the EFSA evaluation of
glyphosate risks was made in a reasonable way); “decision
acceptance” (Q2: I am willing to accept the recommenda-
tion made by EFSA to use glyphosate in the Netherlands);
and “trust in decision-makers” (Q3: I feel that I can trust
EFSA). Participants were asked to indicate their level of
agreement or disagreement with these three legitimacy
statements on a 7-point Likert-type scale, ranging from
“1—Strongly disagree” to “7—Strongly agree.” The order
of the statements was randomized.
Table 2 presents the mean responses to the three
dependent variable questions across all six conditions.
We observe internal consistency among the responses
to
all
three
legitimacy
statements
(Cronbach’s
alpha = 0.91). Consequently, we created a legitimacy
index by averaging participants’ answers across the
three measures. This index served as our outcome vari-
able in the models.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
At
its
core,
our
study
was
motivated
by
Herbert
A. Simon’s (1957) emphasis on the importance of volun-
tary acceptance for the functioning of bureaucratic orga-
nizations.
We
examined
what
characteristics
of
bureaucratic agencies enhance their perceived legitimacy.
We focused specifically on the roles of expertise-based
and reputation-sourced authority as critical bureaucratic
legitimacy foundations. Despite their considerable impli-
cations and theoretical grounding (Carpenter, 2001),
these foundations have not yet been subjected to rigor-
ous empirical scrutiny. We addressed this gap with an
original experiment designed to test the individual and
joint effects of expertise-based and reputation-sourced
authority on perceived agency legitimacy. By doing so,
we advance theoretical developments and present novel
empirical evidence that enhances our understanding of
the
authority-legitimacy
relationship
(Carpenter
&
Krause, 2015). In addition, our study has implications for
bureaucratic agencies, illustrating how legitimacy can be
constructed or eroded through the interplay between
expertise and reputation.
Specifically, we contribute to a better understanding
of how two complementary authority bases—expertise-
based and reputation-sourced authority—affect the per-
ceived legitimacy of agencies among citizens. The results
of the study empirically validate the theoretical proposi-
tions within the bureaucratic politics literature, suggesting
that expertise-based authority substantially enhances the
perceived legitimacy of independent agencies (Bertelli &
Lewis, 2013; Boushey & McGrath, 2017; Carpenter &
Krause, 2015; Gailmard & Patty, 2007, 2012; Krause, 2013;
Maor, 2007; Moffitt, 2010). Our analysis suggests that the
scientific nature of rulemaking is a fundamental catalyst
for legitimizing agencies and their rulemaking processes
and outputs in the eyes of citizens. Furthermore, our anal-
ysis shows the significant influence of bureaucratic repu-
tation on the perceived legitimacy of regulatory agencies.
While we did not find evidence to support the claim that
a positive multidimensional reputation—encompassing
performative, legal-procedural, and moral aspects—leads
to an increase in perceived legitimacy, our data indicate
that possessing an unfavorable multidimensional reputa-
tion significantly diminishes the perceived legitimacy of
regulatory agencies among citizens. This emphasizes the
adverse consequences of an unfavorable reputation on
agency legitimacy. Consequently, our findings offer novel
insights that explain the focus of existing bureaucratic
reputation scholarship on studying how and why agen-
cies communicate in response to negative external
demands that pose substantial reputational threats (Gilad
et al., 2015; Maor, 2020; Maor et al., 2013; Rimkutė & Van
der Voet, 2023).
Furthermore, our study goes beyond examining the
individual effects of expertise and reputation. We uncover
the interactions between these two bases of authority. In
addition to corroborating prior theoretical arguments and
empirical findings, we provide a novel understanding of
the authority-legitimacy relationship by showing that
expertise-based authority moderates the negative impact
of an adverse reputation. Moreover, we identify a moder-
ating effect of reputation on perceived legitimacy: exper-
tise has the largest effect when reputation suffers,
remains important when information about reputation is
unavailable, and has the smallest (though still significant)
effect when an organization enjoys a positive reputation.
This finding suggests that independent agencies are
more vulnerable to reputation-related risks when they
cannot rely on their expertise-based authority, indicating
that expertise is a vital resource, particularly in the con-
text of reputation loss.
Our empirical results correspond with theoretical
observations that bureaucratic expertise acquisition over
time is a core building block for reputation-sourced
authority for regulatory agencies (Bertelli & Busuioc, 2020;
Busuioc
&
Rimkutė,
2020b;
Carpenter,
2001;
Carpenter, 2010; Moffitt, 2010). They also support argu-
ments that explicit references to expertise contribute to
building a durable reputation and are, therefore, essential
for strengthening agency legitimacy (Bertelli & Busuioc,
2020;
Busuioc
&
Rimkutė,
2020b;
Carpenter,
2010;
Maor, 2007).
Our findings have significant implications for both the-
ory and practice, underscoring the importance of bureau-
cratic expertise and reputation management in establishing

Carpenter & Krause, 2012; Huang & Provan, 2007; Krause &
Douglas, 2005; Lee & Whitford, 2013; Provan et al., 2009;
Van der Veer, 2021). In advancing the research agenda, this
study makes several contributions. Firstly, we introduce
expectations informed by diverse literature streams—
bureaucratic politics and cognitive psychology—enabling a
comprehensive account of the multifaceted dynamics
within the authority-legitimacy relationship. Our study theo-
rizes how diverse authority bases contribute to agencies’
legitimacy,
refining
the
theoretical
understanding
of
authority-legitimacy interactions. Importantly, by experi-
mentally testing these interactions, we advance bureau-
cratic reputation theory, offering causal evidence often
limited by the field’s reliance on observational data. Finally,
consistent with psychological literature, our findings dem-
onstrate that negativity bias strongly influences citizens’
perceptions about bureaucratic institutions, revealing that a
negative reputation has far greater consequences for legiti-
macy than a positive one.
Future research could further theorize, test, and
refine expectations about the conditions under which
reputational factors impact bureaucratic legitimacy. This
study sought to clarify conceptual and operational dis-
tinctions by carefully delineating expertise-based and
reputation-based authority sources. However, opera-
tional trade-offs were necessary to isolate the individual
effects of these authority bases, leading us to exclude
the core dimension of technical reputation from the
vignette experiment’s operationalization of bureaucratic
reputation. Future studies could empirically examine
whether excluding the technical reputation dimension
influenced our findings—particularly the absence of an
effect of positive bureaucratic reputation on citizens’
perceptions of legitimacy. Our results, however, indicate
that the distinction between negative and positive rep-
utation is critical in shaping legitimacy perceptions;
notably, the lack of a technical reputation dimension in
both conditions did not prevent positive and negative
reputations from having distinct effects on legitimacy.
Nonetheless, this conclusion warrants further empirical
validation.
Last but not least, our empirical study was con-
ducted
within
a
specific
research
context—the
European Regulatory State (Majone, 1999)—focusing on
one EU country (the Netherlands) and one independent
agency (EFSA). While the study demonstrates high inter-
nal validity, its external validity is limited. The findings
may not be applicable to regulatory states outside the
EU, citizens of different countries, or agencies that,
unlike EFSA, place less emphasis on technical aspects in
their
daily
operations.
Specifically,
expertise-based
authority plays a central role in the principles and foun-
dations of the European regulatory state and EU-level
agencies
(cf.
Bunea
&
Nørbech,
2022;
Busuioc
&
Rimkutė, 2020b). EU bureaucracies, such as agencies,
are mandated to deliver credible and timely science-
based solutions for severe societal risks. If these institu-
tions successfully fulfill their core responsibilities, they
are expected to achieve high legitimacy among relevant
stakeholders (Majone, 1999).
We invite future research to further test the robust-
ness of our findings and conclusions across various empir-
ical settings, including different countries, agencies, and
regulatory domains. For instance, comparing how the two
authority
bases—expertise-based
and
reputation-
sourced—impact legitimacy perceptions in various con-
texts, including regulatory regimes outside the EU, and
across diverse regulatory domains (e.g., health safety,
environmental protection, financial regulation, and secu-
rity), would offer valuable comparative insights. Addition-
ally, examining the generalizability of our results among a
broad range of audiences, including citizens, political
elites, and private interest groups, would further contrib-
ute to a comprehensive understanding of the authority-
legitimacy relationship. Finally, while survey experiments
provide valuable insights by isolating potential con-
founders to observe causal effects, future research should
consider conducting field experiments. They have signifi-
cant potential to reveal how authority-legitimacy dynam-
ics evolve in real-time during actual interactions between
citizens and agencies. Empirical insights gained from
comparative
real-world
settings
could
substantially
advance theoretical development in the field.
Despite its specific empirical scope, the study points
to important practical implications for public adminis-
trators and policymakers working within independent
government agencies. First, both expertise-based and
reputation-sourced authority are critical in determining
how citizens perceive the legitimacy of government
agencies.
This
finding
has
important
implications:
agency officials need to develop and maintain high
levels of technical and scientific expertise and actively
manage the risks of reputation loss. These two bases of
legitimacy interact with each other, therefore, invest-
ment in strengthening the expertise as well as clear
communication of the expertise and scientific rigor
behind regulatory policies can offer effective protection
against (incidental) reputation losses. Second, the study
reveals that while a positive reputation does not signifi-
cantly enhance an agency’s perceived legitimacy, a neg-
ative reputation can severely damage it. As reputational
threats can come from negative publicity and are rein-
forced by the negativity bias of audiences, the develop-
ment of strategies for managing negative publicity to
mitigate the impact of adverse events might be crucial
for bureaucratic legitimacy. Third, when faced with rep-
utational crises, agencies can reinforce their credibility
by emphasizing the expertise and evidence that under-
pin their decisions and policies. This approach can reas-
sure the public that despite negative (often non-expert)
messages, the agency’s performance is based on scien-
tific grounds.

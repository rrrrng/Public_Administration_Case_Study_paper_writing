ABSTRACT
Mandated networks, as a distinct form of collaborative governance, have received increasing scholarly attention in recent years.
Yet their effectiveness remains debated, and the mechanisms through which mandates shape interorganizational collaboration
in crisis response are not well understood. Drawing on institutional and network perspectives, this article examines the direct
and indirect effects of mandates on collaborative tie formation during crises. Using social network analysis of collaborative ties
among 424 organizations—including members of 62 legally mandated safety and disaster committees—during the 2015 epi-
demic in South Korea, the study shows that both direct bureaucratic and network-­mediated mechanisms shaped these ties, with
indirect effects predominating. It reveals how relational dynamics at the dyadic and group levels mediated collaborative ties,
accounting for sequential and feedback effects between levels and comparing these effects in mandated versus voluntary ties.
The findings highlight mandated networks' potential as institutional arrangements for preserving governance stability under
turbulent conditions.
1   |   Introduction
Collaborative crisis governance has gained attention for its abil-
ity to align efforts and foster cooperative problem-­solving during
disastrous events, from natural disasters and public health
emergencies to terrorist attacks (Boin and Bynander  2015).
This approach builds on the broader collaborative governance
regime (CGR) framework, which emphasizes engagement and
cooperation across organizations and sectors (Ansell and Gash
2008; Emerson and Nabatchi 2015). A central challenge in cri-
sis management has been to promote and sustain collaboration
networks that can deliver innovative, flexible emergency re-
sponses while preserving operational stability under turbulent
conditions (Ansell et al. 2023; Comfort et al. 2020; Christensen,
Lægreid, and Rykkja 2016). To address this challenge, a growing
body of literature debates key institutional design choices: ver-
tical versus horizontal, centralized versus decentralized, formal
versus informal, and mandatory versus voluntary approaches
(Caruson and MacManus  2006; Hu et  al.  2014; Kapucu and
Garayev 2016; Nowell et al. 2018).
Despite varying definitions, networks can be understood as
groups of three or more legally autonomous organizations seek-
ing to achieve both collective goals and their own (Provan and
Kenis 2008, 231). Collaborative ties formed to address shared
problems have positioned networks as a distinctive organiza-
tional form in public administration, with patterns of these ties
representing structures of interdependence among participating
organizations and parts thereof (O'Toole  1997; van den Oord
et al. 2023). While networks are typically characterized by vol-
untary participation and self-­regulation through rules, norms,
and values negotiated among participants, crisis governance
networks (CGNs) are often created by mandate. In voluntary
settings, the promise of benefits that individual organizations
cannot attain alone motivates participation in and commitment
to networked efforts (Huxham and Vangen 2004). However,

and working groups, have become integral components of crisis

A growing body of literature on mandated collaboration has
emerged over the past decade, yet research into how collabo-
ration unfolds under mandates during crises remains limited.
Specifically, little is known about whether and how mandates
that require government agencies to form CGNs within and
across sectors actually enhance interorganizational collabora-
tion in response to evolving crises. This gap is surprising given
both the rapid expansion of research on collaborative response
networks (Berthod et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2014; Jung et al. 2019)
and the growing reliance on mandates to establish such net-
works in practice—even in the very countries where these stud-
ies were conducted. In a recent review, Sullivan et al. (2024) call
for better understanding of how mandated collaboration differs
from voluntary collaboration and of the mechanisms govern-
ing collaborative tie formation within mandated arrangements
across policy domains, including crisis management. Filling this
knowledge gap is critical for understanding the effectiveness of
mandates as policy instruments in facilitating collaborative cri-
sis governance. This study thus aims to advance understanding
of whether and how legal and administrative mandates that es-
tablish CGNs affect the formation of collaborative ties among
organizations engaged in crisis response.
The concept of mandated network challenges the traditional
notion of networks as self-­organizing, dynamic structures
and processes. In self-­organizing structures, order arises from
heterogeneous interactions among constituent components
(Kauffman  1993). In other words, the rules and norms that
govern and coordinate participant activities emerge organically
from within networks, based on participants' autonomy—the
ability to direct and govern one's own actions without external
control. This capacity enables CGNs to adapt to rapidly chang-
ing crisis situations through trust-­based, innovative processes
of reciprocal interactions, mutual learning, and shared commit-
ment (Comfort 1994). However, mandates are legal or adminis-
trative directives, such as laws and executive orders, that require
social actors to behave in specific ways, typically backed by
compliance mechanisms (McDonnell and Elmore 1987). When
these mandates establish CGNs, they inherently constrain net-
work participants' autonomy, which may thus limit their collec-
tive capacity for self-­organization within networks. Although
networks and hierarchies are not mutually exclusive and often
coexist (McGuire and Agranoff 2011), this fundamental tension
between mandates and networks as organic organizations has
generated debates about whether and how mandates can foster
or hinder self-­organizing collaborative processes within CGNs
(Krogh  2022; Park et  al.  2021; Willem and Lucidarme  2014).
Scholars have also questioned the broader effects of top-­down
interventions on interorganizational dynamics in CGNs (Zhao
et al. 2023).
To contribute to these debates, this study draws on CGR the-
ory to examine the effects of mandates on collaborative tie for-
mation in crisis response contexts by integrating insights from
collaboration theory, institutional theory, network theory, and
crisis management theory. While prior studies often emphasize
mandates' capacity to foster relational mechanisms, particularly
trust and relational commitment (Park et al. 2021; Willem and
Lucidarme 2014), CGR theory offers a more comprehensive view
of mandates, suggesting that externally imposed collaborative
arrangements can be influenced by both bureaucratic struc-
tures and relational dynamics (Emerson and Nabatchi  2015).
Research demonstrates that bureaucratic logics often dominate
agency decision-­making in mandated networks, as agencies
prioritize their bureaucratic missions and turf, relationships
with constituents, and compliance costs (An and Tang  2023;
Milman et al. 2024). Drawing on these insights, we posit that
mandates have dual effects on collaboration: they directly struc-
ture collaborative ties by imposing rules and requirements that
govern network structure and functioning, and they indirectly
foster collaboration by activating relational dynamics among
participating organizations. Mandates may stimulate relational
dynamics that drive collaboration at both the dyadic and group
levels, yet little is known about how these effects differ across
levels. This study therefore addresses the following questions:
Can mandates that establish CGNs promote the formation of col-
laborative ties among organizations during crises? If so, how do
they directly and indirectly affect the tie formation process? And

This study adopts a network analysis approach that integrates
exponential random graph models (ERGMs) with average mar-
ginal effect (AME) analysis to analyze collaborative ties among
organizations that responded to the 2015 epidemic in South
Korea. Most prior studies of mandated collaboration rely on
qualitative methods or quantitative measures of participants'
perceptions (Sullivan et  al.  2024), yielding mixed findings on
whether mandates foster collaboration through trust-­based
horizontal relationships (An and Tang 2023; Park et al. 2021;
Willem and Lucidarme 2014). Using behavioral interaction data
from a natural setting where both mandated and voluntary col-
laboration occurred in response to the same public health cri-
sis, our models test the mediating effects of trust-­based network
dynamics at the dyadic and group levels on the relationship
between mandates and collaborative tie formation. This com-
parison provides empirical evidence addressing ongoing debates
about whether mandates foster or hinder self-­organizing col-
laboration, allowing us to assess whether relational dynamics
in mandated networks are unique or also emerge in voluntary
settings. We begin by positioning collaborative crisis gover-
nance as a field of CGRs and conceptualizing mandated CGNs
as network-­bureaucratic hybrids. We then develop hypotheses
about the direct and indirect effects of mandates on collabora-
tive tie formation, present our data and methods, and report our
findings. We conclude with implications, limitations, and sug-
gestions for future research.
2   |   Theoretical Background
2.1   |   Collaborative Crisis Governance
and Mandated CGNs
Crises occur when disastrous events, whether natural or man-
made, significantly disrupt the normal functioning of key so-
cietal systems, including infrastructure, public safety, public
health, education, social welfare, and political and economic
institutions (Rosenthal and Kouzmin 1997). Effective crisis gov-
ernance is critically challenging primarily due to system com-
plexity, defined as the limited ability to understand and manage
the components and dynamic interactions of these systems, and
event uncertainty, defined as the inability to predict exactly what
will happen, where, when, and with what impacts. Addressing
these requires a governance approach that brings together di-
verse organizations to collaborate across systems, sharing infor-
mation, pooling resources, and supporting joint decisions and
actions (Comfort 1994).
Drawing on Emerson et al.'s (2012, 2) definition, collaborative
crisis governance can be defined as ‘the processes and struc-
tures of public policy decision making and management that
engage people constructively across [organizational and sec-
toral boundaries to address crisis situations and solve asso-
ciated problems] that could not otherwise be accomplished.’
Such governance arrangements may emerge organically among
participants or be initiated by a motivated convener who re-
cruits voluntary participants into formal or informal networks.
However, achieving effective coordination and thus meaningful
collaboration in these networks is profoundly challenging, es-
pecially during large-­scale crises. As past disasters have shown,
managing catastrophic events inevitably relies on impromptu
interactions not only among traditional response organizations,
such as fire, police, and emergency medical services, but also
among a broader set of organizations and individuals operating
beyond the boundary of conventional emergency management
(Comfort  1994; Kapucu and Hu  2022). Yet these actors often
lack a history of prior collaboration and, at times, even are in
conflict (Boin and Bynander  2015). Crises can exacerbate ex-
isting tensions, further hindering the emergence of timely and
effective collaboration. These challenges become more profound
as the number of participating actors increases, especially in
communities with low social capital (Christensen, Danielsen,
et al. 2016).
The hybridity of networks and hierarchies has emerged as a
promising strategy for reducing coordination failure risks while
preserving the flexibility and adaptability essential for effec-
tive collaborative crisis governance (Christensen, Danielsen,
et  al.  2016). These discussions have focused on integrating
command-­and-­control structures with cross-­boundary collab-
oration among functional units and sectors, as exemplified by
the U.S. Incident Command System.1 Yet governments across
regime types also use top-­down mandates to establish CGNs
to promote collaborative crisis planning and response. For
example, the U.S. has federally mandated State Emergency
Response Commissions (SERCs) and Local Emergency
Planning Committees (LEPCs) as interagency and intersec-
toral coordination bodies, initially for chemical hazards but
expanded after 9/11 to broader crisis management and com-
munity risk communication. Japan's legally mandated Disaster
Management Councils operate at national and local levels, with
the national council focusing on policy coordination while local
councils handle both planning and operational activities (Basic
Act on Disaster Management (Japan) 2016; Japanese Cabinet
Secretariat 2015). South Korea maintains similar mandated ar-
rangements but with key differences: the National Safety Control
Committee (NSCC) serves as an interagency coordination body,
while Local Safety Control Committees (LSCCs) include non-
governmental organizations and experts. China has established
National and Local Committees for Disaster Reduction under
government order to support disaster policymaking and coor-
dinate relief efforts (Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and
Recovery 2020). Norway' National Government Crisis Council
(NGCC), established by administrative order, comprises five
key ministries. Local crisis councils there are not yet mandated
but planned for municipalities to coordinate responses among
public agencies, volunteers, and the private sector (Norwegian
Ministry of Justice and Public Security 2024). Even countries
with agreement-­based central coordination bodies maintain
legally mandated local interagency and intersectoral arrange-
ments, including Local Emergency Management Committees
in Australia (Municipal Association of Victoria 2022; Western
Australia State Emergency Management Committee 2025) and
Local Resilience Forums in the U.K. (United Kingdom Civil
Contingencies Secretariat 2013). Appendix A provides details of
these mandated CGNs.

tics that reflect their hybrid nature, combining network and

ing section, we therefore develop hypotheses about how these
mandates shape interorganizational collaboration during crisis
response. Specifically, we examine the mechanisms through
which bureaucratic rules and requirements produce collabora-
tive ties among organizations in mandated CGNs, in the absence
of strong coercive or incentive measures.
2.2   |   Dual Effects of CGN Mandates
Gray  (1985, 912) conceptualizes collaboration as the pooling
of information, resources, and appreciations among multiple
stakeholders, enabling collective problem-­solving. Building
on this, Emerson and Nabatchi (2015, 58) depict collaboration
in CGRs as a ‘progressively cyclical or iterative’ process of in-
teractions among participants, through which principled en-
gagement, shared motivation, and the capacity for joint action
develop across organizational boundaries. Following this per-
spective, we define a collaborative tie in crisis response CGNs as
a relational connection through which organizations share in-
formation and resources, make joint decisions, and implement
coordinated actions to address crisis-­related problems.
The literature proposes two frameworks for understanding the
hybrid nature of mandated collaboration: one distinguishing
between formal and informal mechanisms (Park et  al.  2021)
and another between vertical and horizontal mechanisms (An
and Tang  2023). Despite different terminological emphases,
both examine how bureaucratic and relational network dynam-
ics are associated with collaboration in mandated settings, yet
reach different conclusions. Park et al. (2021) argue that formal
mechanisms (e.g., top-­down goal setting and oversight) do not
directly produce collaboration; instead, mandates foster collab-
orative processes indirectly by shaping the development of infor-
mal relationships grounded in trust and shared understanding.
However, An and Tang  (2023) underscore the predominance
of vertical considerations—particularly fear of losing control
and protecting bureaucratic turf and autonomy in mandated
networks—over horizontal factors such as collaboration ca-
pacity, prior collaboration, and external facilitation. Although
their analytical foci differ, both studies share a common em-
phasis on the regulative pillar of institutional pressure. They
interpret rule-­following behavior as either passive compliance
with formal authority or strategic responses to maximize in-
centives while reducing risks. As illustrated above, however,
many mandates establishing crisis response CGNs lack strong
sanctions for non-­compliance and explicit incentives, raising the
important question: Can these mandates facilitate collaborative
ties without strong formal mechanisms to compel or incentivize
compliance?
Institutional scholar Scott  (2013) theorizes that institutions
guiding social actors' behavior comprise three pillars: regula-
tive, normative, and cultural-­cognitive. Hodgson (2006) further
emphasizes that these pillars are closely interconnected in the
process by which rules come to shape behavior, as normative be-
liefs and cultural-­cognitive frames influence how rules are inter-
preted and implemented in daily life. In this vein, Emerson and
Nabatchi (2015) argue for the significance of rule acceptance and
cognitive legitimacy, particularly within government-­mandated
networks. Additionally, informal and horizontal mechanisms
can be manifested at multiple levels; mandated participation in
governance collaboratives can work to promote relational bond-
ing among members at the dyadic or group level, with different
implications for the effectiveness of CGN mandates. However, to
our knowledge, such network dynamics under mandates have
not yet been investigated. In this section, we thus examine bu-
reaucratic and network mechanisms to capture the direct and
indirect effects of CGN mandates, respectively.2
2.2.1   |   Direct Bureaucratic Mechanisms
The primary obstacle to developing effective CGNs is identify-
ing relevant organizations and securing their participation and
engagement (Kapucu and Hu  2022). Mandates may address
this by designating key participants or participant categories
responsible for crisis decision-­making and operations, and by
requiring collaboration on crisis-­related problems. Although
regulative elements such as sanctions and incentives can drive
participation and collaborative action in mandated networks
(Sullivan et al. 2024), collaboration may also be shaped by bu-
reaucratic norms, values, and identities embedded in the state's
institutional fabric. These bureaucratic effects may be profound
in crisis response contexts, where governmental actors are nu-
merically dominant and occupy key leadership positions (Hu
et al. 2014; Ku et al. 2024).
Formally codified rules by authorities to mandate the establish-
ment of CGNs explicitly state behavioral requirements: what
must be done, by whom, under what circumstances (Hall and
O'Toole 2004). Hammond (1993) argues that these explicit rules
provide agencies with information ranging from how to inter-
pret problems they face to available options for addressing them.
In other words, they specify not only which actions are avail-
able to actors, but which are considered appropriate. Agencies in
mandated networks may thus behave in accordance with these
rules (e.g., participating in meetings and working with other
member agencies) as they believe such behavior is appropriate.

High situational uncertainty and limited information about
available responses may reinforce bureaucratic rule-­following
behavior in mandated CGNs. Studyding crisis coordination
in six European countries, including the U.K. and Norway,
Christensen, Danielsen, et al. (2016) show that government ex-
ecutives rely more on formal institutional arrangements within
existing governance systems than on ad hoc horizontal work-
ing groups or informal consultation with nonstate stakeholders.
They explain that this tendency stems not simply from instru-
mental considerations but from a ‘logic of appropriateness’ em-
bedded in each country's crisis management system (319). These
executives prioritize the impartial implementation of laws and
rules, along with other bureaucratic values such as professional
expertise and resource efficiency, when making crisis coordina-
tion decisions.
This bureaucratic logic of appropriateness can be so deeply em-
bedded that agencies conform to the rules and requirements
that define CGNs as a taken-­for-­granted practice. Olsen (2006,
3) notes that in contemporary democratic society, bureaucracy
has become ‘an expression of cultural values,’ going beyond
mere instrumentality. Bureaucratic agencies thus seek to fulfill
duties tied to their roles and identities, with rule-­following seen
as ‘obvious, natural, and reasonable’ (March and Olsen  1989,
36; Olsen 2006). Consequently, for agencies, participation and
playing the designated role in CGNs as prescribed by man-
date may be a way to express their identity as ‘state agents’
(Herranz 2008). Lu and Xue (2016) report that China's disaster
management framework, which embeds the mandated Disaster
Reduction Committees, has provided responding agencies and
personnel with a ‘map for sensemaking’ in urgent situations.
Others demonstrate that strongly institutionalized role obliga-
tions shape interactions not only between first responders but
also between them and nongovernmental actors during emer-
gencies across countries (Berthod et al. 2017; Hermansson 2016;
Moynihan 2009). Although not focused on crisis collaboration,
Herranz  (2008) and Krogh  (2022) document that the bureau-
cratic orientation of lead agencies can shape network-­wide inter-
action patterns, guided by values such as rule-­based standards,
stability, and vertical accountability. Kapucu and Hu  (2016)
found that in Florida's local CGNs, ties formed through manda-
tory planning processes have a stronger influence on response
collaboration than pre-­existing friendship ties. We therefore
hypothesize:
Hypothesis 1.  Mandates that establish CGNs have a positive
effect on collaborative tie formation between organizations during
crises.
Hypothesis 2.  Mandates that establish CGNs, even in the
absence of sanctions or incentives, have a direct positive effect on
collaborative tie formation between organizations during crises.
2.2.2   |   Indirect Network Mechanisms
While mandated rules and requirements establish the initial
structure of CGNs, Emerson and Nabatchi (2015, 28) argue that
once formed, participants may rely on network dynamics to cul-
tivate shared understanding, motivated commitment, and the
capacity for joint action—what they call ‘collaboration dynam-
ics.’ They underscore that collaboration is not a one-­time event,
but an iterative process that requires sustained efforts from par-
ticipating organizations. Through sustained interaction, partic-
ipants from different perspectives surface differences and build
consensus through negotiation and bargaining—crucial for de-
veloping innovative responses to novel and complex problems
arising during crises (Comfort 1994). Mandated network may
provide institutional spaces where such interactions can unfold.
Yet it may be trustworthy relationships and network-­level norms
and values that actually coordinate participants' actions, partic-
ularly given that ‘formal rules cannot cover all circumstances’
(Fukuyama 2016, 383).
The literature that studies network dynamics fostering collab-
orative interactions in governance settings has identified two
key network mechanisms that operate to facilitate collaboration
dynamics: reciprocity and transitivity. These mechanisms are
recognized for their role in trust-­building among collaborat-
ing partners at the dyadic and group levels, respectively. Trust
serves several instrumental functions in networks: it lubricates
collaboration processes by reducing opportunistic behavior and
resolving conflicts, fosters relational stability by encouraging
greater investments, and promotes mutual learning by facilitat-
ing communication and information sharing (Klijn, Edelenbos,
and Koppenjan Klijn et al. 2010). However, Provan et al. (2014)
suggest that the role of trust may be even more fundamental
in mandated networks. As O'Toole (2010) describes, mandates
typically target governance spaces where voluntary collabo-
rative efforts among organizations have proven unsuccessful.
Consequently, mandated CGNs likely begin with low internal
network legitimacy (van Raaij 2006). More specifically, although
the legal or administrative directive that establishes a CGN pro-
vides formal legitimacy for its existence and activities externally,
the network must also gain sufficient legitimacy from its mem-
ber organizations as a viable collective entity, with cooperative
interactions seen as vital for achieving both collective and orga-
nizational goals (Provan et al. 2014). Provan et al. (2014) argue
that trust is a prerequisite for building such network legitimacy.
2.2.2.1   |   Reciprocity.  Reciprocal interactions occur when
organizations mutually send and receive actions. For positive
actions such as sharing information, exchanging resources,
and providing support, this pattern builds social capital (Put-
nam 2000). The norm of reciprocity enables an organization to
initiate collaborative behavior toward another without assur-
ance of return, guided by the expectation that the favor will
be reciprocated (Gouldner 1960). The receiving party, in turn,
feels obligated to provide comparable value back to the initia-
tor (Gouldner  1960). In societies and communities with high
social capital, this network dynamic may facilitate collaboration
among organizations, even in the absence of mandates, through
the sharing of information and resources and a commitment
to joint efforts. In contexts with low social capital, however,
CGN mandates and government oversight may provide essen-
tial safeguards for initial interactions among organizations that
lack established relationships or shared understanding. That
is, these formal mechanisms may prompt organizations to ini-
tiate collaborative action not out of trust in their partners, but

3   |   Research Design
3.1   |   Research Setting
Our hypotheses were tested in the context of interorganiza-
tional collaboration during the 2015 Middle East respiratory
syndrome-­coronavirus (MERS) epidemic in South Korea. The
Korean government has used legislative mandates as the pri-
mary policy instrument to strengthen collaborative crisis gov-
ernance. Although government-­wide disaster preparedness and
control committees with individual experts' participation as ad-
visory members began more than a half century ago with the
passage of the 1967 disaster act, these early committees served
as tools for centralized control under the authoritarian regimes
of that era. The current institutional framework for CGNs cen-
tered on interagency and intersectoral coordination was estab-
lished with the Framework Act on the Management of Disasters
and Safety of 2004 and its subsequent amendments. Since then,
the government has significantly expanded mandated CGNs to
enhance nongovernmental actor engagement in crisis planning,
mitigation, response, and recovery, by creating new committees,
such as the Safety Policy Coordination Committee, the Disaster
Broadcasting Committee, and Public-­Private Cooperation
Committee, in addition to the NSCC and LSCCs. Special legisla-
tion has also established separate committees to address specific
threat types, such as infectious diseases, nuclear incidents, and
chemical hazards.
The arrival of MERS in 2015 threatened the country's core systems,
extending beyond public health to the economy, infrastructure,
education, and social welfare. The nationwide outbreak resulted
in 186 confirmed cases, 38 deaths, and 16,693 people quarantined
across all but three provinces and metropolitan areas from May 20
to December 23, 2015. As the most severe public health crisis after
the crisis governance reforms but before the COVID-­19 pandemic,
the outbreak provided a natural experimental setting to examine
the government's decades-­long efforts to address coordination
challenges and build collaboration capacity across organizational
and sectoral boundaries through mandated CGNs.

3.2   |   Data
This study draws on data from content analysis of news articles,
government documents, and organization websites. Because
crisis response often evolves beyond planned arrangements
(Comfort 1994; Moynihan 2009), our analysis included all or-
ganizations that participated in the response process, including
both members and nonmembers of the mandated CGNs. Data
on responding organizations (i.e., actors) and the collaborative
ties connecting them were drawn from Ku et al. (2024), com-
prising 4821 ties among 1385 actors across the country. For this
study, we focus the analysis on Seoul and Gyeonggi Province
during the acute response phase (May 20–July 16, 2015), the
epidemic's epicenters, identifying 1379 collaborative ties among
424 actors. The original study used 6187 online news articles
from four major Korean outlets and the government's 1125-­page
white paper (Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare  2016),
which included extensive records of interagency communica-
tions and interactions, to construct network data. For details on
the data collection and coding process, see Ku et al. (2024) and
Appendix B. Organizational attribute data were collected from
organizational websites. Committee membership data were
newly collected for this study from government records available
online or obtained through Information Disclosure Requests.
3.3   |   Variables
The dependent variable is the presence or absence of a directed
collaborative tie between pairs of actors during the epidemic
response. We operationalized collaborative ties as connections
indicating information sharing (messages and data related to re-
sponse operations), resource sharing (money, staff, services, and
physical materials), or joint decision-­making and implementa-
tion between actors. Tie direction was coded based on interaction
type: for information and resource sharing, ties were directed
from the sender to the receiver; for joint decision-­making or im-
plementation, each actor was coded as both sender and receiver,
creating bidirectional ties. The network was represented as an
n × n binary adjacency matrix, where YAB = 1 if there was a col-
laborative tie from Actor A to Actor B, and YAB = 0 otherwise.
The independent variables of theoretical interest are mandated
relationship, reciprocity, and generalized transitive closure (tran-
sitivity). While reciprocity and generalized transitive closure are
endogenous network variables that capture dependence among
ties in the response network,3 mandated relationship is an exog-
enous tie attribute. We operationalized mandated relationship as
sharing membership in the same mandated committee—specifi-
cally, those established by two major laws governing public health
crises in South Korea: the Framework Act on the Management
of Disasters and Safety (Act No. 12943 2014) and the Infectious
Disease Control and Prevention Act (Act No. 12444 2014). Our
analysis covered four central and 58 local committees in Seoul
and Gyeonggi Province, the focal regions of our response network.
All committees included in the study, except the NSCC (National
Safety Control Committee), are interagency and intersectoral.
Using committee member rosters from government agencies, we
coded whether each pair of actors in the response network were
affiliated with the same committee; YAB = 1 if Actor A and Actor B
shared membership in the same mandated committee, and YAB = 0
otherwise. While the member rosters included both organizational
representatives and individual experts, only organizational actors
were included in our analysis.4
Reciprocity captures the tendency for actors to form mutual ties
with partners who have chosen them previously (Wasserman and
Faust 1994, 507). We measured it using the ‘mutual’ term in the
‘ergm’ package, which counts the number of dyads with recipro-
cated ties. Transitivity—used interchangeably here with gener-
alized transitive closure—was measured using the geometrically
weighted edgewise shared partner (GWESP) statistic; this was
implemented via the ‘gwesp’ term, with a decay parameter of 0.5,
capturing the degree of transitive clustering through outgoing two-­
paths (Hunter 2007). GWESP accounts for the presence of multiple
shared partners between dyads, while geometrically discounting
the marginal contribution of each additional shared partner. The
decay parameter of 0.5 was selected through an iterative model-­
fitting process, indicating a moderate rate at which the influence
of additional shared partners on transitive clustering diminishes.
Our models also included two additional network structural
variables—popularity spread and activity spread—to account
for the skewed distribution of ties centered on a few popular
receivers or active senders. These were measured using the
geometrically weighted indegree and outdegree distribution
statistics, using the ‘gwideg’ and ‘gwodeg’ terms in the ‘ergm’
package, with a decay parameter of 0.2. These variables helped
control for degree heterogeneity in the network and improved
model fit (Snijders et al. 2006).
Four actor attributes—government affiliation, government level,
profession, and region—were included as control variables to
account for sender, receiver, and homophily effects. Previous re-
search documents the influence of regional proximity, as well
as government and professional homogeneity, on collaboration
during disasters (Jung et  al.  2019). Research also shows that
higher government authorities tend to have more resources and
greater ability to mobilize support from other agencies in respond-
ing to large-­scale disasters (Hermansson  2016; Ku et  al.  2024).
Government affiliation was measured as a binary variable: 1 if an
actor is a government agency, and 0 otherwise, while government
level is a categorical variable with three categories reflecting the
country's two-­tier local government structure: lower-­local, upper-­
local, and central government. Profession has four categories:
health, fire and emergency management (EM), law enforcement,
and others. Region is operationalized as the administrative region
where an actor was physically located during the epidemic; its dis-
tribution is reported in Appendix C. Table 1 presents the network
configurations included in the final models.

4   |   Results
4.1   |   Descriptive Characteristics of Actors
and Collaborative Ties
Tables  2 and 3 summarize the characteristics of the 424 actors
and 1379 ties in the response process. The majority of actors were
local government organizations (61%), followed by private organi-
zations (12%), central government agencies (11%), and nonprofits
(7%). Nearly half (42%) were healthcare organizations and agen-
cies. Organizations with primary missions unrelated to emergency
management, such as environmental services, telecommunica-
tions, transportation, and education, accounted for 25% of actors.
Only 0.8% of all possible ties among the actors were realized, in-
dicating a very sparse response network; on average, each actor
was connected to 3.25 others. Of the 932 dyads connected by at
least one collaborative tie, 48% were reciprocally connected (i.e.,
if A → B, then B → A). Meanwhile, 17% of triplets (i.e., sets of three
actors connected through a path) in the network were transitively
closed (i.e., if A → B → C, then A → C). Approximately 54% of actors
were members of at least one mandated committee, potentially ob-
ligating 511 dyads to collaborate. Figures 1 and 2 visualizes the
network of collaborative ties documented during the epidemic.

and Network Dynamics

4.4   |   Reciprocity-­Transitivity Feedback Loops

volving reciprocity and transitivity, given their theoretical in-
terdependence (Desmarais and Cranmer 2017). To explore how
this interplay varies across institutional arrangements, we dis-
aggregated their effects by voluntary and mandated relation-
ships. Figure 3 (middle and bottom rows) shows that reciprocity
significantly and positively mediated the relationship between
generalized transitive closure and collaborative ties in both
mandated (0.053, p < 0.001) and voluntary relationships (0.010,

effect. Yet generalized transitive closure positively mediated
the relationship between reciprocity and collaborative ties in
mandated relationships (0.054, p < 0.001), but showed negative
mediation in voluntary relationships (−0.003, p < 0.001). These
results suggest reinforcing feedback loops between reciproc-
ity and transitivity in mandated relationships, contrasted with
balancing feedback loops in voluntary relationships. Moreover,
reciprocity and transitivity had stronger direct effects on collab-
orative ties in mandated than voluntary relationships, implying
that mandates amplify the influence of these dynamics. Together
with earlier findings, these results indicate that the govern-
ment's mandates facilitated interorganizational collaboration
during the epidemic primarily through reciprocity. Although
transitivity did not directly mediate the mandates' effects, it
accounted for 39.7% of reciprocity's total effect in mandated

5   |   Discussion and Conclusions
This study examined whether and how CGN mandates foster in-
terorganizational collaboration, both directly and indirectly, from
a network structural perspective. Our ERGM and AME analyses
show that mandates can effectively enhance collaborative gover-
nance during acute crises by facilitating collaborative tie forma-
tion. However, nuanced relationships between mandates, network
dynamics, and collaborative ties in the results warrant caution
when relying on top-­down, formal mandates alone to foster CGNs
and address collaboration challenges.
The emphasis on structural properties of CGNs such as flexi-
bility, adaptability, and scalability has drawn scholarly atten-
tion to how informal relational dynamics can be facilitated to
support ad hoc tasks and emergency coordination under tur-
bulent conditions. It is often assumed that once formed, man-
dated networks behave similarly to voluntary, emergent ones.
Yet mandated CGNs exhibit networked features, such as inter-
organizational participation, shared membership, agreement-­
based decision-­making, and parallel relationships, while
operating within formal institutional structures governed by
government procedures and rules that regulate participation,
collaboration processes, and network performance. The posi-
tive direct effects of mandates in our AME results suggest that
these bureaucratic features coexist with relational network
dynamics rather than being negated by them. This supports
Provan and Kenis's (2008) argument that mandated networks
possess distinct structural features beyond their mandated
inception.
Mandated collaboration has been criticized (Hudson  1987;
Hermansson 2016), yet our findings call for a reassessment of
its operational effectiveness in crisis response contexts. The
collaboration literature has long presumed that regulative and
coercive authority underlying mandates is the main driver of
participation (Wood and Gray 1991). Participants, particularly
agencies, have often been described as passive rule followers,
with participation driven by the desire to avoid punishment or
benefit loss expected to result in only perfunctory collaboration
without genuine commitment (Gray and Purdy 2014). However,
the significant effects of mandates found in South Korea's CGNs
challenge these assumptions. Bureaucratic ties that formed even
in the absence of explicit coercive sanctions or strong external
incentives suggest that preset rules alone can initiate collabo-
rative behavior, particularly among agencies within established
bureaucratic systems. During crises that require urgent infor-
mation sharing, resource mobilization, and interagency co-
ordination, such ties may facilitate immediate responses by
channeling support through established procedures. Yet when
bureaucratic ties dominate, mandated CGNs may constrain
innovative problem-­solving by limiting the dynamic, multi-­
directional interactions essential for adaptive collaboration.
The indirect effects of mandates through reciprocity and
reciprocity-­transitivity feedback loops suggest that mandated
CGNs may address these limitations and help sustain collab-
orative efforts to some extent. Previous research suggests that
in mandated networks, as in voluntary ones, informal inter-
actions foster trust and catalyze collaborative processes (Park
et al. 2021). However, the literature is silent on the specific net-
work mechanisms involved in this process. Our findings of rec-
iprocity's mediating role help fill this gap by demonstrating that
even under mandates, mutual collaborative interactions serve as
a foundation for developing social capital within CGNs, consis-
tent with Coleman (1988) and Putnam (2000).
Furthermore, the reinforcing feedback loops—where reciproc-
ity increases transitivity, thereby driving new collaborative
ties—observed in mandated relationships but not in voluntary
ones suggest that not all reciprocated collaborative ties lead to
broader, sustained collaboration. Reciprocity may not always
be motivated by obligation or trust (Korsgaard et al. 2010), as
suggested by the suppressing feedback loops observed in vol-
untary relationships. The institutionalized authority, rules,
and procedures of mandated networks may provide organi-
zations with protected spaces from collaboration risks, where
trust dynamics can better develop. However, in the Korean
case, transitivity's mediation accounts for less than half of the
total effect, indicating that most of reciprocity's influence on
tie formation operates through direct pathways. Even with
positive mandate effects, these findings imply that building co-
hesive CGNs under mandates may be more challenging than
policymakers expect.

ways. Our analyses have revealed that ties formed through direct
bureaucratic and indirect network-­mediated pathways can have
different theoretical and practical implications. Despite these
contributions, this study has some limitations. First, the gener-
alizability of our findings may be constrained by South Korea's
specific institutional context. While the country's comprehen-
sive legal frameworks for collaborative crisis governance offer
an ideal setting to examine mandate effects, these findings may
not translate directly to other political-­administrative systems.
Additionally, the country's mandated CGNs follow Provan and
Kenis's (2008) ‘lead organization’ governance model, which fea-
tures less dense network connections than ‘shared governance’
structures but emphasizes coordination efficiency. For example,
the LSCCs, comprising the majority of committees examined in
this study, are legally required to be chaired by the governors or
mayors of their respective administrative jurisdictions. Despite
provisions governing committee composition, meeting proce-
dures, and decision-­making requirements, lead agencies retain
substantial discretion in convening and organizing meetings,
while coordination across member organizations during disas-
ters rests largely with the committees themselves. Although
how this discretion was exercised and how committees were
internally managed during the epidemic is beyond the scope of
this study, the ‘lead organization’ approach to network design
may help explain the limited transitivity mediation effect ob-
served in the Korean committees. This relationship warrants
further investigation. Moreover, comparative studies across di-
verse political-­administrative systems and disaster types will
be critical for establishing more generalizable insights about
the relationships between mandates, network mechanisms, and
collaborative ties.
Second, while our data sources (i.e., the government's white
paper and news articles) effectively captured formal interactions
between agencies and with private or nonprofit organizations,
they may have underrepresented informal interactions. This
research design is appropriate for capturing formal governance
committee interactions, where organizations typically use for-
mally structured channels to request and provide information,
resources, and support. However, the resulting network data may

underrepresent collaborative ties formed through informal rela-

Finally, while this study focuses on top-­down mandates—par-

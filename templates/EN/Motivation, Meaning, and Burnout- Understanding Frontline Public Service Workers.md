ABSTRACT
Understanding how public organizations can lower employee burnout is important because burnout negatively affects both
employee well-­being and service effectiveness. This study explores whether public service motivation (PSM) can help alleviate
burnout among frontline public sector workers, who frequently encounter high job demands and conflicting roles that heighten
burnout risks. Analyzing two-­wave matched survey data from police officers collected in 2019 and 2021, this study examines
whether PSM reduces burnout by strengthening perceptions of work prosocial impact. Results show that PSM's impact on burn-
out varies by burnout type, with a stronger effect on work depersonalization than on emotional exhaustion. By focusing on work
prosocial impact as a mediating factor, this study helps to explain past conflicting findings. It enhances understanding of the
PSM-­burnout link and suggests that fostering a sense of positive impact could be an effective strategy for public organizations to
reduce frontline worker burnout, especially during crisis periods.
1   |   Introduction
Frontline public sector employees, often referred to as street-­level
bureaucrats, are responsible for achieving complex, demand-
ing, and sometimes conflicting public policy goals, frequently
with limited resources or support (Guy et al. 2008; Lipsky 1980;
Maynard-­Moody and Musheno 2003). Such employees face addi-
tional challenges in their interactions with the public, which are
often emotionally charged, not always well-­received, and may
lack reciprocity (Guy et al. 2008; Maslach 1976). Research on
street-­level bureaucrats shows that the elevated demands of these
roles contribute significantly to job stress, leading many front-
line workers to detach emotionally from their work and their
clients as a coping mechanism (Brodkin 2011; Maynard-­Moody
and Musheno 2003; Shim et al. 2017; Tummers et al. 2015).
During times of crisis and change, the effective delivery of pub-
lic services becomes not only more essential but also more chal-
lenging and stressful. Recent studies (Humphrey et  al.  2024;
Linos et al. 2021; van der Meer et al. 2024) indicate that frontline
public sector employees experienced higher levels of burnout
and stronger intentions to leave their jobs during the COVID-­19
pandemic. These events not only heightened the demand for
public services but also increased the associated risks, funda-
mentally altering how and where government employees carry
out their duties.
This turbulent period provides a fitting context to test the rel-
evance of existing theory on the role of public service motiva-
tion (PSM) in mitigating stress and burnout among frontline
police officers. Public Administration (PA) research identifies

might employ to address employee burnout is to “lean into the

the PSM-­burnout association, however, have produced mixed
results. While some studies suggest that PSM may reduce
burnout (Potipiroon and Faerman 2020; Zhang and Liu 2024),
others find no effect (Basinska and Dåderman 2019; Rayner
et al. 2018; Sciepura and Linos 2024; van der Meer et al. 2024)

et al. 2013; Gould-­Williams et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2015) and
burnout (Rayner et  al.  2018; Shim et  al.  2017; Van Loon
et al. 2015; Zhang and Liu 2024) may vary depending on con-
text and conditions.
Drawing on existing research on public employee burnout and
prosocial motivation, this study enhances our understanding
of the PSM-­burnout relationship by identifying two theoretical
explanations for why research findings often do not support ex-
isting theoretical expectations. First, we propose that PSM's in-
fluence on burnout is mediated by employee perceptions of their
work's prosocial impact. Defined as the experience of making
a positive difference in the lives of others through one's work
(Bolino and Grant  2016; Grant  2007), prosocial impact (PSI)
involves the recognition that one's efforts have meaningful ef-
fects while PSM reflects a desire to benefit society and help oth-
ers. Past studies generally suggest that public employees with
high PSM are better equipped to cope with stressful work and
experience lower burnout because the importance they see in
their work helps them view job demands as manageable chal-
lenges rather than insurmountable obstacles (Bakker 2015; Liu
et  al.  2015; Potipiroon and Faerman  2020; Shim et  al.  2017).
PSM, however, captures only part of this story. While employ-
ees with higher PSM will be more likely to see the value in and
impact of their work, it is the extent to which they actually see
the meaningful impact of their work that helps employees feel
like the job demands can be overcome and result in meaning-
ful outcomes. Consequently, studies that overlook the extent to
which frontline public sector employees derive meaning from
their work may miss an essential pathway through which PSM
influences burnout.
Second, we propose that the PSM–burnout relationship varies
by the burnout dimension examined. Research on this rela-
tionship has typically focused on emotional exhaustion (Grant
and Sonnentag  2010; Pines and Keinan  2005; Potipiroon and
Faerman  2020; Rayner et  al.  2018; Shim et  al.  2017; van der
Meer et al. 2024; Van Loon et al. 2015) or on composite mea-
sures combining multiple burnout dimensions (Basinska and
Dåderman  2019; Grant and Campbell  2007; Sciepura and
Linos 2024). However, recent studies suggest that PSM's influ-
ence on work depersonalization may be uniquely significant
(Jensen and Holten 2025) and potentially distinct from its in-
fluence on emotional exhaustion (Hickey 2014). Consequently,
the PSM–burnout relationship may be stronger and more con-
sistent for work depersonalization than for emotional exhaus-
tion (Hickey 2014). If so, studies that fail to capture how PSI
mediates PSM's influence on emotional exhaustion may be
particularly likely to overlook this relationship (Basinska and
Dåderman 2019; Rayner et al. 2018; Sciepura and Linos 2024;
van der Meer et al. 2024).
Finally, our study advances knowledge about the PSM-­
burnout relationship in highly stressful conditions. Recent re-
search (Humphrey et al. 2024; Linos et al. 2021; van der Meer
et al. 2024) shows that frontline workers experienced elevated
burnout and intentions to leave their jobs during the pandemic,
which intensified service demands and increased health risks.
Studies have also suggested ways in which the potential bene-
fits provided by PSM may disappear or even be reversed under
exceptionally stressful conditions, and more studies are needed
to explore the PSM-­burnout relationship under such condi-
tions (Jensen et al. 2019; van der Meer et al. 2024; Zhang and
Liu 2024).
Next, we review existing research and develop our hypotheses.
We then describe the study sample and data collection proce-
dures, followed by the analysis and presentation of results.
Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings for both re-
search and practice.
1.1   |   Theory and Hypotheses

complex syndrome that results from chronic workplace stress
that has not been successfully managed. It is characterized
by feelings of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and
diminished personal accomplishment (Maslach  1976, 1982;
Maslach et al. 2001). The emotional exhaustion aspect of burn-
out is described in terms of weariness, tiredness, and fatigue,
and individuals who have these experiences generally show
difficulty in adapting to the work environment because they
lack the emotional energy to cope with the work demands
(Maslach et  al.  2001). Depersonalization, the second com-
ponent of burnout, refers to a sense of detachment from one's

that when such workers struggle to manage the conflicting

perform the job effectively (Maslach et al. 2001). Of the three
components, emotional exhaustion is the most frequently stud-

mon. Our study considers PSM's relationship with emotional
exhaustion and depersonalization, as recent studies suggest that
PSM may influence each dimension differently (Hickey  2014;

PSM refers to “an individual's orientation to delivering services
to people with a purpose to do good for others and society”
(Perry and Hondeghem  2008, vii). PSM theory suggests that
public employees who are motivated to serve the public interest
will work harder and be more engaged in their efforts to sup-
port their organization's mission because they find the work to
be meaningful and rewarding (Perry and Wise 1990). While this
effort and importance employees give to their work may push
them to overexert and experience more stress about their ability
to achieve their public service goals, theories and research on
burnout have long suggested that employees can tolerate the de-
mands and stresses of the job because they value the work and
feel they are doing something important (Bakker 2015; Maslach
et al. 2001).
Although PSM is expected to help public employees manage
stress and lower burnout, the empirical evidence is mixed.
Studies have found that the importance that public employees
place on their work is associated with lower burnout (Pines and
Keinan 2005) and evidence that PSM may not only reduce emo-
tional exhaustion (Potipiroon and Faerman 2020) but may also
buffer the negative effects of both job demands on burnout (Shim
et al. 2017) and stressors on mental wellbeing (Liu et al. 2015).
However, other studies cast doubt on this relationship by finding
no clear link between PSM and lower burnout (Basinska and
Dåderman 2019; Rayner et al. 2018; Sciepura and Linos 2024;
Shay and Rauhaus 2025; van der Meer et al. 2024) or suggesting
that PSM may be associated with higher levels of stress (Giauque
et al. 2013; Gould-­Williams et al. 2015) and resigned satisfaction
(Giauque et al. 2012).
One possible explanation for these inconsistent findings is that
many studies fail to fully examine the mechanisms through
which PSM is expected to reduce stress and burnout. While
PSM emphasizes what employees seek from their work, many
of the expected benefits of PSM are not just derived from the
employee's desire to do the work but also from their ability to
see the meaningful impact of their work (Bakker 2015; Bashir
et al. 2023; Stritch and Christensen 2014; Van Loon et al. 2015;
Vogel and Willems 2020). Although rarely fully tested (for an ex-
ception, see Zheng et al. 2020), the underlying rationale of much
of the existing research suggests that PSM's relationship with
burnout may be indirect and mediated by the extent to which
employees perceive their work as providing meaningful public
service.
As noted above, PSM is expected to serve as a key personal re-
source that can mitigate burnout by equipping public employees
to better manage job demands and stressors (Bakker 2015). This
expectation is grounded in the idea that employees high in PSM
are more likely to frame their work expectations and outcomes
positively. Such individuals are thought to be better equipped to
handle difficult and stressful tasks more effectively because they
are less likely to view job demands as insurmountable obstacles
and more likely to see them as challenges that can be overcome
to achieve meaningful outcomes (Bakker 2015; Liu et al. 2015;
Jensen and Holten  2025; Meng et  al.  2023; Potipiroon and
Faerman 2020; Shim et al. 2017). They may also anticipate that
such meaningful work will naturally involve greater challenges,
which, in turn, can provide more opportunities for a sense of
accomplishment (Bakker 2015; Potipiroon and Faerman 2020).
In this way, the challenging nature of the work can inspire em-
ployees, signaling its higher importance and potential reward.
Public employees high in PSM can also become frustrated
when organizational barriers hinder their ability to do mean-
ingful work (Giauque et al. 2012; Steijn and van der Voet 2019;
Vinzant  1998). However, these frustrations can be mitigated
when leaders emphasize the prosocial impact of their employ-
ees' work (Bashir et al. 2023; Belle, Bellé 2014; Schott et al. 2019;
Smith 2016; Steijn 2008; Vogel and Willems 2020). Employees
high in PSM are also inherently more likely to see the meaning-
ful impact of their work as they not only seek opportunities to
benefit society (Ritz et al. 2023; Wright et al. 2017) but are also
more attuned to recognize the beneficial outcomes of their work
(Grant et al. 2009; Stritch and Christensen 2014; Taylor 2014;
Weiss and Piderit  1999; Wright and Pandey  2011). Consistent
with these claims, public employees with higher PSM are more
likely to see the prosocial impact (Steijn and van der Voet 2019;
Stritch and Christensen 2014; Taylor 2014) and meaningfulness
(Meng et al. 2023; Zheng et al. 2020) of their work.
To the extent that PSM fosters employee perceptions that their
work is making a meaningful difference in society, there are sev-
eral ways such perceptions can help lower stress and burnout.
First, recognizing the prosocial impact of their work enables
public employees to experience a sense of meaning and per-
sonal responsibility for achieving outcomes that matter to them-
selves or others (Grant 2007; Hackman and Oldham 1976; Meng
et al. 2023). Seeing the beneficial effects of their work provides
valuable feedback that their work efforts can fulfill their desire
to perform meaningful public service. In turn, these perceptions
trigger positive feelings of self-­efficacy (Clair et al. 2016; Hamm
et al. 2024), accomplishment, and self-­approval, which help sus-
tain motivation (Bakker 2015; Bashir et al. 2023; Grant 2008;
Vogel and Willems  2020; Wright  2007). Experiencing proso-
cial impact also increases employee feelings of competence
(Sonnentag and Grant  2012), making it more likely that they
will view job demands as challenges rather than insurmount-
able obstacles (Bakker 2015). Additionally, perceptions of proso-
cial impact can strengthen feelings of relatedness by deepening
employee connections to both their employing organization
(Conway et al. 2025; Ritz et al. 2016) and the communities they
serve (Brænder and Andersen 2013; Grant 2007; Lilius 2012).
Thus, perceptions of prosocial impact can enhance public

A second way employee perceptions of prosocial impact can

Frontline workers often perform challenging tasks under re-
strictive rules and limited resources, which can hinder their
ability to fully achieve the mission of their organization or meet
community needs. Frontline workers, such as police officers,
are also frequently exposed to emotionally intense and drain-
ing interactions with clients. These negative experiences—such
as conflict, unsuccessful outcomes, or emotionally taxing in-
teractions—can dominate cognitive processing, intensifying
stress and burnout over time. This effect may be even more
pronounced among public employees who have stronger desires
and expectations to help citizens and serve society.
While negative experiences can exacerbate strain and deplete
psychological resources, positive emotions and experiences
can serve as buffers that foster resilience and recovery (Bono
et al. 2013; Gross et al. 2011; Lee and Ashforth 1996). For in-
stance, research showed that employees facing greater adversity
at work reported higher levels of fatigue on days in which they
experienced fewer positive events (Gross et al. 2011), while em-
ployees who regularly reflected on positive work experiences
reported lower levels of stress as well as better physical and psy-
chological health (Bono et al. 2013). These findings underscore
that positive experiences can help mitigate the adverse effects of
negative experiences. As previously noted, seeing the prosocial
impact of their work triggers positive feelings of self-­efficacy,
accomplishment, and self-­approval (Bakker  2015; Bashir
et al. 2023; Clair et al. 2016; Grant 2008; Hamm et al. 2024; Vogel
and Willems  2020; Wright  2007). Perceived prosocial impact
also boosts positive affect by enhancing feelings of competence
and encouraging reflective thinking about positive work events
(Sonnentag and Grant 2012; Sonnentag and Starzyk 2015). Thus,
it is not surprising that perceptions of prosocial impact have
been found to both decrease burnout and reduce the extent to
which perceptions of a job's “necessary evils” (Clair et al. 2016)
or antisocial impact—the belief that one's work may harm oth-
ers—increase burnout (Grant and Campbell 2007).
In sum, existing research suggests that while government em-
ployees with higher PSM are more likely to see the value and
purpose of their work, it is their perception of making a mean-
ingful prosocial impact that replenishes energy, builds resil-
ience, and reduces burnout. Thus, the relationship between PSM
and burnout may be indirect and mediated by employees' belief
that their work positively affects the communities they serve.
Hypothesis 1a.  The negative relationship between PSM and
public employee emotional exhaustion is mediated by the per-
ceived prosocial impact of work.
Hypothesis 1b.  The negative relationship between PSM and
public employee work depersonalization is mediated by the per-
ceived prosocial impact of work.
While the primary way PSM is likely to lower burnout is by
increasing employee perceptions of the prosocial impact of
their work, PSM may also influence burnout more directly.
Employees with higher PSM, for example, may be more engaged
and resilient because of their strong commitment to the public
interest and their willingness to sacrifice time and effort to help
others, even when the prosocial impact of their work is not im-
mediately evident. However, such commitment and sacrifice
can also increase exhaustion when it leads to greater work pres-
sure and stress to perform (Giauque et al. 2013; Gould-­Williams
et al. 2015).
Although existing research has largely focused on the rela-
tionship between PSM and emotional exhaustion—or used
composite measures combining multiple dimensions of burn-
out—recent studies suggest that PSM's influence on deperson-
alization or detachment may be particularly significant (Jensen
and Holten 2025) and even stronger than its influence on emo-
tional exhaustion (Hickey 2014). Frontline workers facing high
stress may detach from their clients and work as a coping mech-
anism, but those with high PSM are less likely to do so because
their stronger levels of compassion, self-­sacrifice, and commit-
ment to the public interest shape their relationships with their
work, agency, and the community they serve. For example, re-
search shows that higher levels of compassion associated with
PSM strengthen employees' sense of community and connection
to others (Grant 2007; Lilius 2012), highlighting the importance
of their work and reducing feelings of alienation (Schabram
and Heng 2022). Similarly, the willingness to self-­sacrifice re-
inforces the significance of their work and their connection
to the community (Brænder and Andersen 2013; Grant 2007).
Overall, employees with higher PSM are less likely to detach
from their work and organization because they strongly identify
with the public service missions that their work embodies (Ritz
et al. 2016).
2   |   Data and Measures
2.1   |   Sample and Data Collection Procedures
Data for this study were collected with two separate surveys that
were distributed in 2019 and 2021 to all sworn personnel of a
state-­level law enforcement organization. The organization is in
the Midwest region of the United States. The primary task of the
organization is the enforcement of laws on state highways and
roads. It also provides emergency response and support services
to city and county-­level police departments and is responsible
for enforcing criminal laws on all state properties and state insti-
tutions. Most of the line-­level/patrol officers in the organization
work in offices/units that are dispersed throughout the state,
covering both urban and rural areas, and some officers work in
specialized units and offices within the state.
At the beginning of the study in 2019, the organization had 2508
employees who worked in offices dispersed throughout the state.
Of these, 1711 were sworn personnel, who are the focus of the
current study, and 797 were administrative employees. Eighty-­
eight percent of the sworn personnel were white, and 9% were
female. Data collection began in March of 2019 with an agency-­
wide climate survey of all employees that focused on employee

PSM and perceptions of their work environment and supervisor
leadership practices of their managers. The research team gath-
ered feedback from the senior leaders, human resources practi-
tioners, and line-­level officers within the organization to further
refine the instruments. After finalizing the survey instrument,
the research team initially administered a recruitment email de-

the first 2019 survey for an overall response rate of 62%. After
removing duplicate and incomplete surveys, there were a total

were line officers. The racial and sex compositions of the survey
respondents match closely the racial and sex composition of the
sworn officers (88% white, 9% female) in the agency, suggesting
the sample is representative of the population.
The second survey was administered in the summer of 2021.
At the time, the agency had a total of 1498 sworn officers. The
survey administration process for 2021 was very similar to the
survey administration process used in 2019. The research team
first sent a recruitment email to all sworn personnel describing
the purpose of the study and informing them that their partic-
ipation was voluntary. One week after sending the recruitment
email, the surveys were sent electronically to all sworn officers.
The survey remained open for three weeks, during which the re-
search team sent multiple reminder emails to increase response
rates. The 2021 survey included many of the items included in
the 2019 survey, including PSM items. It also included items
measuring two dimensions of burnout, emotional exhaustion
and depersonalization, and officers' perception of the prosocial
impact of their work. In total, 748 officers completed the 2021
survey for a response rate of 49.9%. Ninety-­two of the 748 re-
spondents were officers with administrative/managerial re-
sponsibilities, and because these police managers do not have
any enforcement duties, they were not included in this analy-
sis. Of the remaining 656 respondents, 68% were patrol officers
and 32% were sergeants or line-­level supervisors. The racial and
gender composition of the respondents (86% White, 8% female)
closely matched the racial and gender composition of the officers
working in the organization (89% White, 7% female). The aver-
age age of respondents was 39 years, while the average tenure
in the organization was 14.27 years. Forty-­two percent of the re-
spondents had a bachelor's or 4-­year college degree. We matched
the responses of the two surveys using a numeric identifier that
was included in both surveys. Of the 656 line-­level officers who
completed the 2021 survey, 442 also completed the 2019 survey.
2.2   |   Measurement of the Variables
Our outcome variable, burnout, is operationalized with two
measures—emotional
exhaustion
and
depersonalization.
Emotional exhaustion was measured with four items of the 2021
survey that capture feelings of being emotionally overextended
and drained by others. The items are “I feel emotionally drained
by my work,” “I feel fatigued when I have to get up and face an-
other day on the job,” “I feel used up at the end of my work,” and
“I feel burned out from my work.” Our second outcome variable,
depersonalization, was measured with three 2021 survey items
that capture the development of cynical attitudes among police
officers about their work and clients. The items are “I worry that
my job is hardening me emotionally,” “I have become more cal-
lous toward my service recipients,” and “I feel like I don't really
care what happens to my service recipients.” Both the emotional
exhaustion and depersonalization items came from Maslach's
burnout inventory and were measured on a five-­point frequency
of occurrence response scale (1 = never, 5 = very often). The
Cronbach's alpha (internal reliability coefficient) values of the
two outcome measures are 0.84 and 0.75, respectively.
The mediating variable of our study, work prosocial impact,
is measured using two survey items from 2021: “The work I
do helps make the community a safer place” and “What I do
at work makes a big difference in the community.” The items
were measured using a five-­point Likert-­type response scale
(1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) and were adapted
from Grant's (2007) work prosocial impact measure for the law
enforcement context. Although these items were adapted from
Grant's  (2007) prosocial impact measure, they were modified
to better fit the law enforcement context in ways that made it
more like the measure of societal impact that was found to have
a stronger influence than prosocial motivation on the positive
affect, lower turnover intentions, and employees' willingness to
recommend their jobs (Vogel and Willems 2020). The two items
were highly correlated (r = 0.73, p < 0.01), and the Cronbach's
alpha for the scale is 0.84.
Our key predictor variable, PSM, was measured in both the 2019
and 2021 surveys using five items from the United States Merit
System Protection Board Survey, which have been utilized in
many previous studies (Wright et al. 2013). The items included:
“Meaningful public service is very important to me,” “I am not
afraid to stand up for the rights of others, even if it means I will
be ridiculed,” “Making a difference in society means more to me
than personal achievements,” “I am prepared to make enormous
sacrifices for the good of society,” and “I am reminded by daily
events of how dependent we are on one another.” Responses
were measured on a five-­point Likert-­type scale (1 = strongly
disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The Cronbach's alpha values for
the PSM measures in 2019 and 2021 were 0.74 and 0.81, respec-
tively. A pairwise mean comparison test (t-­test) revealed no sig-
nificant difference (p > 0.10) between the 2019 mean (M = 4.01,
SD = 0.51) and the 2021 mean (M = 3.99, SD = 0.63) PSM scores.
Since the PSM scores of officers who completed both surveys re-
mained stable over time, we used the 2019 PSM measure as our
main predictor variable. This approach helps to avoid common
method bias, which can arise when both the predictor and out-
come variables are measured at the same time using the same
instrument (Podsakoff et al. 2003). The two-­year lag also pro-
vides a more robust assessment of the temporal effects of PSM
on frontline officers' perceptions of work impact, emotional ex-
haustion, and work depersonalization.
To accurately estimate PSM's unique associations with work
prosocial impact, emotional exhaustion, and depersonalization,
it is important to account for variables that may be related to
both PSM and the mediator and outcome measures. Therefore,
we include several key covariates in our multivariate analysis.

The first covariate controls for supervisor servant leadership be-
havior, an important job resource that is likely related to both
PSM (Schwarz et  al.  2016) and the outcome variables in our
analysis (Eva et al. 2019; Perrier et al. 2025). In addition to evi-

(Vito et al. 2011), studies on the antecedents of police burnout
(Adams and Mastracci  2020; Andreescu and Vito  2021) have
emphasized the role of supervisory support, a key component
of servant leadership. Servant leadership was measured using
six items from the short Servant Leadership Scale developed by
Liden et al. (2008). Sample items include “Puts your best interests
ahead of his or her own,” “Emphasizes the importance of giving
back to the community,” and “Makes your career development
a priority.” All items used a five-­point Likert scale ranging from
1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. The Cronbach's alpha
for the measure was 0.89.
Research shows that law enforcement officers' work motivation,
stress, and burnout are influenced significantly by the perceived
fairness of their organization's formal policies and procedures
(McCarty and Skogan  2013; Lambert et  al.  2010; Trinkner
et  al.  2016). Therefore, it is important to account for officers'
perceptions of their organization's procedural fairness in the
analysis. Procedural fairness was measured using five items
from the study by Trinkner et al. (2016). Sample items include,
“Employees are recruited objectively, regardless of their ethnic-
ity, gender, race, or religion,” and “Policies defining employee
misconduct are applied consistently across all employees.” The
Cronbach's alpha for the procedural fairness measure was 0.87.
Moreover, the nationwide protests in 2020 against the killing of
George Floyd by police likely contributed to heightened stress
among law enforcement officers in the United States (Mourtgos
et al. 2022). Many officers working in the agency worked the
protests alongside local law enforcement. Hence, in our analy-
sis of the relationship between PSM and emotional exhaustion
and depersonalization, we included protest stress appraisal.
Protest stress appraisal was measured with three items in the
2021 survey that asked officers the extent to which they found
the 2020 protests to be “stressful,” “threatening,” and “challeng-
ing.” The items were adapted from a scale developed by Frenkel
et  al.  (2021) and were measured with five response choices
(1 = not at all, 5 = extremely). The Cronbach's alpha for this mea-
sure is 0.89.
Additionally, we control for officer sex, race, rank, educa-
tion, and organization tenure in our analysis. Sex (female = 1,
male = 0), race (nonwhite = 1, else = 0), rank (line officer = 1,
else = 0), education (i.e., college degree: yes = 1, no = 0) are each
measured with a dummy variable. Tenure in the organization
(in years) is measured as a continuous variable.

3.1   |   Confirmatory Factor Analysis
We conducted confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) to assess
the construct validity of the study measures. Appendix  S1
presents the standardized factor loadings obtained from the
CFA. As shown in the table, all scale items have statistically
significant factor loadings (p < 0.05) for their respective latent
constructs. Although the factor loading of the last PSM item
(“I am reminded by daily events about how dependent we are
on one another”) is lower than 0.50, we did not delete this item
because that would artificially improve the fit of the measure-
ment model. The fit indices of the seven-­factor measurement are
χ2(df) = 640.10 (300), RMSEA = 0.05, CFI = 0.95, and TLI = 0.94.
The fit indices suggest the measurement model fits the data well.
Because work depersonalization and emotional exhaustion are
two key components of the larger burnout construct, the two la-
tent variables are expected to be correlated with one another.
The correlation observed between the two latent factors in the
CFA is 0.62 (p < 0.01). To assess whether the measures are em-
pirically distinct, we compared the fit of the seven-­factor mea-
surement model with the fit of an alternative six-­factor model in
which both depersonalization and emotional exhaustion items
were loaded on a single burnout factor. The fit indices of this
model are χ2 = 848.06 (336), RMSEA = 0.060, CFI = 0.92, and
TLI = 0.91. A sequential χ2 difference test between the two mea-
surement models (χ2
diff (dfdiff) = 207.96 (6)) showed the seven-­
factor model fit the data significantly (p < 0.01) better than the
six-­factor model.
3.2   |   Bivariate Analysis
Table 1 reports the means, standard deviations (SD), and correla-
tion coefficients of the composite scale scores of the predictor
and outcome variables and the covariates included in the analy-
sis. As shown in Table 1, PSM measured in 2019 has a negative
association with depersonalization measured in 2021 (r = −0.19,
p < 0.05), but there is no association between PSM and emo-
tional exhaustion (r = −0.04, p > 0.05). Additionally, we observe
that work prosocial impact is correlated negatively with both
work depersonalization and emotional exhaustion (rs = −0.27
and −0.22, respectively, p < 0.05). Further, the table shows that
officers who reported higher levels of stress due to 2020 com-
munity protests against police also reported higher emotional
exhaustion (r = 0.26, p < 0.05) and depersonalization (r = 0.22,
p < 0.05). Both emotional exhaustion and depersonalization are
associated negatively with procedural fairness (rs = −0.30 and
−0.32, respectively, p < 0.05) and servant leadership behavior
of supervisors (rs = −0.31 and −0.26, respectively, p < 0.05).
Finally, as expected, we observe a strong positive correlation
between emotional exhaustion and depersonalization (r = 0.56,
p < 0.05).
3.3   |   Multivariate Analyses
To test our research hypotheses, we use a two-­step analytical
strategy. In the first step, we examine whether PSM measured in
2019 is associated with higher work prosocial impact and lower
work depersonalization and emotional exhaustion in 2021 using
Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM). We use HLM because
officers included in our sample work in offices/units that are
geographically dispersed throughout the state. As such, officers
working within the same unit may encounter similar job stress-
ors and environmental challenges, thus violating the indepen-
dence assumption necessary for Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)
regression analysis (Nezlek 2008). Our preliminary analysis

also confirmed that officer reports of the perceived community
impact of the work, emotional exhaustion, and depersonaliza-
tion varied by the units (p < 0.01). Hence, HLM is necessary to
account for such variability across units while estimating the
effect of PSM on the mediator and outcome variables. We spec-
ify and interpret the HLM models based on Raudenbush and
Bryk's (2004) guidance.
In the second step, we applied Generalized Structural Equation
Modeling (GSEM) to estimate the direct and indirect effects of
PSM on depersonalization and emotional exhaustion. We use
GSEM because it offers the advantage of specifying a multi-­level
structural model, enabling simultaneous estimation of PSM's ef-
fects on both the mediator and outcome variables. All covariates
from the HLM models presented in Table 1 were included in the
GSEM model (see Appendix S2), with error variances for deper-
sonalization and exhaustion allowed to covary.
Table 1 reports the HLM results showing the association be-
tween PSM measured in 2019 with emotional exhaustion
(Models 1 and 4), depersonalization (Models 2 and 5), and
work social impact (Model 3) measured in 2021. Contrary to
our expectation, we observe (see Model 1) no relationship be-
tween PSM measured in 2019 and emotional exhaustion mea-
sured in 2021 (γ = −0.086, p > 0.10). However, as expected (see
Model 2), we find a significant negative association between
2019 PSM and 2021 work depersonalization. An increase in an
officer's 2019 PSM score by one point is associated with a 0.307-­
point decrease in that officer's depersonalization score in 2021
(p < 0.01). Model 3 shows a significant positive association be-
tween 2019 PSM and 2021 work prosocial impact (γ = 0.343,
p < 0.01), suggesting that higher PSM officers considered their
work to have a greater social impact in 2021. When we look at
the results in Models 4 and 5, we find that work prosocial im-
pact is associated negatively with officers' emotional exhaus-
tion (γ = −0.155, p < 0.01) and depersonalization (γ = −0.227,
p < 0.01). Moreover, when we compare the effect of PSM on
work depersonalization shown in Model 2 against the effect
shown in Model 5, we find that the magnitude of the effect
drops but remains statistically significant (γ = −0.217, p < 0.05).
These results provide preliminary evidence that the association
between PSM and work depersonalization is at least partially
mediated by work prosocial impact (Baron and Kenny 1986;
Preacher and Hayes 2004).
Looking at the results for the covariates included in our analysis
(see models 4 and 5), we find significant differences (p < 0.05)
in reports of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization by of-
ficer race. Specifically, non-­white officers report lower levels of
emotional exhaustion and depersonalization than white officers
(γs = −0.220 and −0.300, for emotional exhaustion and deper-
sonalization, respectively). Officers who report higher levels of
stress due to the 2020 community protests report higher levels of
emotional exhaustion (γ = 0.187, p < 0.01) and depersonalization
(γ = 0.124, p < 0.05). Moreover, frontline officers with supervi-

lower levels of emotional exhaustion (γ = −0.164, p < 0.01).
Consistent with prior research, we also find a significant nega-
tive association between officers' perception of procedural fair-
ness and their emotional exhaustion (γ = −0.181, p < 0.01) and
depersonalization (γ = −0.207, p < 0.01).

lower levels of depersonalization (γ = −0.220, p < 0.01) and emo-

PSM has a significant direct negative association with deperson-

To assess whether work prosocial impact mediates the PSM–
burnout link, we followed the guidance provided by Preacher
and Hayes (2004) and calculated the indirect effects of PSM
and their standard errors using STATA's nlcom function after
estimating the GSEM model. The results, along with 95% con-
fidence intervals, are presented in Table 2. These findings indi-
cate that PSM has a significant (p < 0.05) indirect negative effect
on both depersonalization (γ = −0.073, 95% CI: −0.119, −0.027)
and emotional exhaustion (γ = −0.047, 95% CI: −0.085, −0.007)
through perceived work prosocial impact, with the effect being
stronger on depersonalization. Overall, the HLM and GSEM
results suggest that PSM has a more pronounced influence on
depersonalization than on emotional exhaustion.
To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted some
supplemental analyses. First, although we find that PSM did not
change between 2019 and 2021, it is possible that officer PSM
levels in 2021 may have a different relationship with the proso-
cial impact of their work or feelings of burnout in 2021. Thus,
we estimated HLM and GSEM models using the 2021 PSM mea-
sure as the predictor variable. The results of these analyses are
reported in the appendix and closely align with the findings pre-
sented in the paper. Second, some studies suggest that employ-
ees with higher PSM may also experience greater frustration
with rules and administrative processes that limit their ability
to help others or benefit society (Giauque et al. 2013; Giauque
et  al.  2012; Steijn and van der Voet  2019; Vinzant  1998), and
in such cases, PSM may exacerbate employee stress and burn-
out (Bao and Zhong 2021; Giauque et al. 2013; Gould-­Williams
et al. 2015; Van Loon et al. 2015). Although our findings show
a strong positive relationship between officer PSM and feelings
that their work has a meaningful impact on their community
(Tables  1 and 3), we conducted additional tests to examine
whether work prosocial impact moderates as opposed to me-
diates the relationship between PSM and the two components
of burnout. We estimated two additional HLM models (one for
emotional exhaustion and one for depersonalization) that in-
cluded the work prosocial impact and PSM interaction effect.
These additional analyses showed no evidence of any modera-
tion effect (p > 0.10), suggesting that, at least in the setting and
sample we studied, officers with higher PSM are not more frus-
trated or stressed.
4   |   Discussion and Conclusions
Frontline public sector workers often engage in challenging
and emotionally taxing work that is generally underappreciated
(Guy et al. 2008; Lipsky 1980; Maynard-­Moody and Musheno
2000; Maynard-­Moody and Musheno  2003). During times of
crisis and change, these challenges not only intensify but also
grow more critical. The COVID-­19 pandemic, for example, led
to heightened burnout among public sector employees, with
many leaving their jobs in response to increased demands for
service, substantial changes in work settings, and elevated risks

The goal of the current study was to examine the potential

frontline police officers during this turbulent period. Theory
suggests that employee PSM can play an important role in re-

(Potipiroon and Faerman  2020; Shim et  al.  2017), increase

lationship between PSM and burnout by identifying a critical
mechanism through which PSM is expected to reduce employee
burnout, potentially helping to explain conflicting research
findings. A common theme across past studies is that public
employees with high PSM experience lower levels of burnout

that PSM's relationship with burnout is mediated by the impor-

with a greater perception of work meaning and impact among
frontline police officers, which is in turn associated with lower
levels of employee emotional exhaustion and depersonalization.
So one strategy public organizations might employ to address
employee burnout is to “lean into the mission” by activating or
fostering employee PSM, but they need to go beyond talking
about the importance of the mission and take important steps
to help employees see the social and meaningful impact of their
work (Bashir et al. 2023; Bellé 2014; Grant and Campbell 2007;
Grant and Sonnentag  2010; Grant and Campbell  2007; Grant
and Sonnentag  2010; Vogel and Willems  2020; Wright and
Pandey 2011);
Our study also enhances our understanding of PSM's relation-
ship with employee burnout by showing that this relationship
may depend on which dimension of burnout is studied. Existing
research has primarily focused on the relationship between
PSM and emotional exhaustion. Recent research, however, sug-
gests that the nature of PSM's influence on detachment may be
both important (Jensen and Holten  2025) and different from
that of emotional exhaustion (Hickey  2014). While emotional
exhaustion may lower employees' performance and well-­being,
the depersonalization aspect of burnout may lead to employees
detaching from the community they serve and even dehumaniz-
ing individual community members.
Our findings suggest that employee perceptions of work pro-
social impact mediate the relationship between PSM and both
depersonalization and emotional exhaustion. They also support
previous findings that PSM's relationship with burnout may be
stronger and more consistent when examining depersonaliza-
tion rather than emotional exhaustion (Hickey 2014). While the
relationship between PSM and emotional exhaustion was fully
mediated by work prosocial impact, the relationship between
PSM and depersonalization was only partially mediated by work
prosocial impact. Furthermore, our initial analysis testing the
direct relationship between PSM and burnout found that PSM
was associated with decreased depersonalization but had no re-
lationship with emotional exhaustion.
Recent research provides some potential support and explana-
tion for these findings by identifying ways in which PSM might
have different and even conflicting influences on different as-
pects of burnout. Given that self-­sacrifice is considered a defin-
ing feature of PSM (Kim and Vandenabeele 2010) and the fuel
behind prosocial actions (Brænder and Andersen 2013), it has
been suggested that employees high in PSM may be more likely
to put so much pressure on themselves at work that it depletes
their well-­being (Rayner et  al.  2018; Van Loon et  al.  2015).
Consistent with these claims, PSM has been linked to increased
stress (Giauque et al. 2013; Gould-­Williams et al. 2015), working
when sick (Jensen et al. 2019), and working more unpaid over-
time (Koumenta 2015). Recent experimental work also suggests
that employees who sacrifice their self-­interests to serve others
may increase the likelihood of work exhaustion by taking on
too much work (Bolino and Grant 2016) and engaging in fewer
acts of “self-­compassion” (Schabram and Heng 2022). While the
potential negative effects of self-­sacrifice could weaken or even
cancel out the expected positive effects of PSM and meaning-
ful work on emotional exhaustion, its relationship with deper-
sonalization is likely different. To the extent that self-­sacrifice
fuels prosocial actions, it should only encourage employees to
act on, rather than detach from, their commitment to serve the
public interest and maintain high levels of compassion. It has
even been suggested that employee commitment to the public
interest and self-­sacrifice may compensate for lower compas-
sion levels, as public employees' “willingness to sacrifice their
lives for strangers makes it necessary … to be part of a com-
munity in which those strangers are perceived as significant
others” (Brænder and Andersen  2013). Unfortunately, many
commonly used measures of PSM (including the one used in
this study) not only rely heavily on questions regarding an indi-
vidual's willingness to sacrifice but also fail to separate it from
other dimensions of PSM—compassion and public interest or
values—that may vary in their relationship with different as-
pects of burnout.
Together, these findings may help explain the inconsistent find-
ings in prior studies, which have often failed to find an asso-
ciation between PSM and reduced burnout (Rayner et al. 2018;
Sciepura and Linos 2024; van der Meer et al. 2024). Studies that
do not consider the mechanisms driving this relationship may
be less likely to capture PSM's full influence on burnout. The re-
lationship between PSM and emotional exhaustion is especially
likely to be overlooked in studies that do not account for PSM's
indirect effects on emotional exhaustion.

depersonalization. These results suggest that police depart-
ments manage challenging periods, and critical incidents may
significantly impact officers' stress levels and overall well-­being.
Our analysis also showed that frontline police officers with
supervisors who demonstrated servant leadership behavior
reported lower levels of burnout, aligning with existing re-
search suggesting that supportive supervision can significantly
alleviate officer stress and burnout (Eva et  al.  2019; Adams
and Mastracci  2020; Andreescu and Vito  2021; McCarty and
Skogan  2012; Perrier et  al.  2025; Schaufeli and Buunk  1996).
Additionally, officers who perceived higher procedural fairness
within their organizations experienced lower levels of burnout,
corroborating previous studies that show fairness can decrease
stress by enhancing officers' self-­legitimacy (Lambert et al. 2010;
Trinkner et al. 2016; McCarty and Skogan 2012). These findings
underscore the importance of implementing programs that en-
courage servant leadership behaviors among line-­level supervi-
sors and establish fair and unbiased decision-­making processes
within police departments. Such practices can help mitigate of-
ficer burnout. Furthermore, our findings suggest that by rein-
forcing the positive impact of officers' work on the community,
line-­level supervisors can reduce officers' emotional exhaustion
and strengthen their connections to the community, potentially
counteracting burnout-­related detachment. Such management
practices may contribute to a workplace culture that not only
reduces stress and emotional fatigue but also enhances officers'
commitment and sense of purpose, potentially improving officer
retention (Perrier et al. 2025).
The contributions of this study should be considered in light
of its limitations. As previously noted, we examined the rela-
tionship between PSM and burnout during a particularly chal-
lenging period for frontline police officers. At the time of data
collection, officers faced dual pressures: elevated health risks
from the COVID-­19 pandemic and heightened public scrutiny
and operational stress following the murder of George Floyd.
Officers were also navigating widespread community protests,
increased hostility, and a broader legitimacy crisis. Research
shows that these factors significantly intensified the work de-
mands of frontline policing (Mourtgos et  al.  2022; Stogner
et al. 2020). While this environment provided a valuable oppor-
tunity to show that the potential benefits of PSM can occur even
under conditions of acute strain, it also limits the generalizabil-
ity of our findings. It is important to verify whether the patterns
observed in this study hold in less volatile periods to assess their
robustness over time.
Furthermore, PSM's relationship with burnout may vary de-
pending on the specific components of each construct. As
noted above, the self-­sacrifice and compassion dimensions of
PSM may affect depersonalization and emotional exhaustion
differently. Unfortunately, our study employed a short, global
measure of PSM, which prevented us from examining how
changes in individual subdimensions may differentially in-
fluence burnout. This is a notable limitation, as prior research
on a similar population under stressful and dangerous condi-
tions found that active military deployment decreased compas-
sion and increased commitment to the public interest while
self-­sacrifice remained stable (Brænder and Andersen  2013).
Although we did not find evidence of PSM change over time in
our study, it is possible that the nature of officers' PSM shifted
in ways our measure could not detect. For instance, compas-
sion may have declined while commitment to public interest
increased. Our measure, which weighed heavily toward the
self-­sacrifice dimension (three out of five items), may have ob-
scured such changes. Unfortunately, other studies that have
investigated the relationship between PSM and burnout have
similar weaknesses by using similar short global measures of
PSM or composite scores combining self-­sacrifice with other
PSM dimensions. In addition, commonly used measures of
PSM make it difficult to disentangle important concepts by
asking about an individual's willingness to self-­sacrifice to help
others or benefit society.3 As a result, individuals who express
their willingness to self-­sacrifice are also more likely to care
about (feel connected to) the community they are trying to help
and find meaning in their sacrifice. Future research should ex-
plore the distinct effects of PSM dimensions on various aspects
of burnout among frontline workers and investigate additional
underlying mechanisms, beyond work prosocial impact, that
may mediate these relationships.
Second, our study's sample and measures also limit the gener-
alizability of our findings. The relationship between PSM and
burnout may differ across occupations. Research has shown
that public employees in legal, justice, and security roles tend
to report lower levels of the compassion dimension of PSM
(Anderfuhren-­Biget et  al.  2014) and may experience declines
in compassion over time due to the emotionally taxing na-
ture of their work (Brænder and Andersen 2013). While such
challenges may lead some employees to emotionally distance
themselves from the public, our findings suggest that those
with higher PSM are less likely to do so. This could be because,
although law enforcement officers may generally have lower
compassion than their counterparts in social services, those
with higher PSM still demonstrate more compassion than their
peers, or that a stronger commitment to public interest and self-­
sacrifice may compensate for lower compassion (Brænder and
Andersen  2013). Additionally, the relationship between PSM
and burnout may be weaker when measures of prosocial im-
pact focus on individuals rather than communities, as was the
case in our study (Ritz et al. 2020; Schott et al. 2019; Vogel and
Willems 2020). These factors warrant further investigation in
future research.
Finally, the study's research design limits our ability to draw any
causal inferences about the relationship between PSM and of-
ficers' perceptions of work prosocial impact, depersonalization,
and emotional exhaustion. The study is also limited in its ability
to identify and control for alternative explanations for variation
in these outcome variables. Moreover, we were unable to assess
within-­officer changes in emotional exhaustion and depersonal-
ization over time, as the 2019 survey did not include these mea-
sures. Future research should employ a longitudinal design to

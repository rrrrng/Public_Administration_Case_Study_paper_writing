ABSTRACT
President Trump altered the U.S. federal civil service system by reducing merit-­based protections for bureaucratic expertise and
expanding the scope of political appointments, shifting the balance long established under the Pendleton Act of 1883. Similar
reforms have occurred at the state level with moves to at-­will employment. These shifts raise questions about what shapes public
support for merit system protections. Using data from the 2023 Cooperative Election Survey, we examine how public service
motivation (PSM), political knowledge, and ideology influence support for political neutrality and protection from political co-
ercion. We find that political knowledge and PSM are positively correlated with favorable perceptions of current merit system
protections. Interestingly, there is no significant association between ideology and support for merit protections. These findings
suggest that informed and motivated citizens are more likely to support meritocratic principles, highlighting the need for public
education on merit systems' role in sustaining democratic governance.
1   |   Introduction
Across U.S. presidential administrations, the executive branch
has pursued different federal human resource management
reforms. From government reinvention and decentralization
during the Clinton administration to organizational restruc-
turing in response to September 11th and performance-­based
contracting during the Bush administration, presidents typi-
cally responded to the trends and events of their time in reform
efforts. Meanwhile, Congressional reform movements tend to
ebb and flow over time (Light 1997; Light 2006). During his first
term, Donald Moynihan  (2022) details how President Trump
undermined the administrative state by attacking the public sec-
tor and administrative capacity while asserting political control
by weakening federal employee protections and infusing politi-
cal loyalty. President Trump's efforts have continued at a rapid
pace in his second term, as Mary Guy (2025) writes: “America's
HR system is as fragile as the republic it serves…overnight, merit
principles and legal protections began crumbling. Rules that
provide a sense of certainty are being ignored” (p. 241). Peters
and Pierre (2004) define the politicization of the civil service as
“the substitution of political criteria for merit-­based criteria in
the selection, retention, promotion, rewards, and disciplining
of the public service” (p. 2). With this democratic backsliding
(Donald Moynihan 2022) and current efforts reinstating politi-
cal partisanship in the federal bureaucracy, do today's attacks on
the federal government reflect the views of the public?
Given today's political polarization and the political rhetoric sur-
rounding attacks on the civil service system, does public service
motivation (PSM) or the drive to help others grounded in pub-
lic institutions, political knowledge, and political ideology cor-
respond to support for merit system protections? Politicization
of the civil service can have grave consequences, including loss
of trust, accountability, and expertise (Peters and Pierre 2004).

2   |   Merit System Protections in U.S. Civil Service
Merit system protections in the U.S. federal government date
back to the Pendleton Act of 1883, which enabled government
jobs to be awarded based on merit rather than political patron-
age. President Andrew Jackson is well-­known for establishing
the “spoils system” or political patronage, where government
jobs were awarded to supporters. The patronage system proved
to be catastrophic in 1881 when Charles Guiteau, who wrote a
speech that he thought helped Garfield win the election over
Winfield Scott Hancock expecting a job in return, shot President
Garfield in the back. Following this assassination, President
Chester A. Arthur led efforts to create the U.S. Civil Service
System, signing the Pendleton Act of 1883. The Pendleton Act
set forth that federal government jobs should be awarded based
on merit rather than political patronage through three key provi-
sions: neutral competence, prohibiting firing without just cause,
and prohibiting political pressure or coercion. The Pendleton
Act created the U.S. Civil Service Commission to serve as a pro-
tective buffer from the partisan pressures from the executive
and legislative branches.
The Pendleton Act has since been reformed to link human re-
source management with broader management activities and
performance. The Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA 1978) cod-
ified the merit system principles for the first time, including a
specific ban on partisan treatment. The second merit system
principle details fair and equitable treatment: “All employees
and applicants for employment should receive fair and equita-
ble treatment in all aspects of personnel management without
regard to political affiliation, race, color, religion, national or-
igin, sex, marital status, age, or handicapping condition, and
with proper regard for their privacy and constitutional rights”
(Civil Service Reform Act 1978). The CSRA also replaced the
U.S. Civil Service Commission with the Office of Personnel
Management (OPM) to coordinate the federal civil service and
the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to hear employee
appeals and investigate merit system violations. The MSPB
was, in part, created as a separate agency to prevent partisan
abuses, such as those during the Nixon Administration (West
and Durant 2000). The MSPB consists of the board that adjudi-
cates federal employee appeals about personnel actions and the
Office of Special Counsel that “should not passively await em-
ployee complaints, but rather, vigorously pursue merit system
abuses on a systematic basis” (U.S. Congress 1978, 32). Federal
civil service reforms are always political as Ingraham  (2010)
states “improved performance in pursuit of presidential objec-
tives is the point… (p. 4).” Across transitions, presidents tend to
start from a place of distrust in controlling the federal bureau-
cracy yet trust and cooperation are needed to advance presi-
dential agendas (Resh 2015). President Trump took distrust to
a new level by undermining the administrative state, fostering
distrust of the public sector, reducing administrative capacity,
and removing federal protections to promote political control
(Moynihan 2022).

4   |   Hypotheses
Given the political nature of the reforms and the political at-
tack on the federal government, what shapes public support
for merit system protections? Is it political ideology, political
knowledge, or PSM that drives public opinion in support or op-
position to merit system protections? Government has multiple
accountabilities—political, legal, professional, and bureaucratic
(Romzek 2000). Understanding factors that shape political ac-
countability to ensure bureaucratic expertise is protected is
vital. For example, Romzek and Dubnick (1987) review the case
of the Challenger explosion to demonstrate how succumbing to
political pressure at the expense of bureaucratic expertise can
lead to tragedy. First, we examine public service motivation
(PSM) is the drive to help others or concern for others grounded
in public institutions, and may extend to support of merit sys-
tem protections for the civil service. Since people have varying
levels of knowledge and interest in government, politics, and
policymaking, we next examine the role of political knowledge.
Lastly, since the reform efforts to remove merit system protec-
tions have almost exclusively been by republican administra-
tions and those with conservative political ideology, we examine
the role of political ideology.
4.1   |   Public Service Motivation
Public service motivation (PSM) can be defined as “the drive to
help others based in public institutions” (Piatak and Holt 2021,
6). While there have been many different conceptualizations
and measures of PSM since Perry and Wise (1990) first coined
the term, PSM arose from the literature on job sector differences
(e.g., Perry and Rainey  1988; Rainey et  al.  1976) and exam-
inations of what motivates people to work in the public sector
(Rainey  1982). In a survey of human resource management
professionals, Condrey and Battaglio Jr (2007) found those with
only a public sector background were more likely to see at-­will
employment as promoting unfair employment practices. Sector
influences may influence perceptions of merit system protec-
tions. However, PSM extends even beyond the public sector. The
initial measures of PSM captured several dimensions: commit-
ment to the public interest, civic duty, social justice, attraction to
policymaking, compassion, and self-­sacrifice (Perry 1996). PSM
has been conceptualized as both a predisposition and social
identity (Perry and Hondeghem 2008). Since PSM is grounded in
public institutions, we expect people with higher levels of PSM
to be more supportive of merit system principles.
PSM corresponds to behaviors and attitudes within and outside
of the workplace. People with higher levels of PSM have higher
levels of organizational citizenship (Piatak and Holt  2020),
higher job satisfaction (Homberg et al. 2015), and reduced job
turnover (Naff and Crum 1999). High PSM individuals are also
more likely to volunteer with PSM having a greater influence
than the job sector (Holt and Piatak 2023). PSM corresponds
to policy preferences and positions, including a concern for the
commons, a focus on accountability, and support for government
institutions over politics (Piatak and Holt 2021). PSM has also
been found to correspond to trust in government (Jensen and
Piatak 2024) and institutions (Kim and Kim 2024). Since PSM is
grounded in public institutions and corresponds with trust and
support for government and institutions, we argue those with
high PSM will place a higher value on bureaucratic expertise
and merit system protections. Therefore, we hypothesize that:
H1.  People with higher levels of PSM will be more supportive of
merit system protections.

of political information that a person possesses, and has been
important in examinations of voting behavior and policy po-
sitions in political science (Gallina 2023). The simplest way to
measure political knowledge is whether the public has infor-
mation about politics (Carpini and Keeter 1993). People's level
of political knowledge tends to depend on the information that

4.3   |   Political Ideology
Political ideology plays a large role in shaping public opinion,
particularly for policy positions. For example, those with a
more liberal ideology tend to support environmental protection
policies. Demonstrating partisan elite influence, Slothuus and
Bisgaard (2021) found policy opinions changed immediately in
line with the political party for drastic policy position changes.
Given the political nature of human resource management re-
forms in the states to at-­will employment and federal efforts to
gain political control of the federal bureaucracy, political ideol-
ogy likely influences public support or opposition to merit sys-
tem protections.
Donald P. Moynihan (2025) details how President Trump is
politicizing the federal government by focusing on political
loyalty, governing by fear and threats to public servants, and
removing civil service protections. Given the political support
for President Trump in winning the election and the contin-
ued support from the Republican party in Congress, people
with a more conservative ideology are likely to follow the
policy positions of the President. In addition, since human
resource management reforms at both the state level and for
the federal civil service system to reduce merit system protec-
tions have been enacted by political conservatives, we expect
people with more conservative political ideologies will be less
supportive of merit system protections. As such, in response to
political motives and an undervaluing of bureaucratic exper-
tise, we hypothesize that:
H3.  People who are more conservative will be less supportive of
merit system protections.
5   |   Data and Methods
To test these hypotheses, we included survey questions on
public opinion of merit principles, public service motiva-
tion (PSM), and political knowledge in the 2023 Cooperative
Elections Study (CES). The CES is a collaborative survey
coordinated by scholars at Harvard University and Tufts
University and administered by YouGov. It features a nation-
ally representative sample of 24,500 individuals, with scholars
from various universities contributing survey items to specific
modules. The CES typically gathers information about public
opinion on political and policy issues with a two-­wave survey
surrounding elections in even years and one-­wave surveys in
odd years, like our module in 2023. Given the political vari-
ables on the common content and the opportunity to place
our own novel questions about public views of merit system
protections, the CES was an ideal survey for our study. Our
survey items were included in a subset of 1000 respondents.
After accounting for missing data on key variables, our final
analytic sample consists of 816 respondents. For additional de-
tails on the 2023 CES, see: Schaffner et al. (2024).
5.1   |   Dependent Variable
We examine agreement with the merit principles that underpin
the merit-­based system of public personnel administration in
the United States, especially at the federal level of government
(Brewer et al. 2022). These merit principles include a politically
neutral civil service and protections from politically motivated
removal or interference (Brewer et al. 2022). To measure agree-
ment with existing merit service principles, we asked three ques-
tions with five ordinal response categories ranging from one
(“strongly disagree”) to five (“strongly agree”). First, we asked
respondents whether they agreed that “government employees
should be politically neutral.” Second, respondents stated their
agreement with the statement, “Government employees should
be protected from political pressure.” Third, do you agree with
the “removal of government employees for political reasons?”.
For the third question, the response options were presented in
reverse order, using a Likert scale from one (“Strongly agree”)
to five (“Strongly disagree”). Figure  1 presents the mean lev-

5.2   |   Primary Independent Variables
The first primary independent variable in our analysis is pub-
lic service motivation (PSM). We use the five-­item measure of
PSM (originating from Perry 1996; for details, see Piatak and
Holt  2021; Wright et  al.  2013). Scholars have validated this
measure since it was originally included on the Merit System
Protection Board Survey (the MSPB5 scale; e.g., Naff and
Crum  1999). Our additive PSM scale has a Cronbach's alpha
of 0.819.
Pragmatic political sophistication is the amount of political
information that a person possesses and is important in ex-
aminations of voting behavior and policy positions in polit-
ical science (Gallina  2023). To operationalize this concept,
we relied on prior operationalizations of political sophisti-
cation, specifically work by Gomez and Wilson  (2001, 912),
which asked respondents to identify which party controls
each congressional chamber. At the time of our survey in

conservative” and five “very liberal.”
5.3   |   Control Variables
Descriptive statistics for each variable may be found in Table 1.
In addition to our dependent and primary independent vari-
ables, we control for several other factors that may be relevant to
the public's opinion of merit principles. Specifically, we control
for income, race, gender, trust in government, education level,
and whether the respondent has children under the age of 18
who live with them. We include these control variables because
prior research suggests they influence public attitudes toward
merit principles and government institutions. Socioeconomic
factors like parental status, income, and education shape per-
spectives of trust in the civil service (Peters and Pierre 2004;
Van Ryzin 2011), while demographic characteristics such as race
and gender can affect trust in government and perceptions of
bias in public administration (Herd and Moynihan 2025; Ray
et al. 2023).

7   |   Discussion
With recent changes on the federal U.S. Civil Service System
removing merit system protections in favor of political loy-
alty, does political ideology drive public opinion of merit sys-
tem protections or does PSM and political knowledge play a
role? Using original survey questions on the 2023 Cooperative
Election Study in the U.S., we examine how PSM, political
knowledge, and political ideology correspond to support for
merit system protections, including political neutrality, insu-
lation from political pressures, and protection from politically
motivated removals. Rather surprisingly, despite the reforms
being driven by President Trump and the Republican Party,
we do not find a significant relationship between political ide-
ology and support (or opposition) for merit system protections.
However, in line with our hypotheses, we find PSM and po-
litical knowledge correspond with greater support for merit
system protections. Findings have implications for research
and practice on human resource management, reform efforts,
and public service.
First, we find no ideological divide in views on the Merit
Principles System among the electorate. Despite the political
motivation, rhetoric, and division around human resource man-
agement reforms, we do not find any statistical significance be-
tween political ideology and views on merit system protections.
Similar to the Partnership for Public Service  (2024) finding,
most Americans support that having “a nonpartisan civil ser-
vice” (87%) and “competent civil servants” (91%) are “import-
ant for having a strong American Democracy” across political
lines. Like Condrey and Battaglio Jr (2007), surveying human
resource management professionals about at-­will employment,
political ideology is unrelated.
While it may initially seem surprising that political knowledge,
rather than ideology, is the strongest predictor of support for
merit-­based civil service, this finding aligns with a broader lit-
erature on the limitations of mass ideological coherence (e.g.,
Converse 1964; Zaller 1992). Most members of the public possess
limited and often inconsistent ideological frameworks, making
it less likely that their views on bureaucratic professionalism or
merit principles are grounded in stable partisan or ideological
commitments. In contrast, political knowledge, particularly
knowledge of which party controls various branches of govern-
ment, may act as a proxy for greater attentiveness to institutional
norms, including the value of nonpartisan administration. That
is, those who are better informed about government structure
may also be more likely to appreciate the functional role that a
politically neutral and professional civil service plays in demo-
cratic governance. This helps explain why political knowledge,
even in its basic forms, corresponds with greater support for
merit principles.
With political ideology seemingly unrelated, one may wonder
about the motivation behind such reform efforts when they have
been undertaken overwhelmingly by conservatives at the fed-
eral and state levels. These reforms instead seem entirely politi-
cally motivated, like many state reform efforts moving to at-­will
employment (Battaglio Jr and Condrey 2006) and to be about
political control and democratic backsliding (Moynihan 2022;
Moynihan  2025). Merit system protections are vital across
political ideology; future research and the field should con-
sider how to preserve and protect public service and American
democracy.
Second, political knowledge positively corresponds with support
for merit system protections. People who can correctly identify
the party that controls the U.S. House of Representatives, our
measure of political knowledge, are more supportive of politi-
cal neutrality and protection from political pressures in the U.S.
Civil Service and are more likely to disagree with the politically
motivated removal of public servants. As hypothesized, people
with higher levels of political knowledge are more supportive of
merit system protections, perhaps due to their knowledge about
how the government and the U.S. Civil System works and the
need for merit system protections. This finding aligns with the
Knowledge Deficit Model (KDM), which argues experts have
an understanding of specific issues that the general public does
not, and this allows them to better evaluate the risk associated
with that issue (Kellstedt et al. 2008). Engaging with theories of
political efficacy, our findings suggest political knowledge may
empower citizens to value and defend institutional mechanisms
designed to protect against undue political influence. Research
finds political knowledge corresponds to support for democratic
principles and political participation, highlighting the role of
civic education (Galston 2001). Particularly in today's age of mis-
information, disinformation, and conspiracy theories, the role of
political knowledge and educating the public on the foundations
of our government and democracy is vital.
Third, PSM corresponds with support for merit system protec-
tions. As hypothesized, we find people with higher levels of PSM
are more likely to support merit system protections like polit-
ical neutrality and protection from political pressures. Similar
to findings on people with a public sector background (Condrey
and Battaglio Jr  2007), people with high PSM prefer political
neutrality and protection from political coercion. This may be
due to PSM being grounded in public institutions, where high
PSM individuals are more supportive of government institutions
over politics (Piatak and Holt 2021). Theoretically, this research
contributes to the literature on PSM by reaffirming the finding
that the concept shapes support for public policies that protect
the public interest (Piatak and Holt 2021), in this case, public
sector merit systems. Relatedly, PSM corresponds to higher
levels of trust in both government and institutions (Jensen and
Piatak 2024; Kim and Kim 2024). The benefits of PSM extend
beyond the workplace and prosocial behaviors to support for
government institutions and core principles of America's democ-
racy like merit system protections. Our findings reinforce the
growing importance of PSM in public policy research, where fu-
ture research should examine the role of PSM in other contexts.
Like any research, our study is not without limitations. First,
the timing of the study may influence the results. The 2023
Cooperative Election Study was fielded in the Fall of 2023
during President Biden's administration. Merit system protec-
tions were undermined during President Trump's first admin-
istration through a series of executive orders and Schedule F.
However, President Biden revoked the executive orders before
Schedule F was implemented. Since the survey was fielded at
a time when merit system protections in the U.S. Civil Service
were restored, our findings may differ from if fielded amid

new flurry of executive orders removed many merit system pro-
tections in favor of political control of the bureaucracy and po-
litical loyalty. However, we imagine our findings would only be
more apparent during a time when attacks on merit system pro-
tections are particularly salient. Findings may also vary by the
timing of the survey over the summer as opposed to around elec-
tion time when issues may be more politicized. Future research
should examine the stability or malleability of public views of
merit system protections and how political actions and elections
may influence those views. Second, the debate about measures
of political knowledge continues. However, our simple check of
political knowledge of which political party controls the U.S.
House is in line with the pragmatic approach and past research
(Gomez and Wilson 2001). Perhaps different measures and a dif-
ferent time period would influence the strength of the results;
the findings would likely stand. Future research should further
examine the public's views on merit system protections from
partisan political pressures. Lastly, our study focuses on the U.S.
Civil Service System at the federal level. Future research may
want to examine how the public views accountability across lev-
els of government as protection of merit and government em-
ployee accountability may be viewed differently at the federal,
state, and local levels. With the politicization of the civil service,
we also have much to learn from comparative research across
country contexts.
8   |   Conclusion
Understanding factors that influence preferences for merit sys-
tem protections can help shape the political conversation, pol-
icymaking, and checks and balances to protect the U.S. Civil
Service System and America's democracy. People who are in-
formed with political knowledge and have higher levels of PSM
support merit system protections like political neutrality and
protection from political coercion. Meanwhile, political ideol-
ogy is unrelated. Our findings show that current federal reform
efforts removing merit system protections in favor of political
loyalty do not reflect the will of the people.
Federal government employees and the transparency and pro-
fessionalism of the civil service ensure administrative capac-
ity to carry out the public functions, programs, and services
in the U.S. Yet political rhetoric demonizes public service, un-
derstaffing weakens administrative capacity, and political loy-
alists replace bureaucratic expertise (Donald Moynihan 2022).
Politicization of the civic service “can be arbitrary, chaotic, ram-
pant, and overly focused on partisan and individual interests”
(Halligan 2021, 6). This can lead to severe consequences like
loss of trust, corruption, limited accountability, loss of and un-
dervaluing bureaucratic expertise (Kim et al. 2022; Peters and
Pierre  2004). Public servants have long provided professional
expertise to not only effectively provide essential services but
also safeguard the public. When political accountability takes
priority over bureaucratic, legal, and professional accountabili-
ties, disasters ensue, as was the case with the Challenger tragedy
where political pressures trumped the warnings from bureau-
cratic experts (Romzek and Dubnick 1987). Merit system protec-
tions are needed to ensure adequate administrative capacity and
protect from partisan pressures.
Our findings highlight the role of political knowledge and PSM
in support for merit system protections. The public supports gov-
ernment employees and their protection from political influence
when they have the information and care about public institu-
tions. Future research should further examine factors that shape
public opinion toward our administrative state. Research finds
policymaking is more responsive to public opinion than people
often think (Caughey and Warshaw 2022). Understanding how
and why the public supports the government can drive media
portrayals, political debates, and policymaking.
The field of public administration should strive to educate the
public. Political knowledge has often been found central to sup-
port for democratic principles and participation, but so too we
find PSM plays a role. In today's age of democratic backsliding,
scholars, practitioners, and educators alike have a responsibility
to protect the administrative state and America's democracy.

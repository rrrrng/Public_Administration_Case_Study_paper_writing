ABSTRACT
This study examines the interplay between digital modernization and transformation in the public sector, challenging the no-
tion that they are mutually exclusive strategies. Through interviews with public sector leaders, we find that modernization and
transformation can be jointly implemented rather than managed as competing efforts. Thus, we highlight hybrid strategies that
integrate both approaches, allowing public organizations to balance operational stability with innovation in their overall digital
evolution. Our findings offer practical insights for practitioners seeking to align system structures, methodologies, and procure-
ment strategies with the realities of digital change. For scholars, this article lays the groundwork for future research on how
modernization and transformation can co-­evolve in public administration, thus challenging existing dichotomies and opening
new avenues for investigation.
1   |   Introduction
The public sector continues to invest heavily in digital tech-
nologies. Despite significant investment and attention, studies
have found that 55% of the public sector's digital projects fail
to scale, and that fewer than 20% are considered successful
(Fakhoury 2018; Gartner 2021). While the terms digital mod-
ernization and digital transformation are often used inter-
changeably, these terms refer to different strategies, each with
distinct goals and methodologies. Understanding the differences
between them is important to strategically harness technology
to grow, enhance efficiency, and innovate in the digital world.
As one of our interviewees said, “…one of our first issues is prob-
ably from a strategic perspective [is] making the differentiation
in terms of what is a modernization effort and what is a trans-
formation effort.”
Digital modernization updates and scales older systems to en-
hance efficiency, security, and service delivery, often through
digitizing records, strengthening cybersecurity, and migrating
to cloud platforms. Modernization efforts generally focus on op-
erational efficiency and maintaining service quality but with-
out changes to the underlying processes, structures, and people.
Modernization projects tend to be less disruptive, as they usually
build on existing technology and often can be implemented grad-
ually, and modernization is often seen as a safer and cheaper in-
vestment with clear short-­term benefits (Dawson 2018; Dawson
et al. 2024).
Conversely, digital transformation overhauls public operations,
leveraging digital technologies to fundamentally change service
delivery and citizen engagement. Digital transformation can
often involve cutting-­edge (e.g., artificial intelligence—AI) and
even emerging technologies (e.g., generative AI) to alter how or-
ganizations conduct their operations and deliver public value.
However, this transformation is complex, requiring signifi-
cant changes in organizational culture, skills, and structures,
which can be challenging in the often rigid and risk-­averse

While digital modernization and transformation can be viewed
as trade-­offs or two ends of a continuum, most recent digital
projects probably fall somewhere in between, with elements
of the two tangled. A digital project might begin as a modern-
ization effort, but with any technology uplift, there might also
be implications for how the organization needs to transform as
well. Similarly, a transformation effort may be down-­scoped due
to a lack of funding, necessary infrastructure base, or end user
support, or even as a change in vision from leadership.
Success in the digital age requires both modernization, to ensure
that systems are up to date, and transformation, to meet evolv-
ing citizens' expectations. We posit that public organizations
should move beyond a binary decision regarding modernization
versus transformation and seek to balance modernization and
transformation simultaneously. (Baiyere et al. 2023; Bharadwaj
et al. 2013). Yet integrating these approaches is nontrivial, and
one of the reasons that so many public sector digital projects fail.
Public sector organizations struggle with differentiating be-
tween defining a modernization effort vs. a transformation ef-
fort. This ambiguity makes it a challenge to develop strategies
that successfully integrate legacy systems with new digital tech-
nologies. Compounding this, the sheer ubiquity and persistence
of embedded legacy systems in the public sector make their
retirement very difficult. As a result, most of the funding and
focus of government organizations typically shifts to maintain-
ing current government operations, leaving little budget or focus
for either modernization or transformational activities (Irani
et al. 2023). Even taking time and attention that is required to
decommission a legacy system presents problems, since opera-
tional continuity is required while phasing out the legacy system
and implementing the new system (Alexandrova et al. 2015). As
one interviewee told us, “…the retirement of legacy [is] almost
impossible in terms of big government agencies.”
In this context, we pose the following research question: how
can public managers navigate the tensions of balancing digital
modernization and transformation?
To answer this question, we examined practices within pub-
lic organizations that attempted to modernize and transform
operations simultaneously using digital technologies. Semi-­
structured interviews with senior public executives and chief
information officers (CIO) responsible for these modernization
and transformation efforts were undertaken. Where relevant,
we also analyzed publicly available material (e.g., reports, blogs,
and podcasts) to enrich data collection. We then conducted a rig-
orous qualitative analysis of the data, categorizing and coding
the data. This enabled us to identify patterns, common methods,
best practices, and approaches that should be avoided. Synthesis
of these strategies resulted in an evidence-­driven actionable
framework. Our approach was inductive/exploratory, focusing
on understanding the key tensions/challenges experienced by
public managers and how they responded.
The results of this analysis are threefold. First, we provide a
theoretical contribution by clarifying the distinction between
digital modernization and transformation, a trade-­off often con-
flated in practice and academic literature. Second, by examining
the unique challenges and strategic dilemmas that arise when
public organizations attempt to balance these two approaches,
we offer an evidence-­based understanding of the most important
factors for the successful implementation of digital initiatives
in the public sector. Finally, we propose a framework to assist
public managers in navigating the complexities of integrating
digital modernization and transformation. This framework can
help balance the need for immediate operational improvements
with the long-­term demands of transformative change, thereby
enhancing their capacity to deliver effective, citizen-­centric dig-
ital services with added public value.

2.1   |   The Concept of Digital Transformation
The concept of digital transformation is a subject of considerable
debate in academia, as there is no agreed definition or a widely
accepted framework (Eom and Lee  2022; Haug et  al.  2023;
Mergel et  al.  2019; Piccoli et  al.  2024). Understanding digital
transformation requires first clarity on what digital is. Digital re-
fers to a transformative condition that fundamentally reshapes
how organizations conceive, enact, and evolve their operations
(Baiyere et al. 2023). Digital is not just a label for technologies
but a qualitative shift in context marked by new patterns of
agency, meaning making, and value creation. To describe some-
thing as “digital,” is to hold an ontological view that its function-
ality and significance arise from both its technical properties
and its embeddedness in broader social and infrastructural

of IT and business strategy, which are no longer aligned but born
integrated (Bharadwaj et al. 2013). Digital infrastructures are
socio-­technical platforms that evolve through co-­constitution
with organizational systems (Tilson et al. 2010). Digital inno-

and user experiences are co-­created, not sequentially deployed
(Fichman et al. 2014). To be digital, then, is not about the pres-
ence of technology but rather about how that technology is
woven into the logic of organizing and engaging with the world.
Next, to understand digital transformation, one requires distin-
guishing it from digitization and digitalization, as there is often
confusion related to these terms. Digitization refers to convert-
ing analog services to digital ones, whereas digitalization fo-
cuses on applying digitized information and digital technologies
to broader organizational and societal contexts beyond merely
digitizing existing processes (Mergel et al. 2019). Some authors
consider digitization and digitalization as different stages of
digital transformation where the former is a precursor to or
subordinated by the latter (Bharadwaj et al. 2013; Hoessler and
Carbon 2022; Verhoef et al. 2021). Others consider digitization
a more incremental change and digitalization a more disruptive
process (Vial 2019; Verhoef et al. 2021). Essentially, digitization
evolves and updates the infrastructure while digitalization re-
configures and creates value for the enterprise—one encodes
and optimizes while the other reimagines and reorganizes—
and so while digitization may support modernization, only
through digitalization can transformation truly unfold (Baiyere
et al. 2023).
In the information systems (IS) field, both Bharadwaj et al. (2013)
and Baiyere et al. (2023) distinguish digital transformation from
traditional IT modernization: while modernization focuses on
upgrading existing systems to enhance operational efficiency,
digital transformation involves a fundamental rethinking of
strategy, identity, and value creation. One of the most cited defi-
nitions of digital transformation is from Vial (2019), who defined
digital transformation as “a process that aims to improve an en-
tity by triggering significant changes to its properties through
combinations of information, computing, communication, and
connectivity technologies” (p. 121).
There is a consensus that the key ingredients of digital trans-
formation (1) are induced by digital technologies, (2) lead to
transformational changes that go beyond the technology and
organizational level, and (3) are continuous (Eom and Lee 2022;
Haug et al. 2023; Manny et al. 2021; Mergel et al. 2019; Nielsen
et al. 2024; Weißmüller et al. 2023). However, Piccoli et al. (2024)
added that the end state of digital transformation has not been
clearly defined in previous articles. They argue that digital trans-
formation has a unique ontology and suggest that the end state
of digital transformation goes beyond an IT enabled transfor-
mation or a digitally induced change. Instead, they envision this
end state as a metamorphosis into “a digital organization char-
acterized by the strategic primacy of digital resources” (p. 4).
According to Piccoli et al. (2024), the key to digital transfor-
mation is the organization's explicit strategic decision to make
digital resources the most important part of its digital tech-
nology setup. So, a digital organization is an organization that
decides to make these digital resources the focus of its strat-
egy. They commit to creating, reusing, and combining digi-
tal resources to drive what they offer and how they operate.
According to Piccoli et al. (2024), these digital resources can
be purely digital (like a software program) or a mix of phys-
ical and digital (like a smart device). The point, however, is
that they can be used and combined in new ways via a digital
interface to create value. This is where digital resources dif-
fer from traditional IT resources, which are usually used in a
fixed way.
These three new components introduced in Piccoli et al. (2024)'s
definition (i.e., explicit strategic decision, digital organization
and digital resources), have significant implications. First, dig-
ital transformation is not just a technology/IT/digitally induced
transformation. Since it is a result of an explicit strategic de-
cision, it is actually “a socio-­technical phenomenon” (p. 4) re-
sulting from the synergy between technology, organizational
structure, people and processes. Secondly, the digital transfor-
mation of an organization is not just about organizational/cul-
tural/relational change, but about making digital resources core
to strategy and operations.
We then employ the Piccoli et al. (2024) view of digital transfor-
mation, adding to the previous definitions that this transforma-
tion must be a deliberate strategic decision, and that the final
product should be a digital organization, one where value is gen-
erated through the recombination of digital resources.
2.2   |   Digital Transformation in the Public Sector
In the private sector, the literature on digital transformation
often focuses on new business models, organizational change,
supply chains, interaction with customers and value creation
(Lanzolla et al. 2020; Plekhanov et al. 2022). Similarly, the pub-
lic administration literature also emphasizes organizational
change and public value creation. However, these constructs are
different from the private sector due to the nature of the public
sector (Bozeman and Bretschneider 1994). An additional dis-
tinction lies in the literature's interest in how digital technolo-
gies can improve public sector service delivery for its citizens
(Haug et al. 2023).
Digital transformation in the public sector can be understood as
“a holistic effort to revise core processes and services of govern-
ment […] it evolves along a continuum of transition from analog
to digital to a full stack review of policies, current processes,
and user needs and results in a complete revision of the existing
and the creation of new digital services” (Mergel et al. 2019, 12).
Furthermore, digital transformation is linked to regulatory, ad-
ministrative, and digital infrastructures, and thus considered a
long-­term project (Nielsen et al. 2024).
Haug et  al.  (2023) found that the main external drivers to it
are technological infrastructure and citizens' digital literacy.
However, the political system, including bureaucratic tradi-
tions, political leadership, and legal requirements, also plays a
crucial role in the adoption and implementation of digital ser-
vices (Hellberg and Grönlund 2013). Lastly, political priorities
and economic conditions are also important.

When it comes to the implementation of digital transformation
in the public sector, several factors play a role at multiple levels
(Haug et al. 2023). At the organizational level, an open and in-
novative organizational culture, coupled with a clearly defined
digital strategy, is essential (Chen and Aklikokou 2021; Schulz
and Newig  2015). At the relational level, effective collabora-
tion between internal and external stakeholders is crucial, and
poorly planned collaboration can hinder digital transformation
(Cordella and Paletti 2019; Zhang et al. 2017). And at the indi-
vidual level, leaders and employees with digital skills support
and legitimize digital transformation, and their competencies
facilitate the adoption and integration of new technologies
(Tassabehji et al. 2016).
Finally, Haug et al. (2023) found that the outcomes of digital
transformation efforts in the public sector improve citizens'
access to online services and information, leading to better re-
lationships and improved/new forms of service delivery. Not
only that, but overall interaction with citizens is also improved
through better communication and feedback mechanisms pro-
vided by digital government platforms, thus leading to increased
government's accountability, transparency, legitimacy and citi-
zen empowerment. In parallel, digital transformation in public
organizations can influence processes, structures and routines
(e.g., the introduction of agile methods) resulting in several ben-
efits ranging from cost reductions to substantial structural reor-
ganization (Haug et al. 2023; Mergel et al. 2019).
Digital transformation has the potential to support the public
sector to move toward greater efficiency and capacity and create
an environment for continuous improvement of public services
and empowerment of citizens. However, to achieve this poten-
tial, changes must be more profound and transform organiza-
tional culture, leadership and collaboration. (Curtis 2019).
2.3   |   Digital Modernization Versus Digital
Transformation
Digital modernization is often viewed as being more limited in
scope than digital transformation or a precursor to it (Dawson
et al. 2024), such as when large-­scale infrastructure upgrades
(modernization) precede strategic process overhauls leading to
new citizen engagement models (transformation). Public man-
agers must make real-­time decisions about where to allocate
resources, often within political and financial constraints that
push them toward short-­term, incremental modernization rather
than longer-­term, disruptive transformation efforts. Existing
research does not sufficiently explore how managers reconcile
these competing pressures in practice (Dawson et  al.  2024).
However, a conceptual distinction emerges between modern-
ization and transformation, and their ontological counterparts:
digitization and digitalization. As noted earlier, modernization
is about upgrading and refining, increasing efficiency and pre-
serving functionality value, with a short-­to-­medium term time
horizon. The underlying process of digitization refers to the en-
coding of information into digital formats, transforming content
but not context (Baiyere et al. 2023). Essentially, the incremen-
tal IT housekeeping needed to “keep the lights on” (Dawson
et  al.  2024). Digital modernization involves scaling and up-
grading existing systems to improve performance, security, and
operational continuity. In contrast, digitalization is a sociotech-
nical process that leverages digital affordances to reconfigure
how work is conceptualized and enacted, as the effort to rede-
fine how the business of government gets accomplished (Baiyere
et al. 2023). Digital transformation aims for a major overhaul of
systems and processes, aligning with future opportunities and
possibilities due to disruptive innovation (Dawson et al. 2024).
Transformation is thus not merely about adopting new technol-
ogies, but about enabling new ways of working, new modes of
interaction, and new sources of public value.
As all programs to generate public value have a cost, differences
in budget allocation and the willingness to change hinder the
balance between these approaches, creating tension and slow-
ing progress. Budget realities are often a central issue, as mod-
ernization projects often require less upfront investment than
full transformations. Public organizations operate within strict
budgetary constraints, often driven by election cycles, short-­
term goals, and public pressure to reduce spending (Exmeyer
and Hall 2023). Because modernization projects typically pres-
ent lower immediate costs, they are often seen as safer, less fi-
nancially risky options compared to the extensive investment
transformation requires. Yet, the institutional and budgetary
tensions that often cause agencies to conflate modernization
with transformation, limiting bold innovation in favor of risk-­
averse, incremental upgrades (Dawson et  al.  2024). Choosing
modernization over transformation can lead to getting trapped
in higher long-­term expenses due to the technical debt associated
with repeatedly upgrading outdated systems rather than invest-
ing in entirely new frameworks (Rinta-­Kahila et al. 2023). At the
U.S. federal level, for instance, the Government Accountability
Office notes that roughly 80% of the IRS $100 billion annual
IT budget goes to operating and maintaining legacy systems
(Committee on Oversight 2025). This crowds out the very in-
vestments required for digital transformation. Yet digital trans-
formation also carries significant costs because new platforms
must run alongside legacy systems during transition. In the
United Kingdom, the Universal Credit program's whole-­of-­life
cost rose from £2.2 billion to about £12.8 billion as legacy and
new systems ran in parallel during replacement (Ballard 2023).
Together, these dynamics create a persistent budget tension, as
departments must decide whether to pursue short-­term gains at
the potential expense of longer-­term sustainability.
The willingness to change is another significant tension, influ-
enced by factors such as organizational culture, employee com-
fort with legacy systems, and the level of perceived risk associated
with transformation (Exmeyer and Hall 2023). Modernization
typically involves updating technology with minimal disrup-
tion, appealing to employees and leaders accustomed to legacy
systems (Alexandrova et al. 2015; Irani et al. 2023). In contrast,
transformation requires a fundamental shift in processes, work-
flows, and often organizational structure, which can be intim-
idating for public sector employees who are accustomed to the
status quo. Resistance to change within public institutions, par-
ticularly among long-­tenured staff, reinforces a preference for
modernization, even when transformation would offer more
lasting improvements.
Additionally, digital transformation requires extensive planning,
training, and restructuring efforts, which are often politically

theoretically yield faster, more visible improvements that pro-

Ultimately, the tension between digital modernization and trans-
formation in government sectors is rooted in competing priorities:
the need for immediate, budget-­conscious improvements versus
the potential for comprehensive, long-­term benefits (Exmeyer
and Hall 2023). While modernization is essential to keep govern-
ment systems functioning in the short term, relying on it alone
can impede innovation and delay the adoption of digital services
that meet modern citizen expectations (Latupeirissa et al. 2024).
Addressing this tension head-­on requires both cultural shifts
within government organizations and political frameworks that
support sustained, bipartisan investment in transformational
change, allowing governments the opportunity to build resilient,
adaptable technology infrastructures (Manda 2022).
The reality is that organizations need to step back from bimodal
thinking and shift to hybrid thinking. Within most transfor-
mational programs, there is a large-­scale disruptive digitization
process that modernizes the technical stack to allow the new
technology, functions, and processes to integrate into it. The ob-
verse needs to be true too, in that modernization programs can
conduct small-­scale incremental digitalization pilot projects that
modify the technology stack in such a way not only to address
the immediate business need, but also to set the conditions for
full-­scale transformation in the future. For example, the U.S.
Department of Veterans Affairs is prioritizing the implementa-
tion of its new electronic health record system as “foundational
work” that will allow them to “continue to innovate and deliver
technologies” in the future by operating on the same technologi-
cal baseline (Graham 2025). While there is a need to continue to
fund legacy systems, not all value is equal, and not all capabili-
ties are equal. The challenge is finding a way to keep the legacy
system going (short-­term requirement) while gradually shifting to
modernization and transformation.
Figure  1 positions digital modernization and digital transfor-
mation along two dimensions: digitization–digitalization and
incremental–disruptive change. Consistent with prior literature,
we argue that digitization is most often incremental, focusing on
technical updating and standardization for efficiency, while dig-
italization tends to be more disruptive, involving socio-­technical
reconfigurations that alter business processes and citizen en-
gagement models (Verhoef et al. 2021; Vial 2019). Yet, neither di-
mension is absolute: digitization can also be scaled disruptively
(e.g., full infrastructure migrations), while digitalization can
occur incrementally (e.g., small agile pilot projects). Our sche-
matic illustrates these tensions and overlapping paths between
modernization and transformation, particularly where there are
competing priorities within projects involving both digitization
and digitalization processes as well as incremental and disruptive
changes.

provided a wealth of qualitative data, offering real-­world in-

Although the podcasts did not originate as a research project, we
are convinced that we could rely on the principles of qualitative
secondary analysis (Heaton 2008). According to Heaton (2008),

tensions public managers face and the strategies they employ to
balance these competing priorities.
3.1   |   Data Collection
Primary data were collected through semi-­structured inter-
views with senior public executives and CIOs directly involved
in digital modernization and transformation projects within
their respective organizations. Participants were selected
using a purposive sampling strategy to ensure representation
from a diverse range of public sector organizations, including
state and federal government organizations. We also ensured
there were digital transformation and modernization projects
that were not very successful or somewhat successful in the
sample. The selection emphasized participants who had exten-
sive experience with both modernization and transformation
efforts, providing nuanced insights into their complexities and
interdependencies. The selection was mainly focused on two
countries: United States and Australia, where the researchers
are located.
The interview protocol was designed to explore participants' ex-
periences in digital modernization and transformation projects
in the public sector. Questions focused on their approaches to
addressing legacy systems, managing organizational culture
shifts, implementing emerging technologies, and aligning dig-
ital initiatives with strategic goals. Open-­ended questions en-
couraged detailed narratives, allowing participants to elaborate
on both successes and challenges. Interviews were conducted
via virtual platforms, audio-­recorded with consent, and subse-
quently transcribed for analysis. For a listing of the interviewees'
backgrounds (positions, and organizations), see Appendices A
and B.
Secondary data was obtained from podcasts that featured dis-
cussions with public sector leaders and senior managers. These
podcasts provided additional perspectives on the trends, strate-
gies, and barriers associated with digital initiatives in govern-
ment settings. Episodes were selected based on their relevance
to the study's focus, with priority given to those that addressed
themes of modernization, transformation, and digital public
value creation. The podcasts served to contextualize and en-
rich the primary data, offering broader industry and sectoral
insights.
We reviewed more than 97 available podcast episodes released
from January 2022 to October 2024. We set 2022 as a cut-­off
date due to the significant impact of the COVID-­19 pandemic
on digital transformation efforts. The pandemic accelerated the
adoption of digital technologies across the public sector, forcing
organizations to rapidly modernize their systems while also re-
thinking long-­term transformation strategies. By focusing on
podcast episodes from January 2022 onward, we aimed to cap-
ture insights from experts and CIOs who had firsthand experi-
ence navigating this period of accelerated digital change.
From the initial 97 podcasts, we selected 17 suitable podcast
episodes regarding their fit for our research project and tran-
scribed them. Considering that we did not prepare and conduct
the interviews ourselves, we engaged in a rigorous selection of
podcast episodes following three criteria (Etikan et  al.  2016):
the episode (a) features a renowned digital transformation ex-

4   |   Results
The findings challenge the common perception that moderniza-
tion and digital transformation are mutually exclusive choices.
Initially framed as a trade-­off—where modernization offers
incremental improvements while transformation demands
comprehensive change that disrupts existing processes—our
research reveals that these approaches exist on a spectrum and
are deeply interconnected and even intertwined. Through inter-
views and analysis, it became clear that public managers can
move beyond an either-­or mindset and instead develop strat-
egies to navigate the tensions between both up to the point of
integration.
Rather than choosing one over the other, managers can learn to
balance short-­term modernization efforts with long-­term trans-
formation goals, recognizing their mutual complementary na-
ture. Modernization efforts, such as infrastructure upgrades and
data center consolidations, provide the technical and organiza-
tional foundation necessary for transformation, while trans-
formation efforts, such as cloud migration and service delivery
innovations, can free the scarce capital and human resources
needed to undertake modernization projects. Public managers
can also adopt hybrid strategies that fuse modernization and
transformation across system architecture, project manage-
ment, vendor engagement, and cybersecurity to ensure both sta-
bility and innovation.
To support this shift, we propose pathways toward hybrid
digital modernization-­transformation thinking, enabling
public sector organizations to integrate both approaches stra-
tegically. This includes rethinking funding models to sustain
long-­term transformation while maintaining legacy systems,
adopting flexible system architectures that balance modular-
ity and stability, and leveraging hybrid project management
methodologies like “wagile” to incorporate agile principles
within structured government processes. By embracing this
integrated perspective, public managers can drive more ef-
fective, sustainable, and resilient digital advancements in
government.
4.1   |   Monolithic and Modular
Recognizing the tensions between monolithic and modular sys-
tem design requires an understanding of the trade-­offs between
flexibility and simplicity.
Monolithic systems are built as a single unified structure, where
all components are tightly integrated and interdependent.
Monolithic systems have the advantage of being stable because
most, if not all, use cases are envisioned before design and de-
ployment. As such, these systems generally only need regular
patches and minor updates. Monolithic systems can often be eas-
ier to deploy and maintain in the short term because there is no
need to manage multiple moving parts or deal with integration
challenges. However, the trade-­off is that monolithic designs
lack flexibility and often end up as silos. Modifying a part of the
system often requires significant changes to the entire structure,
making it harder to innovate or scale (Auer et al. 2021; Lewis
and Fowler 2017). Over time, as the system grows, this can lead
to increased technical debt and reduced agility.
These characteristics closely align monolithic systems with the
incremental improvements aimed at maintaining performance
and reliability without altering core business processes or struc-
tures that are at the core of digital modernization (Dawson
et al. 2024). As such, monolithic architectures support stability
but inhibit adaptability, making it difficult to reconcile with
disruptive digital transformation agendas. Transformation re-
quires a shift in both organizational identity and technological
agency—demands that monolithic systems inherently resist
(Baiyere et al. 2023). Digital infrastructures gain their genera-
tive power through layered modularity, a trait absent in tightly
coupled, inflexible architectures (Tilson et al. 2010). As digital
contexts grow more fluid and combinatorial, the structural ri-
gidity of monolithic systems can act both as a brake on innova-
tion and a source of accumulating technical debt.
On the other hand, modular systems are composed of discrete,
interchangeable components that can be developed, tested, and
maintained independently. This promotes greater flexibility,
as modules can be updated or replaced without disrupting the
entire system. Modularity fosters innovation by allowing one
to experiment in isolated parts of the system. However, this
increased flexibility comes with the cost of added complexity.
Managing the plug-­and-­play aspect of modules requires one
to think through the overall architecture and interaction pro-
tocols between modules upfront that envision future use cases
(Campagnolo and Camuffo 2010).
This architectural flexibility is closely aligned with the demands
of digital transformation. Digital systems are inherently fluid,
agential, and co-­constitutive—characteristics that modular de-
signs are well positioned to support (Baiyere et al. 2023). In con-
trast to monolithic systems that reinforce organizational rigidity,
modular architectures enable the kind of iterative reconfigu-
ration and recombinability that are central to transformation
efforts. Modularity is a cornerstone of digital infrastructures,
where layered modular architecture enhances generativity by
allowing innovation to occur at different layers without destabi-
lizing the core (Tilson et al. 2010). The combinatorial innovation
that is at the core of digitalization and digital transformation is

enabled by modules to be assembled, replaced, or extended with
minimal friction (Fichman et al. 2014). This architectural view
allows digital transformation not to be just a continuous layer-

the ability to reuse and orchestrate digital resources across plat-

a truly digital organization (Piccoli et  al.  2024). Thus, modu-

of agility, scalability, and innovation, which are key require-

Public organizations' CIOs aspire to build transformative mod-
ular, flexible systems to enhance agility. However, these efforts
often fall short of their goals. One interviewee (A) noted the
paradox: “Our technology aim is to get to a simpler modular
world […] but what we are actually finding is the opposite: the
new platforms are just new monoliths. We are going to modular,
but we are actually creating increasing amounts of complexity.”
Piccoli et  al.  (2024) highlight this paradox, noting that many
organizations pursue digital transformation rhetorically while
maintaining architectures that reflect IT-­enabled change rather
than a digital-­first logic. The result is what they call a mismatch
between strategic intent and architectural realization, where
modular aspirations are undermined by embedded complexity
and tightly coupled legacy infrastructure masquerading as dig-
ital platforms.
This issue is more acute when organizations try to integrate
best technologies with large platform providers. While modu-
lar solutions promise flexibility, the integration of these systems
can lead to an increasingly complex digital landscape, inhibiting
the agility originally intended. This is confirmed by Interviewee
C: “I have a high dependency on them, and they have a high de-
pendency on me. So, we manage dependencies. We have weekly
dependency meetings, and we talk a lot about this because we
are massively integrated between ourselves as well as all these
legacy systems. So, there is a large cobweb of complexity occur-
ring.” These trade-­offs of modularity-­monolithic systems con-
tribute to the reality that most public sector efforts remain closer
to incremental modernization rather than disruptive digital
transformation: “What we actually see in government is proba-
bly more an incremental modernization,” (Interviewee A).
We propose that public organizations must navigate these
trade-­offs by choosing the right design approach based on their
goals, scalability needs, and long-­term vision. Some may even
adopt hybrid models, combining elements of both modular and
monolithic architectures to balance flexibility and efficiency.
Understanding these tensions allows for more informed decision-­
making, aligning system design with organizational priorities.
As Gerald Caron of the International Trade Association of the
U.S. Chamber of Commerce noted, “Even before I got here, they
were working on modernizing and they were built since such a
way that neither sustainable nor [something] that we could mod-
ularly move. So how can we make sure that we don't engineer
ourselves into a corner. That we can sustain and take on what
we want for a longer term. That is very important.”
Unfortunately, organizational budgets rarely accommodate
a modular approach, often requiring systems to be framed as
unified monoliths to secure and sustain funding and staffing. As
a result, entities frequently shift away from the optimal modular
design toward a more monolithic structure. However, by antic-
ipating these constraints, public organizations can proactively
plan for a hybrid approach, incorporating elements that enable
future modularity. Additionally, the shift to a digital organiza-
tion requires not only technical reconfiguration but an explicit
strategic decision to prioritize digital resources and approaches
in design, governance and funding (Piccoli et al. 2024). Without
this foresight to create fallbacks and a commitment to modular-
ity, agencies risk reinforcing siloed systems rather than foster-
ing integration. Addressing this challenge is crucial for enabling
more transformational initiatives to take root.
4.2   |   Agile and Waterfall Methodologies
Public sector technology projects often present unique chal-
lenges, from strict regulatory requirements to large, complex
systems with long lifecycles. When choosing between agile and
waterfall methodologies, both offer advantages and disadvan-
tages that can significantly impact project outcomes.
The waterfall methodology, with its structured and linear ap-
proach, has traditionally been favored in government projects
due to its alignment with regulatory and compliance require-
ments. Its sequential phases provide clear documentation and
measurable progress, essential for environments characterized
by high staff turnover and shifting political oversight every
2–4 years (Mergel  2016). Waterfall's predictability facilitates
effective management of timelines, budgets, and risks, as its
detailed planning phase establishes clear expectations and de-
liverables that endure beyond the tenure of individual contrib-
utors. Mega-­projects such as London's Crossrail, CERN's Large
Hadron Collider, and the Burj Khalifa show how disciplined
stage gates, comprehensive documentation, and upfront plan-
ning can deliver complex outcomes (Nieto-­Rodriguez  2023).
However, its inherent inflexibility presents challenges in gov-
ernment technology initiatives, where unforeseen technolog-
ical advancements, policy shifts, evolving citizen needs, and
emerging cybersecurity threats often necessitate mid-­course
adjustments. The rigid nature of waterfall, which assumes that
all requirements are known upfront, can lead to delays, cost
overruns, and solutions that fail to meet contemporary demands
(Mergel  2016; Mergel et  al.  2021). This structured, linear ap-
proach aligns well with predictable environments, but it stands
in contrast to digital contexts where infrastructures are never
complete and evolve continuously through layered modularity
(Tilson et al. 2010). Specifically, digital transformation based on
a fundamental re-­architecture of the organization around digi-
tal resources, occurring in a state of ongoing emergence, where
business logic, technology, and identity co-­evolve—conditions
that the rigidity of waterfall may struggle to accommodate
(Baiyere et al. 2023; Piccoli et al. 2024).
Agile offers substantial advantages in government technology
projects, particularly in its flexibility and adaptability to rapidly
evolving requirements. Agile allows for iterative development,
enabling government teams to continuously improve software,
incorporate stakeholder feedback, and deliver functional in-
crements over time. This is particularly beneficial for complex

initiatives where anticipating every requirement at the outset
is impractical (Mergel 2016; Mergel et al. 2018, 2021). This it-
erative, feedback-­driven model reflects combinatorial innova-
tion where digital technologies enable novel combinations of
capabilities and services (Fichman et  al.  2014). Additionally,
agile fosters collaboration and transparency by facilitating reg-
ular communication between developers, users, and govern-
ment stakeholders, ensuring alignment with end-­user needs
(Mergel 2016; Mergel et al. 2018, 2021).
However, despite these benefits, agile presents challenges in
government settings due to its less rigid structure and reduced
emphasis on formal documentation, which can conflict with
strict procurement processes, regulatory compliance, and audit
requirements. The methodology's reliance on active stakeholder
engagement can be difficult to sustain in large bureaucratic
organizations where decision-­making can be hierarchical and
slow. Lastly, the iterative nature of agile can also lead to chal-
lenges in budget control, as the flexible scope may cause costs to
increase unpredictably (Mergel et al. 2021). The institutional and
budgetary constraints of public sector organizations often pri-
oritize predictability and auditability, traits better aligned with
traditional methodologies (Dawson et al. 2024). This structural
rigidity can make sustaining agile's continuous engagement
and flexibility difficult in practice, even when its underlying
logic fits the transformation agenda. One cautionary tale un-
derscoring the complexity of the agile approach is Australia's
AUD 74 million Parliamentary Expense Management System
project. The late and over-­budget iterative and agile delivery was
criticized because the client (the Independent Parliamentary
Expenses Authority) expected a “fully functional and easy to
use system” but received instead a technical solution that digi-
tized some manual processes, built with the expectation of “fu-
ture upgrades on an iterative basis” (Godwin 2024, 4).
Recognizing that neither methodology fully addresses the socio-
technical complexity of public sector transformation, there is the
emerging need for approaches that balance structure and adapt-
ability, reflecting both the enduring need for governance and
the generative capacity of modular, evolving systems (Baiyere
et al. 2023; Tilson et al. 2010). Given the distinct advantages and
disadvantages of both agile and waterfall, many government
organizations are moving toward hybrid models that blend ele-
ments of both methodologies, emphasizing the strengths of each
within the constraints of the public sector context. As one inter-
viewee (F) mentioned: “we cannot do agile as it was intended but
are doing it as best as possible within government processes.”
Blending the two approaches, which the same interviewee de-
scribed as “wagile—agile principles in a waterfall approach” can
help public sector projects benefit from the strengths of each
while minimizing their respective drawbacks.
We argue that this hybrid approach can provide the structural
rigor and documentation required for regulatory compliance
and accountability while simultaneously affording the flexibility
and iterative development necessary for responsive project man-
agement. For instance, an initiative may begin with a waterfall-­
like phase focused on comprehensive planning, procurement,
and the establishment of high-­level requirements. Once these
are in place, the development phase may transition to an agile
methodology, piloting small-­scale, incremental digitalization
projects thereby enabling iterative progress, the incorporation
of stakeholder feedback, and adaptive adjustments to emerging
requirements until the system is developed at scale and is fully
operational.
By blending the two, projects can achieve the best of both
worlds: the predictability and governance of waterfall, com-
bined with the innovation and responsiveness of agile. As Burns
of the FDIC described it, “We are held back from pure agile by
organization processes. It is not pure…and we cannot do agile as
it was intended. But, we are doing it as best as possible within
government processes.”
4.3   |   New and Established Vendors
Public organizations face the complex challenge of balancing es-
tablished vendors with emerging digital solution providers.
Established vendors offer familiarity, proven track records, and
a deep understanding of government procurement processes,
which can reduce risk and ensure compliance with regulations.
Their solutions, tested in similar contexts, provide reliability and
security, while frequent staff exchanges between vendors and
public organizations foster trust and collaboration. Additionally,
these vendors benefit from organizational slack, reinforcing
their perceived dependability.
However, their complex structures and reliance on legacy sys-
tems may, absent a governance structure that promotes new
strategies, hinder innovation and slow the adoption of new tech-
nologies. This reflects the inertia of digital infrastructures—sys-
tems that, once embedded, become difficult to reconfigure due
to deep technical, institutional, and procedural entanglements
(Tilson et al. 2010). Moreover, high switching costs and vendor
lock-­in, which can be caused by complex integrated systems,
make it difficult for public organizations to transition to alter-
native providers. Legacy vendors that lack an orientation on
change management to address evolving needs can reinforce
their own monolithic system logic and fixed-­function IT assets,
leading to bias toward incremental change, which contradicts
the core premise of a digital organization: one where modular
digital resources are the basis for transformative strategy, value
creation, and rapid adaptability. This misalignment reflects the
gap between digital transformation rhetoric and the actual ar-
chitecture of IT-­enabled organizations (Piccoli et al. 2024). As
one interviewee (A) noted: “public organizations have that mod-
ular, fit for purpose technologies they want to bring on. But that
sort of does not actually play out easily with the big platforms.”
While traditional players have the advantage of history on dig-
ital initiatives and scale, the track record of successful delivery
on transformative digital efforts is inconsistent.
On the other hand, non-­traditional digital solution providers,
such as start-­ups and tech disruptors, may offer innovative
and agile solutions that can address complex problems in new
ways that are essential for modernization and transformation.
Becoming a digital organization involves a strategic shift to-
ward prioritizing programmable, reusable, and orchestratable
digital resources (Piccoli et al. 2024). These non-­traditional ven-
dors often operate from this logic by default, offering solutions

required for digital transformation, despite their potential for
innovation. Another interviewee further illustrated this chal-

[smaller] contractor [and] they do some work. Maybe they don't
get it all done in that contract and now we're bringing into an-

ongoing tension forces public organizations to weigh the prom-
ise of disruptive and transformative innovation against the risk
of operational instability.
To address this challenge, public organizations must find cre-
ative ways to integrate the strengths of both traditional and
non-­traditional vendors. This requires rethinking supplier per-
formance assessment and fostering a more innovative ecosys-
tem. Yet, rigid contracting processes make it difficult for new
entrants to engage with the public sector, particularly on large-­
scale projects, as responding to public requests for proposals is
often overly complex and time-­consuming. Institutional and
budgetary constraints often prioritize predictability over adapt-
ability, creating structural barriers to engaging with innovative
vendors who operate in more flexible, iterative development
models (Dawson et al. 2024).
One way to address this challenge is through innovative con-
tracting. For instance, Interviewee C noted that fixed-­price
contracts with strict deadlines further limit flexibility, stating,
“With a fixed price contract, the systems integrator (SI) has to
deliver by a certain date. Because of that, they are not going to be
flexible [and] are not going to support your organization's [goal]
to transformation.” A potential solution to this issue is adopting
more open contracts, as the interviewee explained, which would
allow the SI to better support flexibility and facilitate transfor-
mation: “With a more open contract, the SI will more support
greater [flexibility] and how transformation you could poten-
tially get.”
Indeed, to bridge this gap, public organizations must culti-
vate ecosystems of collaboration rather than viewing vendors
as competing forces. Open data platforms, sandbox environ-
ments, and innovation labs can create spaces where traditional
and non-­traditional providers co-­develop solutions that better
align with government needs. Digital transformation reshapes
organizational identity and agency, requiring a move from
static vendor-­client relationships to fluid, co-­constitutive part-
nerships that evolve alongside the services they enable (Baiyere
et al. 2023). Ultimately, transformation is not achieved by tech-
nology alone, but by rethinking organizational architecture and
strategy around digital resources (Piccoli et al. 2024). Engaging
vendors who embody this design logic, whether established or
emergent, is essential to aligning external partnerships with
internal transformation goals. By fostering partnerships across
the vendor spectrum, public organizations can leverage the agil-
ity and innovation of start-­ups while maintaining the stability
and scale of established suppliers.
As Osmond of Virginia, integrating vendors as part of the inter-
nal team strengthens collaboration: “I view them as an exten-
sion of my team and … rely on them to bring in expertise and
run the infrastructure. We have 300 core employees but around
a thousand contractors…we follow the contractual processes and
requirements but, once they are inside, we treat them like team
members”. This strategic approach enhances the agility and ef-
fectiveness of public services, ensuring organizations remain
equipped to navigate the rapid pace of digital change.
4.4   |   Accessibility and Security
Digital transformation is not a gradual layering of new tools but
a strategic and architectural metamorphosis, where digital re-
sources become the organizing logic of the enterprise (Piccoli
et al. 2024). Security, then, cannot remain peripheral or reac-
tive but rather should be designed into the fabric of modular
digital infrastructures, where data, services, and interfaces
are constantly recombined and reused. As public sector orga-
nizations manage vast amounts of sensitive data—including
citizen records, financial information, and critical infrastruc-
ture details—they have become prime targets for increasingly
sophisticated cyber threats such as ransomware, phishing, and
nation-­state cyber espionage. At the same time, governments are
expanding their digital footprint by adopting cloud computing,
IoT, and artificial intelligence, making cybersecurity a critical
priority. Thus, modernization or transformation without robust
cybersecurity measures could expose vulnerabilities, leading to
breaches that can erode public trust, disrupt essential services,
and result in significant financial losses (Dawson et al. 2024).
However, a rigid focus on security without considering accessi-
bility can hinder digital transformation efforts by restricting ac-
cess to essential data and services (Saeed et al. 2023). As Bonnell
of the Air Force Research Lab said, “…cybersecurity is absolutely
critical, right?… We have to approach cyber to both protect our
informational advantage but not to the point of disadvantaging
ourselves by making it too hard to find the right information or
cut people off from critical data.”
To navigate this tension, public sector organizations need to move
beyond traditional perimeter-­based security models, which are no
longer sufficient to address evolving threats, and instead adopt
hybrid cybersecurity approaches. This involves integrating ad-
vanced security frameworks—such as zero-­trust architectures,
encryption, multi-­factor authentication, and AI-­powered threat
detection—while ensuring that security measures do not impede
legitimate and remote access to critical systems and information.

By embedding these technologies strategically within both mod-
ernization and transformation initiatives, governments can
safeguard critical infrastructure and maintain the continuity of
operations and accessibility for users in the face of cyber threats.
This approach aligns with views of digital infrastructures as lay-
ered, evolving systems (Tilson et al. 2010) as hybrid cybersecurity
frameworks must mirror this structure, embedding security not
just at the perimeter but throughout the modular layers of plat-
forms, data flows, and user interactions.
A key component of this hybrid approach is workforce readiness.
With cyber threats becoming more sophisticated, governments
must invest in human capital by hiring skilled cybersecurity
professionals capable of managing advanced security tools and
responding to potential breaches, as well as training public sec-
tor employees in security best practices. Human error remains
one of the leading causes of security breaches, and ensuring a
cybersecurity-­aware workforce is essential for mitigating risks
without creating unnecessary barriers to data access.
Finally, integrating robust cybersecurity measures into digital
transformation efforts is fundamental for sustaining public trust
and ensuring compliance with regulatory standards. As citizens
demand more secure handling of their personal data and expect
the integrity of public services, any security breach has the poten-
tial to undermine both public confidence and the success of digital
initiatives. Additionally, public sector organizations must comply
with data protection regulations. Digital transformation is not only
a change in systems but a redefinition of organizational identity
(Baiyere et al. 2023), so a mature cybersecurity posture must be
part of that identity, shaping not only infrastructure but also how
public organizations build credibility, authority, and citizen rela-
tionships in the digital space. As digital transformation depends
on an explicit strategic commitment, not just to using digital tools
but to reorganize around digital resources (Piccoli et al. 2024), em-
bedding cybersecurity into this transformation requires similar
intentionality as it must be treated as a first-­order design consid-
eration, not a bolt-­on feature. By incorporating cybersecurity into
the core of their digital transformation and modernization strate-
gies, governments can not only protect against breaches but also
ensure that they meet regulatory requirements, fostering greater
public trust in their digital transformation journey.
However, issues remain. Digital innovation is increasingly
driven by combinatorial reuse, where systems are assembled
from modular, third-­party, and often open-­source components
(Fichman et al. 2014). While this approach fuels speed and ex-
perimentation, it also introduces new dependencies and vul-
nerabilities that require proactive governance. For example, as
Fletcher of CMS noted, “So about 80%–90% of code that's writ-
ten nowadays…is all open source and…there are security vulner-
abilities with that. And people don't always update to the most
[recent] version.” Managing this tension is essential for informa-
tion security.
5   |   Conclusions and Research Agenda
This article reframes the discussion around digital transforma-
tion and modernization in the public sector, arguing that these
approaches should not be seen as mutually exclusive but as
interconnected strategies. Rather than treating modernization
and transformation as trade-­offs, public sector leaders must learn
to balance and manage them simultaneously. Modernization
provides the technical and organizational foundation neces-
sary for transformation, while disruptive transformation often
depends on that base of incremental modernization to succeed.
Based on insights gathered from interviews with public leaders,
our findings emphasize the necessity for public managers to
move beyond viewing these two approaches as mutually exclu-
sive. Managers should focus on developing strategies that enable
them to effectively navigate the tensions that arise when these
approaches are managed in tandem and to plot paths between
modernization and transformation.
In this context, we underscore the significance of adopting
hybrid, integrated strategies that fuse modernization efforts
with broader transformation objectives. Rather than treating
modernization as an isolated goal, it should be leveraged as a
foundational and thus important step toward achieving more
comprehensive and long-­lasting changes in the public sector.
Looking ahead, the success of digital initiatives in the public
sector will depend on a fundamental shift in decision-­making
practices. We recommend that public managers move away from
rigid frameworks that mandate trade-­offs and instead embrace
a more flexible, adaptive approach. Just as we have learned to
view digital strategy as being more than its IT and business com-
ponents, we must learn to view digital evolution as being greater
than its modernization and transformation underpinnings. In
our view, this transition will be essential for fostering long-­term
digital strategies that are capable of evolving with changing cir-
cumstances and addressing the complex challenges of digital
modernization and transformation in the public sector.
Once public organizations acknowledge that modernization
and transformation are not opposing forces but rather comple-
mentary priorities that can be woven together, they can develop
digital strategies that are more resilient, adaptable, and ulti-
mately more effective. Instead of treating modernization as a
short-­term, incremental improvement and transformation as a
disruptive, large-­scale change, organizations can integrate both
perspectives to create a more balanced and sustainable approach
to digital evolution. This recognition enables them to better nav-
igate the complexities of technological advancement, policy
shifts, and evolving public expectations.
By adopting this integrated approach, public sector entities can
achieve several key benefits. First, it can lead to more efficient
and responsive service delivery, ensuring that digital initiatives
are not only innovative but also practical and accessible to the
people they serve. Second, it enhances organizational agility by
allowing institutions to pivot and adapt as new challenges and
opportunities emerge, rather than being constrained by rigid,
predetermined strategies. Third, this shift fosters greater public
value creation, ensuring that digital investments yield tangible
benefits for citizens, communities, and stakeholders.
For policymakers and practitioners, our findings offer a struc-
tured framework for designing hybrid system architectures,
selecting appropriate methodologies, and refining procurement

goals, which may include both modernization and transforma-

ment more strategic, forward-­thinking approaches to digital

For scholars, this article serves as a foundation for future re-
search on the dynamic interplay between and fusion of mod-
ernization and transformation in public administration. By
challenging traditional dichotomies that separate these con-
cepts, our work opens new avenues for investigation, encour-
aging deeper exploration into how these processes co-­evolve
over time and co-­deliver value to agencies and the citizens
they support. This perspective invites further inquiry into
the conditions, factors, and institutional contexts that shape
successful digital transformation efforts in the public sector,
ultimately contributing to a more nuanced understanding of
digital governance.
Indeed, while much of the literature focuses on digital transfor-
mation failures, there is a lack of empirical research on success-
ful public sector digital transformation projects. Future research
should explore how to systematically collect and analyze data on
successful cases to identify replicable strategies and lessons that
could inform best practices. The study by Brown et al. (2017) is
one of the few that examines a successful digital transformation
project, but more research is needed to create a balanced under-
standing of both success and failure in digital transformation.
A second area that warrants further exploration is the role of
public procurement in shaping the outcomes of digital trans-
formation efforts within government institutions. Despite its
significant influence on the success or failure of digital initia-
tives, public procurement remains an understudied domain in
the broader discourse on digital governance. Given that pro-
curement policies determine how governments acquire digital
technologies and services, understanding their impact on digital
transformation is essential.
Future research should delve into how specific procurement
policies and regulations affect various aspects of the digital
transformation process, particularly in relation to vendor se-
lection, contractual agreements, and overall project flexibility.
For instance, procurement policies often establish the criteria
by which vendors are evaluated and selected, shaping the pool
of technology providers that public organizations can engage
with. These policies may inadvertently favor traditional, well-­
established vendors over smaller, more innovative firms, poten-
tially limiting access to cutting-­edge digital solutions.
Additionally, contractual structures play a crucial role in deter-
mining the scope, adaptability, and long-­term viability of digi-
tal modernization and transformation initiatives. Rigid, overly
prescriptive contracts may constrain an organization's ability to
respond to emerging technological advancements or evolving
service delivery needs. Conversely, more flexible contractual
frameworks could allow for iterative development, enabling
government agencies to refine their digital strategies in collabo-
ration with vendors over time.
One particularly pressing question for future studies is how dif-
ferent contracting mechanisms influence an organization's abil-
ity to partner with vendors capable of delivering projects that
demand high levels of innovation, customization, and adapt-
ability. Traditional procurement processes, which often empha-
size cost efficiency and risk aversion, may not be well-­suited for
initiatives that require continuous iteration, agile development,
and cutting-­edge technological expertise. Exploring alterna-
tive procurement models—such as outcome-­based contracts,
phased funding approaches, or innovation partnerships—could
shed light on strategies that enable public organizations to en-
gage vendors more effectively in dynamic, fast-­evolving digital
environments.
By examining these procurement-­related dynamics in greater
depth, future research can provide valuable insights into how
governments can refine their procurement strategies to better
support digital transformation efforts. This knowledge could
help policymakers design procurement frameworks that bal-
ance accountability and oversight with the need for flexibility,
innovation, and long-­term digital success.
Finally, another crucial area that warrants deeper investigation
is the extent to which public organizations rely on external ven-
dors for digital expertise and technological capabilities. As gov-
ernments increasingly turn to private-­sector partners to support
their digital transformation efforts, it is important to understand
how this dependence shapes not only the implementation of dig-
ital projects but also the long-­term sustainability of digital initia-
tives within the public sector.
Future research should explore how reliance on external ven-
dors influences a public organization's ability to effectively eval-
uate and negotiate contracts, ensuring that vendor partnerships
align with the organization's strategic digital objectives. Many
public agencies may lack the in-­house technical knowledge
needed to critically assess vendor proposals, leading to potential
challenges in selecting the most suitable partners for complex
digital projects. Without a strong internal knowledge base, or-
ganizations may struggle to differentiate between vendors offer-
ing truly innovative, high-­value solutions and those providing
standardized services that may not fully meet evolving public
sector needs.
Additionally, vendor dependence has significant implications
for project oversight and management. If public agencies lack
sufficient digital expertise, they may face difficulties in monitor-
ing vendor performance, ensuring accountability, and making
informed decisions throughout the project lifecycle. This chal-
lenge can become particularly pronounced in long-­term con-
tracts, where evolving technologies and shifting policy priorities
may necessitate adjustments that agencies may not be equipped
to evaluate or negotiate effectively.
Another key area for research is how vendor reliance affects an
organization's ability to sustain digital initiatives beyond the ini-
tial contract period. Many digital transformation projects require
ongoing updates, maintenance, and adaptation to remain relevant
and effective. If public organizations do not develop sufficient in-
ternal digital capacity, they risk becoming perpetually dependent
on external vendors for system updates, troubleshooting, and

future enhancements. This dependency could lead to increased
long-­term costs, reduced flexibility, and potential challenges in re-
taining control over critical digital infrastructure.
To address these concerns, future studies could examine strategies
for building internal digital capabilities within public organiza-
tions. This may include approaches such as investing in workforce
training programs, fostering knowledge transfer agreements with
vendors, or developing hybrid models where internal teams col-
laborate closely with external providers. By understanding how to
balance vendor partnerships with internal capacity-­building ef-
forts, public organizations can ensure they are not only leveraging
external expertise effectively but also maintaining the ability to
manage, evaluate, and sustain digital initiatives over the long term.
By systematically exploring these research gaps, future studies
can generate a more comprehensive understanding of the key
factors that contribute to the successful execution of digital
transformation initiatives in the public sector. A deeper exam-
ination of these issues will offer valuable insights into the struc-
tural, procedural, and strategic elements that influence how
public organizations adapt to technological advancements and
integrate digital tools into their operations.
Such research can help identify best practices for navigating the
complexities of modernization and innovation, providing public
organizations with evidence-­based strategies to enhance their
digital capabilities. It can also shed light on the challenges that
governments face when implementing digital initiatives, such
as resource constraints, regulatory barriers, and organizational
resistance to change. By addressing these challenges, future
studies can offer practical recommendations that policymakers,
public managers, and practitioners can use to refine their ap-
proaches to digital governance.
Moreover, by bridging existing knowledge gaps, future research
can support public organizations in developing more adaptive,
resilient, and forward-­thinking digital strategies. This can enable
governments to respond more effectively to emerging technolo-
gies, shifting policy landscapes, and evolving citizen expectations.
Ultimately, these studies will contribute to the broader goal of
helping public sector institutions not only implement digital solu-
tions more effectively but also sustain and expand them over time,
ensuring that digital evolution efforts lead to meaningful, long-­
term improvements in public service delivery and governance.

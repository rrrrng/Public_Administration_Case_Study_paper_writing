ABSTRACT
Partner selection is critical for interlocal collaboration. However, governments often face challenges in identifying suitable part-
ners, which can undermine the potential gains of collaboration. While previous studies suggest that conforming to peers can re-
duce uncertainty in decision-­making, its role in partner selection remains underexplored. This study explores the effects of peer
conformity on interlocal collaboration. We posit that, in collaborative settings with high uncertainty, local governments tend
to align with peer choices in partner selection to mitigate uncertainty, driven by either instrumental or legitimacy motivations.
Empirical explorations of 55,278 city dyads in China's nationwide interlocal collaboration for service delivery reform support
this argument. Moreover, the peer conformity effect is stronger for city dyads lacking network connections established through
leadership position transfers and counterpart-­assistance relationships, suggesting that peer conformity is probably based on
instrumental considerations, with peer choices serving as information cues and endorsements that help governments make in-
formed collaborative decisions.
1   |   Introduction
In recent decades, collaborative governance has emerged glob-
ally as a key approach in public administration to address cross-­
boundary challenges (Ansell and Gash 2008). Along this trend is
the surging interest of local governments in forming collabora-
tive arrangements with one another to jointly carry out assigned
tasks across various domains. The growing popularity of interlo-
cal collaboration is largely driven by its potential to address fis-
cal austerity, achieve economies of scale, and enhance regional
coordination (Agranoff and McGuire  2004; Minkoff  2013;
Warner et al. 2021).
Despite its multiple recognized benefits, interlocal collabo-
ration is not without challenges (Shrestha and Feiock  2011;
Zeemering  2019; Aldag et  al.  2020; Mumtaz  2023; Elston
et al. 2024). One critical condition for achieving successful col-
laboration is the selection of suitable partners (Bodin et al. 2022;
Maor et al. 2023). Thomson and Perry (2006) posit that whether
potential partners possess resources that others need and
whether their goals are aligned represent important anteced-
ents of collaboration. A partner mismatch can lead to wasted
resources, partnership breakdown, and damage to organiza-
tional image.
Although choosing suitable partners matters for collaboration,
making informed decisions is often costly. Governments typi-
cally face information asymmetries in partner selection (van
der Heijden 2023). The limited information available about po-
tential partners' willingness, preferences, and capacities puts

Building upon these ideas, understanding how governments
overcome the information asymmetries in interlocal collab-

studies have examined the conditions under which a locality
establishes a partnership with another locality, particularly
by building upon the rationale of transaction cost minimiza-

collaborate with partners where information asymmetries are
easier to resolve, considering factors such as geographical prox-
imity, socioeconomic similarity, leadership connections, and
collaboration histories, which can lower the barriers to directly
obtaining information about potential partners.
Despite offering valuable insights, most existing studies implic-
itly assume that partner selection is primarily driven by a gov-
ernment's direct assessment of whether its interests align with
those of prospective collaborators. Alternatively, interlocal col-
laboration is treated as a matter between involved partners only,
with limited attention to the influences of peers outside them.
The insufficient investigation into the role of peers in partner
selection is particularly surprising, given its relevance for gov-
ernment behaviors.
On the one hand, governments seldom act independently; rather,
their behaviors are adaptable to those of their peers (Ben-­Aaron
et al. 2017; Yi and Liu 2022). This tendency is particularly evi-
dent when governments engage in activities involving unclear
costs and benefits, in which case they frequently look to peers
to manage uncertainty (Berry and Berry  2018). On the other
hand, partner selection is characterized by significant uncer-
tainty, as governments struggle to identify suitable collaborators
from a diverse pool of candidates. This challenge can be further
exacerbated in large-­scale collaborative settings—as widely ob-
served in practice—where geographical distance and jurisdic-
tional boundaries hinder information exchange (Ki et al. 2020).
Therefore, relying solely on direct assessments of potential
partners becomes costly and infeasible (van der Heijden 2023).
Instead, governments may turn to alternative strategies to navi-
gate the information dilemma in partner selection. Referencing
peers can be one option.
To fill the gap noted above, this article investigates how, if at all,
the collaborative choices of peers affect the formation of collab-
oration between two local governments.
We argue that local governments may go beyond direct assess-
ment and, instead, follow their peers' partner choices to man-
age uncertainty in partner selection. The tendency toward peer
conformity can stem from instrumental considerations, where
peers' choices serve as endorsement signals and provide a due
diligence shortcut to assess a potential partner's reputation, thus
facilitating more informed decision-­making. Alternatively, this
tendency may be driven by legitimacy considerations, where
aligning with peer choices helps governments maintain their
image to external audiences and avoid the legitimacy challenges
associated with being viewed as outliers. Based on this logic, we
expect that local governments are more likely to partner with
others that have established collaborative relationships with
their peers.
We conduct empirical explorations in the context of the Chinese
nationwide interlocal collaboration for service delivery (ICSD)
reform, during which city governments have been encouraged
to collaborate with other cities to offer shared government ser-
vices to migrant workers by reaching collaborative agreements
nationwide. We focus on bilateral agreements among 333 pre-
fectural cities and build a data set containing 55,278 undirected
city dyads. Dyadic logit regression models suggest a pattern that
is consistent with our theoretical expectation. We find that for
a given city dyad, a higher proportion of each city's peers that
have previously formed ICSD partnerships with the other city
is associated with an increased likelihood of the dyad reaching
an ICSD agreement in the following year. Moreover, this peer
conformity effect becomes more pronounced when city dyads
lack direct network connections, suggesting an instrumental
logic—peer choices act as signals to assess potential partners'
reputation and compensate for information gaps due to missing
communication channels.
This study advances the literature by proposing and testing a
peer-­based explanation for partner selection. Because uncer-
tainty in partner selection arises in many forms of collaborative
governance, our findings may inform efforts to address similar
challenges beyond interlocal collaboration. Moreover, by high-
lighting the conditional effects of peer choices across intercity
network connections, this study reveals how peer referencing
operates as a due-­diligence shortcut to mitigate uncertainty and
shape collaborative decisions. Finally, this study is among the

2   |   The Role of Peers in Shaping Government
Behaviors
Peers refer to other entities and organizations that are seen as
counterparts sharing similar roles within the same institutional
context. They are considered “significant others” who offer
valuable external cues about appropriate behaviors and serve as
benchmarks for social comparison (Silitonga et al. 2019).
Peer conformity captures actors' tendencies to adjust their be-
haviors to align with those of their peers (Ben-­Aaron et al. 2017).
Various strands of organizational studies use terms such as mi-
metic isomorphism (DiMaggio and Powell  1983), bandwagon
effects, and fashion-­following patterns (Abrahamson  1991) to
capture this phenomenon. Peer conformity represents a com-
mon organizational response to uncertainty. According to Staw
and Epstein (2000), referring to other organizations is simply a
low-­cost heuristic for identifying solutions under conditions of
uncertainty. It enables organizations to gather critical informa-
tion on how certain practices perform in similar circumstances,
which helps assess the costs and benefits of those practices.
Additionally, it allows organizations to gain legitimacy by align-
ing with socially accepted norms, thereby reducing the risk of
being viewed as outliers.
In public administration, numerous studies have highlighted
the role of peer behaviors in shaping government behaviors, par-
ticularly in contexts characterized by uncertainty (Zhang and
Zhu 2019; Yi and Liu 2022; Fan et al. 2025; Liu 2025). For exam-
ple, the literature on innovation diffusion has extensively shown
that governments often refer to the actions of their peers when
new policies are adopted (Berry and Berry  2018). Similarly,
Zhang and Zhu (2019) reported that the adoption of marketiza-
tion innovation by Chinese city governments is positively associ-
ated with the percentage of peer governments that have already
implemented such innovations. With respect to the fulfillment
of public records requests, which also involve uncertain political
costs, US county governments tend to fulfill citizens' requests
only when they realize that peer governments have already done
so (Ben-­Aaron et al. 2017).
Social network studies offer another lens through which to
understand peer influences on government behaviors. These
studies focus on how the structural characteristics of the net-
work in which actors are embedded affect their interaction
patterns (Feiock et al. 2012). Network concepts, such as bond-
ing structures, triadic closures, and overlapping cliques, are
used to explore how networks naturally form tightly connected
groups, where connections between actors tend to be transitive
(Siciliano et al. 2021). In these contexts, new actors tend to part-
ner with well-­connected or popular actors. This is because tight-­
knit groups, with shared connections with third parties, provide
less risky environments for all involved actors in collaboration.
Such structures help build trust, maintain reputation, and ad-
dress commitment issues through collective monitoring (Provan
and Sebastian 1998; Shrestha and Feiock 2021; Ki et al. 2020).
Ofem et al. (2018) demonstrated that a larger number of shared
collaborators for two organizations increases chances for the
dyadic partnership to initiate collaborative advocacy. Huang
et al. (2019) reported that clique-­bridging ties positively influ-
ence joint administrative advocacy. The more collaborators that
a dyad shares within a service delivery network, the more likely
the actors in the dyad are to collaborate.
In summary, recent developments in the literature have opened
opportunities for exploring peer influences on interlocal col-
laboration; however, theoretical development on this topic is
still lacking. First, although the role of peer behavior in various
government actions has been extensively documented, its influ-
ence on partner selection within interlocal collaboration—an
area involving considerable uncertainty—has received limited
attention (Walter and Thurmaier 2021). Furthermore, while so-
cial network studies suggest the relevance of peer behaviors for
interorganizational collaboration, most adopt a network-­wide
perspective, where peer influence is channeled through an ac-
tor's position in the broader network. These studies focus more
on how overall network structures create opportunities for peer
influence than on how specific peers influence an actor's be-
havior. Although this approach is effective in revealing network
structural effects, it overlooks the dynamics of partner selection
within specific dyads and fails to capture the nuances of how
peer influence operates in pairwise interactions. Therefore,
further research is needed to explore how, if at all, peer choices
influence the achievement of interlocal collaboration within
a dyad.
3   |   Hypotheses Development
By building on the risks associated with interlocal collaboration,
this section examines how local governments reduce uncer-
tainty by observing the collaborative choices of their peers when
making collaborative decisions. We first propose the hypothesis
that peer choices positively influence the formation of interlo-
cal collaboration. Then, we consider the contingencies that may
strengthen or weaken the effects of peer conformity, which
helps identify the mechanisms through which peer choices fa-
cilitate collaboration.
3.1   |   Uncertainties in Partner Selection
Choosing reliable partners is crucial to the success of collab-
oration. Poor partner selection—where partners ultimately
prove to be unreliable and incompetent—can make collabora-
tion time-­consuming, prone to breakdowns, and damaging to
the participants' reputation. However, local governments often
struggle to collect reliable information and thus have inadequate
knowledge about potential partners, such as their willingness
to engage, their preferences regarding collaboration goals, and
their capabilities in terms of resources and expertise (van der
Heijden 2023).
On the one hand, local governments have a wide range of po-
tential partners to choose from, and initiating direct inter-
actions with numerous parties can be inefficient (Hamilton
and Lubell 2018). On the other hand, achieving collaboration

organizational autonomy. The divergent interests (e.g., differ-

the alignment of shared goals in negotiations. To secure more
favorable agreement terms that maximize individual gains in
bargaining, local governments often have incentives to hide or
withhold their true intentions at the initial stage of collabora-
tion (Clay and Owsiak 2016). These challenges make achieving
interlocal collaboration with a potential partner a costly pro-
cess that typically involves risk and uncertainty (Walter and
Thurmaier 2021).
The extant literature has extensively revealed that information
asymmetry among collaborators creates various transaction
costs, for example, coordination, negotiation, enforcement,
and agency costs, which may significantly undermine trust-­
building activities and hinder collaboration implementation
(Feiock 2007; Shrestha and Feiock 2011). For example, Carr and
Hawkins (2013) reported that local service collaboration in the
US usually confronts risks originating from coordination, di-
vision, and defection costs. Hansen et al. (2020) demonstrated
that collaboration risk in local government services comes not
only from the likelihood of collaboration failure but also from
the severity of its consequences. Elston et al. (2024) revealed that
interorganizational collaboration in shared services can make
“ex post” interdependence more troublesome than the “ex ante”
trigger for collaboration, which eventually leads to partnership
dissolution.
3.2   |   Peer Conformity and Partner Selection
Considering that uncertainty is a powerful force encourag-
ing peer conformity (DiMaggio and Powell  1983; Berry and
Berry 2018), we speculate that when local governments make
collaborative decisions with potential partners, they may refer to
peers' behaviors toward those partners. In particular, local gov-
ernments tend to conform to their peers' choices to reduce the
uncertainty inherent in interlocal collaboration. Two rationales
may exist underlying peer conformity in partner selection.
First, peer conformity can be driven by governments' instru-
mental considerations, with the aim of alleviating the infor-
mation gap in partner assessments. Local governments often
have a limited ability to discern each other's true collaborative
intentions, which prevents them from fully locating overlaps in
mutual bargaining ranges through direct exchanges (Clay and
Owsiak 2016). Consequently, local governments need alterna-
tive mechanisms to assess each other's willingness and prefer-
ences to collaborate reliably. Peer choices regarding potential
partners can serve as signals that convey supportive information
about a partner's willingness to engage in collaboration (Clay
and Owsiak 2016). Specifically, if a local government observes
that its peers have successfully established agreements with a
particular partner, it signals that the signatory partner is willing
and prepared to follow through on collaborative efforts in sim-
ilar contexts, that is, the partner is probably both interested in
and capable of finding a compromise. Therefore, collaborating
with such a partner may involve less information asymmetry,
making it easier for the involved parties to locate mutually ac-
cepted bargaining ranges. In essence, prior collaboration with
multiple peers acts as a positive endorsement by third parties,
providing a due diligence approach to assessing the reputation
of potential partners.
Second, governments may also conform to peers based on le-
gitimacy considerations, with the aim of demonstrating their
legitimacy to others within collaborative settings. Uncertainties
often prompt governments to engage in isomorphic mimicry as
a symbolic gesture aligned with prevailing norms, rather than
as a purely instrumental decision. An action widely adopted
by peers can be considered socially legitimate (DiMaggio and
Powell 1983; Yi and Liu 2022). Accordingly, a government may
imitate the popular behaviors of others to enhance its image and
appear appropriate in terms of established norms, regardless of
the actual impacts on organizational performance (Deephouse
and Carter 2005). This logic extends to interlocal collaboration
where decisions can also be driven by considerations of main-
taining legitimacy. The literature suggests that governments
often weigh how their collaborative decisions will be perceived
by external audiences (Oliver 1990; Busuioc 2016). When a local
government observes that many others within its peer reference
group have established agreements with a particular partner,
it may feel compelled to follow suit. Doing so allows the gov-
ernment to demonstrate its image as a well-­integrated member
of the collaborative network. In contrast, independently choos-
ing a different partner carries the risk of being perceived as a
nonconformist by the rest of the network. Any failure in such
partnerships is likely to be attributed to the involved parties,
thus damaging their image as reliable collaborators. These legit-
imacy challenges may further result in reduced access to shared
resources and exclusion from future collaborative opportunities.
Therefore, in such cases, referencing peer choices is intended
not necessarily to assess potential partners' reputation through
third-­party endorsement but, rather, to maintain a government's
own legitimacy within the network.
In summary, conforming to peer choices provides governments
with an alternative method of mitigating uncertainty in partner
selection. From the perspective of a given Actor A in a dyad, if
its peers have already established collaborations with the other
Actor B, B is seen as an attractive partner, one likely to be open
to future collaboration. Similarly, from Actor B's perspective, A
is regarded as an appealing partner if many of B's peers have
chosen to collaborate with A. The success of reaching a collab-
oration requires mutual recognition, where both A and B agree
to form partnerships with each other. Therefore, as illustrated
in Figure 1, Actor A has established agreements with B's peers
(PB1, PB2), whereas Actor B has done the same with A's peers
(PA1, PA2). This peer-­based collaboration presents opportuni-
ties for A and B to form new collaborative links. This leads to the
following peer conformity hypothesis for dyadic analysis:
H1.  (Peer conformity hypothesis): For a given dyad, the more
collaborations each actor has established with the other's peers,
the greater the likelihood of forming an interlocal collaboration.

peer conformity effects are moderated by preexisting network
connections between local collaborators. In research on collab-
orative governance, a burgeoning body of literature adopts a
network perspective to explore how connections among govern-
ments foster collaborative arrangements (Feiock et al. 2012; Ki
et al. 2020; Yi et al. 2024). The central argument is that network
connections provide direct channels for intergovernmental com-
munication, allowing governments to obtain straightforward
information about potential partners and, thus, reduce trans-
action costs in bargaining (Shrestha 2018; Siciliano et al. 2021).
These connections can emerge through either individual-­level
or organizational-­level networks. At the individual level, exist-
ing research has primarily focused on public managers' personal
networks. One notable example is the leadership position trans-
fer network, which traces the career paths of public managers
across jurisdictions (Teodoro 2009; Yi and Liu 2022). Position
transfers between localities enable public managers to build
and sustain social contacts that bridge jurisdictions. At the or-
ganizational level, scholars have extensively examined preexist-
ing intergovernmental collaborative arrangements. Numerous
studies have shown that a history of collaboration between lo-
calities in one policy area can influence the likelihood of part-
nership in other areas (O'Leary and Gerard 2012; Shrestha and
Feiock 2021; Bodin et al. 2022). The reason is that existing ar-
rangements create opportunities for communication, enabling
governments to directly assess one another's willingness, goals,
and interest in new collaborations.
Testing the moderating role of interlocal network connections al-
lows us to infer whether peer conformity is primarily motivated
by instrumental or legitimacy considerations. If instrumental
considerations—viewing peer referencing as an alternative due
diligence approach to assessing a prospective partner's repu-
tation—dominate peer conformity, then we would expect the
positive effects of peer conformity to change across dyads with
varying levels of established network connections. Specifically,
in the absence of direct network connections, local governments
have limited capacity to assess each other's willingness and are
more likely to rely on peer endorsements, such as those signaled
by peers, as an alternative information source. This makes peer
referencing more necessary in such contexts.
Conversely, if peer conformity is primarily driven by legitimacy
considerations, that is, efforts to enhance a government's own
image as a well-­integrated member of the network, which is
unrelated to functional concerns about information asymmetry
in assessing potential partners, then we would not observe the
moderating effects of network connections. The observable im-
plication is that the extent to which local governments conform
to peer choices does not significantly change across dyads, re-
gardless of whether direct network connections exist. Therefore,
we propose two competing hypotheses to test the underlying
mechanisms of peer conformity:
H2.  (Instrumental considerations hypothesis): The peer con-
formity effect is stronger in local dyads without direct network
connections.
H3.  (Legitimacy considerations hypothesis): The peer confor-
mity effect does not vary significantly across local dyads, regard-
less of whether they have direct network connections.
4   |   Empirical Context
We test our hypotheses in the context of Chinese ICSD reform.
ICSD emerges when two or more localities work collectively
to deliver public services on a cross-­regional basis (LeRoux
et al. 2010). In Western countries, ICSD has become a popular
approach to improving public service efficiency and cutting
costs (Bel and Warner 2015; Elston et al. 2018). Recent evidence
shows that ICSD is more prevalent than for-­profit contracting in
the United States (Warner et al. 2021). In England, as of 2017,
97% of local authorities had participated in shared service part-
nerships (Dixon and Elston 2020).
The aim of the ICSD reform in China was to facilitate the
social inclusion of migrant workers in service delivery. The
Chinese household registration system (hukou) requires indi-
viduals to register as residents of a specific locality (where they
were born), and this identification has further implications
for their access to a range of government services. Under the
hukou system, city governments mainly offer services exclu-
sively to their own residents, and floating populations whose

With the recent acceleration of large-­scale population flows

of government services has become increasingly severe. The
COVID-­19 pandemic and subsequent strict travel restrictions
and city lockdowns have further highlighted the imperative to
address this social issue.
The central government officially initiated the ICSD reform in
September 2020, encouraging city governments to collaborate
with each other to jointly offer services to migrant workers, re-
gardless of their places of household registration. Joint service
delivery is achieved mainly by agreements signed between
two cities, which typically specify the institutional arrange-
ments for collaboration, including the scope, responsibility,
duration, financial terms, and governance structure (Wang
and Fan 2024). Similar to typical interlocal agreements, these
contracts—approved by both parties—formalize collabora-
tive arrangements through official legal or policy documents.
However, unlike the commonly observed service-­specific
agreements in the United States, the signing of agreements
in China does not necessarily coincide with the immediate
specification of jointly delivered services. Instead, local gov-
ernments often sign a general framework agreement first and
later determine the specific services to be included, along with
standardizing delivery procedures.2 Moreover, local govern-
ments often publicize agreement-­signing ceremonies as ac-
complishments through media outlets. These announcements
make the collaborative actions visible to other jurisdictions,
enabling local governments to observe their peers' partner
choices.
Notably, while this reform was initially proposed by the central
government, official documents issued by upper authorities do
not explicitly impose mandatory regulations specifying which
governments should form connections. In contrast, city govern-
ments have relative discretionary powers in choosing partners
based on their local conditions. Considerable variations exist
among cities in the number of partnerships established between
their peers and prospective collaborators, which we can leverage
to empirically identify peer effects on interlocal collaboration.
The ICSD reform has largely changed the landscape of local gov-
ernments' aspiration for “self-­sufficiency” in service delivery.
For example, before the reform, migrant workers who registered
their household in Citya but worked in Cityb could only enjoy
the services offered by public authorities by returning to their
hometown Citya. However, after Citya and Cityb reach an ICSD
agreement, migrant workers can directly access services in Cityb
without returning to their hometowns.
China represents an appropriate empirical setting. First, inter-
local collaboration has recently proliferated in non-­Western
contexts, such as China, where governments have adopted it
to address complex regional issues (Yi et al. 2018, 2024). The
decentralization reform since 1978 in China has given local
governments increasing autonomy, which offers fertile ground
for voluntary interlocal collaboration (Wang and Fan  2024).
Previous studies have revealed that both China and its Western
counterparts share several similarities in the formation mech-
anisms underlying interlocal collaboration, enabling more
generalizable implications to be drawn from the Chinese con-
text (Chen et al. 2019).
Second, Chinese local governments face great uncertainties in
ICSD reform. On the one hand, local governments need to al-
locate large volumes of administrative and financial resources
to support joint service delivery and to address coordination
issues, with no guarantee of tangible career rewards for local
officials (Wang and Fan 2024). On the other hand, the ICSD re-
form was implemented nationwide, and local governments were
encouraged to seek potential partners across wide geographical
regions. Owing to China's vast territory and socioeconomic di-
versity, many governments find their potential partners located
remotely or in other provinces—where prior interactions are
limited—rather than among neighboring jurisdictions. This
lack of familiarity prevents governments from directly recog-
nizing the true collaboration incentives of prospective partners.
5   |   Data and Methods
5.1   |   Data on Chinese ICSD
We collected information about intercity ICSD agreements
from 333 Chinese cities across 27 provinces from 2021 to 2022.3
Specifically, drawing primarily on official media reports, vali-
dated by additional data sources,4 we built a data set containing
55,278 undirected city dyads that capture nationwide intercity
partnerships. The collection process proceeded as follows: for
each city dyad, three team members searched for official media
reports referencing signed ICSD agreements by local govern-
ment portals and the Baidu (the Chinese equivalent of Google)
search engine. We used Chinese keywords related to ICSD in
our searches.5 These keywords were applied using an “OR” rule,
which means that any media report containing at least one of
the keywords was retrieved. By further reviewing these reports,
which typically include the names of the participating cities
and the date of agreement signing, we were able to determine
whether a given city dyad had reached an ICSD agreement in
a specific year. In addition, many local governments maintain
dedicated sections on their digital service platforms to display
active ICSD partnerships. We reviewed these sections to vali-
date and complement the information gathered from the media
reports. By cross-­checking data from multiple sources—official
media reports, Baidu search results, and government service
platforms—we were able to comprehensively identify relevant
news on ICSD and validate the reliability of the data. Data col-
lection focused on agreements signed in 2021 and 2022 and was
conducted in mid-­December of each respective year.
This article examines how the collaborative choices of peers
in 1 year (2021) influence the partner selection of city gov-
ernments in the following year (2022). Although the data set
is cross-­sectional, it is acceptable for our research purposes,
given the specific timing of the partnership expansion period
in our case. The majority of partnership formation activities
occurred during this period. The reform was announced in
late 2020, with an emphasis on expanding partnership. For
most cities, 2021 marked the first year of actively selecting
and engaging with new partners. However, in October 2022,
the central government issued follow-­up guidance, shifting

We followed the dyadic approach to build a data set based on
city pairs (e.g., Citya and Cityb) for two reasons. First, collabo-

of dyads are common in studies examining interlocal collab-
oration (Yi et  al. 2024). By pairing local governments with
other potential localities with which they can collaborate,
scholars can delineate dyadic relationships via information on
collaborative agreements and incorporate relational variables
into estimation models. This is relevant to our theoretical hy-
potheses based on relational factors. Second, Chinese ICSD is
implemented mainly based on bilateral agreements reached
by city dyads (Wang and Fan 2024), which makes the dyadic
approach especially relevant.
5.2   |   Dependent Variable
The dependent variable is whether a given city dyad (Citya and
Cityb) reached an ICSD agreement in 2022. In line with existing
approaches (Chen and Yi 2023; Ki et al. 2020), we determine
whether Citya and Cityb chose each other as partners by examin-
ing the presence or absence of interlocal agreements, as without
these agreements, a series of substantive collaborative actions
cannot be implemented. This variable is coded as 1 when Citya
and Cityb reached an agreement and coded as 0 when no such
agreement exists.
Figure 2 illustrates the connections among 333 prefecture-­level
cities in China in 2022. There are a total of 5070 bilateral con-
nections, with 932 being partnerships within the same province
and 4138 being partnerships across provinces. This indicates
that the ICSD program has been implemented across the whole
country (not limited to a specific region), with the majority of
city pairs spanning provincial jurisdictions. The example of the
ICSD achieved between Huizhou and Meizhou in Guangdong
Province highlights intercity partnerships within the same prov-
ince.6 A notable case of intercity partnership across provinces
is the collaboration between Hengyang in Hunan Province and
Xiamen in Fujian Province.7 For details, please see Appendix S1:
Part A.
5.3   |   Peer Choice Variable
The partner selection choices of peers in 2021 constitute the
independent variable. We defined a city's peers as all other
cities within the same province. That is, every city sharing
the same province is a member of every other city's reference
group. These cities all receive the political leadership of the
same provincial governments and comply with the same di-
rectives. They are frequently convened by the provincial gov-
ernment for meetings, mutual communications, and rankings.
As a result, they are familiar with each other, and the behavior
of one city should be informative to the others. Specifically,
for any certain Citya and Cityb, we used the proportion of
Citya's peers that signed agreements with Cityb added to the
proportion of Cityb's peers that signed agreements with Citya
as a measurement. Additionally, since the number of cities in
different provinces varies, we standardize this indicator. A
higher value implies that Citya and Cityb are chosen more by
each other's peers.
5.4   |   Network Connection Variables
We focus on network connections established through lead-
ership position transfers and counterpart-­assistance rela-
tionships in China. Both factors have received substantial
attention in the literature and are widely considered import-
ant channels for intergovernmental information exchange (Yi
et al. 2024).
5.4.1   |   Leadership Position Transfers
In China, higher-­level governments regularly transfer local
officials from one locality to another. This mobility of offi-
cials forms network connections, fosters social ties among
local governments, and provides fertile ground for informa-
tion exchange. We focused on the past work experience of a
city's mayor in another city to operationalize leadership posi-
tion transfers, an approach that has been adopted in previous
studies (Chen and Yi 2023; Feiock et al. 2012). For any specific
city dyad, if at least one of a city's chief officials has previously
served in the other city, we code it as 1. If neither chief official
has past work experience in the other's city government, we
code it as 0. We specifically examined the position transfers
of city mayors rather than party secretaries because Chinese
ICSD reforms have focused mainly on the responsibility of
city governments (Wang and Fan  2024). We reviewed the
mayors' CVs of all 333 cities from official portals in December
2022. For officials whose resumes were insufficiently detailed,
we checked them through other databases (e.g., Baidu search)
for accuracy.
5.4.2   |   Counterpart-­Assistance Relationships
Counterpart-­assistance relationships represent a typical example
to capture preexisting intergovernmental collaborative arrange-
ments in China (Yi et al. 2024). These relationships are designed
by their upper-­level authorities, such as central or provincial gov-
ernments, rather than being initiated by cities themselves. With
the aim of reducing regional disparities, counterpart-­assistance
arrangements are established through administrative mandates
that pair economically developed cities with underdeveloped
counterparts. Cities that share the same counterpart-­assistance
relationship are encouraged to explore collaborative opportuni-
ties across various policy areas and often share a history of joint
initiatives. This arrangement implies the presence of formalized

channels for information exchange. Accordingly, Yi et al. (2024)
have reported that counterpart-­assistance city networks in
China facilitate direct information exchange between cities. We
created a dummy variable to capture the counterpart-­assistance
relationship of a city dyad. A city dyad with a counterpart-­
assistance relationship is coded as 1; otherwise, it is coded as 0.

useful indicator for measuring public demand. Higher num-

meeting the joint service needs of migrant workers. Following

5.5.2   |   Socioeconomic Similarity
Previous studies have demonstrated that local governments tend
to partner with localities that share similar socioeconomic condi-
tions. The underlying rationale is that similarities reveal similar
preferences, which can reduce transaction costs in collaboration
(Feiock 2007; Song et al. 2018; Dixon and Elston 2020). When
governments share similar socioeconomic conditions, they are
likely to have comparable policy preferences and motivations
for collaboration (Wang and Fan  2024). Socioeconomic simi-
larity can manifest itself in multiple forms. We create two mea-
sures as proxies: one is the similarity in the industrial structure
(Industrial Similarity), and the other is the difference in fiscal
health (Fiscal Differences).
5.5.3   |   Administrative Status Asymmetry
Power imbalance is another factor that can shape the collabo-
rative process. The transaction costs involved in collaboration
between two localities with equal status are usually greater
in Chinese administrative systems where vertical hierarchies
are preferred (Chen et al. 2019; Wang and Fan 2024). This is
because both parties often seek to impress their superiors by
attempting to lead the collaboration rather than being labeled
followers, which complicates the division of costs and returns.
We create a dummy variable for a given city dyad, which is
coded as 1 if the two cities have different administrative sta-
tuses (e.g., prefectural cities and vice-­provincial cities) and 0
otherwise.
5.5.4   |   Geographical Distance
Geographical distance is also believed to affect interlocal
collaboration (Dixon and Elston 2020; Lee et al. 2012; Chen
et al. 2019). This is not only because geographically close local-
ities (e.g., neighboring governments) tend to have more com-
munication opportunities but also because these governments
often face similar cultural conditions and policy concerns,
thereby reducing potential transaction costs in the collabo-
rative process (Feiock 2013; Lee et  al.  2012; Minkoff  2013).
We use the geographical distances from the Baidu Map as a
measurement.
5.5.5   |   Shared Provincial Jurisdiction
Local governments from different provinces are less familiar
with one another due to the barriers created by jurisdictional
boundaries (Ki et  al.  2020). Accordingly, local governments
within the same jurisdiction, for example, city dyads within
a province in China, tend to exchange information more fre-
quently. To capture this distinction, we created a dummy vari-
able that codes city dyads within the same province as 1 and
those from different provinces as 0.

6   |   Empirical Results
Table 3 presents the results based on the dyadic logit model.
To ensure the robustness of the estimation and better assess
the contributions of different factors, we follow a stepwise
approach in regression analyses. Model 1 provides the base-
line model that includes only the independent variable. Model
2 further includes the two network connection variables.
Model 3 is a full specification incorporating control variables.
The coefficients of peer choices in all three models are sig-
nificantly positive at the 0.01 level. That is, for a given city
dyad, if they were chosen as partners by a larger number of
peers in 2021, they are more likely to establish ICSD agree-
ments in 2022. These findings indicate the existence of a peer
conformity effect in partner selection. Chinese city govern-
ments are likely to follow the choices of their peers in selecting
partners. Thus, H1 is confirmed.
For the effects of network connections, we find that city dyads
with counterpart-­assistance relationships are more likely to
form agreements. Interestingly, while leadership transfers show
a significantly positive effect in Model 2, their influence becomes
nonsignificant once the control variables are included in Model
3. One likely explanation for this inconsistency is that network
connections based on leadership transfers reflect individual-­
level ties that are inherently informal and person-­dependent.
Their effectiveness is largely contingent upon the willingness
and capacity of transferred officials to promote agreements
between jurisdictions. In contrast, counterpart-­assistance re-
lationships represent organizational-­level connections that are
formally embedded in long-­term institutional arrangements,
which are supported by structural incentives for sustained inter-
actions (e.g., performance assessment). Due to their institution-
alized nature, counterpart-­assistance relationships offer a more
stable foundation for ICSD.
Most control variables are consistent with conventional knowl-
edge. In explaining the demands of migrant workers, the ag-
gregated number of migrant workers flowing between two
cities is positively related to interlocal collaboration. Regarding
administrative status asymmetry, city dyads with asymmetric
administrative statuses, such as vice-­provincial and prefectural
cities, are more likely to reach ICSD agreements. Moreover, the
negative impacts of fiscal differences suggest that city govern-
ments likely prefer to collaborate with cities that have similar
fiscal health. In comparison, industrial similarity does not have
a significant effect. Geographical distance negatively affects in-
terlocal collaboration, which means that geographically closer
city dyads are more likely to engage in interlocal collaboration.

7   |   Discussion
Using the case of the Chinese nationwide ICSD reform, this
study explores the relationship between peer choices and part-
ner selection. We find that for a given city dyad, if both actors
have established more collaborations with each other's peers,
then they are more likely to reach an agreement in the follow-
ing year. Moreover, this peer conformity effect is stronger in city
dyads without established network connections. These findings
contribute to the literature in several ways.
First, this study enriches the discussion on interlocal collaboration
by providing a peer-­based account of partner selection. We show
that local governments, faced with the uncertainty of choosing
suitable partners, may look beyond direct assessments of partner
compatibility and instead rely on the collaborative choices of their
peers. Further analyses reveal that the peer conformity effect is
contingent upon the presence of established network connections
between localities. As direct network connections become more
available, the influence of peer conformity diminishes. This pat-
tern supports the idea that peer conformity is probably driven by
instrumental considerations—where peer choices serve as posi-
tive endorsements signaling a potential partner's willingness to
collaborate. However, the legitimacy-­based mechanisms—where
peer conformity helps protect governments' image—are not sup-
ported by our studied case. In other words, local governments rely
on peer choices as informational cues to reduce the uncertainties
arising from information inadequacy in partner evaluation, par-
ticularly in contexts of uncertainty where they encounter difficul-
ties in directly gathering partner information.
Interestingly, our findings on the differing effects of leadership
transfers and counterpart-­assistance relationships echo the re-
cent discussion on how network connections shape collaboration.
While prior studies have emphasized the importance of individ-
ual-­ and organizational-­level preexisting networks for facilitating
voluntary intergovernmental collaboration, our analysis reveals a
more nuanced picture. Specifically, both leadership transfers and
counterpart-­assistance relationships—top-­down managed net-
works in China—exhibit some influence on interlocal collabora-
tion (Yi et al. 2024), which is in line with classic wisdom regarding

counterpart-­assistance relationships, leadership transfer networks
tend to show less consistent effects on ICSD formation after con-
trolling for other factors. This result is likely due to their weaker
institutionalization and greater dependence on individual officials'
capacity and willingness, thus reducing the strength of relation-
ships. This finding aligns with recent evidence suggesting that the
strength of relationships can influence the network effects, and it
highlights the need to further explore how different network char-
acteristics foster collaboration (Shrestha 2018; Ki et al. 2020).
Moreover, this study deepens our understanding of the mecha-
nism driving the formation of ICSD, a globally growing form
of interlocal collaboration, in non-­Western contexts. Unlike its
Western counterparts, where efficiency improvements and cost
reductions are important imperatives (Bel and Warner  2015;
Elston et al. 2018), China's ICSD is designed to address the need
for inclusive service delivery amid increasing population mobility.
Chinese local governments often sign general framework agree-
ments, which are typically followed by the specification of lists of
multiple services to facilitate joint actions. This contrasts with the
service-­specific agreements in the US and the joint administra-
tive bodies in Europe (Bel and Warner 2015). Against this back-
drop, our findings—based on widely recognized variables—show
that Chinese city governments tend to collaborate with cities that
are geographically proximate, share similar fiscal health and
counterpart-­assistance relationships, and exhibit asymmetric ad-
ministrative status. This pattern aligns with the logic of transac-
tion cost minimization, consistent with conventional knowledge
(Feiock 2007; Dixon and Elston 2020). Therefore, even within dis-
tinct institutional settings, local governments still probably follow
similar considerations when forming partnerships.
Finally, in addition to the direct contribution to the interlocal
collaboration literature, this study also engages with the broader
literature on the impacts of peers on governmental behavior.
Peer influence has been widely recognized in areas involving
uncertainty (Ben-­Aaron et al. 2017; Zhang and Zhu 2019; Fan
et al. 2025). However, its role in shaping interlocal collaboration
has received limited attention. While social network studies ap-
proach this issue from a network structure perspective, they typ-
ically do not explore how peer influences operate at the dyadic
level. Our study complements this discussion by theorizing the
peer conformity effect in interlocal collaboration. The approach
of testing mechanisms of instrumental and legitimacy consider-
ations can also be employed by future research.
In practice, local governments often have limited capacity in col-
lecting partner information, especially in large-­scale collabora-
tive settings. Our findings suggest that partner selection choices
made by peers could provide alternative endorsement signals.
Peer referencing constitutes a due diligence shortcut that helps
assess the reputation of potential partners. In this context, prac-
titioners could build peer benchmarking platforms that offer up-
dated information on the collaborative actions of existing local
governments to address information problems.
In contrast, traditional wisdom focuses primarily on the role
of factors such as geographical proximity and socioeconomic
similarity in shaping partner selection, which are relatively stable
and are not easily altered through policy intervention in the short
term. Building upon this, the practical implications of peer in-
fluences suggest more flexible and targeted approaches that help
reduce the information uncertainty faced by local governments
in collaboration and aid them in finding more suitable partners.
8   |   Conclusion
While partner selection matters for collaboration outcomes, gov-
ernments often face uncertainty in selecting suitable partners.
This study explores the role of referencing peer choices as an alter-
native approach to mitigating such uncertainty. Hypotheses are
tested using the nationwide ICSD reform in China. The results
show that the likelihood of a given city dyad reaching an agree-
ment increases when both cities have been chosen as partners by
each other's peers in the previous year. Moreover, the peer confor-
mity effect is stronger for city dyads without direct network con-
nections through leadership transfers and counterpart-­assistance
relationships. This finding indicates that local governments
tend to partner with peer-­endorsed cities, especially in collabo-
rative settings involving higher uncertainty in partner selection.
Although we do not consider the peer conformity effect to be
the only explanation for partner selection, the increasing flow of
labor, capital, and data across jurisdictions has made large-­scale
interlocal collaboration an inevitable trend (Fusi 2021). The cost
of relying solely on direct assessment in partner selection has
increased significantly. Therefore, our peer-­based explanation
should be incorporated into future collaboration studies.
There are several limitations that warrant attention. First, prior
research has highlighted that the nature of the service field itself
may influence interlocal collaboration (Shrestha and Feiock 2011;
Carr and Hawkins  2013). However, in the Chinese ICSD, the
signing of agreements and the confirmation of specific services
are not necessarily interdependent. Even in the few cases where
jointly delivered services are specified in the agreements, these
service lists are unfortunately not publicly available and are sub-
ject to ongoing adjustments. Therefore, although the nature of
services is important, we are unable to test it in the studied case.
Future research could extend our analyses by exploring the het-
erogeneity of interlocal agreements themselves. Second, due to
the unidirectional nature of the interlocal agreement data, we are
unable to directly capture local governments' collaborative will-
ingness. While this limitation does not undermine our testing of
the peer conformity hypothesis, future studies could benefit from
using survey data to directly measure who contacts whom. This
would help provide more straightforward evidence. Third, the
Chinese ICSD is implemented primarily through bilateral collab-
oration between two cities. However, multilateral collaboration
may follow different logics in partner selection. For example, the
current partners of a local government (with peer endorsement)
may veto the expansion of the partnership due to concerns about
losing preferential treatment. This highlights the need for further
research to examine the applicability of the peer conformity ef-
fect in more complex forms of interlocal collaboration. Finally,
considering that collaboration is a multistage process, we call for
future research to further examine the effects of peer influences
at other stages of collaboration, such as the adoption and imple-
mentation of interlocal collaboration.

ABSTRACT
The “racist cop” stereotype is one of the most prominent social representations of law enforcement in the United States. Drawing
on theories of stereotype threat and stigma, this article suggests that this negative stereotype creates an identity threat that
heightens anxiety and stress among law enforcement officers, increasing the risks of their burnout. Using survey data collected
from officers in a state-­level law enforcement agency in 2021, we find that White officers, as well as those who reported greater
stress during the 2020 protests, express stronger concerns about being perceived as racist. These concerns are associated with ele-
vated levels of burnout, and efforts to maintain a positive self-­image partially mediate this relationship. These findings hold even
after controlling for burnout levels reported by the same officers in 2019. Overall, our results extend stereotype-­threat research
to a high-­power public service context and underscore the need for organizational interventions that reduce stereotype-­related
stress and support officer well-­being.
1   |   Introduction
A growing body of public management research highlights the
critical role of stereotyping in shaping citizens' perceptions of
public organizations and employees, as well as their satisfaction
with and evaluations of public services (Bertram et  al.  2024;
Döring and Willems  2023; Hvidman  2019; Hvidman and
Andersen 2016; Neo et al. 2023; Miao et al. 2026; Willems 2020).
Stereotyping refers to the beliefs and expectations associated
with particular social groups, which often lead to generalized
assumptions about individuals within those groups (Dovidio
et al. 2010; Ellemers 2018). Much of the existing literature has fo-
cused on citizens' cognitive biases when evaluating public sector
institutions or on broad negative stereotypes of public employ-
ees, portraying them as boring, inflexible, inefficient, lazy, or
even corrupt (Bertram et al. 2024; Willems 2020). However, lit-
tle is known about how these stereotypes affect frontline public
servants, particularly how they shape their work attitudes and
behaviors (Dinhof et al. 2023; Miao et al. 2026). This gap is nota-
ble, as evidence suggests that awareness of negative stereotypes
and the associated stigma can undermine frontline workers'
motivation, performance, and the quality of their interactions
with the public (Ashforth and Kreiner 1999; Kreiner et al. 2006,
2022). When stereotypes function as identity threats, they may
also increase job-­related stress and negatively impact employee
well-­being and increase work withdrawal (Miao and Guo 2025).
The current study focuses on the “racist cop” stereotype, de-
scribed by Trinkner et al. (2019, 421) as “one of the most en-
during stereotypes of law enforcement in America.” The
murder of George Floyd renewed public attention to police use
of excessive force against Black and Brown communities in the
United States, further amplifying this stereotype. At the same
time, extensive research documents racial bias and significant

by examining how the “racist cop” stereotype contributes to
burnout among officers in a state level law enforcement agency.
Drawing on theories of stigma (Goffman 1963) and “dirty work”
(Ashforth and Kreiner 1999, 2014; Kreiner et al. 2022, 2006), as
well as research on stereotyping and identity threat (Dovidio
et  al.  2010; Ellemers  2018; Steele and Aronson  1995; Steele
et  al.  2002), we argue that this negative stereotype functions
as a form of identity threat. Officers who are concerned about
being perceived as racist may experience heightened anxiety
and engage in increased self-­monitoring to avoid confirming the
stereotype. This form of behavioral regulation, while intended
to deflect stigma, imposes cognitive and emotional burdens, de-
pleting mental resources and increasing vulnerability to burn-
out (Burke 2023; Schmader et al. 2008). We test this hypothesis
using survey data that were collected in 2021 from line level of-
ficers and their supervisors in a state law enforcement agency,
as part of a study assessing the impacts of the 2020 community
protests following George Floyd's killing.
Consistent with prior research and stereotype threat theory,
our findings show that White officers expressed significantly
greater concerns about being perceived as racist in interactions
with minority community members than their non-­White coun-
terparts. Officers who reported greater stress in response to
the 2020 protests also reported elevated concerns about being
labeled as racist. Critically, these concerns were significantly as-
sociated with higher levels of emotional exhaustion, depersonal-
ization, and overall reports of burnout. In addition, the analysis
showed that efforts to protect and project a desirable self-­image
(Crocker and Canevello 2008; LaCosse and Plant 2020) partially
mediates these relationships. To our knowledge, this is the first
public management study to empirically link stereotype threat
and self-­focused behavior to elevated burnout among frontline
public sector workers, offering new insights into how concerns
about public perceptions can shape the stress and well-­being of
frontline workers.
Next, we review existing research on stigma and stereotype
threat and develop theoretical arguments linking stereotype
threat to elevated burnout among law enforcement officers. We
then describe the data and methods used to analyze the associ-
ation between stereotype threat and burnout. After presenting
our results, we discuss the implications of our findings for re-
search and for the management of identity-­related stress and the
promotion of officer well-­being.
2   |   Theory and Hypotheses
2.1   |   Stereotype Threat
As noted by Dovidio et al. (2010), Lippmann (1922) introduced
the term stereotype to describe the mental image that often
comes to mind when thinking about a particular social group.
Stereotypes are generalized beliefs or expectations about traits
associated with specific groups and play a central role in shaping
human judgment and decision-­making. While much of the re-
search in social psychology and public management has empha-
sized the perspective of the perceiver, Goffman's (1963) seminal
work Stigma shifts attention to the experiences of individuals
who are the targets of such stereotypes. Building on this foun-
dation—and drawing on earlier insights from Hughes  (1951)
on occupational taints—subsequent organizational research
on “dirty work” (Ashforth and Kreiner  1999, 2014; Kreiner
et al. 2006, 2022) offers valuable perspectives on how certain
lines of work become associated with moral, physical, and so-
cial taints, as well as the psychological and social consequences
for individuals who perform such work (e.g., sanitation workers,
morticians, welfare case workers, and prison guards). From this
perspective, even though police officers possess considerable

power and discretion, policing may be understood as a form of
“dirty work” due to its routine engagement with people involved
in criminal activity, frequent exposure to physical danger, and
the morally ambiguous tactics that some officers may employ in
the course of their duties (Mastracci 2022).
Because dirty work often involves moral, physical, and/or so-
cial taints (Ashforth and Kreiner  1999, 2014; Hughes  1951;
Kreiner et  al.  2006, 2022), individuals engaged in such work
may be particularly vulnerable to both stereotype threat and
stigmatization, especially when societal narratives reinforce the
stigmas associated with their occupation. Stigma involves the
devaluation of a person or group based on discredited charac-
teristics (Goffman 1963; Ashforth and Kreiner 1999; Link and
Phelan 2001). It often results in individuals being categorized as
members of a group that is broadly devalued or rejected by so-
ciety (Link and Phelan 2006). As such, stigma is experienced as
a threat to an individual's social identity, which can undermine
their self-­esteem (Ellemers 2008). This identity threat emerges
when individuals become aware of a negative stereotype asso-
ciated with their group and fear being perceived or evlatuated
through that lens. It can also arise when individuals worry that
their actions may inadvertently reinforce the stereotype (Steele
and Aronson  1995; Steele et  al.  2002). Put simply, stereotype
threat reflects anticipated stigma—the fear that one's behavior
will be seen as confirming a negative stereotype about a group
one is perceived to represent.
A substantial body of research in social psychology shows that
the fear of confirming a negative stereotype can trigger phys-
iological stress responses, negative emotions and thoughts,
and maladaptive coping strategies—ultimately impairing per-
formance in ways that seem to validate the very stereotype
being resisted (Schmader et al. 2008). However, most research
on stigma and stereotyping has focused on social categoriza-
tion based on individual characteristics—such as race, gender,
sexual orientation, disability status, and criminal history—ex-
amining their psychological and behavioral effects (Dovidio
et al. 2010; O'Donnell and Foran 2024; Link and Phelan 2001).
Additionally, this literature typically highlights the stigmatiza-
tion of individuals occupying low-­power, low-­status positions
(Link and Phelan 2001). Yet emerging scholarship reveals that
stigma can operate at multiple levels and that it can also affect
high-­status, high-­power groups (Chatterjee and Ryan  2020;
Kreiner et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2021). Even occupations with
significant power, such as law enforcement, can become stig-
matized and experience a crisis of legitimacy. In such contexts,
chronic exposure to negative societal and media narratives—
such as portrayals of the “racist cop”—can reinforce moral taint
and pose lasting threats to law enforcement officers' self-­concept
and psychological well-­being (Burke  2023; Chatterjee and
Ryan 2020; McCarthy and Murphy 2023; Trinkner et al. 2019).
We contend that understanding these chronic effects is essen-
tial, as they may erode line level officers' motivation and mental
health, strain their relationship with the community, and ulti-
mately undermine both organizational effectiveness and public
trust (Burke 2023).
In criminal justice research, stereotype threat has traditionally
been examined from the perspective of minority community
members (e.g., Najdowski 2023; Najdowski et al. 2015). However,
growing attention is being paid to the influence of stereotype
threat among law enforcement officers. Specifically, studies
show that officers concerned about being perceived as fitting
the “racist cop” stereotype may respond with increased support
for, or use of, force against Black civilians (Trinkner et al. 2019;
McCarthy et al. 2021). As suggested by Burke (2023), this re-
sponse may stem from heightened threat sensitivity, diminished
moral legitimacy, and reduced reliance on non-­coercive engage-
ment strategies. When officers experience concerns about their
self-­legitimacy and the amount of legitimacy granted to them by
individuals in the community, they may be more likely to inter-
pret the behavior of citizens they interact with as challenging
their moral or legal authority, thereby requiring the use of more
aggressive policing tactics to manage the encounter. In addition,
the increased cognitive load associated with enhanced self-­
monitoring and regulation may make it more difficult to inhibit
the implicit racial biases and stereotypes that may increase the
likelihood that they interpret interactions with Black civilians as
more threatening, requiring more aggressive responses. Despite
these important insights, limited research has explored how
chronic exposure to occupation-­based stereotype threat affects
officer well-­being.
2.2   |   Impact of the Racist Cop Stereotype on
Officer Burnout
Burnout, as defined by the World Health Organization, is a
complex syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that
has not been successfully managed. It is characterized by three
core components: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization,
and reduced personal accomplishment (Maslach et  al.  2001).
Emotional exhaustion refers to feelings of weariness, fatigue,
and a lack of emotional energy to cope with work demands,
often resulting in difficulty adapting to the work environment
(Maslach et  al.  2001). Depersonalization, the second compo-
nent, involves a sense of detachment from one's work or service
recipients. This response often arises from the interpersonal de-
mands of service work and the absence of reciprocal interaction
(Maslach 1976). Research on street-­level bureaucrats highlights
that such detachment can lead to negative, callous, or dehu-
manizing responses toward clients, particularly when workers
struggle to manage conflicting priorities and the emotional toll
of their roles (Brodkin 2011; Lipsky 1980; Maynard-­Moody and
Musheno 2003; Shim et al. 2017; Tummers et al. 2015). The final
component, reduced professional efficacy, reflects negative self-­
evaluations and doubts about one's ability to perform effectively
(Maslach et al. 2001).
Law enforcement work is highly taxing, and frontline officers
routinely encounter conditions that elevate their risk of psy-
chological strain (Violanti et al. 2017). Many factors contribute
to this heightened stress, including the physical dangers of the
job, demanding shift schedules, exposure to traumatic events,
and public scrutiny and hostility (Adams and Mastracci 2020;
Burke 2017; Violanti et al. 2017; Webster 2014). Moreover, front-
line officers often need to engage with citizens who may be
experiencing a crisis (Euwema et al. 2004). To respond effec-
tively to these situations, officers must exercise self-­regulation
and suppress their own emotions (Guy et al. 2008). The emo-
tional labor involved in law enforcement work can take a toll on

Research and theoretical models of stereotype threat identify
several mechanisms through which the “racist cop” stereotype
may contribute to burnout among law enforcement officers.
As discussed earlier, stereotype threat arises from the fear of
confirming a negative group stereotype and the anticipation
of being stigmatized by others (Ashforth and Kreiner  2014).
This threat is theorized to elicit heightened physiological and
emotional stress responses (Burke 2023; Schmader et al. 2008).
Empirical evidence supports this perspective, showing that
even members of high-­status groups experience increased anxi-
ety and physiological arousal when concerned about being per-
ceived as racist in interracial interactions (Trawalter et al. 2012).
Extensive research further demonstrates that chronic exposure
to stress in the workplace is a key predictor of employee burn-
out (Koutsimani et al. 2019). Taken together, these insights and
existing evidence suggest that concerns about stereotype confir-
mation can be a significant stressor for police officers, thereby
increasing their risk of burnout.
In addition to eliciting heightened physiological stress re-
sponses, stereotype threat increases individuals' vigilance to
cues that others may be perceiving them through a stereotype-­
consistent lens. This hypervigilance amplifies anxiety and
negative thoughts (Cadinu et al. 2005) and prompts greater ef-
forts to regulate those emotions and suppress stress responses
(Burke 2023; Schmader et al. 2008). Research shows that simply
being told one has high levels of prejudice can induce stereotype
threat (Goff et al. 2013), which in turn heightens self-­monitoring
(Monteith et al. 2002), depletes cognitive resources, and impairs
regulatory functioning (Richeson and Trawalter  2005). These
cognitive and emotional regulation demands can further tax
executive functions responsible for attention control, emotional
regulation, and goal-­directed behavior (Johns et al. 2008).
For frontline police officers, who already operate under high
cognitive load and stress (Swencionis and Goff 2017), the added
strain of stereotype threat can overwhelm their cognitive capac-
ity. This depletion has been empirically linked to increased phys-
iological stress responses (Amodio 2009) and risk of employee
burnout (Deligkaris et al. 2014). Consistent with these perspec-
tives, two studies provide direct empirical evidence linking
stereotype threat to elevated burnout in non–law-­enforcement
work contexts. In a diary study of female engineers, Hall
et al. (2015) found that interactions with male colleagues that
triggered social identity threat—specifically, concerns about
competence and professional belonging—were associated with
greater mental exhaustion and burnout. Similarly, Bedyńska
and Żołnierczyk-­Zreda (2015) showed that women's experiences
of stereotype threat at work led to heightened negative emotions
and higher levels of burnout. Building on these theoretical and
empirical insights, we test the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1.  Concerns about being perceived as racist will
be positively associated with burnout among law enforcement
officers.
Heightened concerns about being perceived as racist may
increase burnout among law enforcement officers through
multiple pathways. We examine one potential mechanism: of-
ficers' efforts to protect and promote self-­image goals at work.
Following LaCosse and Plant  (2020), we refer to this as self-­
focused behavior. As we noted previously, the “racist cop” ste-
reotype represents a form of identity threat to law enforcement
officers. When experiencing such threat, officers are likely to
take actions to restore or affirm a positive image and identity.
Steele et al. (2002) suggest that environmental cues threaten-
ing individuals' self-­concept often trigger heightened vigilance
and self-­monitoring aimed at protecting their social identities.
Consistent with this perspective, LaCosse and Plant (2020) show
that concerns about being perceived as prejudiced are associated
with increased efforts to protect and promote self-­image goals.
Self-­image goals are interpersonal goals—goals to “attain,
maintain or avoid specific end states for the partner relation-
ship” (Fitzsimons and Bargh  2003, 150)—and reflect individ-
uals' motivation to present a positive image of themselves to
others (Crocker et al. 2017). More broadly, interpersonal goals
include self-­focused as well as other-­focused goals (referred to
as self-­image and compassionate goals, respectively) (Crocker
et al. 2017). Such goals are central to self-­regulation and serve
as standards against which individuals monitor and adjust their
behavior (Bandura 1991). To achieve their self-­image goals, peo-
ple often behave in ways intended to construct, maintain, and
defend a public and private self-­image of competence, morality,
and good intention (Crocker and Canevello 2008; Canevello and
Crocker 2015). Although self-­image goals are moderately stable
motivational tendencies, the relative importance people assign
to such goals varies meaningfully within people across time
and across interaction partners (Canevello and Crocker  2015;
Crocker and Canevello 2008). Individuals are especially likely
to focus on protecting a desirable self-­image in professional con-
texts characterized by close evaluation and the threat of negative
judgment or stigmatization (Steele 1997). Moreover, these con-
cerns are likely to be particularly pronounced during interac-
tions between members of different social identity groups (Plant
and Devine 2003; LaCosse and Plant 2020).
Although self-­image protection and enhancement efforts stem
from a fundamental need for acceptance and belonging, they
can foster defensiveness when driven by fear of rejection, failure,
or moral disapproval (Crocker and Canevello 2008). In such in-
stances, individuals more often engage in heightened impression
management and self-­monitoring to shape how others perceive
them (Bergsieker et al. 2010). They may also distance themselves
from socially undesirable traits or group stereotypes (LaCosse
and Plant 2020). We contend that the increased vigilance and

For law enforcement officers, pervasive public narratives about
racial bias in law enforcement can threaten both their private
self-­concept as well as their public image. Anticipating that
others may view them through the lens of the “racist cop” ste-

to demonstrate—to themselves and to others—that they are not
prejudiced, intensifying their motivation to manage impressions
and avoid confirming the stereotype.
Although individual efforts to protect and promote a positive
self-­image serve important social functions, they can entail
significant psychological costs. Research shows that individu-
als attempting to present a positive self-­image are more likely
to perceive interpersonal interactions as threatening and
stressful, and relationships as zero-­sum in nature (Canevello
and Crocker  2017; Crocker et  al.  2009; Duarte and Pinto-­
Gouveia 2015). In interpersonal contexts where concerns about
being perceived as biased are salient, these dynamics can inten-
sify. Heightened anxiety about being misjudged can translate
into hypervigilance to cues that others see them as prejudiced,
and a heightened need to convince others that their negative as-
sessments are inaccurate. Studies by LaCosse and Plant (2020),
for example, show that White individuals more concerned about
appearing prejudiced are more likely to focus on self-­focused be-
haviors to avoid blame, rather than seeking to understand and
respect the needs of Black interaction partners. This stress can
undermine the quality of intergroup interactions and contribute
to increased emotional exhaustion and emotional distance from
others (Roos et al. 2022). Thus, we expect that concerns about
being perceived as racist will be indirectly associated with el-
evated burnout among officers through increased self-­focused
behaviors. Moreover, by heightening officers' need to manage
impressions and protect their self-­concept, stereotype threat
may drain emotional and cognitive resources, thereby acceler-
ating burnout.
Hypothesis 2.  Concerns about being perceived as racist will
be indirectly associated with burnout through increased self-­
focused behavior among law enforcement officers.
3   |   Data and Measures
3.1   |   Sample and Procedures
We test the two research hypotheses using data collected from
a 2021 survey administered to all sworn officers working in
a state-­level law enforcement organization. The organization
is in the Midwest region of the United States, and its primary
task is enforcing laws on state highways and roads. The or-
ganization also provides emergency response and support
services to city and county-­level police departments and is re-
sponsible for enforcing criminal laws on all state properties
and state institutions. Most of the line-­level/patrol officers
in the organization work in offices/units that are dispersed
throughout the state, covering both urban and rural areas,
and some officers work in specialized units and offices within
the state.
At the time of data collection, the organization had a total of
1498 sworn officers. The research team worked with the orga-
nization's human resource management staff to develop and
refine the survey instrument. Once the instrument was estab-
lished, the researchers sent an email to the sworn personnel
describing the purpose of the study and informed officers that
their participation was voluntary, and their responses would be
kept confidential. The law enforcement organization provided
the research team with a personnel list of employees along with
their email addresses, which the research team used to send the
email. One week after sending the initial message, the research
team distributed the survey to all sworn officers using Qualtrics.
The survey remained open for 3 weeks during which the re-
search team sent multiple reminder emails to increase response
rates. In total, 748 officers completed the survey with an overall
response rate of 49.9%. Ninety-­two of the 748 respondents were
officers with administrative/managerial responsibilities, and
because these police managers do not have any enforcement du-
ties, they were not included in this analysis. Of the remaining
656 respondents, 68% were patrol officers and 32% were line-­
level supervisors or managers. The racial and gender composi-
tion of the respondents (88% White,2 8% female) closely matched
the racial and gender composition of the officers working in the
organization (89% White, 7% female). The average age of respon-
dents was 39 years, while the average tenure in the organization
was 14.27 years. Forty-­two percent of the respondents had a
bachelor's or 4-­year college degree.
3.2   |   Dependent Variables
We use three measures to operationalize burnout. Burnout,
as we previously noted, is a multidimensional construct
(Maslach 1982), and emotional exhaustion and work deperson-
alization are considered two key components of burnout. In
our study, we measure officers' emotional exhaustion with four
items that capture feelings of being emotionally overextended
and drained by others. The items are “I feel emotionally drained
by my work,” “I feel fatigued when I have to get up and face
another day on the job,” “I feel used up at the end of my work,”
and “I feel burned out from my work.” The second outcome
variable, depersonalization, is measured with three items that
capture the development of cynical attitudes among police of-
ficers about their work and clients. The items are “I worry that
my job is hardening me emotionally,” “I have become more cal-
lous toward my service recipients,” and “I feel like I don't really
care what happens to my service recipients.” Both the emotional
exhaustion and depersonalization items come from Maslach's
burnout inventory and were measured on a five-­point response
scale (1 = never, 5 = very often). The internal reliability coeffi-
cients (Cronbach's alphas) of the two outcome measures are 0.84
and 0.75, respectively.
In addition to these two measures, our study includes a single-­
item measure of overall burnout. Specifically, the respondents

this measure, we can account for prior burnout levels, ensuring
a robust examination of the association between concerns about
being perceived as racist3 and officer burnout.
3.3   |   Primary Independent Variables
The primary predictor variable, concerns about being perceived/
labeled as racist or stereotype threat, was measured using three
survey items. These items were: “How much do you worry that
people you deal with on the job might misinterpret something
you do or say as racist?”; “How much do you worry about coming
across as racist when dealing with people from racial or ethnic
minority groups?”; and “How much do you worry that people
may perceive you as racist because you are a law enforcement
officer?” These items were adapted from a scale developed by
Trinkner et al. (2019) to measure chronic concerns associated
with the racist police officer stereotype. Responses were re-
corded on a five-­point scale: 1 = “I do not worry,” 2 = “I worry
a little,” 3 = “I worry somewhat,” 4 = “I worry a fair amount,”
and 5 = “I worry a great deal.” The measure demonstrated high
internal reliability, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.89.
The mediator variable, self-­focused behavior, was mea-
sured using three survey items adapted from Crocker and
Canevello's (2008) self-­image goals scale. Our measure captures
officers' efforts aimed at protecting and promoting their self-­
image at work and, therefore, should better reflect the stress
associated with self-­monitoring and behavioral regulation. This
approach is consistent with LaCosse and Plant's (2020) adapta-
tion of Crocker and Canevello's self-­image goals construct to as-
sess self-­focused behavioral intentions. Line-­level officers were
asked: “How much do you do or try to do the following while
at work: (1) avoid being blamed or criticized, (2) avoid showing
any weaknesses or vulnerabilities, and (3) avoid the possibility
of being wrong?” Responses were recorded on a five-­point scale
ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 5 (Extremely). The scale demon-
strated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.82).
3.4   |   Control Variables
We included several important factors in our analysis that may
be associated with both the predictor and outcome variables.
We included supervisor servant leadership behavior as a control
variable in our analysis given evidence supporting its relation-
ship to burnout (Eva et al. 2019; Perrier et al. 2025) as well as
the relevance of servant leadership in policing (Vito et al. 2011).
Servant leadership was measured using six items from the short
Servant Leadership Scale developed and validated by Liden
et al. (2008). Sample items from the scale include: “Puts your
best interests ahead of his or her own,” “Emphasizes the im-
portance of giving back to the community,” and “Makes your
career development a priority.” Responses were recorded on a
5-­point Likert scale ranging from 1 = “Strongly Disagree” to
5 = “Strongly Agree.” The Cronbach's alpha value of the servant
leadership scale was 0.89.
Research demonstrates that law enforcement officers' work mo-
tivation, stress, and burnout are significantly influenced by their
perceptions of the fairness of their organization's formal policies
and procedures (Trinkner et al. 2016). Furthermore, as organiza-
tional procedural fairness is positively associated with officers'
self-­legitimacy (Trinkner et al. 2016), it may also correlate with
stereotype threat. Therefore, we control officers' perceptions of
their organization's procedural fairness. Procedural fairness was
measured with five items in both the 2019 and 2021 surveys.
The items were taken from Trinkner et al. (2016). Sample items
are “Employees are recruited objectively regardless of their eth-
nicity, gender, race or religion,” and “Policies defining employee
misconduct are applied consistently across all employees.” The
Cronbach's alpha of the measure was 0.87.
The nationwide protests in 2020 against the killing of George
Floyd by police likely contributed to heightened stress among
law enforcement officers in the United States (Mourtgos
et al. 2022). As a watershed moment for law enforcement, the
protests may have also increased officer concerns about being
labeled as racists. Many officers working in the agency worked
the protests alongside local law enforcement. Hence, in our
analysis of the relationship between stereotype threat and offi-
cer burnout, we included protest stress appraisal. Protest stress
appraisal was measured with three items in the 2021 survey that
asked officers the extent to which they found the 2020 protests
to be “stressful”, “threatening”, and “challenging.” The items
were adapted from a scale developed by Frenkel et al. (2021) and
were measured with five response choices (1 = not at all, 5 = ex-
tremely). The Cronbach's alpha for this measure was 0.89.
Research suggests that public service motivation (PSM) is a
critical psychological resource that helps frontline public sec-
tor workers manage the heightened demands and stressors of
their jobs (Bakker 2015; Shim et al. 2017). PSM may also reduce
burnout among public employees by emphasizing the prosocial
impact of their work (Potipiroon and Faerman  2020; Wright
et al. 2025). Therefore, we included PSM as a control variable
in our analysis. PSM was measured using five items from the
2019 survey: “Meaningful public service is very important to
me,” “I am not afraid to stand up for the rights of others even
if it means I will be ridiculed,” “Making a difference in society
means more to me than personal achievements,” “I am prepared
to make enormous sacrifices for the good of society,” and “I am
reminded by daily events about how dependent we are on one
another.” This global measure of PSM has been widely used and
validated in prior research (Wright et al. 2013). Responses were
recorded on a five-­point Likert scale ranging from 1 = “Strongly

Disagree” to 5 = “Strongly Agree.” The measure demonstrated
acceptable internal reliability, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.74.
Finally, stereotype threat as well as burnout may be correlated
with officers' characteristics. Hence, our analysis controls for
sex, race, rank, education, and organization tenure in our anal-
ysis. Sex (female = 1, male = 0), race (nonwhite = 1, else = 0),
rank (line officer = 1, else = 0), and education (i.e., college de-
gree: yes = 1, no = 0) are each measured with a dummy variable.
Tenure in the organization (in years) is measured as a continu-
ous variable.
4   |   Analysis and Results
4.1   |   Preliminary Analysis
We conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess
construct validity of the study measures. Because overall burn-
out was measured with a single item, it was excluded from
the CFA. The measurement model showed acceptable fit, χ2
(358) = 940.66, RMSEA = 0.045, CFI = 0.948, TLI = 0.941. As re-
ported in Appendix A, all items loaded significantly (p < 0.05)
on their respective latent factors. Standardized factor loadings
(λ) for all items exceeded 0.50, and average variance extracted
(AVE) estimates for all factors except PSM were above 0.50; the
AVE score for PSM was 0.48. We assessed discriminant validity
of the measures using the Fornell and Larcker (1981) criterion.
For each construct, the square root of its AVE exceeded its cor-
relations with all other latent variables. Although work deper-
sonalization and emotional exhaustion, the two components
of burnout, as expected, were strongly correlated (r = 0.65), the
square root of their AVEs (0.73 and 0.84, respectively) remained
higher than this correlation, supporting discriminant validity
among all constructs. To further examine the discriminant va-
lidity of our stereotype threat and self-­focused behavior mea-
sures, we estimated an alternative measurement model that
included items for stereotype threat and self-­focused behavior
into one single factor. This alternative model fit the data worse,
χ2 (444) = 1431.40, RMSEA = 0.065, CFI = 0.899, TLI = 0.887,
and a sequential χ2 difference test confirmed that the proposed
measurement model provided a significantly better fit to the
data, suggesting the two measures empirically distinct.
Additionally, because both the predictor and outcome vari-
ables were collected using the same survey instrument, there
is a potential for common method bias in the data (Podsakoff
et  al.  2003). To assess this concern, we conducted Harman's
single-­factor test by estimating a measurement model in which
all items loaded onto a single latent factor. The single-­factor
model showed very poor fit, χ2 (464) = 7140.64, RMSEA = 0.160,
CFI = 0.316, TLI = 0.269, and SRMR = 0.157. A sequential χ2
difference test further indicated that our proposed multi-­factor
measurement model fit the data significantly better than the
single-­factor model. We also conducted a principal components
analysis (PCA) of all items to assess whether the first factor ex-
plained more than 50% of the total item variance in the data-
set indicating common method bias. The PCA results showed
that the first factor, with an eigenvalue of 7.1, accounted for 23%
of the total item variance. These results suggest that common
method bias is not a serious threat to our results.4
Table 1 displays the means and standard deviations of the study
measures, while Table 2 provides the correlation coefficients. As
indicated in Table 1, there is a significant difference in stereo-
type threat between nonwhite and white officers (M = 2.301 vs.
2.671, respectively, p < 0.05). This finding aligns with previous
research suggesting that white police officers experience greater
concerns about being labeled or perceived as racist compared
to nonwhite officers (Trinkner et  al.  2019). Significant differ-
ences were also observed in two of the three burnout measures
(p < 0.05), with white officers reporting higher levels of emo-
tional exhaustion (M = 3.375 vs. 3.108) and depersonalization
(M = 2.869 vs. 2.476) compared to their nonwhite counterparts.
Additionally, nonwhite officers perceived higher levels of pro-
cedural fairness within their organization than white officers
(M = 2.521 vs. 2.066, p < 0.01).
As shown in Table 2, we found a positive association between
stereotype threat and emotional exhaustion (r = 0.27, p < 0.01),
work depersonalization (r = 0.32, p < 0.01), and overall burnout
(r = 0.24, p < 0.01). Stereotype threat was associated with self-­
focused behavior (r = 0.39, p < 0.01), which was also positively
correlated with all three measures of burnout (rs = 0.28, 0.23,
and 0.21, for exhaustion, depersonalization, and overall burnout,
respectively; p < 0.01). Furthermore, stereotype threat was posi-
tively associated with protest stress appraisal (r = 0.28, p < 0.01),
indicating that officers who found the 2020 community pro-
tests more stressful also reported greater concerns about being
labeled or perceived as racist by community members. Finally,
emotional exhaustion and depersonalization are strongly cor-
related with the single-­item overall burnout measure (rs = 0.73
and 0.49, respectively, p < 0.01).
4.2   |   Multivariate Analysis
We employed Multilevel Modeling (MLM), also known as
Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM), to test our hypotheses.
Officers in our study were assigned to units that are geograph-
ically dispersed across the state. As a result, officers within the
same unit may experience similar job stressors and environ-
mental conditions, potentially violating the independence as-
sumption required for Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression
(Nezlek  2008). Moreover, officers' primary duty locations are
likely correlated with the racial composition of the communities
they routinely interact with (Roh and Robinson  2009), which
may influence their exposure to the “racist police officer” stereo-
type and, in turn, their levels of burnout. For these reasons, it is
essential to account for unit-­level variation in the analysis. MLM
appropriately addresses these concerns by modeling variability
across units while estimating the effects of stereotype threat and
self-­focused behavior on officer burnout. The intercepts of our
null (empty) models were statistically significant (p < 0.01), indi-
cating that scores on all three burnout dimensions—as well as
self-­focused behavior—varied across units. This between-­unit
variation supports the use of MLM as the appropriate analyti-
cal strategy. Model specification and interpretation followed the
guidelines outlined by Raudenbush and Bryk (2002).
We hypothesized that concerns about being perceived as racist
would be positively associated with burnout, positively associ-
ated with self-­focused behavior, and that self-­focused behavior,

Following Preacher and Hayes's (2004) guidelines for estimat-

enhance interpretability. We estimated two models: the first in-

who reported greater concerns about being perceived as racist
engaged in more self-­focused behavior at work. Specifically, a
one–standard deviation increase in stereotype threat score was
associated with a 0.347–standard deviation increase in self-­
focused behavior score (p < 0.01).
We estimated three multilevel regression models for each of the
burnout measures. The results of this analysis are reported in
Tables 4–6. As shown in the tables, Model 1 included only the
control variables; Model 2 added the main predictor, stereotype
threat; and Model 3 introduced the proposed mediator, self-­
focused behavior. Because overall burnout was measured using
a single item with ordinal response categories, we also estimated
multilevel ordered logit models to assess the robustness of the
associations. These results, reported in Appendix B, are consis-
tent with those reported in the main analysis below.
Our findings provide empirical support for the hypothesized
relationship between stereotype threat and burnout. After con-
trolling for officer characteristics (sex, race, rank, education, and
tenure), PSM, perceptions of procedural fairness, supervisor ser-

threat remained positively associated with emotional exhaus-
tion, work depersonalization, and overall burnout. As shown in
Model 2 of Tables 4–6, the standardized regression coefficients
for stereotype threat were 0.193 (p < 0.01), 0.269 (p < 0.01), and
0.196 (p < 0.01), respectively. These results suggest that stereo-
type threat is more strongly associated with work depersonal-
ization than with emotional exhaustion.

causal relationships with cross-­sectional survey data, the results
highlight anticipated stigma and self-­focused behavior as cor-

These findings point to the broader toll that heightened public
scrutiny and criticism of law enforcement during and after the
nationwide protests in 2020 may have taken on officers' psy-
chological well-­being. This is particularly concerning given
the well-­established link between burnout and declines in em-
ployee performance, increased turnover, and adverse health
outcomes (Andreescu and Vito 2021; Baek et al. 2021; Hassan
et  al. Forthcoming; Rief and Clinkinbeard  2021). Thus, our
results also provide some indirect insights into increases in
officer resignations and retirements, as well as the result-
ing staffing shortages reported in police departments across
the United States (Adams et al. 2023; Mourtgos et al. 2022).
Together, these findings underscore the urgent need for law
enforcement agencies to adopt practices that not only support
their workforce but also rebuild public trust and address the
criticisms raised during the 2020 Black Lives Matter (BLM)
protests following the murder of George Floyd. At the same
time, persistent evidence of racial disparities in policing prac-
tices in the country (Ba et al. 2021; Epp et al. 2017; Goncalves
and Mello  2021; Hoekstra and Sloan  2022; Headley and
Wright 2020; Shoub 2022) underscores the continued urgency
of advancing racial equity and accountability in law enforce-
ment organizations.
Although not central to our main hypothesis, our analyses
yielded several additional noteworthy findings. White officers
reported higher levels of stress related to the BLM protests, as
well as greater emotional exhaustion and depersonalization
than non-­white officers. While stereotype threat can affect indi-
viduals regardless of race, one possible explanation for this pat-
tern is that white officers may have faced more frequent public
accusations of racism during this period, or they may have expe-
rienced threats to two salient identities—being both white and
police officers. Additionally, consistent with prior research, our
findings highlight the importance of supportive leadership (Eva
et al. 2019; Perrier et al. 2025; Potipiroon and Faerman 2020)
and organizational procedural fairness (Trinkner et al. 2016) in
buffering against the negative effects of stereotype threat and
burnout.
While our findings align with prior theoretical work (e.g.,
Schmader et al. 2008) and are supported by a small but grow-
ing empirical literature (e.g., Bedyńska and Żołnierczyk-­
Zreda  2015; Hall et  al.  2015), the study is not without
limitations. First, the use of cross-­sectional survey data re-
stricts our ability to draw causal inferences between stereo-
type threat and burnout measures. Second, our study was
limited in its ability to control for variation in workload or
the frequency of officer-­citizen interactions that may trigger
stereotype threat. As noted previously, officer workload and
negative officer-­citizen interactions were likely higher than
normal for all officers given the stresses due to the COVID-­19
pandemic and the nationwide protests for racial justice re-
form. Third, the data were collected from a single state-­level
law enforcement agency, which may limit the generalizability
of our results. Notably, many officers in our sample worked
during the 2020 racial justice protests, and a primary motiva-
tion for administering the survey was the agency's interest in
understanding how the protests and subsequent criticism af-
fected its officers. Nevertheless, relationships between stereo-
type threat and burnout may differ in urban law enforcement
settings that involve more frequent community interactions
or are subject to greater scrutiny due to high-­profile negative
incidents. More research, particularly longitudinal and com-
parative studies, is needed to replicate these findings and to
examine whether the mechanisms linking stereotype threat to
burnout vary across law enforcement work contexts.
Additionally, our findings suggest that self-­focused behavior is
one potential pathway linking concerns about being perceived
as racist to elevated burnout among law enforcement officers.
However, we can't rule out a bidirectional relationship where of-
ficers who are chronically motivated to protect or enhance their
self-­image experience heightened concern about being labeled
as racist when interacting with minority community members,
which in turn could reinforce stereotype threat and further con-
tribute to burnout. It is difficult to disentangle the directionality
of these relationships or rule out reciprocal influences without
a longitudinal or experimental research design. Importantly,
however, our finding that self-­focused behavior is consistently
associated with elevated burnout in all regression models, and
that the association between stereotype threat and burnout re-
mains statistically significant even after controlling for the de-
sire to protect self-­image, suggests that both are related to officer
burnout.
Furthermore, our study did not examine all potential mech-
anisms linking stereotype threat to employee burnout. One
important factor not considered here is emotional labor (Guy
et al. 2008; Mastracci 2022). Frontline law enforcement offi-
cers are frequently portrayed in the media as public agents
who misuse their authority and enforce laws in racially biased
ways. Like stereotype threat, emotional labor involves height-
ened monitoring and regulation of emotional expression to con-
form to occupational expectations (Mastracci 2022). Officers
attempting to manage negative occupational stigma may try to
counteract public perceptions by displaying positive emotions
and/or suppressing authentic responses (Rivera  2015). Such
emotion regulation can deplete psychological resources and
generate negative emotional states that contribute to burnout
(Brotheridge and Lee 2003; Humphrey 2023). While our study
was unable to directly test the mechanisms by which stereo-
type threat may increase burnout due to emotional labor, the
self-­focused behaviors studied here are similar to surface act-
ing in their focus on presenting a positive image to others as
opposed to strengthening their image by understanding and
addressing the expectations of others. Recent research, for
example, has found that occupational stigma may increase
employee emotional exhaustion by increasing employee sur-
face acting and decreasing deep acting (Miao and Guo 2025).
Future research should therefore examine whether emotional
labor mediates the relationship between “racist cop” stereo-
type threat and burnout among line-­level officers. It is also
important for future studies to consider individual and contex-
tual factors that may strengthen or weaken this relationship.
For example, officers with a stronger moral identity (Aquino
and Reed II 2002) may react more strongly to threats to their
self-­concept as well-­intentioned, moral public servants when
exposed to the “racist cop” stereotype. Future research should
also examine individual, organizational, and situational con-
ditions that may heighten the salience of the “racist cop” ste-
reotype in officers' day-­to-­day work.

Given the potential negative influence of the “racist cop” ste-
reotype on officer well-­being suggested by the results of our
study, as well as its potential negative impact on police–citizen
relations (K. C. Burke 2023; Trinkner et al. 2019), law enforce-
ment leaders and agency administrators should consider imple-
menting practices that reduce self-­image burdens and foster a
supportive, identity-­safe organizational climate. Although prior
research has identified strategies to mitigate stereotype threat
(Liu et al. 2021), most of this work has focused on threats tied
to individuals' social identities (e.g., race or gender) and their
performance outcomes. Further research is needed to assess
whether such interventions are equally effective in addressing
the long-­term psychological consequences of chronic occupa-
tional stereotype threats.
Recent studies suggest that low-­cost interventions aimed at
enhancing social support and belonging can reduce employee
burnout and associated stressors (Allgood et al. 2024; Linos
et al. 2022; Shay and Rauhaus 2025). Yet these strategies may
be less effective in countering the “racist cop” stereotype be-
cause they emphasize strengthening officers' relationships
with coworkers and reinforcing identification with the orga-
nization and occupation. The stress and fears associated with
this stereotype, however, are rooted primarily in interactions
and relationships with individuals outside the organization.
Strong workplace relationships can indeed help reduce stress
when coworkers affirm officers' value, provide emotional sup-
port, and assist with work-­related tasks, while also bolster-
ing organizational and occupational identification (Allgood
et al. 2024; Kurtessis et al. 2017; Linos et al. 2022). However,
reinforcing these identities may also heighten officers' sensi-
tivity to the “racist cop” stereotype when interacting with the
public (Burke 2023; Mastracci 2022). This is consistent with
research showing that stereotype threat is often most acute
when an individual's self-­concept and identity are closely
tied to the group or behavior being negatively stereotyped
(Steele 1997).
Our finding that self-­focused behavior partially mediates the
relationship between stereotype threat and burnout suggests
new potential ways to lower stereotype threat and burnout. As
noted previously, research on interpersonal goals typically ex-
amines two types of interpersonal goals: self-­image and com-
passionate goals (Crocker et al. 2017). While self-­image goals
are more self-­oriented and describe the desire to construct,
maintain, and defend positive images of oneself, compassion-
ate goals are more other-­oriented and driven by the desire to
build a supportive environment characterized by feelings of
closeness and connection to others. In direct contrast to self-­
image goals, compassionate goals see interactions in nonzero-­
sum ways such that what is good for one person can be good
for others and vice versa (Canevello and Crocker  2020).
Although self-­image and compassionate goals can represent
competing desires and strategies, research suggests that indi-
viduals have both types of goals and the strength of these two
types of impersonal goals can even be positively correlated
(Canevello and Crocker 2010; Crocker and Canevello 2008).
Research also suggests that people with stronger compassion-
ate goals are not only more supportive and responsive to their
relationship partners but also experience more trusting and
higher quality relationships because their partners reciprocate
with greater support in return (Crocker et al. 2017). Thus, en-
couraging officers to focus on compassionate goals may help
reduce the stress associated with the racist cop stereotype
by redirecting the focus of public facing interactions away
from concerns about appearing prejudiced and toward im-
proving the quality of both the interaction and relationship.
Individuals with stronger compassionate goals tend to expe-
rience increased social support, self-­esteem, and belonging
(Canevello and Crocker 2017) as well as lower levels of social
anxiety, stress, and depression (Crocker et  al.  2010; Duarte
and Pinto-­Gouveia 2015; Erickson et al. 2018).
Although compassion-­based interventions have shown promise
in reducing employee stress and burnout (Martins et al. 2025)
and may encourage officers to adopt compassionate goals
in public interactions, most evidence for their effectiveness
comes from education and healthcare settings. More research
is needed to determine whether such interventions are effective
in policing contexts. By contrast, stronger evidence currently
supports the use of mindfulness interventions in law enforce-
ment (Vadvilavičius et al. 2023). While compassion-­based ap-
proaches may more directly align with compassionate goals,
mindfulness interventions have not only been shown to reduce
burnout and stress among officers (Vadvilavičius et al. 2023) but
also to foster attributes associated with compassionate goals,
including greater compassion (Lim et al. 2015), empathy (Han
and Kim  2020), prosocial helping intentions (van der Schans
et al. 2024), and a greater tendency to withhold premature judg-
ments about others (van der Schans et al. 2024; Vadvilavičius
et al. 2023).
Another potential strategy to reduce the stress associated with
the “racist cop” stereotype is to reframe the professional role and
identity of the police in ways that minimize this negative stereo-
type (Mastracci 2022). For example, agencies can cultivate a cul-
ture that emphasizes a guardian rather than a warrior identity.
Officers who identify as warriors see themselves as crime fighters
in a perpetual battle to preserve order, treating every interaction
as a potential threat to society and their safety (Stoughton 2014,
2016). As a result, they are more likely to endorse the use of force
and aggressive tactics, which can reinforce the “racist cop” ste-
reotype. By contrast, officers who adopt a guardian identity view
themselves as serving and protecting the public by building trust
and strong community relationships, recognizing that their au-
thority rests on public consent. Research shows that such of-
ficers are more supportive of procedural justice, community
partnerships, and de-­escalation strategies, and that a stronger
guardian identity can buffer against stereotype threat (Murphy
and McCarthy 2023; McLean et al. 2020). Importantly, however,
police training and early socialization often promote the warrior
identity by emphasizing that “in order to be guardians, they must
first be warriors” (Simon 2023, 1022). Agencies should therefore
carefully evaluate and adjust training and socialization strate-
gies to minimize the warrior identity and strengthen the guard-
ian identity. Notably, the guardian identity shares many features
with compassionate goals, particularly its emphasis on empathy,
communication, and collaboration. Incorporating mindfulness
and compassion-­based training into early career development
may not only reduce officers' self-­focused behavior, stereotype
threat, and burnout but also reinforce their guardian identity
while weakening their warrior identity.

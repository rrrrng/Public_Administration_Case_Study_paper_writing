ABSTRACT
A central aim of contracting is to introduce competition into public service delivery. Public organizations are facing calls to
generate more competition for their contracts, yet there is conflicting evidence about the effects of competition in practice. This
research aims to explain some of those mixed findings by investigating the effects of different levels of competition on contract
management. It examines two crucial elements of contract management that previous literature has shown can substantially in-
fluence performance outcomes: flexibility and monitoring. In highly contested markets, relationships are likely to be shorter and
the payoffs from more restrictive styles of management are higher, thereby encouraging managers to be less flexible and monitor
more. Findings, from an online survey experiment with 576 public managers, indicate that high competition does indeed lead to
less flexibility and more monitoring, while low competition leads to less intense monitoring.
1   |   Introduction
Introducing market pressures into public services was central to
early arguments for contracting (Hood  1991). Competition be-
tween suppliers should promote efficiency and innovation and
the threat of replacement encourages the incumbent to perform
(Niskanen 1971; Osborne and Gaebler 1992; Savas 1987). Recently
a renewed focus on competition for government contracts has led
to calls for more competition in public procurement from interna-
tional organizations (European Commission 2016; OECD 2019)
and national governments, such as in the Netherlands (Ministry
of Economic Affairs and Climate Policy 2021), Sweden (Ministry
of Finance 2017), and the UK (Cabinet Office 2020). The UK gov-
ernment even claim that increasing competition could generate
savings of £7.7 billion (Cabinet Office 2022).
Given the political pressure to inject more competition into con-
tracting markets, it is important to understand what the impli-
cations are for contract management. Problems associated with
low competition are well understood. They include a lack of
accountability (Johnston and Romzek 2023), over-­dependence
on suppliers (Greasley  2019), and corruption risks (Fazekas
and Kocsis 2020). However, the effects of increasing competi-
tion are less clear, as empirical evidence is mixed. Some studies
find positive effects on cost (Lindholst et al. 2018) and quality
(Holum 2018; Rho 2013), but others have found no effect (Broms
et al. 2020; Lamothe and Lamothe 2010) or even negative effects
(Brunjes 2020; Brunjes 2022; Carril et al. 2022; Fernandez 2007)
on performance.
This article aims to illuminate this conflicting evidence by in-
vestigating an intermediate step linking competition to perfor-
mance: contract management. Public managers' interactions
with suppliers can have a substantial impact on how suppli-
ers perform (Andrews et  al.  2015; Provost and Esteve  2016).
Management approaches, such as the level of monitoring, can
also significantly influence the transaction costs, and therefore
the overall efficiency, of contracting (Johnston and Romzek 2010;
Petersen et al. 2019). This is particularly true when contracts are
complex because public managers must decide how to specify
and monitor elements of public services that are challenging to
describe and measure (Brown et al. 2010a).

Evidence about management responses to low competition
abounds (Brown and Potoski 2004; Girth et al. 2012; Romzek
et al. 2012; Walker et al. 2006), but we know much less about
the influence of high competition. This is understandable, as
governments more often struggle with too little than too much
competition (Girth et al. 2012; Kang and Miller 2022). Yet, in
the context of persistent calls to increase competition and evi-
dence that competition may worsen performance (Brunjes 2020;
Brunjes  2022; Fernandez  2007), understanding the effects of
high competition is important. Existing research suggests that
competition makes suppliers less financially stable (Romzek
and Johnston  2005), prevents economies of scale (Wang and
San Miguel 2013), and overloads managers awarding contracts
(Walker et al. 2006). This article contributes a further explana-
tion that competition can harm performance through its effect
on how public managers manage contracts.
The research examines two aspects of contract management that
previous research suggests are likely to influence performance:
the level of flexibility suppliers have; and how closely they are
monitored. Giving suppliers more flexibility enables them to
experiment, innovate, and adapt to changing circumstances
(Ball and Gibson 2022; Bredgaard and Larsen 2008; Considine
et al. 2011; Malatesta and Smith 2014). Meanwhile, the inten-
sity of monitoring and the burdens it places on the supplier can
undermine goodwill, sap intrinsic motivation, and harm the
quality of their work (Frey 1993; Lambright 2009; Poppo and
Zenger 2002; Van Slyke 2007). Finally, both flexibility and mon-
itoring influence the transaction costs of contracting. The re-
sources spent writing and enforcing detailed specifications or
collecting and analyzing extensive performance information
can undermine the efficiency of contracting (Brown and Potoski
2005; Johnston and Romzek 2010; Petersen et al. 2019).
The article presents a theory that when competition is high, con-
tract managers will control suppliers' behavior more tightly—
through less flexibility and more monitoring—and that when
competition is low, they will allow suppliers more freedom and
monitor them less intensively. The rationale for this argument
is that competition causes more frequent changes of supplier
(Brown and Potoski 2004; Lamothe and Lamothe 2010). Shorter
relationships incentivize standardization over flexibility, and
the availability of alternative suppliers increases the payoffs
from monitoring by enabling managers to act on performance
information (Girth  2014; Lamothe and Lamothe  2012). The
theory applies when contracts are complex and where public
managers are required to subject contracts to competitive bid-
ding at regular intervals. For complex contracts, the scope for
managers to alter the level of flexibility they give suppliers and
vary their monitoring intensity is greater (Brown et al. 2010a).
The requirement to open contracts to competition means man-
agers cannot easily protect relationships with suppliers from
market pressures.
The article presents data from a preregistered online survey
experiment with 576 public managers, in which information
about competition—operationalized as the number of bids,
closeness of the award decision, and the supplier's threat of re-
placement—is randomly manipulated through a vignette. Three
of the article's four hypotheses find support. High competition
leads public managers to expect less flexibility in how suppliers
deliver a contract and closer monitoring, while low competition
leads managers to anticipate less monitoring.
The findings advance our understanding of competition's in-
fluence on government contract management and thus the
performance of contracted services. Its primary contribution,
in contrast to previous studies, is to examine different levels
of competition. Past research has either assessed the impact of
using competitive procurement procedures in comparison to in-­
house provision or direct awards, or has only explored the im-
plications of low competition for contract management (Brown
and Potoski 2004; Girth et al. 2012; Romzek et al. 2012; Walker
et al. 2006). The few studies that do capture different levels of
competition within a market (Broms et al. 2020; Brunjes 2020;
Carril et al. 2022) look at effects on overall performance rather
than on contract management.
By examining the management implications of high as well as
low competition, the article exposes a possible link in the chain
between competition and performance. The finding that highly
contested markets lead public managers to adopt a more rigid
approach to contract management may explain why competi-
tion has not always had the expected benefits. Previous research
has shown that such approaches can undermine the gains from
contracting (Fuertes and Lindsay  2016; Petersen et  al.  2019;
Tomkinson 2016). By focusing on complex contracts, the article
also illuminates evidence that competition is beneficial for sim-
ple but not complex public services (Carril et al. 2022; Petersen
et al. 2015).
The findings are a caution to policymakers who call for in-
creased competition for public contracts. They indicate that,
as governments attempt to stimulate contracting markets, they
must pay attention to changes in management styles. If more
competition encourages approaches that undermine efficiency
or other benefits of contracting, like agility, cost-­efficiency, or
access to expertise, evermore competitive markets may not be
the panacea governments believe them to be.

The article adopts the narrower idea of contract management as
the management of a contract between a buyer and a supplier,
rather than management of contracting in general. The research
responds to recent calls to increase competition for government
contracts. Consequently, it is concerned with competition's ef-
fect on public managers' behavior when managing individual
contracts. Decisions about when and how to contract out and
efforts to manage contracting markets are themselves part of
the drive to increase competition. From the perspective of this
study, they come before the independent variable of competition
for a government contract, not as part of the dependent variable
of contract management.
There is a large body of literature on the impact of competition
on other parts of the contracting process, such as the decision to
contract out (Alonso et al. 2016; Hefetz and Warner 2012), con-
tract design and structure (Kim et al. 2016; Torfing et al. 2017),
and the design of procurement processes (Coviello and
Mariniello 2014; Girth et al. 2012). This study extends that liter-
ature by examining the impact of competition on the way public
managers manage contracts once they are signed.
Within this definition of contract management, the research
focuses on two elements: the flexibility granted to suppliers in
how they deliver a contract, and how closely public managers
monitor their behavior. Flexibility and monitoring correspond
to the two key challenges posed by complex contracts, which are
a perennial problem for government. As Brown et al. (2010a) set
out, “complex products […] cannot be easily and clearly spelled
out in advance and are difficult to verify after the product has
been delivered” (12). Flexibility describes how a buyer spells
out what a supplier must deliver. Monitoring describes how the
buyer verifies that the supplier has delivered it.
The difficulties posed by complex contracts are the reasons
flexibility and monitoring are linked to contract performance.
Complexity creates a need for flexibility because not all tasks
or eventualities can be predicted and included in the written
contract (Malatesta and Smith 2014). Governments may benefit
from leaving room for suppliers to use their expertise to help
them define what is needed or respond to changing contexts
(Considine et al. 2011; Malatesta and Smith 2014). Complexity
also raises transaction costs because writing detailed specifi-
cations for complex tasks and confirming that those tasks have
been completed to the required standard is difficult and expen-
sive (Johnston and Romzek 2010; Petersen et al. 2019; Potoski
et al. 2025). Intensive monitoring may also be riskier when ser-
vices are complex because it can harm relationships with suppli-
ers (Poppo and Zenger 2002; Van Slyke 2007), which are needed
to align goals and navigate uncertainty (Brown et  al.  2010b;
Chuang et  al.  2020). Flexibility and monitoring are therefore
promising candidates to explain how competition shapes con-
tract performance when services are complex.
The theory put forward here is unlikely to apply to simple ser-
vices, because the same pressures on contract management are
not present. When buyers' requirements are easy to define, there
is little reason not to write tightly specified contracts—indeed,
it would be hard to write a vague one. When quality is easy to
measure, the information needed to assess performance can be
easily acquired, so there is less danger of rising transaction costs
or harming relationships.
The theory and findings are still likely to apply to many gov-
ernment contracts, as they are frequently complex (Brown
et al. 2010a). The nature of government work and public services
is inherently complex. The values, goals, accountability systems,
and stakeholder pressures that government organizations operate
under are all more complex than those of their private sector coun-
terparts (Boye et al. 2024). Even initially simple services like clean-
ing can become complex in a public sector context when linked to
the organization's complex goals (Elkomy et al. 2019). Contracted
services are also becoming ever more complex as low-­hanging fruit
are picked and the practice becomes politically neutral (Petersen
et al. 2015). Finally, the calls for increased competition that moti-
vate this article are likely to affect complex service domains more
than simple ones, as they are where governments most struggle to
attract competition (Brown et al. 2010a; Van Slyke 2003).
2.1   |   Flexibility
Public managers may tightly constrain how suppliers complete
contracted tasks or offer them considerable flexibility. They can do
this through the formal contract specification—using terms that
are very detailed or open to negotiation—and how they enforce
it—following the contract to the letter or allowing some deviation
(Lamothe and Lamothe 2012). Flexibility influences contract per-
formance because the freedom a supplier has influences how they
can adapt and apply their expertise. Standard procedures constrain
suppliers' ability to experiment, modify delivery methods based
on their experience, or adapt to variations in service users' needs
(Fuertes and Lindsay 2016; Rice et al. 2018; Tomkinson 2016).
Stimulating innovation and adaptation in public services was
one of contracting's original goals. Proponents expected suppli-
ers with domain-­specific expertise and more dynamic working
practices to produce services that were better quality and more
agile and responsive (Hood 1991; Osborne and Gaebler 1992).
Contracting did not deliver on this promise but instead produced
greater standardization (Bredgaard and Larsen 2008; Considine
et al. 2011; Considine et al. 2020; Fuertes and Lindsay 2016),
primarily driven by restrictive contract specifications and
management (Considine et al. 2011; Fuertes and Lindsay 2016;
Tomkinson 2016).
Where contracting has managed to produce agile and innovative
public services, the freedom governments give their suppliers
has been a key determinant of success (Ball and Gibson  2022;
Bredgaard and Larsen 2008; Considine et al. 2011; Malatesta and
Smith  2014). Suppliers that are given more autonomy are also
more satisfied and committed to the contracting relationship, fur-
ther enhancing their performance (Chuang et al. 2020). As well as
improving performance by enabling innovation, adaptation, and
greater supplier commitment, flexibility can reduce transaction

are (Boye et al. 2024), a simpler, more open contract can be much
cheaper to write and enforce and saves making costly modifica-
tions when circumstances change (Petersen et al. 2019).
We might assume that suppliers from competitive markets
would be given more flexibility than those appointed without
much competition. Managers might expect those suppliers to be
more competent, so allow them more scope to apply their ex-
pertise (Brown and Potoski 2004; Moon and Welch 2000). This
article presents an opposing argument that competition under-
mines stable buyer–supplier relationships, which are needed to
manage the risks that flexibility poses.
While flexible contract management has many benefits, it also
comes with risks. Along with scope for suppliers to bring their
expertise to bear comes scope for them to pursue their own in-
terests. Considine et al. (2011) find that standardization in con-
tracted services was driven by “firms us[ing] the flexibility they
enjoyed under early contracts to undermine key public policy
goals in the interests of increasing their profits” (826).
Because of the inherent risks, more flexible styles of contract man-
agement require both sides to develop trust, shared goals, and com-
mon working practices (Amirkhanyan et al. 2012; Lamothe and
Lamothe 2012; Young et al. 2021). Building a relationship that can
withstand the risks that come with flexibility takes time. Brown
et al. (2018) emphasize the importance of repeated low-­stakes in-
teractions in which both sides can build confidence that the other
will not exploit them when given the opportunity. Expectations
that the relationship will continue are also important to persuade
both parties that the initial effort to build a relationship is worth-
while (Brown et al. 2007). For a supplier, it could be going beyond
the letter of the contract, and for a buyer, it could be providing a
supplier with additional support. Investing in the relationship only
makes sense when buyers and suppliers can expect to work to-
gether for a long time because such investments have time to bear
fruit (Grafton and Mundy 2017; Poppo et al. 2008).
As well as creating the conditions for investment in relation-
ships, low supplier turnover requires that parties build strong
relationships. When governments expect long relationships
with suppliers, perhaps because of weak markets, fostering trust
and cooperation becomes even more important because they
cannot change suppliers if the relationship sours. Whether out
of choice or necessity, the result is the same: expectations that
the relationship will continue encourage buyers and suppliers
to develop trust and cooperation that in turn facilitate flexi-
ble contract management (Bertelli and Smith 2010; Poppo and
Zenger 2002).
In contrast, competition reduces the stability of buyer–sup-
plier relationships (Fernandez 2009; Smith and Lipsky 1993;
Romzek and Johnston  2002). A core feature of competition,
and the primary reason for introducing it into public services,
is the threat of replacement (Savas 1987). The more suppliers
there are in a market and the closer they are in quality, the
greater the chance that the incumbent will be replaced. For
this reason, buyer–supplier relationships are shorter in more
competitive markets (Brown and Potoski 2004; Lamothe and
Lamothe 2009, 2010).
Governments may not even be able to protect high-­performing
incumbents from market pressures. Procurement rules mean
that managers do not have complete discretion over contract
awards. If another supplier gets a higher score on the formal
criteria or submits a lower bid, government buyers are often
obliged to switch suppliers rather than preserve their relation-
ship with the incumbent (Carril et al. 2022). While governments
may be able to renew low-­value contracts without competition,
procurement regulations usually require them to open higher-­
value contracts to competition at regular intervals and restrict
when they can be extended without competition.1 Complex con-
tracts are likely to fall above the thresholds set by these regula-
tions, as they tend to be larger and of higher value than simple
contracts (Bajari et al. 2009; Brown et al. 2018; Lamothe and
Lamothe 2009).
One might suppose that more competition means public man-
agers are able to select the best supplier and thereby ensure the
best performance. However, for complex services, that assump-
tion often does not hold in practice. When services are complex,
procurement criteria are as difficult to define as the contracts
themselves (Brown et  al.  2010a, 2010b), so bidding processes
are just as open to gaming as the resulting contracts. It can be
hard to tell whether a superior bid will translate into superior
performance in delivering the service. Meanwhile, the govern-
ment forgoes the benefits of allowing the incumbent supplier
the freedom to adapt and innovate and may have sapped their
commitment. Even if the incumbent does win the next contract,
merely the threat of replacement may already have had a damp-
ening effect on their investment in the relationship (Smith and
Lipsky 1993). As Romzek and Johnston (2002) note, competition
makes contract management simpler in theory, but in reality,
parties need stability to “cushion the unforeseen impacts” of
contracting for complex services (449).
To summarize, shorter relationships in competitive markets will
encourage public managers to grant suppliers less flexibility.
When competition is low, public managers have more reason to
adopt a flexible approach because investments needed to facili-
tate flexibility have time to pay off, and they are more dependent
on that relationship. When it is high, the instability of those re-
lationships inhibits the trust needed for flexibility.
H1a.  Buyers will give suppliers more flexibility when competi-
tion is low.
H1b.  Buyers will give suppliers less flexibility when competition
is high.
2.2   |   Monitoring
Gathering information about a supplier's behavior is an import-
ant tool for contract managers to curb opportunism and ensure
suppliers execute work to the required standard. Monitoring both
puts pressure on suppliers to perform well and detects when they
do not (Brown and Potoski 2005; Barthélemy and Quélin 2006).
Insufficient monitoring can lead performance to deteriorate and
costs to rise (Park and Moon 2023). Monitoring thus has a major
impact on performance when it allows governments to respond
to problems and incentivizes suppliers to perform well.

Yet monitoring comes at a price. Gathering and analyzing
performance information is resource-­intensive (Girth  2014;
Van Slyke 2007), especially when quality is difficult to verify
(Johnston and Romzek 2023; Petersen et al. 2019). Transaction
costs, such as monitoring costs, influence overall efficiency
(Johnston and Romzek  2023; Perez-­Lopez et  al.  2015) and
often erode savings (Brown and Potoski 2005; Johnston and
Romzek 2010; Petersen et al. 2019).
Excess monitoring can also undermine suppliers' trust in the
buyer and their intrinsic motivation (Frey 1993; Lambright 2009;
Poppo and Zenger 2002; Van Slyke 2007). In complex contracts,
buyers rely on suppliers' goodwill and cooperation, so eroding
trust through what suppliers perceive as unreasonable surveil-
lance could harm performance. Monitoring can also be onerous
for the supplier if they must provide information themselves
or facilitate access to staff, users, or premises. These burdens
can further weaken their relationship with the buyer and lead
them to cut costs elsewhere, potentially harming service quality
(Lambright 2009; Van Slyke 2007). Public managers must strike
a balance between preventing opportunism on the one hand and
curbing transaction costs and promoting harmonious working
relationships on the other.
Monitoring involves a complex mix of decisions and activities.
Existing studies focus on various elements of monitoring, but
several key stages emerge from the literature. Buyers must de-
cide what type of information to collect (Brown and Potoski
2003; Marvel and Marvel  2007), who collects it and how
(Anguelov 2020), how to analyze and interpret it (Hevenstone
et al. 2023; Lazzarini et al. 2022), and how to act on it (Marvel
and Marvel  2009; Van Slyke  2007). There are also questions
about how formal each stage should be (Van Slyke  2007;
Blomqvist and Winblad 2022) and how to involve suppliers and
other stakeholders (Amirkhanyan 2009).
Scholars have responded to the variety of monitoring elements
with a variety of operationalizations. Some treat monitoring
as a single construct (Isaksson et  al.  2018; Van Slyke  2007),
others measure component activities to represent the whole
(Lambright 2009; Mitchell and Mohr 2019), while others sep-
arate monitoring into distinct phases (Amirkhanyan  2009;
Marvel and Marvel 2007). This article follows the first group
and treats monitoring as a single construct: how closely buyers
monitor suppliers' behavior overall.
While relatively simple, this view of monitoring is appropriate
for the article's theory, which does not make predictions about
how competition affects specific monitoring activities, but about
the overall level of scrutiny a supplier receives. It does not, for
example, say whether competition will change the type of data
buyers use or who collects it. Focusing on how closely buyers
monitor their contracts also aligns with how monitoring af-
fects performance. Closer monitoring has clear implications for
transaction costs and for the degree to which a supplier feels
under surveillance by the buyer. Monitoring closeness also has
the necessary parsimony to facilitate a reliable and feasible ex-
perimental design, discussed further in the section on methods.
On first inspection, we may expect public managers to make
up for lacking market accountability with more managerial
accountability in the form of monitoring (Brown and Potoski
2004) and ease off when there is a competitive market to hold
suppliers accountable (Moon and Welch  2000). This article
presents the alternative argument that competition is likely to
increase monitoring because competition changes how buyers
can act on monitoring information and the value of their rela-
tionship with a supplier.
Monitoring improves performance when it enables governments
to punish poor performance and applies pressure on suppliers to
perform well. When the market can supply suitable alternatives,
public managers can respond to information about poor per-
formance through contract termination or by choosing another
supplier at the next contract award (Van Slyke 2007). They can
also use within-­contract sanctions, as they are less dependent
on the supplier's goodwill, financial stability, or continued inter-
est in the contract (Girth 2014; Lamothe and Lamothe 2012). In
fact, buyers need to monitor their suppliers to take advantage of
competition. The market only works if buyers have information
that allows them to identify and retain high-­performing suppli-
ers and replace poor performers. Buyers in competitive markets
face incentives to collect up-­to-­date and comprehensive infor-
mation about a supplier's performance.
In contrast, public managers are far less able to act on perfor-
mance information in weak markets (Girth 2014; Lamothe and
Lamothe 2012). They cannot easily terminate the contract or re-
place the supplier at the next contract award. They may also be
reluctant to impose within-­contract sanctions because they are
dependent on maintaining a good relationship with the incum-
bent and on them continuing to bid (Johnston and Girth 2012).
Performance information holds less value when buyers cannot
act on it. Moreover, the experience of being monitored applies
less pressure on suppliers because they face fewer or lighter con-
sequences for poor performance.
Buyers may also be wary of harming performance by un-
dermining their relationship with a supplier. Monitoring too
much may erode suppliers' goodwill and intrinsic motivation
(Frey  1993; Lambright  2009; Poppo and Zenger  2002; Van
Slyke 2007) or place onerous burdens on them (Lambright 2009;
Van Slyke 2007). Good working relationships are more valuable
when governments expect to be working with a supplier for a
long time, so managers may be warier of souring them through
close monitoring (Lamothe and Lamothe  2012; Marvel and
Marvel 2009). While buyers in competitive markets do not need
to be as careful about irritating their current supplier, buyers in
weak markets face incentives to limit the scope and frequency
of monitoring.
As with flexibility, being able to pick the best supplier from a
competitive market may not outweigh the transaction costs of
monitoring, especially when the service is complex. While pub-
lic managers may gain information, the cost of obtaining it may
have harmed the contract's efficiency (Brown and Potoski 2005;
Johnston and Romzek 2010; Petersen et al. 2019). Furthermore,
by signaling distrust in the supplier, they may have prevented
them from making investments in the contract that would
have improved performance in the long term (Frey  1993;
Lambright  2009; Poppo and Zenger  2002; Van Slyke  2007).

more reason to monitor because they have greater scope to act

their relationship with an incumbent. In weak markets, the
payoffs from monitoring are lower and the dangers are more
consequential.
H2a.  Buyers will monitor suppliers less closely when competi-
tion is low.
H2b.  Buyers will monitor suppliers more closely when compe-

3   |   Methods
3.1   |   Data
The article presents results from a preregistered vignette exper-
iment conducted through an online survey of public managers.2
Participants were recruited using a survey recruiter, Prolific
Academic, and through three professional organizations, the
UK Government Commercial Function, the procurement group
of the Local Government Association, and an international pro-
fessional body, World Commerce and Contracting.3 The survey,
fielded through the Qualtrics platform from October 2021 to
December 2022, received 576 complete responses. Participants'
characteristics are summarized in Figure  A1 in the online
appendix.
The decision to use non-­probability, convenience sampling was
taken to prioritize recruiting participants with experience of
managing contracts or external relationships in the public sec-
tor. Such samples are justified when experience or characteris-
tics are essential to the internal validity of the research (Landers
and Behrend 2015; Reichardt and Gollob 1999). However, the
result is that the sample may not be representative of the wider
population of public managers.
All participants currently worked in government and public ad-
ministration and had experience in negotiating and managing
external relationships. However, not all had direct experience
in government contract management.4 Such a sample may re-
flect reality, as many government contracts are managed by
staff with little experience or expertise in contract manage-
ment (Cabral  2017; Lamothe and Lamothe  2010; Provost and
Esteve 2016). Nevertheless, the analyses below examine robust-
ness to government contracting experience.
3.2   |   Experiment Design
The experiment manipulated participants' perceptions of com-
petition for a hypothetical contract through a short text-­based
vignette. Vignette experiments benefit from the internal validity
of randomization, while boosting external validity by embedding
treatments within real-­life situations (Atzmüller and Steiner 2010;
Auspurg and Hinz 2015). They are particularly valuable for inves-
tigating the way judgments, beliefs, and norms shape behavior
(Aguinis and Bradley 2014; Atzmüller and Steiner 2010; Jilke and
Van Ryzin 2017). As such, they are a useful tool to test how judg-
ments about the payoffs of different management approaches and
beliefs about relationships with suppliers combine with public
sector norms to influence managers' behavior.
The survey was designed in partnership with World Commerce
and Contracting to ensure that it was comprehensible to public
managers with varying levels of contracting experience within
different jurisdictions and service domains. However, they had
no influence over the analysis or the publication of findings.
The survey was also pre-­tested with five participants famil-
iar with different types of government contracts in a range of
countries. The participants completed the survey followed by
an interview, which assessed their understanding of the sce-
nario, treatments, and questions. Some minor edits were made
to the wording and survey presentation to remove possible am-
biguities and improve ease of navigation. Both measures—in-
volving experts in survey design and pre-­testing—improve the
internal validity of vignette studies (Hughes and Huby 2004).
Participants read a scenario describing a hypothetical contract
for the support and maintenance of a local government housing
system, in which information about the level of competition at
the last contract award varied randomly. There were two treat-
ment conditions—low and high competition—and a control
condition. In the control condition, information about competi-
tion was omitted. The supplier's performance was held constant
so as not to conflate competition with supplier performance or
quality. Actively controlling beliefs that could be influenced
by an experimental treatment is recommended to increase the
precision of information treatments (Dafoe et al. 2018; Haaland
et  al.  2023). The full experiment protocol, including treat-
ments, manipulation check, and outcome measures, is given in
Tables B1 and B2 in the online appendix.
Technology was chosen as the service in the vignette in con-
sultation with World Commerce and Contracting. They ad-
vised that it would be familiar to public managers in a range
of contexts as it is widely contracted out, an observation that
aligns with academic research (Marco-­Simó and Pastor-­
Collado 2020; Ya Ni and Bretschneider 2007). The support and
maintenance of a technology system also correspond to the
scope of the theory as it is a relatively complex service, with
many tasks that are difficult to define and measure (Brown
et al. 2018; Gantman and Fedorowicz 2020). That complexity
means that we can expect variation in the two outcome vari-
ables. Governments frequently contract for technology to take
advantage of suppliers' superior knowledge, skills, and inno-
vation so they stand to benefit from giving them freedom to
apply that expertise (Gantman and Fedorowicz 2020; Su and
Wang 2022; Verhoest et al. 2024). At the same time, it is diffi-
cult to specify requirements and standards because suppliers
understand the service better (Su and Wang 2022; Ya Ni and
Bretschneider 2007).
When it comes to monitoring, quality measures for technol-
ogy contracts are likewise commonly difficult to define, and

collecting and analyzing performance data is often costly (Su
and Wang 2022). Consequently, public managers may monitor
suppliers closely to prevent opportunism or try to protect cost-­
efficiencies by refraining from close monitoring. Buyer–supplier
relationships are also crucial for achieving good performance in
technology contracts (Verhoest et al. 2024), so in line with the
theory, managers may monitor less closely to protect relation-
ships. These trade-­offs mean it is reasonable to expect diversity
in participants' approaches to managing contracts, and they
leave room for competition to play a role in decision-­making.
To increase experimental realism and thus internal validity
(Aguinis and Bradley 2014; Haaland et al. 2023), participants
were provided with information about the wider service con-
text: a municipal government's housing service. This con-
text was chosen with the guidance of World Commerce and
Contracting to minimize the possibility that participants as-
sociated the vignette with a particular public body or juris-
diction. Housing services are widely provided by municipal
governments, which themselves are a very common type of
government buyer. Technology procurement for public hous-
ing, and by municipal governments in general, is also one of
the areas where practitioners are pushing for more competition
(see for example Future Housing Tech Coalition 2023; Local
Government Association 2025; and TPXimpact 2023 in the UK;
and Price, Forland, and Price et al. 2022 in the US). It therefore
aligns with the study's motivation to investigate the potential
effects of calls for more competition in public procurement.
The vignette did not provide further detail about the technology
required in the contract, the housing service, or the municipal
government. Leaving the scenario relatively broad balances in-
ternal validity with external validity and parsimony. The aim
was to provide a scenario that aligned with the theoretical scope
of the research and to which participants could apply their own
expertise and experience. Giving too many details could unduly
narrow the applicability of the findings, overload participants,
and create demand effects, where participants alter their be-
havior in response to their perceptions of the study's purpose
(Haaland et al.'s 2023).
The nature of the sample also meant that more service-­
specific detail would have been redundant. Participants were
not necessarily experts in technology, housing, or munici-
pal government, so could not be expected to make nuanced
judgments based on the type of technology or housing being
provided or the local government context. Instead, as Hughes
and Huby  (2004) find, participants likely applied their own
experience from a range of service contexts. Leaving the sce-
nario sufficiently broad created scope for participants to draw
on their experience, thus enhancing the external validity of
the study. Nevertheless, it remains a limitation that the study
only presented participants with vignettes about one type of
service, government, and supplier.
The experiment assesses the effect of information about com-
petition, which was operationalized as the number of bids, the
closeness of the award decision (indicating whether competing
bids were genuine alternatives), and the likelihood that the sup-
plier will be replaced at the next contract award. Low compe-
tition was indicated by few bids, an easy decision, and a low
likelihood of replacement. High competition was indicated by
many bids, a close decision, and a high likelihood of replace-
ment. Combining all three dimensions of competition captures
the pressure competition puts on suppliers to perform and the
options it gives buyers in the face of poor performance. For ex-
ample, the number of bids alone would not adequately convey
the threat of replacement, as a buyer could receive many bids,
but few of which were viable alternatives.
Making the likely length of the relationship explicit in the treat-
ment introduces the assumption when generalizing the findings
that contract managers infer that a supplier is more likely to be
replaced when competition is high than when it is low. This is
a reasonable assumption to make, as relationships are indeed
shorter in more competitive markets (Brown and Potoski 2004;
Lamothe and Lamothe  2009, 2010), and contract managers
make judgments about how a lack of competition will affect the
length of their relationships with suppliers (Girth 2014; Lamothe
and Lamothe 2012). The treatment also implies that the govern-
ment is required to run a competitive bidding process when the
current contract comes to an end.
Competition was described in general language, rather than
giving a specific number of bids, to accommodate diversity in
participants' service and government contexts. The same num-
ber of bids may indicate high competition to one participant and
moderate or even low competition to another. Describing com-
petition qualitatively can more reliably manipulate participants'
perceptions of the pressure on the supplier and the likelihood
of replacement. The experimental design is unable to untan-
gle the individual effects of the three elements of competition.
However, including all three elements was necessary to have a
strong first-­stage effect on participants' theoretically relevant
beliefs, in line with recommendations from the methodological
literature on vignette experiments (Aguinis and Bradley 2014;
Haaland et al. 2023). The research did not aim to test the effects
of different components of competition, but rather its overall in-
fluence on flexibility and monitoring.
The operationalization does not capture all aspects of com-
petition. Other market features can also influence perfor-
mance, so they might influence contract management. They
include competitors' market shares, financial stability, profit
margins, and market turnover (Romzek and Johnston  2005;
Walker et  al.  2006), access to economies of scale (Wang and
San Miguel 2013), and burdens on contract managers (Walker
et al. 2006). To reduce cognitive load on participants and maxi-
mize their attention, the design balances parsimony with detail
and focuses on aspects of competition that are most relevant to
the motivating theory. The approach does, however, represent
an improvement on previous literature. Most previous studies
compare contracts awarded by competitive procedures either to
contracts awarded without competition or to direct public provi-
sion (with the exception of Broms et al. 2020; Brunjes 2020; and
Carril et al. 2022). They test the effect of the presence or absence
of a market, not competition within that market. By investigat-
ing the effects of competition at different levels, this research
uncovers how the effects of low and high competition vary.
The survey asked participants how much flexibility they ex-
pected the government in the scenario to give the supplier in

they would monitor the supplier. Both variables were measured
on a 0–10 scale, with flexibility ranging from “none at all” to
“a great deal” and monitoring from “not at all” to “extremely
closely”. Like competition, the two outcome variables were
described in general terms to make them comprehensible to a
broad range of public managers with varying experience. This
aligns with the theory, which does not make predictions about
the specifics of how buyers monitor or give flexibility to suppli-
ers but rather their overall levels.
Both questions were framed in terms of the actions of the gov-
ernment. Previous studies have demonstrated that presenting
decisions as those of another actor reduces social desirability
bias, while allowing participants to draw on their own experi-
ence and beliefs (Constant et al. 1994; Hughes and Huby 2004).
It rests on the assumption that participants' expectations about
the actions of others are similar to their own behavior, which is
supported by evidence that expectations of others are informed
by our own values and behavior (Bogdan et al. 2023). The two
outcome measures are reported, not behavioral, opening up
the possibility that participants behave differently in practice.
Studies of survey experiments' external validity suggest that they
have a substantial ability to predict real-­world behavior (Findley
et al. 2017; Hainmueller et al. 2015; Petzold and Wolbring 2019).
Nevertheless, readers should consider this when assessing how
the results translate into actual contract management practices.
4   |   Results
The research used OLS regression to assess the effects of the
treatments on participants' reported flexibility and monitoring
behavior. As the study assesses several outcome variables, p
values were adjusted for multiple comparisons across all models
using the Holm-­Bonferroni procedure (Holm 1979). To address
concerns that the moderate sample size would lead to an un-
derpowered experiment, F tests were conducted for all models
and produced powers of 0.98 or higher at the 0.05 level (Kutner
et al. 2005). Results from a manipulation check demonstrate
that the treatment manipulations had the intended effect on
participants' perceptions of the level of competition in the mar-
ket. They are reported in the online appendix (Table C1), along
with model specifications and power analyses (Appendix D).
Table 1 shows results from OLS models of the effect of informa-
tion about competition on the reported measures of flexibility
and monitoring. It includes multivariate models with controls
for experience with government contracting to address a slight
imbalance between treatment groups (present at the 0.1 level in
a Chi-­squared test). Treatment groups were otherwise balanced
across age, gender, education, type and size of organization,
location, and recruitment route. The effects from the bivariate
models are also displayed in Figure 1.
While low competition does not have an effect on flexibility
compared to the control, high competition has a significant neg-
ative effect, with no change to the significance or direction of the
effect when controlling for experience. The results support H1b,
but not H1a. When given information that competition is high,
public managers expect less flexible contract management ap-
proaches, but information about low competition does not influ-
ence the flexibility they expect suppliers to be given. The finding
points to a trade-­off between competition's potential to hold
suppliers to account and its inhibiting effect on managers' flex-
ibility, which could prevent governments from realizing some
of the promised gains from contracting. If high competition
leads managers to be more rigid in their approach, governments

Turning to monitoring, the results support both hypotheses.
Low competition has a significant negative effect on the in-
tensity of monitoring (H2a), while high competition has a
significant positive effect (H2b), with no change in the sig-
nificance or direction of the effects when controlling for ex-
perience. Participants expect closer monitoring when the
market in the scenario is very competitive, whereas they ex-
pect a lighter-­touch approach when competition is low. The
results again suggest that increasing competition for pub-
lic contracts comes with trade-­offs. Increasing competition
could paradoxically undermine performance if it produces
excessive monitoring, which erodes  cost-­savings (Johnston
and Romzek 2010; Petersen et al. 2019) and discourages co-
operation (Lambright  2009; Poppo and Zenger  2002; Van
Slyke 2007).
Nonetheless, the effect of low competition on monitoring is also
concerning. It supports Johnston and Girth's (2012) finding that
a lack of competition encourages public managers to step back
from monitoring in favor of “managing with resignation” (17)
and Park and Moon's (2023) concern that long-­term buyer–sup-
plier relationships, which emerge in uncompetitive markets,
lead to insufficient monitoring and supplier opportunism. While
the research presented here cannot determine an ideal level of
monitoring—which is likely to depend on the nature and con-
text of the contracted service—the results indicate that the level
of competition informs public managers' monitoring choices in
potentially problematic ways.
One might be concerned that flexibility and monitoring are cor-
ollaries of one another. As the order of the two outcome measures
was not randomized, the effect of competition on monitoring
may be the result of priming when participants consider their
approach to flexibility. Correlation tests reported in the online
appendix (Appendix E) address this concern. They show a very
weak relationship between flexibility and monitoring (Pearson
coefficient of −0.13 and Spearman's rank coefficient of −0.11),
suggesting that one does not influence the other and competi-
tion is influencing both independently.
Participants' level of experience may also present another con-
cern. While all participants were public managers and had expe-
rience in external relationship management, some did not have
experience in managing government contracts. For the most
part, the findings are robust to participants' contracting expe-
rience. Interaction models reported in the appendix (Table F1
and Figure F1) do not find a significant interaction with con-
tracting experience. The negative effect of low competition on
monitoring does lose its significance, so this effect must be in-
terpreted with some caution, as it is driven by less experienced
participants.
5   |   Discussion
Previous research into the impacts of competition on con-
tract management has focused on weak markets, examin-
ing public managers' attempts to strengthen accountability
missing from the market (Brown and Potoski 2004; Romzek
et al. 2012; Walker et al. 2006). This article advances this re-
search by considering the effect of high as well as low com-
petition on two crucial components of contract management:
the flexibility public managers give suppliers and how closely
they monitor them. In doing so, it contributes the surprising
finding that too much as well as too little competition might
be counterproductive. The findings suggest that competition
encourages more restrictive styles of management, which
previous research has found can limit governments' ability to
realize the benefits of contracting out in terms of innovation
(Bredgaard and Larsen 2008; Considine et al. 2011; Malatesta
and Smith 2014) adaptation (Ball and Gibson 2022; Malatesta
and Smith 2014), and efficiency (Johnston and Romzek 2010;
Petersen et al. 2019).
The article contributes a potential explanation for why intro-
ducing competition into public service delivery has often not
produced the expected improvements in performance (Broms
et  al.  2020; Brunjes  2020; Brunjes  2022; Fernandez  2007;
Lamothe and Lamothe  2010). The theory applies to complex
services, where flexibility is needed to manage changing cir-
cumstances and navigate information asymmetries (Brown
et  al.  2016; Considine et  al.  2011; Malatesta and Smith  2014;
Tomkinson 2016) and where costly monitoring can threaten ef-
ficiency (Brown and Potoski 2005; Johnston and Romzek 2010;
Petersen et  al.  2019). Consequently, the theory and findings
could explain why competition's beneficial effects have been con-
fined to simple services (Carril et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2015).

Restrictive management styles may not be such a problem for
simple services, where it is easy to write a relatively complete
contract and performance is easy and therefore comparatively
cheap to measure.
The article adds to previous studies that find no or even nega-
tive effects of competition on performance. It suggests a poten-
tial mechanism (contract management) by which competition's
beneficial effects are eroded or even reversed and a context
(complex contracts) in which the mechanism should occur.
Because complexity is prevalent across the public sector (Boye
et al. 2024), the effect of competition on the management of com-
plex contracts could explain a substantial portion of the link be-
tween competition and government contract performance. The
findings suggest that connecting competition to performance
through contract management could be a fruitful area for fur-
ther investigation.
Future research could also build on this article's findings by
investigating whether they hold across different types of gov-
ernments, suppliers, and services. Participants had a wide va-
riety of expertise and experience on which they could draw
when responding to the experiment (Hughes and Huby 2004),
and the study was not confined to participants with experi-
ence of the service in the vignette. Nevertheless, participants
may have responded differently to vignettes about different
services in other contexts. Skills and capacity in contract
management vary across different types of public organi-
zations (Lamothe and Lamothe  2012; Petersen et  al.  2019),
while approaches to performance management (Marvel
and Marvel  2009) and relationship-­building (Amirkhanyan
et al. 2012; Chuang et al. 2020) vary across different types of
suppliers.
Another area for future research suggested by this study is to
examine the constituent elements of competition. The experi-
ment operationalized competition as a single construct to facil-
itate a feasible and parsimonious survey design, but that means
it cannot separate the effects of different aspects of competition.
Future studies could examine individual effects of the number
of bids, the closeness of the decision, or the length of buyer–sup-
plier relationships. They could also explore other features of
competitive markets that could influence contract management,
such as tight profit margins and financial instability (Romzek
and Johnston 2005), access to economies of scale (Wang and San
Miguel  2013), and high burdens on public managers (Walker
et al. 2006). A more complete assessment of the implications of
contracting markets for management could be facilitated by ob-
servational research that conceptualizes competition as a con-
tinuous variable, or by field experiments that can manipulate
actual competition or simulate it more fully. Moreover, elements
of contract design, such as provisions for renewing the contract
without competition, may alter how competition comes to bear
on contract management, for example, by insulating relation-
ships from the threat of replacement.
Likewise, further research is needed to unpack which elements
of flexibility and monitoring are affected by competition.
Flexibility can be given through the contract itself or whether
contract terms are enforced (Lamothe and Lamothe  2012),
so future studies could usefully assess whether the level of
competition changes the former, the latter, or both. Monitoring
also involves a range of decisions about what will be measured
with what type of data (Brown and Potoski 2003; Marvel and
Marvel  2007), how the data will be collected and analyzed
(Anguelov 2020; Hevenstone et al. 2023; Lazzarini et al. 2022),
and who performs these tasks (Amirkhanyan 2009). Research
that unpicks which of these decisions are altered by compe-
tition could further our understanding of the links between
market conditions, contract management, and performance.
Future studies could also examine the effect of competition on
other areas of contract management, such as how buyers and
suppliers communicate with one another, deal with conflict,
or resolve problems. Research designs that use behavioral out-
come measures to assess effects on management styles would
also address the limitations of the reported measures used in
this research.
If governments can have too much as well as too little compe-
tition for their contracts, what should they do? It is important
to note that, while the research found that high competition
has potentially counterproductive effects on two crucial ele-
ments of contract management, it cannot determine at what
point those effects outweigh any benefits from competition.
Government organizations must therefore scrutinize how
contract management changes when they respond to calls
to increase competition (e.g., European Commission  2016;
OECD 2019). The article's findings suggest that they should
pay particular attention to new restrictions on how suppliers
can fulfill contracts and increases in the amount of perfor-
mance information collected. Governments should also assess
whether changes in contract management hinder their abil-
ity to achieve efficiencies, access supplier expertise, or adapt
to new circumstances. They may wish to provide training or
guidance to contract managers preemptively to make manag-
ers conscious of the possible effects of competition on their
behavior and thereby reduce those effects.
Existing literature suggests that governments could also
counteract the effects identified in this research by investing
in contract management capacity and leadership. The level
of contract management capacity can substantially influence
an organization's ability to develop and maintain successful
relationships and curb transaction costs (Brown and Potoski
2004; Andrews et  al.  2015). Recent research also suggests
that leaders' characteristics (AbouAssi et al. 2025) and lead-
ership styles (Grøn  2025) can influence how collaborative
team members are when managing external relationships.
Governments could alleviate the tensions created by compe-
tition by appointing leaders that encourage collaborative con-
tract management or by building more contract management
capacity within teams.
6   |   Conclusion
How does competition shape public managers' approach to man-
aging contracts? This article has taken a novel perspective and
examined the influence of both low and high competition on
contract management. The results suggest that highly contested
markets may lead managers to define suppliers' work more nar-
rowly and monitor it more closely, while weak markets could

11

encourage them to step back from monitoring, especially when
contracts are complex. When viewed in the context of previous
research, which suggests that more restrictive styles of manage-
ment can hamper innovation, agility, and efficiency, these re-
sults indicate that contract management may be an important
link between competition and performance. The findings offer
an explanation for contradictory evidence about the effect of
competition on public service performance.
The results of this study can inform a wider policy debate about
the extent to which governments should stimulate competition
in markets for their contracts. The problems associated with too
little competition are well documented. This article suggests
that it may also be possible to have too much competition. The
research does not assess what levels of flexibility or monitoring
optimize performance outcomes in which contexts. However,
when combined with existing evidence, it does suggest that
highly contested markets have the potential to undermine some
of the benefits from contracting out, namely accessing exter-
nal expertise and making public services more responsive and
cost-­efficient. Governments must therefore pay close attention
to changes in management styles as they attempt to create more
competitive markets and either rein in such efforts or put in
place measures to compensate for any adverse effects.

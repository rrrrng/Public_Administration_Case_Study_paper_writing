ABSTRACT
How people are treated in the workplace is of increasing concern, with many scholars arguing that the government, as an em-
ployer, should set the standard for equitable treatment. While attention to equity in public administration has grown in research
and practice, minimal work has comparatively explored discrimination in the public and non-­public sectors. Drawing on data
from the 2018 European Social Survey, this study explores possible differences in how public and non-­public employees perceive
discrimination and how perceptions vary across countries. In most countries, the differences between the sectors were not signif-
icant, but the findings show that the country, administrative culture, and sociodemographic variables play a role in perceptions
of discrimination. This exploratory study makes an important empirical contribution by accumulating evidence of perceptions of
discrimination across countries and has practical implications for human resource management practitioners.
1   |   Introduction
In 2025, there is increasing competition for talent across sectors
(e.g., public, private, nonprofit) (Barigozzi and Burani 2019; De
Smet et al. 2022), with record job openings in many countries
and organizations struggling to attract and retain the human
capital necessary to perform at high levels. All organizations
need to consider what human resource management (HRM)
policies and practices will lead to working environments that
promote employee interests, well-­being, and retention to ensure
the organization's long-­term performance (Boxall 2012). While
many aspects of the working environment are critical for em-
ployee performance, a component well within the control of
managers is the degree to which an organization operates a fair
and just working culture, preventing discrimination and promot-
ing broader organizational justice (Cassar and Buttigieg 2015;
Leasher and Miller 2012).
In the public sector, in particular, scholars and practitioners have
championed public employers as the leaders in fair employment
practices, arguing for the government as a “model employer”
(Long 1975; Løkke 2021). When people start working in public
sector organizations, they assume they will be treated fairly, as
the government represents the rule of law and has a responsibil-
ity to respect the basic rights of citizens and support such values
as equal treatment. However, given the rising political polariza-
tion, democratic backsliding, and heightened rhetoric around in-
dividual differences across countries (Bersoff 2023; Bauer 2024;
Brock and Mallinson 2024; Hernandez and Pratt 2024), scholars
must confront this assumption critically and question whether it
is true. Is the government a model employer in terms of provid-
ing equal treatment to workers? Do public sector employees feel
less discriminated against than other employees? These ques-
tions are at the heart of the present exploratory study's analysis
of the 2018 European Social Survey (ESS, n.d.) data.

The public sector is estimated to comprise 18%–40% of the work-
ing population (Hammouya 1999; OECD 2021).1 Therefore, an
understanding of these employees' perceptions of discrimina-
tion—its nature and extent—is important, both normatively
and empirically. Public administration, as a field of study and
practice, commits to the value of equity. While not all agree that
it should be treated separately from other public values, social
equity is often called the fourth pillar of public administration,
a core value of public administration, alongside economy, ef-
ficiency, and effectiveness (Svara and Brunet  2005; Norman-­
Major 2022).2 The importance of equity as a pillar of public
administration was established at the Minnowbrook conference
in 1968 (e.g., Blessett et al. 2019; Cepiku and Mastrodascio 2021;
Norman-­Major 2011) and has more recently been reaffirmed
and recentered today (Gooden  2023; Clark et  al.  2024). The
National Academy of Public Administration (NAPA) refers to
equity as “the fair, just and equitable management of all insti-
tutions serving the public directly or by contract, and the fair
and equitable distribution of public services, and implementa-
tion of public policy; and the commitment to promote fairness,
justice, and equity in the formation of public policy” (in Svara
and Brunet 2005, 256).
For the government to fulfill its mandate to serve citizens, it
must consider how it delivers services and how its employees are
treated. This article argues that understanding the latter point
requires exploring the perceptions of workers in government in
terms of how they experience discrimination and comparing
them with those in the private (or non-­public) sector to under-
stand whether the government can still claim model employer
status or whether a critical reckoning is necessary. Moreover, we
argue that it is essential to adopt a comparative perspective on
the subject. A comparative perspective is missing in the literature
(Yeo and Jeon 2023). While many scholars examine discrimi-
nation through a single country case study and highlight fea-
tures at the individual level (e.g., Andrews and Ashworth 2015;
Guul et  al.  2019; Bishu and Headley  2020; Oberfield and
Incantalupo 2021; Krause and Park 2023), expanding the scope
to the sector and country level is essential for different reasons.
First, we can better understand how diverse sectors, country-­
level, and administrative features influence the phenomenon,
which can draw our attention to features we did not consider
in our own country. Second, studying discrimination within
a comparative context is vital because different trends are not
only relevant to one country but often tend to spread to others
(e.g., waves of integration, populism, and backsliding) (Stone
and Ladi  2015; Stanton and Oliver  2025). Lastly, comparing
discrimination in diverse sectors and countries can lead us to
highlight countries that offer valuable insights into handling the
phenomenon and could merit further examination. Therefore,
the uniqueness of this study is its dual perspective—between
sectors and between countries, a lens largely missing from up-
dated literature about discrimination.
Drawing on data from the ESS Round 9 and looking at six coun-
tries, this exploratory comparative study addresses the follow-
ing questions. First, are there differences in how public and
non-­public employees perceive discrimination? Second, what
factors affect perceptions of discrimination in general? What
factors affect perceptions of different kinds of discrimination?
Third, what factors affect the perceptions of discrimination in
different countries? The following section comprises a brief re-
view of what we know about equity and discrimination in the
workplace, centering the review on a larger normative argument
as to why we should be concerned about it in the public sector.
Specifically, it should be explored at the country and sector levels
to reveal any macro-­patterns that could affect the ability of gov-
ernments to hire and retain a competitive workforce. The study
methodology and findings follow this. While the study did not
find statistically significant differences between the public and
non-­public sectors in the sample as a whole, there were some dif-
ferences within specific countries (e.g., Germany and Sweden).
The findings highlight the importance of sociodemographic
variables and administrative culture in understanding discrim-
ination perceptions and demonstrate to public administration
scholars and HRM practitioners that the equity of public sector
workplaces can be improved. We highlight the importance of
maintaining commitment to being model employers for equity
and inclusion, particularly in the context of democratic back-
sliding and illiberal movements. Based on the country-­specific
administrative cultures, practitioners could build collaborative
administrative cultures alongside legal protections to reduce
perceptions of discrimination. In countries with significant im-
migration, nationality was a substantial factor in perceptions of
discrimination, while also addressing socio-­demographic fac-
tors when designing inclusion programs. The article concludes
with reflections on the findings and proposes avenues for fur-
ther research.

3   |   Differences in Discrimination Between Sectors
A continuing question in public administration is what makes
public organizations distinct from private (or non-­public) orga-
nizations and why that matters, theoretically and practically.
The broader differences between the public and private sectors
have been explored extensively, with scholars recognizing dif-
ferences in outcomes, values, and institutional constraints (e.g.,
Rainey and Bozeman 2000; Lee and Sabharwal 2016; Sánchez-­
Sánchez and Fernández Puente  2021; Lahat and Ofek  2022).
Scholars studying sectoral differences and similarities have
demonstrated that the public interest as a broader goal remains
an important difference, and public and nonprofit sectors can
outperform the for-­profit sector in certain ways (Amirkhanyan
et al. 2008; Ben-­Ner and Ren 2015). While scholars have pro-
vided evidence of differences and similarities between the
sectors, not many have explored differences in discrimination
between sectors across national contexts or in different coun-
tries (Leasher and Miller 2012; Yeo and Jeon 2023).
Work on discrimination can be divided into two research
streams: one focuses on differences across sectors in terms of
employee discrimination against service users, and the other fo-
cuses on differences between sectors in discrimination against
workers or employees. This study took the latter route, explor-
ing the experiences of workers in the public sector globally and
comparing them to the experiences of workers in the non-­public
sector. Before turning to the study, a brief review of the literature
is provided to explain the overall climate of discrimination (or
inclusion) that exists across sectors.
As mentioned, some research focuses on sector differences
in discrimination against service users. Taghizadeh and
Adman  (2022) explored discrimination against clients in re-
quests for school registration based on differences in socioeco-
nomic status and Arabic or Swedish-­sounding names, finding
discrimination but no differences between private and public
schools. Other studies support this result; in a study of elderly
care facilities in Flanders, Belgium, Jilke et al. (2018) found no
discrimination in response rates between sectors, but they noted
discrimination in the provision of information in the private
sector, whereby those with Maghrebian names received less in-
formation than those with Flemish names. In an experimental
study of US nursing homes, Xu and Lee (2024) found signifi-
cant discrimination against Asians and noncitizen immigrants.
Overall, there is no definitive evidence of systematic differences
in rates of discrimination against clients in the public versus pri-
vate sectors. Does this also hold for the experiences of workers?
Does the government promote a more inclusive work climate
than non-­public organizations?
While progress has been made across the globe, systematic pat-
terns of discrimination remain in the workplace. Public orga-
nizations' literature as well as practice gives growing attention
to diverse kinds of discrimination, such as gender (Foley and
Williamson 2019; Bishu and Headley 2020; William et al. 2024),
age (Krause and Park 2023), disabilities (Schwitter et al. 2025)
and the intersectionality of individual features such as gen-
der and disability or ethnicity (Chordiya et al. 2024; Schwitter
et  al.  2025). For example, the difference between how men
and women are treated is an ongoing issue; differences in-
clude access to leadership positions and a well-­documented
pay gap (Sabharwal 2015; Bishu and Alkadry 2017; Foley and
Williamson 2019; Bishu and Headley 2020). However, our focus
is on the differences between sectors and countries, which, as
we mentioned, are scarce.
Regarding the public versus the private sectors, while both sec-
tors are obligated by law not to discriminate, the public sector, as
a representative of the state, is expected to uphold higher stan-
dards against discrimination, awareness of the subject, as well as
prevention procedures (l'Horty et al. 2022; William et al. 2024).
However, this might be a double-­edged sword; increased aware-
ness could lead to a higher number of claims against discrim-
ination in a more aware and unionized public sector, making
it a challenge to differentiate between subjective and objective
discrimination (William et  al.  2024). Scholars have argued
(and some evidence has shown) that the public sector may be
better able to address systemic discrimination against women
and persons of color in the workplace. For example, Hoffnar
and Greene (1996) found differences in the salaries of male and
female workers (regardless of race), with these gaps more prom-
inent in the private than the public sector. Other studies sug-
gest work conditions and discrimination are better for people of
color in the public than the private sector (Long 1975; Wilson
and McBrier 2005) and that while more claims have been made
in the public sector, fewer were found justified than in the pri-
vate sector (William et al. 2024). Others have found no differ-
ence in discrimination between the public and private sectors
(Leasher and Miller  2012; Oberfield and Incantalupo  2021;
l'Horty et al. 2022). Based on the obligation of the public sector
to equity and these studies, we posit:
H1.  Public sector employees will present lower discriminatory
perceptions than those who work in the non-­public sector.
The presence of discrimination in public organizations is
well documented. It highlights the importance of examining
the intersection of different characteristics, such as disability
with age, gender, and ethnicity (Jones et al. 2018; Krause and
Park  2023; Chordiya et  al.  2024). The differences between

4   |   Different Countries, Different Administrative
Cultures
What might explain differences between countries in the pub-
lic sector? We argue that to gain a deeper understanding, we
should connect the theory of organizational justice with liter-
ature on administrative culture. The theory of organizational
justice emphasizes that representation is important (Oberfield
and Incantalupo 2021; Krause and Park 2023); however, in the
field of PA, scholars claim it is not sufficient, and at times it may
also be perceived as unfair and as tokenism rather than foster-
ing organizational justice (Hoang et  al.  2022). An alternative
is fostering an inclusive and collaborative culture and a liber-
atory public service that relies on an ongoing process of social
equity, dismantling power asymmetries, involving active in-
clusion, identifying implicit biases, and critical thinking. This
approach enables the creation of equal opportunity, voice, and
space for diverse individuals in the organization, regardless of
their characteristics (disability, gender, ethnicity, as well as the
intersectionality of these features), benefiting both employees
in the public sector and service users (Butz and Gaynor 2022;
Hoang et al. 2022; Chordiya et al. 2024). Based on the centrality
of this effort in the public sector, we suggest that it is reasonable
to assume that the presence of the phenomenon will differ be-
tween countries based on their cultures, laws, institutions, and
administrative history.
Countries have administrative cultures that influence how
people experience the government workplace. The concept
of administrative culture encompasses the norms and values
prominent in public sector organizations (Jamil et  al.  2013)
and is closely connected to general social norms and political
culture. It includes the level of trust, the place of hierarchy and
law, the emphasis on merit versus a more personal culture, and
the relationship of a country to authority (Peters 2009). This
culture affects the practice of behavioral actions, observed or
hidden, that affect discrimination within public sector organi-
zations. For example, it is reasonable to assume that in coun-
tries with a stricter alignment with the law, such as Germany,
under the administrative culture of the rule of law and the
“Rechtsstaat,” the tendency to discriminate will be less ob-
served than in countries with less strict and more informal
cultural behavior, such as in Israel (Lahat et al. 2023; Vedder
et al. 2023).
H3.  We will find differences in perceptions of discrimination
patterns between countries.
There are different typologies of administrative culture regimes.
This study examined three regimes: Continental European,
Nordic, and Anglo-­Saxon.3 The Continental European adminis-
trative culture is characterized by a strong emphasis on the rule
of law, a relatively powerful state, and a bureaucratic and cen-
tralistic approach. Countries in this category include Germany
and Belgium. A subcategory is the Napoleonic regime, which
leans towards party patronage and clientelism, and includes
France, Spain, and Italy. The Nordic administrative culture
embraces the tradition of the rule of law but endorses a more
collaborative and citizen-­centered culture; countries include
the Netherlands, Sweden, and Finland. The culture stresses
transparency and participation of various stakeholders in pol-
icymaking, the implementation of public services, and the ad-
ministration's accountability to citizens. It thus reflects a more
collaborative and inclusive environment than an adversarial
one. Finally, the Anglo-­Saxon administrative culture is more
pragmatic and pluralistic, emphasizing the public interest and
includes the United Kingdom and Ireland (Greve et  al.  2016;
Kickert 2002; Jamil et al. 2013; Kuhlmann and Wollmann 2019;
Lahat and Ofek 2022). Since the literature refers to the impor-
tance of organizational norms, such as inclusiveness, as cru-
cial to organizational justice (Bishu and Headley 2020; Hoang
et  al.  2022; Chordiya et  al.  2024), and diverse administrative
culture encompasses diverse formal and informal norms, we
posit that:
H4.  Countries with features of collaborative legalistic adminis-
trative culture will demonstrate lower perceptions of discrimina-
tion than countries with other administrative cultures.
To the best of the authors' knowledge, no updated studies
have explored discrimination between sectors (public and
non-­public) from a comparative perspective. Most studies
take a case study approach (e.g., in one state, Leasher and
Miller  2012) or explore how public sector employees dis-
criminate against citizens (e.g., Jilke et  al.  2018). Quillian
et  al.  (2019) conducted a meta-­analysis of 100 experimental
studies on hiring discrimination across nine countries, but re-
ferred more generally to discrimination in the labor market
than differentiating between sectors. Their findings suggest
some countries tend to discriminate more than others. For
example, in France and Sweden, on average, whites receive
callbacks for jobs 65%–100% more often than non-­whites.
In Germany, the United States, and Norway, whites receive
20%–40% more callbacks. The authors argue that it is diffi-
cult to pinpoint one factor that explains differences between
countries, and more explicit policies are needed to address the
issue (Quillian et al. 2019), and others support this need (Yeo
and Jeon 2023).
Due to the scarcity of comparative studies on discrimination
across sectors, it could be useful to turn to studies on rep-
resentative bureaucracy for insight. However, comparative

5   |   Methods
5.1   |   Participants and Data
This study used data from the ESS, Round Nine, 2018. The ESS
explores questions based on samples from 29 countries using
strict random probability sampling and high-­quality standard-
ized fieldwork procedures to ensure cross-­country compara-
bility. Sampling was designed to represent all persons aged 15
and over living in private households, regardless of nationality
or language. Quota sampling and substitution were not permit-
ted at any stage. The ESS addresses topics such as well-­being,
political orientation, and trust. The sample size is 1500 and 800
(for countries smaller than 2 million) participants.4 Data was
collected through face-­to-­face Computer-­Assisted Personal
Interviewing (CAPI) conducted by trained interviewers who
followed standardized protocols across all participating coun-
tries. Each country aimed for a minimum 70% response rate
while maintaining non-­contact rates below 3%. The fieldwork
period extended for at least 6 weeks, during which interviewers
followed structured contact schedules that included multiple
contact attempts and comprehensive documentation to ensure
data quality. National coordinators and designated survey orga-
nizations in each country implemented the survey under close
supervision by the ESS Core Scientific Team and Sampling and
Weighting Expert Panel. These rigorous quality control mea-
sures ensured that samples were both robust and comparable
across countries.
The N for the present analysis was 49,519. As Table 2 shows,
women are slightly more represented (53.5%) in the data than
men (46.5%). Participants have an average of 13 years of educa-
tion; 33% work in the public sector, and 67% in the non-­public
sector.
5.2   |   Study Variables
5.2.1   |   Dependent Variable: Perceptions
of Discrimination
Discrimination was categorized as one if the respondent felt
discriminated against and zero if not. Two questions from the
ESS survey were used to identify this variable. First, interview-
ees were asked whether they belonged to a group discriminated
against in their country (C18). If yes, they were referred to an-
other question: “On what grounds is your group discriminated
against?” (C19). Options included: color or race 01; nationality
02; religion 03; language 04; ethnic group 05; age 06; gender 07;
sexuality 08; disability 09; other 10.
5.2.2   |   Independent Variable: Belonging to
the Public Sector
The primary independent variable was employment in the pub-
lic sector, as the goal was to determine whether individuals who
work in the public sector report lower perceptions of discrimina-
tion. The public sector was categorized as one if the respondent
belonged to the public sector and zero if the respondent be-
longed to the non-­public sector. A question asking interviewees
about their work organizational type (F32) was used to identify
this variable. Other studies have used this question to delineate
public-­sector employees from non-­public-­sector employees (Van
de Walle and Lahat 2017; Lahat and Ofek 2022). Those who an-
swered categories 1–3 (central and local government and other
public or state-­owned enterprises) were defined as public sector
employees, and those who placed themselves in categories 4–6
(private firm, self-­employed, and other) were considered private
or non-­public sector. This division yielded a sample of 45,035
observations: 14,908 public sector and 30,127 non-­public sector
employees.
5.2.3   |   Control Variables
The study included sociodemographic variables used in other
studies (e.g., Wilson and McBrier  2005; Byron  2010; Quillian
et al. 2019; William et al. 2024) on perceived discrimination in
public sector organizations. The analysis controlled for the fol-
lowing variables: age, gender, income, education, marital status,
number of children, health status, and whether the interviewees
were born in the same country. Table 1 presents the measure-
ment and descriptive statistics of the variables.
5.3   |   Data Analysis
To answer the three main questions and explore the research
hypotheses, the analysis started with descriptive statistics and
a chi-­square test (crosstabs) to check for significant differences
between public and non-­public sectors in the perceived level of
discrimination, based on the kind of discrimination (e.g., age,
gender), and between countries.
Multi-­level logistic regressions were used to explore the fac-
tors affecting different kinds of discrimination, as people are
nested within countries (Hox 2010). These multi-­level regres-
sions assessed effects at the individual and country levels. The
first model assessed the effect of working in the public sector
on the probability of experiencing discrimination in general
(Model 1, Table 2). Models 2–5 (Table 2) focused on four spe-
cific kinds of discrimination: age, gender, disability, and na-
tionality. These are prominent in the discourse of public sector
organizations.
To explore differences between countries, we chose six coun-
tries reflecting a relatively high percentage of discrimination
(more than 7%) (see Figure  2) and/or showing a significant

5.4   |   Limitations
First, the study used cross-­sectional big survey data, which
could lead to a risk of common method bias (CMB). CMB occurs
when the same source is used to explore independent and depen-
dent variables and thus might inflate the data. To address this,
the analysis used Harman's single-­factor score, as suggested
by Podsakoff et al. (2012) and George and Pandey (2017). The
total variance for a single factor was 11%, lower than the recom-
mended threshold of 50% (George and Pandey 2017; Rodríguez-­
Ardura and Meseguer-­Artola  2020). Additionally, the study
verified the variance inflation factor (VIF) values and tolerance
values to check for multicollinearity issues; they were accept-
able, indicating no significant inflation factors. Finally, the ESS
survey's reliability and its rigorous nature help to address this
issue. Another potential bias in surveys is social desirability
bias, whereby survey respondents provide answers perceived as
more socially appropriate. Second, cultural differences between
countries, norms, and interpretations of discrimination pose a
challenge, as in other comparative studies. Third, we focused
only on European countries; future studies could focus, for ex-
ample, on the global south (e.g., Berman et al. 2025). Fourth,
we refer to perceptions of discrimination among diverse popu-
lations working in the public and non-­public sectors; more di-
rect measurement within organizations and specific professions
could yield more nuanced empirical findings. Lastly, the distinc-
tion between public and non-­public employees underscores the
important role of non-­governmental organizations, which often
provide services to citizens close to public organizations; this
point is addressed later in the discussion.
6   |   Results

The second research question asked about the factors affecting
discrimination in general and different kinds of discrimination.
Table 2 shows the results of the multi-­level logistic regression

demographic variables played a role in the expected direction.
In the general category of discrimination, younger people with
higher incomes and healthier people born in the country (i.e.,
not immigrants) perceived less discrimination, while people
with more children and more education perceived higher lev-
els of discrimination. This suggests that as individuals increase
their education, they may become more aware of the nuances
associated with discrimination and recognize problematic be-
havior. In addition, it is possible that as people begin to expand
their families, they recognize barriers and social constructions
around having children that can affect how others perceive
them. This possibility is explored in the discussion.
Across all categories of discrimination, older participants felt
less discriminated against; not surprisingly, however, they felt
more discriminated against in the age category. Women per-
ceived more discrimination based on gender and age but less
on nationality. Healthier people felt less discriminated against
based on disability and all the other categories of discrimina-
tion. More educated people felt they were discriminated against
in all categories except for discrimination based on nationality.
Interestingly, belonging to the public sector did not significantly
explain discrimination perceptions. While there were differ-
ences between sectors in the descriptive statistics, after con-
trolling for individual sociodemographic variables and utilizing
a multi-­level analysis, these differences were insignificant, sup-
porting other studies (Leasher and Miller 2012; Oberfield and
Incantalupo 2021; l'Horty et al. 2022; Schwitter et al. 2025).
Therefore, the findings refute H1—the public sector will present
fewer discriminatory practices than the non-­public sector—and
H2—there will be differences between sectors for the different
types of discrimination.

7   |   Discussion
The study adds to the expanding literature on equity in public-­
sector organizations. As discussions around what constitutes
inclusive societies and workplaces become more contentious
worldwide, it is critical for public administration scholars to
continue to research this topic and understand the complicated
and nuanced relationship between structural and personal fac-
tors influencing both perceptions of discrimination and actions.
Despite its exploratory nature, the study and the distinctive dual
perspective it provides, which includes both sectors and coun-
tries, make several contributions.
Its empirical contribution is the finding that the differences
between sectors do not play an important role in perceptions
of discrimination. This result aligns with studies finding no
fundamental differences between clients across sectors (Jilke
et al. 2018; Taghizadeh and Adman 2022; Schwitter et al. 2025)
but contradicts studies dealing more specifically with employee
discrimination (Long 1975; Hoffnar and Greene 1996; Wilson
and McBrier 2005; Byron 2010; Leasher and Miller 2012; William
et al. 2024). In the present study, the finding of no statistically
significant differences between the sectors held not just in gen-
eral but also when different kinds of discrimination, such as
age, gender, disability, and nationality, were more closely exam-
ined. These findings could be understood in different ways. An
optimistic interpretation is that dialogue around the problems
associated with discrimination has positively impacted both
sectors and reduced the degree to which people perceive they
are experiencing discrimination. However, if the public sector
sets the standard for how people are treated in the workplace,
these findings could suggest that perhaps the public sector is
beginning to lag in its promise to be a model employer. More re-
search is needed to add nuance to these comparative exploratory
findings. In addition, the ESS data do not allow differentiation
in the portion of the sample classified as non-­public employees.
In many countries, nonprofits or civil society organizations
present a desirable option for people who want workplaces that
emphasize inclusion and social equity outside the government.
Future research that allows for comparative studies differenti-
ating between public employees and those working in the non-
profit or civil society sector would add depth to this ongoing
conversation.
While the employment sector was not statistically related
to perceptions of discrimination, the importance of spe-
cific sociodemographic characteristics cannot be underesti-
mated. For example, age and health status were relevant to
all kinds of discrimination, and education level and gender
to most of them. While the gender gaps in many countries
around pay (e.g., Bishu and Alkadry  2017; Choi  2018; Foley
and Williamson  2019; Bishu and Headley  2020; Krause and
Park 2023) and career opportunities are well documented and
help explain this finding, more research is needed to better un-
derstand the relationship between education and perceptions
of discrimination. The fact that more educated individuals
felt more perceived discrimination says something about the
connection between awareness and perceptions of discrimina-
tion, and the double-­edged sword it brings to the public sector
(William et al. 2024). Scholars need to explore how this rela-
tionship is affected by national and administrative cultures.
Do individuals with more education feel more agency and em-
powerment to call out discrimination? If yes, how can organi-
zations give all workers a voice to express how they feel treated
in their organizations and in society?
It is interesting to note that out of the six countries, a migration
background was significant only in France and Germany. With
increased political polarization globally around issues of mi-
gration, difference, and national origin, it is essential for public
administration scholars to gather more data on how this polar-
ization is affecting perceptions of inclusion in the public sector
workplace and beyond. The complexity of the personal factors
influencing perceptions of discrimination requires human re-
sources (HR) that opt for organizational justice to understand
the interplay between many of these factors and consider how
programs preventing discrimination and fostering active inclu-
sion can address these nuances. Individuals identify in different
ways with aspects of their identity, and these are shaped by so-
cial constructions, both in their employment venue and in soci-
ety in general (Jones et al. 2018; Chordiya et al. 2024; Schneider
and Ingram 2019). Better understanding the perceptions of tar-
get populations with different identities may help to create pol-
icy tools to reduce discrimination.
The findings demonstrate that country context matters, and
more comparative work on critical public administration pro-
cesses and outcomes is needed. While sectors did not play a role
in influencing perceptions of discrimination, countries and their
administrative culture did. To the best of the authors' knowl-
edge, no studies have comparatively explored perceptions of

discrimination in different sectors and countries; therefore, the
findings are exciting. Interestingly, the differences between the
sectors were significant in two of the six countries. In Germany,
belonging to the public sector led to a higher tendency to feel dis-
criminated against, whereas in Sweden, belonging to the pub-
lic sector led to a lower tendency to feel discriminated against.
While Germany has a Continental European administrative cul-
ture, Sweden has a Nordic one. However, both countries have a
legalistic tradition (Kickert 2002; Jamil et al. 2013; Kuhlmann
and Wollmann 2019; Lahat and Ofek 2022). This suggests the
need to investigate this further and reveal the interplay between
the traditions underpinning the administrative cultures in
countries and the enacted reality of those cultures in the lives of
citizens and public administrators.
Based on the results, it seems the collaborative nature of the
Nordic administrative culture yields different outcomes; we
joined other studies that suggest that the legalistic culture is
not enough to safeguard employees from feeling discrimina-
tion, and different actions need to be taken within this broader
administrative culture (i.e., adding a collaborative culture to
strong legal institutions and traditions) (see, e.g., Bishu and
Headley 2020; William et al. 2024). This finding could also
rest on how people experience the rule of law, which is in flux
across the world and could differ in practice from what is es-
poused. Trust of many different kinds—social trust, trust in
institutions, and trust in the rule of law—is on the decline
in some countries, perhaps causing concerns about how well
the law will protect people from discrimination (Citrin and
Stoker 2018; Mewes et al. 2021). In other countries, the rule of
law might create an atmosphere of awareness of discrimina-
tion, with official steps taken to deal with it. The combination
of a legalistic administrative culture with collaboration in the
Nordic administrative culture (Greve et al. 2016) was shown
to be more supportive of reduced perceptions of discrimina-
tion in this study, but more research is needed to unpack the
complex relationship between individual factors, administra-
tive culture, trust in institutions, and current perceptions of
discrimination. For example, examining specific features of
collaborative public organizations' surroundings as mecha-
nisms that reduce feelings of discrimination may be a prom-
ising approach.
Those born in Germany and France reported lower perceptions
of discrimination, highlighting the need to monitor national or-
igin discrimination closely. These two countries have absorbed
large numbers of immigrants in the last decades (IOM 2024),
arguably creating more differences between natives and non-­
natives, which is a critical aspect of discrimination perceptions.
Women in two Nordic administrative traditions, Iceland and
Sweden, felt more discriminated against than men, even though
Nordic countries implement policies advancing gender equal-
ity. It is possible that awareness and expectations are higher in
these countries than elsewhere. When individuals are shown
avenues (legally and culturally) to improve their rights and es-
tablish their place at the table in organizations (and in society),
they may feel empowered to push for the full implementation
of those desired rights. For example, in a study of work incivil-
ity in local government in the United States, Smith et al. (2021)
found women of color reported lower levels of incivility than
white women, thus challenging common assumptions. Those
who have been traditionally disenfranchised or have experi-
enced discrimination (structurally or personally) may decline to
report problematic behavior or use alternative coping strategies
because they feel their claims will not be taken seriously (Smith
et al. 2021). Further research is needed to study the degree to
which different individuals across country contexts feel empow-
ered to varying degrees to challenge discrimination and argue
for inclusion.
This exploratory study joins other comparative studies of public
administration (e.g., Suzuki and Hur 2024), asking how national
institutional and political structures affect how government op-
erates in relation to government employees and the broader cit-
izenry. Understanding the administrative culture, the country
context, and sociodemographic characteristics could suggest
ways to address discrimination more effectively. Future studies
should explore these initial findings using other datasets and
methods, including looking at objective measures of discrimi-
nation and/or implementing experiments among public and
non-­public sector employees. For example, analyzing employee
complaints or comparing subjective perceptions with objec-
tive data could provide more knowledge on the phenomenon
of discrimination in public organizations from a comparative
perspective. More specifically, we suggest public organizations
should stay committed to being model employers for equity and
inclusion, especially amid democratic backsliding and illiberal
movements. Practitioners could develop collaborative cultures
alongside legal protections to lower these perceptions. In coun-
tries with high immigration, nationality significantly influences
discrimination perceptions, relevant amid political polarization
and migration. Sociodemographic factors like age, health, edu-
cation, and gender also affect perceptions. Practitioners should
consider these in inclusion efforts, noting that more educated
employees tend to report more discrimination.
The study contributes to the growing interest in equity in public ad-
ministration (Gadson and Park 2024; Lahat and Nathansohn 2025;
Park and Park 2025), an area of theoretical and practical impor-
tance. Although belonging to the public sector was not the stron-
gest explanatory variable in perceptions of discrimination, it is
still important for public administrations to emphasize how their
organizations are perceived. At a time of growing democratic
backsliding and the rise of populist, illiberal, and autocratic gov-
ernments across the world (Bauer and Becker 2020; Bauer 2024),
government as an employer must guard against the more problem-
atic values associated with many of these movements and focus
on building inclusive and equitable organizations that treat their
employees and citizens with care and equity.

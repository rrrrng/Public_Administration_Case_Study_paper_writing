ABSTRACT
Do populist politicians increase the number of political appointments when they assume power? While the existing literature
identifies politicization and political appointments as leading populist strategies, empirical evidence remains limited. Given the
elusive nature of political appointments, it is challenging to assess their true extent in various contexts. Our research highlights
how exemptions from a merit-­based process are a major indicator of politicization. Through a systematic analysis of all exemp-
tions from competition or a merit-­based selection process in the Israeli civil service from January 1, 2000, to April 30, 2024, we
provide empirical evidence linking populism and political appointments, suggesting deep and widespread politicization within
the Israeli civil service. Our empirical evidence implies that Israel is probably among the leaders in this regard among developed
democratic countries. In discussing our findings, we argue that, given the current global populist trend, public administration
scholars should adopt a more critical stance toward political appointments.
1   |   Introduction
Contemporary global political landscapes have been character-
ized by an unprecedented trajectory of democratic backsliding.
This systematic incremental erosion of democratic norms by
ruling political entities has emerged as a more pervasive and
insidious threat to democratic institutions than traditional de-
stabilizing mechanisms such as revolutionary upheavals, mili-
tary coups, or external interventions (Mudde 2004). Frequently,
it is populist political actors who, upon gaining power, promote
these transformative and corrosive processes (Bermeo  2016;
Devinney and Hartwell 2020; Rogenhofer and Panievsky 2020).
Scholarly discourse has extensively examined and meticulously
documented these complex dynamics, with an expanding body
of research exploring populism's multifaceted implications for
democratic governance (Norris 2017; Norris and Inglehart 2019;
Pierson 2017).
With the global wave of populism and democratic backslid-
ing, public policy and administration scholars have explored
the various challenges that these phenomena pose for admin-
istrative systems (Bauer  2024; Bauer et  al.  2021; Peters and
Pierre  2019, 2022), as well as the bureaucracy's response to
this trend (Guedes-­Neto and Peters  2021; Lotta et  al.  2024;
Story et al. 2023). Democratic erosion involves the systematic
reconfiguration of democratic institutional frameworks with
the goal of increasing political control (Bermeo 2016; Levitsky
and Ziblatt 2018; Koliba 2025). Part of this change includes the
transformation of the civil service, which occurs through col-
lusion and the marginalization or systematic dismantling of
oversight institutions (Bauer et al. 2021) alongside broader orga-
nizational degradation (Haggard and Kaufman 2021). Existing
state bureaucracies face strategic interventions including re-
source deprivation (manifested in fiscal and personnel reduc-
tions), institutional restructuring that modifies accountability

While the literature identifies politicization and political
appointments as leading populist strategies, empirical sub-
stantiation is limited. Given the elusive nature of political
appointments, it is challenging to accurately assess their true
extent in various contexts. Do populist politicians indeed in-
crease the number of political appointments when they as-
sume power? This question is extremely important, as scholars
tend to believe that the public administration's response to
populism could undermine democracy's resilience (Bauer
et al. 2021), strengthen it (Box 2022; Goodsell 2022), or even
save it (Yesilkagit  2021; Bauer  2024, 1019). Framed as effi-
ciency reforms, radical politicization of the civil service risks
facilitating long-­term administrative backsliding by under-
mining bureaucratic expertise, autonomy, and the quality of
governance (Suzuki  2025). An increase in political appoint-
ments could impact public bureaucracies and thus change their
response to democratic backsliding.
This article seeks to present, for the first time, a clear, objec-
tive, data-­driven overview of exemptions from competition or a
merit-­based selection process in the Israeli civil service, which
we believe are inextricably linked to the phenomenon of polit-
ical appointments. To accomplish this goal, we examined the
decisions made regarding such exemptions and developments
in this area over the past 25 years. The Israeli context offers
unique scholarly insights, as it represents an archetypal case
of populist phenomena, demonstrating extensive politically
motivated interventions in public administration tradition-
ally based on merit-­based systems. Moreover, it is currently
the sole example worldwide of populist governance during a
national emergency.
We begin by critically examining the literature on populism and
political appointments to establish an analytical framework for
the Israeli case study and to delineate points of convergence/
divergence with prevailing theoretical perspectives. We then
contextualize the Israeli landscape. The following sections ex-
plicate our methodological approach and present our findings.
The article concludes with a comprehensive analysis of the
results, exploring their theoretical significance and practical
ramifications.
2   |   Literature Review
2.1   |   Are Political Appointments and Populism
Linked Phenomena?
2.1.1   |   Political Appointments
The discussion of political appointments is part of a broader re-
search debate on the interaction between politicians and bureau-
crats. This topic was central to public administration scholarship
long before the rise of populism, as the quality of government
largely depends on how well the relationship between politi-
cians and bureaucrats is organized, with systems that balance
political control and bureaucratic professionalism leading to
better governance outcomes (Dahlström and Lapuente 2017).
The traditional view of this relationship involved a clear di-
chotomy between politicians and bureaucrats. Politicians, as
representatives of the public, shaped policy, while bureaucrats,
as subordinates, saw to its efficient implementation. Woodrow
Wilson (1887) famously stated, “Administration lies outside the
proper sphere of politics….” Since then, others have challenged
this view (Peters and Pierre 2004), recognizing that bureaucrats
often dominate policy, while politicians sometimes defer due to
an information deficit (Aberbach et al. 1981). Peters (2018) ar-
gues that bureaucrats' expertise gives them significant influence
over the policy process, sidelining political leaders. Bureaucrats
often have incentives to expand their organizations and maxi-
mize their budgets to increase their power (Niskanen 1971). As
a result, politicians and bureaucrats typically operate in a world
of asymmetric information, leaving politicians to find ways to
counteract bureaucratic dominance (Bendor et al. 1987).
One strategy that politicians can use to control bureaucracies
is political appointments (Peters and Pierre 2004). In principle,
this is an appointment made to serve the political motives of
the appointing officials, whether ideological, partisan, or even
personal. It involves elected officials in the appointment pro-
cess, rather than relying solely on the merit-­based procedures
of the bureaucracy. As such, “political appointment” serves as
an umbrella term for various types of appointments, depending
on the interpretation of the word “political” and the identity and
motives of the appointing entity. Naturally, it is often impossi-
ble to know the motivation of the appointee. Consequently, as
we will discuss later, existing definitions of political appoint-
ments present challenges for empirical analysis and operational
implementation. It is difficult to assess or make unambiguous
determinations about the general phenomena, as opposed to a
specific appointment, especially when the appointment requires
legal and public scrutiny.
Given its profound implications for bureaucratic dynam-
ics and the fundamental politician-­bureaucrat relationship
(Wilson 1887), the politicization of public service appointments
has generated substantial scholarly discourse (Aucoin  2012;
Christensen et  al.  2014; Cooper  2018; Dahlström and
Niklasson  2013). More sophisticated politicization tactics in-
volve systematically modifying civil service regulations and
labor protections, effectively undermining merit-­based systems
and institutional safeguards to facilitate the replacement of per-
sonnel (Moynihan 2022a; D. P. Moynihan 2022b).

•	 We propose examining exemptions from competition

political appointments.
•	 We provide evidence linking populism and political

•	 We advocate a more critical scholarly approach to

Political appointments are often viewed as part of politicization
and patronage processes, where office-­holders leverage their
power to assign individuals public administration positions
(Kopecký et al. 2012). Presidentialization, for example, fosters
politicization by concentrating power in individual leaders,
reducing collective cabinet and party control and encourag-
ing loyalty-­based appointments (Poguntke and Webb  2005).
Politicization is a growing global trend, driven by political de-
mands for control, but it risks weakening bureaucratic neutral-
ity, expertise, and effective governance (Gallo and Lewis 2012;
Halligan 2021; Oliveira et al. 2024). Panizza et al. (2019, 148)
define patronage appointments as “the power of political actors
to appoint individuals by discretion to nonelective positions in
the public sector, without necessarily considering the legality
of the decision.” Importantly, this definition is neutral. It avoids
assumptions about the appointees' motivations, professional ca-
pabilities, or the potential impact of such appointments on the
overall quality of public administration. Moreover, discretion
is not always a binary concept. It may be challenging to defini-
tively identify the precise boundary between having and lacking
discretion, as well as the level of discretion in a specific process.
Sancino et al. (2018, 787) define patronage or public appoint-
ments as “the capacity of political actors to appoint people to
positions in public and semipublic (e.g., hybrid) organizations.”
Similarly, Cocciasecca et  al.  (2021) define public appoint-
ments as those made by politicians for positions not included
in the traditional merit system of the permanent public service
(Makita  2022; Sancino et  al.  2018). Patronage appointments
represent the discretionary power of political actors to populate
non-­elected public sector roles, transcending legal and merit-­
based considerations (Kopecký et al. 2012; Panizza et al. 2019).
Accordingly, political appointments to management and advi-
sory positions allow a margin of discretion in choosing who is
responsible for the appointment.
However, consider a hypothetical scenario where a politician
appoints a bureaucrat to a position outside the traditional merit-­
based public service system, with express intent to select the
most qualified candidate for this role and with a genuine be-
lief in those qualifications. That is, the politician's motivation
is purely ideological and professional. Can we classify this ap-
pointment as patronage or a political appointment?
Our point is that it is extremely challenging to define a political
or patronage appointment. It requires identifying (1) exceptions
to traditional merit-­based public service systems and (2) deter-
mining the politician's motivation. However, this definition
remains purely theoretical due to a major empirical challenge.
How can we definitively ascertain a politician's true intentions
when his/her motivation is crucial to identifying an appoint-
ment as political? Indeed, a systematic literature review of pub-
lic appointments highlights the absence of explicit theoretical
approaches and a lack of empirical research in various areas.
Thus, there is a need for empirical studies grounded in formal
and clear theoretical frameworks (Cocciasecca et al. 2021).
We therefore propose two arguments. First, a political appoint-
ment is one that (1) serves the political motives of the appointing
officials—whether ideological, partisan, or personal—and (2)
involves elected officials in the appointment process, rather than
relying solely on merit-­based bureaucratic procedures. Second,
given the extreme difficulty in assessing an appointee's moti-
vation, we propose to examine exemptions from merit-­based
procedures as an indicator of political appointments. Hence, an
increase in such exemptions is a meaningful sign of political ap-
pointments and, consequently, politicization. Following Peters
and Pierre's (2004) definition of politicization as the substitution
of political criteria for merit-­based criteria in the selection, re-
tention, promotion, rewards, and disciplining of public service
members, we argue that when an administrative system experi-
ences an increase in exemptions from merit-­based procedures—
accompanied by increased political involvement in the selection
process—we may rightly suspect rising politicization.
2.1.2   |   Populism and Political Appointments
Over the past two decades, populism has surged globally, pos-
ing significant challenges to liberal democracies worldwide.
As with many concepts in the social sciences, the precise defi-
nition of populism is open to debate (Bauer  2024; 2021). We
conceive populism mainly as a political strategy character-
ized by thin-­centered ideology that frames politics as a moral
struggle between the “people” (us) and a “corrupt elite” (them)
(Caramani 2017; Mudde 2004). Thus, populism argues that po-
litical processes should channel the collective will of ordinary
citizens (Müller  2016). Accordingly, populist manifestations
span the entire political spectrum from radical right-­wing
(Kriesi 2014) and left-­wing movements (Clarke 1998; Philip and
Panizza  2013; Ramiro and Gomez  2017) to centrist political
groups (Buštíková and Guasti 2019; Hanley and Sikk 2016).
Populism is a major global phenomenon. In the United
Kingdom, populist forces fueled the Brexit vote to leave the
European Union. In the United States, borrowing from the rhet-
oric of the Tea Party movement, Donald Trump was elected to a
second term. Various other populist parties and politicians have
come to power, such as Jair Bolsonaro in Brazil, Viktor Orbán in
Hungary, the populist Law and Justice party in Poland, the popu-
list coalitions formed by the Five Star Movement and the League
party in Italy, the growth of AfD in Germany, the Candlelight
Revolution in South Korea, the populist Hindu nationalism pro-
moted by Prime Minister Narendra Modi and the BJP in India,
the Sweden Democrats in Sweden, and Recep Tayyip Erdoğan
in Turkey. Thus, populism is not only a contemporary political
zeitgeist (Mudde 2004) but also a durable political phenomenon
likely to spread to other contexts around the globe.
Strategically, populism typically materializes through two
primary mechanisms. The first is by championing readily un-
derstandable policy positions like “America First” slogans or
universal basic income proposals. The second is by systemati-
cally eroding public confidence in non-­populist opponents by
portraying them as puppets of entrenched, corrupt elites (Bellodi
et al. 2024; Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser 2018).
The ascendance of populist parties to governmental power has
intensified the scholarly focus on public bureaucracies within
affected nations (Bauer et  al.  2021; Peters and Pierre  2019).
This heightened attention emerges from a fundamental para-
dox: even the most ardently anti-­establishment administrations

Simultaneously, while populist leaders depend on state bureau-
cracies to operationalize their policy objectives, they frequently
and methodically deconstruct the liberal democratic founda-
tions of these administrative systems as a means of entrenching
their political dominance. As Bauer (2024, 1019) explains, under
populist governance, the bureaucracy assumes an ambivalent
role, simultaneously functioning as an instrument and a casu-
alty of the deterioration of democratic norms and institutions.
Scholars examining the intersection of populism, democratic
backsliding, and public bureaucracies emphasize politiciza-
tion—particularly political appointments—as a leading strategy
that populist leaders use to reshape public administration (Bauer
et  al.  2021; Bauer and Becker  2020; Peters and Pierre  2019,
2022). Populist leaders frequently do so with minimal regard for
institutional boundaries or expert assessments of policy feasibil-
ity and potential consequences (Paxton 2019). When confronted
with resistance from professional bureaucracies, these leaders
characteristically respond by replacing expert personnel and
those whom they regard as “disloyal” civil servants with ideo-
logically aligned supporters, thereby ensuring the unobstructed
implementation of their political objectives (Bauer et al. 2021;
Christensen et  al.  2014). Their actions hurt employee morale
(Resh et al. 2024) and the motivation of public servants (Gilad
et al. 2025).
Despite these theoretical insights and empirical findings from
around the world, empirical data and a systematic methodology
remain lacking. Moreover, as mentioned previously, due to the
numerous empirical challenges involved in identifying politi-
cal appointments, it is extremely difficult to accurately assess
growth in politicization trends in general and in political ap-
pointments in particular, even without considering populism.
We therefore propose to examine exemptions from merit-­based
procedures as an indicator of political appointments. An in-
crease in such exemptions is a meaningful sign of political ap-
pointments and, consequently, politicization. When the political
environment includes additional populist strategies, such as
attacking institutional gatekeepers, increasing centralization
through top-­down command structures, implementing budget
cuts to weaken liberal forces within public administration, un-
dermining the democratic norms of civil service management,
reducing external accountability, and limiting societal partici-
pation in government (Bauer 2024, 1022; Bauer et al. 2021), this
indicator becomes even more significant.
2.2   |   The Israeli Context
Israel's public policy landscape has distinctive features that
emerge from its historical, political, and social context. Eight
fundamental characteristics define Israeli public policy-­making
and have implications for populism and politicization (Cohen
and Sabah 2023, 2024). First, security considerations dominate
the formulation of policy, with defense and counterterrorism
taking precedence. Second, tensions arise from Israel's dual
identity as a Jewish and democratic state. Third, the country's
deep societal divisions stem from its demographic diversity. As
of 2024, Israel was comprised of 73.6% Jews, 21.1% Arabs, and
5.3% others. Fourth, neo-­liberal trends have transformed the
welfare state since the 1970s. Fifth, a centralized public admin-
istration, rooted in European governance concepts, maintains
tight control through institutional mechanisms. Sixth, political
instability is evident in frequent changes in ministerial leader-
ship and coalition-­based governments. The seventh feature is
non-­governability, characterized by the inability to design and
implement sustainable policies. Short-­term political consid-
erations often override long-­term strategic planning. Finally,
Israel has a distinctive “alternative politics” culture, where cit-
izens bypass formal institutions to fulfill their needs directly.
Together, these features shape Israel's complex policy environ-
ment, creating challenges for liberal forces seeking to oppose the
current populist trend (Cohen 2025).
Political appointments and political patronage have been part
of Israel's reality since its earliest days. Basically, Israeli law
does not permit political appointments. The civil service was
originally a largely closed bureaucratic system, modeled on UK
administrative traditions from the British Mandate period prior
to the state's establishment in 1948. However, the 1959 State
Service (Appointments) Law introduced a formal mechanism
for granting exemptions from the merit-­based selection process.
By the time of its enactment, 10 years after the Mandate ended,
legislative compromises had already produced a somewhat hy-
brid bureaucratic model.
The exemption process is inherently a political act initiated by
a cabinet minister or their director-­general, who directly asks
the Civil Service Commissioner to convene a special session of
the State Service Committee authorized by law to recommend
adoption of the draft decision. The Commissioner, appointed by
the government upon the recommendation of the prime min-
ister (and whose own appointment is exempt from standard
appointment procedures), then brings the exemption request
before the full Committee. This Committee is chaired by the
Commissioner and includes five directors-­general (all appointed
by the government based on personal recommendations from
the relevant ministers) and five additional members of the gen-
eral public, selected by the prime minister.
In practice, during the first decade of statehood, appointments
based on party affiliation were extremely common, continuing
the practice of the pre-­State organizations when Jewish com-
munity leaders sought to establish Jewish hegemony according
to the dominant community stream's worldview and through a
“party key” (Galnoor 2011). The Mapai party, which ruled be-
fore and after the founding of the state, made extensive use of
this tool. Political appointments were part of a power-­building
trend focused on caring for “our people” (Cohen 2016).
After the state's establishment, Prime Minister David Ben-­Gurion
worked to reduce political appointments, replacing patronage with
what he called “statehood” (mamlachtiut). Ben-­Gurion's approach
was based on the understanding that “civil servants are effectively
the state's rulers, although legally and practically they are subor-
dinate to the government and the law. The execution of the state's
actions is in their hands, and the state's honor and citizen's trust
depend on their behavior and actions…” (Dery and Sharon 1994,
38). In practice, within a few years, through a struggle between

is derived its obligation to refrain from appointing a candidate
solely based on their political affiliation…” (HCJ 313/67, 80).
The 1977 electoral victory of the Likud party represented a piv-
otal transformation in Israeli political dynamics, decisively ter-
minating the left's long-­standing hegemony and inaugurating an
era of conservative governance (Filc  2010). In the aftermath of
this watershed moment, the right wing dominated Israeli poli-
tics throughout most of the 1980s, with only a brief interruption
by a unity government with Ma'arach (1984–1986). During the
1990s, Benjamin Netanyahu's leadership transformed the Likud
party by introducing a neoconservative ideology (Avigur-­Eshel
and Filc 2021, 512). Serving concurrently as prime minister and
finance minister in the late 1990s and mid-­2000s, he promoted
two fundamental neoconservative principles: a geopolitical strat-
egy prioritizing national power through military, economic, and
diplomatic assertiveness, coupled with aggressive neoliberal eco-
nomic restructuring (Filc and Avigur-­Eshel  2024). Intermittent
challenges to Likud's supremacy emerged, with the left briefly se-
curing power under Ehud Barak (1999–2001) and centrists under
Ariel Sharon (2001–2006) and Ehud Olmert (2006–2009).
Following the Likud's disappointing electoral performance in
2006, Netanyahu astutely recognized the waning resonance
of neoconservatism within Israeli political culture. He strate-
gically repositioned the party using an exclusionary populist
narrative—a pivotal ideological transition that crystallized de-
finitively after his political resurgence in 2009 (Filc 2018). Since
then, the Likud's political influence has remained consistently
robust, reaching its apotheosis during Netanyahu's prolonged
tenure from 2009 to 2021. Since the 2010s, populism has in-
creased (Filc and Avigur-­Eshel 2024).
As of 2016, calls for greater control over the bureaucracy and
claims of the existence of a “deep state” waging a “battle” against
the elected leadership have intensified, increasing pressure from
politicians on the professional echelon. Indeed, starting with
Netanyahu's fourth government (2015–2019), and especially
from 2018 onward, public officials have expressed extremely
harsh criticism of senior civil service professionals and have
made increasingly virulent attacks on gatekeepers. Ministers
and Knesset members from the coalition claimed that the se-
nior bureaucracy was deliberately trying to thwart government
policy and create an alternative agenda to ministerial decisions
under the guise of neutrality and professionalism. The govern-
ment's actions weakened the civil service and the independence
of the bureaucracy (Tzur and Cohen 2024).
The weakening of the Israeli bureaucracy is backed by an in-
ternational survey. In the third wave of the 2020 Quality of
Government (QoG) Expert Survey (Nistotskaya  2020), which
evaluates public bureaucracies worldwide, Israel ranked in the
bottom third of 117 countries in terms of meritocratic recruit-
ment and political interference in bureaucratic appointments
(Nistotskaya et  al.  2021). Merit-­based recruitment is mea-
sured by the extent to which central government appointments
are based on skills and experience. In Europe, Norway, the
Netherlands, Sweden, the UK, and Denmark rank among the
highest. Patronage is assessed separately, with those same coun-
tries also ranking among the lowest in politically or personally
based appointments. This suggests that appointments in Israel's
central government are often influenced by political or personal
connections rather than merit, reflecting broader trends of po-
liticization and weakening bureaucratic impartiality. While the
survey is based on expert perception rather than direct empir-
ical data, it offers a valuable indication of trends in Israel and
supports the observation that the country has experienced in-
creasing politicization in recent years (D. Moynihan 2025).
After a short hiatus from power (2021–2022), Netanyahu or-
chestrated a dramatic political comeback in January 2023. His
reconstituted coalition represented a complex amalgamation
of political forces: his own populist Likud party, two ultra-­
Orthodox parties (Shas and Yahadut Hatora), and two radical-­
right entities (Religious Zionism and Otzma Yehudit).
In January 2023, Netanyahu's government introduced radical
judicial reforms through Justice Minister Yariv Levin, seek-
ing to limit judicial oversight. The proposed reforms encom-
passed a fundamental restructuring of the judicial selection
committee, effectively ensuring unilateral control over judicial
appointments, and introduced a provision empowering the
Israeli Knesset to nullify judicial decisions through a minimal
parliamentary majority of 61 members. The governing coalition
simultaneously set out to further weaken and disrupt the profes-
sional independence of the state bureaucracy, both by legislative
initiatives and by increasing political appointments, as we will
demonstrate. Despite widespread public demonstrations and so-
cial upheaval, the Knesset ratified the initial components of the
proposed constitutional revolution in July 2023. However, the
geopolitical landscape shifted dramatically on October 7, 2023,
when Hamas and Islamic Jihad executed a coordinated and
devastating assault on Israel. In response, a national unity gov-
ernment emerged to manage the ensuing conflict and mutually
agreed to suspend the constitutional revolution's implementa-
tion. The Supreme Court ultimately overturned the limitations
on judicial review in January 2024.
However, even during the war, the government has used var-
ious populist strategies extensively (Tucker 2024), such as un-
checked attacks on gatekeepers (Harel 2024), budget cuts, and
threats of personnel downsizing (Peretz 2024). In this context,

3   |   Methodology
The main pathway to appointment in the Israeli civil service
is through a public competitive process (Cohen 2016), but the
law provides for a range of exemptions from this requirement.
Currently, there are five main types of exempt positions:
1.	 Personal trust positions in the offices of the prime minister,
ministers, deputy ministers, directors-­general, and deputy
directors-­general, appointed in accordance with a series
of government decisions, culminating in the 2017 decree
establishing an exemption for deputy directors-­general
(Government Decision 3075). These positions are filled at
the discretion of the minister, deputy minister, or director-­
general and include junior and senior positions (“trust-­
based positions”).
2.	 Senior professional positions whose candidates are selected
or approved by the government. Some of these appoint-
ments require a recommendation from the appointments
committee, chaired by the Civil Service Commissioner.
Others require a recommendation from a committee tasked
with identifying candidates, which is appointed by the Civil
Service Commissioner (“senior professional positions”).
3.	 “Temporary,” non-­senior civil service positions (“tempo-
rary posts”).
4.	 Positions, mainly in the defense and healthcare sectors,
that have been exempt from competition since the enact-
ment of the 1959 Appointments Law.
5.	 Junior civil service positions are provided the individuals
are accepted into the service through employment offices.
The Appointments Law abolished the requirement for
entry through employment offices.
The first two categories, especially the senior professional staff,
but also the trust-­based positions, represent the most influen-
tial and significant ramifications regarding politicization in the
Israeli civil service; our discussion and conclusions will focus
mainly on them. Conversely, as the last two categories do not
carry significant concerns about political appointments, they
are disregarded in our analyses.
We examine the scope and nature of the positions exempt from
competition as an indicator of the phenomenon of political ap-
pointments in the Israeli civil service. To determine the number
of such positions, we sought to systematically identify and ana-
lyze all exemption decisions that the Civil Service Commission
discussed and approved, as the Appointments Law stipulates
that any exemption from competition requires a decision or rec-
ommendation from the Commission.
These decisions and recommendations are published on the web-
site of the Civil Service Commission, where data are available as
of 1997. To locate all relevant decisions, we used the following
search terms on the website: Civil Service Law, exemption from
competition, competition, Section 37, and Section 21 (the sec-
tions of the Appointments Law that allow such exemptions). We
also examined the relevant sections in the civil service regula-
tions (tachshir) and the guidelines issued by the Civil Service
Commission. In particular, we explored those related to em-
ployees in the offices of ministers, deputy ministers, directors-­
general, and their appointees, as some exemption decisions
regarding these trust positions occurred before 1997. Our find-
ings refer only to the civil service, not to the appointment pro-
cesses in various statutory authorities such as the Israeli army,
the security agencies, and the Bank of Israel, or to the appoint-
ment processes in local government and government-­owned
companies.
Note that the number of exempt positions largely exceeds the
number of exemption decisions. This is because several single
decisions granted exemptions for a large number of positions;
these generally involved junior, temporary, or trust-­based posi-
tions. In contrast, exemptions for senior professional positions
or key roles were usually made through specific decisions and
not approved as a whole. To complicate matters further, several
decisions granting exemptions did not specify the number of po-
sitions involved, although the wording of the decision suggested
more than one position. In such cases, we erred on the side of
caution, assuming each decision granted an exemption for only
two positions.
From this list of decisions, we sought to extract the number of
positions for which the Civil Service Commission approved an
exemption from competition or a merit-­based selection process.
We maintain that their number may provide important insights
into what is happening within the civil service, in line with the
idea that “quantity shapes quality.” It is clear that these num-
bers change frequently over the years, both in accordance with
the number of decisions made and due to the number of minis-
ters and deputy ministers agreed upon in coalition agreements.
Therefore, in counting exempt positions, we focus on only two
points in time: the base year we defined (2000) and the most
recent date for which updated information is available (April 30,
2024). All other analyses refer to exemption decisions over the
entire period (2000–2024), regardless of the number of exempt
positions. We believe that the number of decisions to exempt po-
sitions from competition or a merit-­based selection process may
teach us as much as the number of positions actually approved
for exemption.

there are now 1641 positions in the Israeli civil service exempt
from the competition or merit-­based selection requirement, ac-

number of exemptions in the history of the Israeli civil service.
As mentioned, the number of positions exempt from merit-­
based selection is more difficult to accurately determine than
the number of decisions; accounting for our choice to exercise
caution and undercount the former. While focusing on deci-
sions does not indicate the exact number of positions involved,
it does provide a measure of the dynamics in Israel's political
appointments.

current populist trend continues at the same pace, we anticipate
a record high in 2024 of 48 exemption decisions (Figure 1).

the years, with occasional minor and temporary declines. This
trend has strengthened since the inauguration of the current

The main goal of this study has been to empirically test the ar-
gument that populist politicians increase the number of political
appointments when they assume power. While the literature
identifies politicization and political appointments as leading
populist strategies (Bauer 2024; Peters and Pierre 2019), empir-
ical substantiation remains limited. Given the elusive nature
of political appointments, it is challenging to accurately assess
their true extent.
We argue that a political appointment is one that serves the po-
litical motives of the appointing officials and involves elected of-
ficials in the appointment process; rather than relying solely on
merit-­based bureaucratic procedures. However, this definition
remains largely theoretical, as we usually cannot definitively as-
certain a politician's true motivations.
Given the difficulty in assessing motivations, we have pro-
posed that exemptions from merit-­based procedures serve as
an important indicator of political appointments. Our reason-
ing is that an increase in such exemptions is a meaningful sign
of political appointments and, by extension, politicization. We
further argue that, when an administrative system experi-
ences a rise in exemptions from merit-­based procedures, ac-
companied by increased political involvement in the selection
process, this signals a clear trend of growing politicization.
Thus, the best way to obtain a data-­driven picture of the phe-
nomenon is to focus on positions exempt from the competition
process.
As we noted throughout, not all of the 1641 exempted positions
represent political appointments, since at least 518 were tempo-
rary. Nonetheless, following the rationale of others (Cocciasecca
et  al.  2021; Sancino et  al.  2018), we believe that a systematic
examination of exempt positions is an important indicator for
assessing and analyzing the phenomenon, as there is an insep-
arable connection between the number of exempt positions and
the phenomenon of political appointments. Thus, through a sys-
tematic analysis of all exemptions from competition or a merit-­
based selection process in the Israeli civil service from 2000 to
2024, we have provided evidence linking populism and political
appointments.
The vision of the founding fathers of the State of Israel was that
the recruitment of employees for public service and the pro-
motion processes within it should be based on the principles
of professionalism, equality, and transparency. By creating the
Civil Service Commission and passing the Appointments Law,
they clearly indicated that exemptions from competition or a
merit-­based selection process for public service appointments
should be made sparingly and in accordance with the values
of impartiality, equal opportunity, the merits of the candi-
dates, and their qualifications. However, as we demonstrated,
over the years, the practice of making improper appoint-
ments has resurfaced. Our analysis reinforces the findings
of the QoG, suggesting that Israel's civil service is vulnera-
ble to politicization and highlighting the need to strengthen
merit-­based recruitment to improve bureaucratic quality and
governance outcomes. Indeed, in recent years—especially
under the current government—this undesirable phenome-
non has reemerged in an unprecedented manner. The data we

tentative comparison between the Israeli civil service and the US

of positions that are not based only on competitive, merit-­based
selection is published every 4 years by the American president

the total number of employees that year is less than one third of
1% (0.0031). The percentage of political appointments in Israel,
according to the indicator we propose here, is thus significantly
higher than in the United States.
The Civil Service Commission is responsible for managing the
organizational framework within the Israeli civil service. It
is tasked with initiating, guiding, imparting, and overseeing
the human resources management system there. It is also re-
sponsible for ensuring its values, proper functioning, and ex-
cellence, as the civil service is expected to be impartial and
professional. As head of the Civil Service Commission, the
Commissioner is expected to be a critical institutional gate-
keeper in matters related to political appointments. Although
appointed by the prime minister and administratively subor-
dinate to him/her, the law grants the Commissioner indepen-
dent authority in areas such as appointments, particularly in
processes related to granting exemptions from competition or
a merit-­based selection process. The data we have presented
suggest that the Commission has been unwilling or unable to
halt the growing demand for such exemptions for more and
more positions, raising concerns that it is not serving as gate-
keeper of the civil service.
Given that political appointments under populist regimes often
occur alongside other strategies—for example, attacks on in-
stitutional gatekeepers, increased centralization through top-­
down command structures, budget cuts to weaken liberal forces
within public administration, undermining of the democratic
norms of civil service management, reduced external account-
ability, and limited societal participation in government (Bauer
et  al.  2021; Bauer  2024, 1022)—public administration schol-
ars should adopt a much more critical stance toward political
appointments.
5.1   |   Contributions, Limitations, and Future
Research
Our article contributes to the existing literature in several
ways. First, we propose exemption from competition or a
merit-­based selection process as a crucial metric for assessing
politicization, thereby providing scholars with an additional
tool for measuring this phenomenon. Second, applying this
methodological approach to the case of Israel, we present em-
pirical evidence for the contention that populist politicians
increase political appointments when they come to power. To
date, empirical evidence supporting this argument has been
scarce. We hope that our empirical grounding will further
support theory building and future comparative work, in-
cluding examination of cross-­ministerial variation, especially
given any differences in party affiliation and populism levels.
While our dataset is Israel-­specific, our proposed method can
be adapted to other contexts, assuming sufficient transpar-
ency and data availability.
Notwithstanding these contributions, our article has limita-
tions that open up new avenues for research. First is our sole
focus on the Israeli context. While case studies can serve ex-
ploratory, descriptive, or explanatory purposes (Yin  2009),
we cannot claim that findings in the relatively small and
young parliamentary democracy of Israel are representative of
larger, more established presidential democracies, especially
given that this case study has unique characteristics in terms
of time and policy domain. Therefore, results should be inter-
preted with caution.
Second, privacy protections in Israel limit access to individual-­
level personnel records, preventing accurate and ethical com-
parisons of candidate quality. As a result, our study could not
assess whether exemptions systematically lead to less-­qualified
appointments.
Last, our research design does not always allow for clear cau-
sality, making it difficult to determine how much of the politici-
zation phenomenon can be explained by the rise of populism as
opposed to other factors, like emergencies or presidentialization.
Nonetheless, there are several explanations that support our ar-
gument that populism, more than any other factor, drives the
increase in political appointments. First, as described above,
especially since 2016, we are witnessing not only an increase
in politicization but also a shift in the populist context marked
by unchecked attacks on gatekeepers, budget cuts, and threats
of personnel downsizing, centralization, and especially anti-­
institutional attacks on senior civil servants. Second, while wars
and crises have been the bane of many Israeli governments, only
in recent years has politicization increased. Third, the increase
in exemptions resulting from recent crises (war and pandemic)
has been manifested more in temporary posts than in senior
professional positions.
Despite these limitations, our findings offer valuable insights for
countries that face populist forces. We argue that, given the cur-
rent global rise of populist movements, public administration re-
searchers must develop a more rigorous and critical approach to
examining political appointments. While political appointments
can offer certain strategic advantages, in populist regimes their

Maintaining the integrity of appointments and ensuring that the
most qualified individuals work in the civil service strengthens
government institutions as a whole and contributes to the system
of checks and balances in democracy in particular. In contrast,
political appointments harm democratic institutions, lead to au-
thoritarianism, promote autocracy, and undermine the rule of
law. They impede proper governance and decision-­making pro-
cesses, erode the professionalism and impartiality of the civil ser-
vice, and drive away talented individuals who do not see it as a
place with development opportunities (Gilad et al. 2025). Political
appointments result in the replacement of skilled public servants
with often unqualified political operatives and ultimately leave
the civil service ineffective and powerless in the face of crises and
national tasks. When decision-­makers adopt such an extreme po-
liticization strategy, they deliberately choose to weaken the sover-
eign state and strengthen their own absolute power.
Based on our analysis, we maintain that the Israeli civil service
is on a very slippery slope of accelerated politicization. This
trend could lead to the eventual destruction of the civil service
and, as a consequence, the collapse of Israel's democratic re-
gime. This phenomenon is occurring precisely at a time when
Israel must establish and maintain a strong, professional, impar-
tial, capable civil service that can address the security, political,
economic, and social challenges it faces—challenges which are
unfortunately increasing in intensity.

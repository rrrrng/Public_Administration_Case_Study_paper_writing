Abstract
How do the professional backgrounds of senior bureaucrats affect their competence and political responsiveness? This article fills a gap by
examining these questions in a meritocratic context that accommodates nuanced but potentially consequential variations in the recruitment of
senior bureaucrats. Using a paired survey experiment with citizens, representatives, and administrators in Norway, the article demonstrates that
agency heads are perceived as less competent and—to a lesser extent—more politically responsive if their profile deviates from the meritocratic
ideal of the career civil servant with mission-specific expertise. The article also compares perceptions between groups of stakeholders, filling
another gap in the literature. Treatment effects go in the same direction across groups, but the results reveal a mismatch between popular and
insider perceptions of bureaucracy: whereas citizens are practically indifferent, administrators are deeply concerned about the competence of
an agency head who is a former politician rather than a career bureaucrat. Perceptions of substantive expertise are more aligned: all stakeholder
groups view agency heads with mission-specific expertise as more competent and less politically responsive than generalists. Overall, the
results demonstrate that variations in who is recruited to senior bureaucrat positions may either strengthen or undermine stakeholders’ views
on good governance.

Introduction
Senior bureaucrats play a key role in both formulating and
implementing public policies, which provides them with consid­
erable influence over policy decisions. Therefore, the power of
appointment is an important instrument for politicians to con­
trol bureaucrats’ exercise of delegated power. A significant body
of scholarship has studied how politicians use their appoint­
ment powers (Hollibaugh, Horton, and Lewis 2014; Kopecký
et al. 2016; Krause and O’Connell 2019) and the consequences
of politicized as opposed to merit-based appointments (Cooper
2018; Dahlström and Lapuente 2017; Lewis 2008; Oliveros and
Schuster 2018). Most studies find that merit-based appointments
have positive effects on different dimensions of good govern­
ance (see Oliveira et al. 2023 for a recent review). This article
addresses the consequences of senior bureaucrat appointments
within meritocratic contexts. The key defining feature of merit-
based bureaucracies is that they “select and reward candidates or
employees based on their individual merit and hard work rather
than for their political connections” (Suzuki and Hur 2022, p.
1229). Although this often means that senior bureaucrats—such
as agency heads—are recruited internally and possess mission-
related expertise, the professional backgrounds of senior
bureaucrats hired on merit have become increasingly diverse and
contested, as illustrated by the following examples.

Moving beyond anecdotal evidence, observers of coun­
tries with strong meritocratic traditions have noticed that
recruitment criteria for senior bureaucrats are shifting away
from a focus on mission-related expertise, giving prefer­
ence to generic leadership credentials or deep knowledge of
politics (‘t Hart and Wille 2006; Bourgault and Van Dorpe
2013; Institute for Government 2022; Trangbæk 2023). This
article seeks to shed light on the consequences of variations
in bureaucratic appointments in meritocratic contexts, fo­
cusing on two key aspects of delegation from politicians to
bureaucrats: the competence of those appointed to senior
positions  and  their balancing of being responsive political
superiors and their bureaucratic autonomy when advising
politicians and implementing policies.
A key finding in the literature on the consequences of
appointments is that the differential effects of merit-based
versus politicized appointments on administrative perfor­
mance emerge because of differences in senior bureaucrats’
professional backgrounds (Lewis 2007, 2008). Merit-based
recruitment, on average, results in the selection of more com­
petent people because political appointments involve loy­
alty—competence trade-offs (Krause and O’Connell 2019;
Ouyang, Haglund, and Waterman 2017). While there is limited
variation in hiring procedures for senior bureaucrats within
meritocratic contexts, those selected for leading positions
vary in their professional backgrounds. Senior bureaucrats
may either conform to the—implicit—ideal of an internally
recruited career civil servant with mission-related expertise or
may be considered hybrids when they have generalist mana­
gerial skills or when they have a background in politics.
This article asks how the professional backgrounds of senior
bureaucrats in meritocratic contexts affect stakeholders’
perceptions of those bureaucrats’ competence and political
responsiveness. To study the effect of variation in bureau­
cratic appointments, we use a pre-registered paired survey ex­
periment (N = 4,090) in Norway in which respondents were
shown a vignette with randomized information on the profes­
sional background of a hypothetical agency head. They were
subsequently asked about their views on the agency head’s
competence and his balancing of political responsiveness and
professional autonomy. The experiment was conducted with
three stakeholder groups—representatives, administrators,
and citizens—which allowed us to assess the differential
effects of bureaucratic appointments across all relevant ac­
tors in the democratic chain of delegation.
This article makes three main contributions. First, we con­
tribute to the research agenda on appointments and their
consequences (Dahlström and Lapuente 2017; Gallo and
Lewis 2012; Oliveira et al. 2023; Story, Lotta, and Tavares
2023) by adding to the few existing experimental studies
(Batista 2023; Hollibaugh 2016; Rogowski 2020). We ex­
pand this evolving scholarship by investigating whether
senior bureaucrats’ professional profiles have differential
effects on perceptions of senior bureaucrats’ competence and
how they balance political responsiveness and bureaucratic
autonomy in dilemma situations in the advisory and imple­
mentation stages of policymaking. We find lower levels of
perceived competence and higher levels of perceived political
responsiveness for agency heads deviating from the ideal of
the career bureaucrat with mission-specific expertise.
Second, we expand this literature’s scope to meritocratic
contexts, where any vacant position is, as a matter of prin­
ciple, given to the applicant with the best professional and
educational qualifications, and the competition is open and
fair (Cooper 2021; Suzuki and Hur 2022). Most existing
scholarship on effects of appointments is from the United
States—which represents an outlier case among developed
countries, with an exceptionally large number of political
appointees (Lewis 2008). Whereas recent comparative schol­
arship has highlighted the positive effects of merit-based, as
opposed to politicized, appointments on good governance
(Dahlström and Lapuente 2017), the article provides novel
insights into different types of bureaucratic competence and
the relationship between politics and administration within
merit-based bureaucracies. Moreover, our findings have the­
oretical implications that supplement previous insights that
citizens tend to prioritize professional expertise over political
experience when it comes to ministers (Rojon et al. 2023;
Vittori et al. 2023). Whereas our study focuses on adminis­
trative rather than political positions, we show that having
political experience is not problematic from the point of view
of citizens. Rather it is the lack of professional expertise that
citizens react to the most in their assessments of candidate
competence.
Finally, the study contributes to the growing literature on
elite–citizen gaps in political behavior and public opinion
(Dellmuth et al. 2022; Kertzer 2022; Sheffer et al. 2018). Even
in a context where the values associated with having a party-
political neutral bureaucracy are widely shared, perceptions
of senior bureaucrats’ attributes and these attributes’ effects
are likely to vary among stakeholders. We assess the differen­
tial effects of bureaucratic appointments on the perceptions
of two types of elites—administrators and representatives—
alongside citizens. In terms of appointee attributes’ effects on
elite and citizen respondents’ perceptions, the direction of the
treatment effects is surprisingly homogenous. However, treat­
ment effect sizes differ between citizens and administrators,
with administrators considering former politicians to be sig­
nificantly less competent than career bureaucrats. Whereas
both observational (Lewis 2007, 2008) and experimental evi­
dence (Hollibaugh 2016) indicate that hiring individuals who
are not career bureaucrats has deleterious consequences for
good governance, we show that citizens are practically indif­
ferent to the appointment of former politicians when it comes
to competence. The absence of a public opinion backlash, as
well as the expected gains in political responsiveness, may ex­
plain why governments chose to appoint former politicians
to senior bureaucratic positions. Overall, the findings dem­
onstrate that politicians may either strengthen or undermine
stakeholders’ views of good governance through their ap­
pointment practices (Rogowski 2020), even in contexts with
a highly meritocratic civil service.

The next section reviews the relevant literature. We sub­
sequently develop our hypotheses and provide an overview
of the article’s research context, followed by an account of
the data collection and research design. We then present and
discuss our experimental findings, including the implications
of stakeholders in a high-trust context displaying a degree of
skepticism about tasking ex-politicians to run government
agencies.
Bureaucratic appointments, competence, and
public opinion
A significant body of literature has examined how politicians
balance the importance of competence (a candidate’s
qualifications for the position) and loyalty (a candidate’s
alignment with policy preferences) for positions over which
politicians have discretionary authority (Bach and Veit 2018;
Hollibaugh, Horton, and Lewis 2014; Kopecký et al. 2016;
Krause and O’Connell 2019; Lewis 2008; Ouyang, Haglund,
and Waterman 2017). Moreover, research has highlighted the
problematic consequences of the politicization of bureau­
cratic appointments, including its potential for undermining
professional policy advice to political executives (Boräng
et al. 2018; Christensen and Opstrup 2018) and its nega­
tive effects on organizational performance (Gallo and Lewis
2012; Lewis 2007; Moynihan and Roberts 2010; Oliveira et
al. 2023; Wood and Lewis 2017) and the work environment
(Story, Lotta, and Tavares 2023).
As an alternative to the politicization of bureaucratic
appointments, senior bureaucrats can be appointed using
merit-based principles (Dahlström and Lapuente 2017;
Nistotskaya et al. 2021; Suzuki and Hur 2022). In substan­
tial terms, merit-based recruitment prioritizes candidates’
qualifications and competence over their political or other
connections with political leadership. In procedural terms, it
is characterized by minimal involvement of the political lead­
ership in the appointment process. There is empirical evidence
that merit-based recruitment has positive effects on various
aspects of good governance, such as administrative perfor­
mance and control of corruption (Dahlström and Lapuente
2017; Oliveira et al. 2023).
A key explanation for these effects is that merit-based re­
cruitment leads to the selection of more competent individuals.
For instance, Lewis (2007) shows how differences in the
performance of federal programs managed by either career
bureaucrats or political appointees are related to program
managers’ qualifications, which are higher among career
bureaucrats than among political appointees. Similarly, re­
cent studies have shown that senior bureaucrats’ professional
backgrounds affect citizens’ trust in government (Hollibaugh
2016) and how bureaucrat respondents assess top-level
bureaucrats’ task performance (Batista 2023; Lee and Park
2020).
Bureaucrats’ mission-related expertise has been identified
as a key driver of public trust (Hollibaugh 2016; Rogowski
2020). However, research on how background characteristics
matter for stakeholders’ views on appointees’ competence
and responsiveness to political leaders is currently lacking. In
addition, research fails to address the views of a key type of
actor in the delegation of authority—politicians. This article
sets out to fill these gaps.
Several experimental studies have investigated the
consequences of bureaucratic appointments on citizens’
perceptions of the government in the US. Hollibaugh’s (2016)
experimental study of the relationship between presidential
appointments to administrative agencies and public trust
found that appointing subject experts is associated with
increasing levels of perceived competence and trust in the
government. Interestingly, these findings apply to both ca­
reer bureaucrats and former politicians. Hollibaugh (2017)
studied the relationship between appointee characteristics
and respondents’ views on presidential priorities and their
support of the president’s policy. He found that higher levels
of competence are associated with a perception of higher
policy priority (as respondents assume that presidents ap­
point the most competent administrators to the positions that
matter the most to them) and higher policy support among
respondents. Again, the results are largely similar for career
bureaucrats and former politicians.
Rogowski (2020) used a survey experiment with vignettes
describing departmental staffing to investigate how bu­
reaucratic expertise and ideology affect citizens’ confidence
in bureaucratic institutions in the US. He found that loss
of expertise significantly reduces public confidence in bu­
reaucracy, which he explained as an informational shortcut
in which voters’ “evaluations of bureaucracies may depend
upon whether they perceive bureaucrats as well-informed
and competent” (Rogowski 2020, p. 33). He could not find
significant effects of bureaucrats’ ideologies on citizens’ con­
fidence in bureaucracy. A key takeaway from these studies
is that popular support for public policies and the govern­
ment may suffer if citizens consider bureaucrats to be poorly
qualified.
We are not aware of comparable public opinion studies
on the effects of staffing practices in meritocratic systems.
A recent study from Denmark showed that political mas­
ters rarely appoint pureblood party politicians to admin­
istrative leadership positions and that career trajectories
between politics and administration are largely separated
(Binderkrantz et al. 2023). However, administrative leaders
who either have a predominantly political career back­
ground or a mixed profile are occasionally recruited,
as shown in studies from Norway and Sweden (Askim
and Bach 2021; Dahlström and Niklasson 2013). These
practices may raise concerns about their suitability for the
position, as in the abovementioned example of the minister-
turned-bureaucrat in Norway.
As for the appointment of senior bureaucrats without
mission-specific expertise, Dutch scholars have denounced
indications of “a shift towards generic managerial qualities
rather than specialized knowledge and long-time experi­
ence in a policy domain” (‘t Hart and Wille 2006, p. 140).
One explanation for this shift is that governments have be­
come more committed to deliver on pledges to implement
major reforms, whereby procedural knowledge has become
more important and technical knowledge less important for
senior bureaucrats (Trangbæk 2023, pp. 612–613). Another
rationale is to encourage collaboration across departmental
boundaries and ministers attempting to “crowd out the old,
deep-rooted sectarian parochialism and in-fighting” in the
civil service culture (‘t Hart and Wille 2006, p. 140). Similar
observations of a declining relevance of specialist expertise
among senior bureaucrats have been made in several other
meritocratic countries (Askim and Bach 2021; Bourgault
and Van Dorpe 2013; Institute for Government 2022;
Trangbæk 2023).

have sought to explain differences in political attitudes,
turning to individual-level explanations and those related to
the characteristics of the institutions themselves (Dellmuth et
al. 2022). In a meta-analysis of studies comparing elites and
citizens, Kertzer (2022) found that elite–citizen gaps are often
exaggerated because scholars conflate dependent variables
and underestimate compositional differences between citizen
and elite respondents.
This article contributes to the literature on elite–citizen
gaps by comparing the effects of experimental treatments
on the attitudes of three types of elites toward bureaucratic
appointments. We follow Kertzer’s (2022, p. 544) approach
by investigating “whether elites and masses make significantly
different decisions as a result of the experimental treatments.”
Whereas separate studies have investigated the views of citizens
(Hollibaugh 2016, 2017; Rogowski 2020) and bureaucrats
(Batista 2023; Lee and Park 2020) on attributes of top-level
bureaucrats, studies including politicians as respondents are
missing. Moreover, we are unaware of existing studies di­
rectly comparing the attitudes of citizens, representatives,
and administrators regarding bureaucratic appointments in
an experimental design. To fill the abovementioned gaps in
existing scholarship, this article experimentally investigates
how appointee background affects perceived competence and
political responsiveness across elite and citizen respondents.
Analytical model: appointee background,
perceived competence, and political
responsiveness
Our point of departure is that, in a meritocratic context, bu­
reaucratic appointments that challenge the implicit ideal that
senior bureaucrats should be career bureaucrats with mission-
specific expertise have a negative impact on respondents’
views on appointees’ attributes and behaviors. We focus on
senior bureaucrats’ competence and their balancing of polit­
ical responsiveness and professional autonomy—two key out­
come variables in the literature on appointments (Hollibaugh,
Horton, and Lewis 2014; Lewis 2008; Ouyang, Haglund, and
Waterman 2017) and political–administrative relationships
(Christensen and Opstrup 2018). In this section, we develop
hypotheses about perceptions of senior bureaucrats’ compe­
tence (H1–H2), perceptions of their balancing professional
autonomy and political responsiveness in the policy advisory
(H3) and implementation stages of policymaking (H4), and
differences in treatment effects between elites and citizens (H5).
Meritocratic systems emphasize candidates’ qualifications
and competence for the position at hand. First, this often
means experience directly related to the core task of the or­
ganization in question, but meritocratic recruitment may
also prioritize generic leadership expertise over mission-
specific technical expertise (Hood and Jackson 1991, pp.
40–42). The example from the Netherlands in the article’s
introduction illustrates that the lack of such expertise could
be considered problematic, especially in agencies where
leaders are expected to have expertise at the same level as
their employees.
Second, meritocratic systems are characterized by the sep­
aration of political and administrative careers (Dahlström
and Lapuente 2017, p. 14). In an archetypical meritocratic
system, bureaucrats’ political connections are irrelevant to
their career progress, with the only relevant criterion being
their qualifications and competences. Moreover, Dahlström
and Lapuente (2017) consider the internal recruitment of
top civil servants to be a key component of meritocratic
bureaucracy, which clearly contributes to the separation
of political and administrative careers. Although hiring
someone with a political background may deviate from the
meritocratic ideal, it may still be justified as merit-based re­
cruitment if the candidate has the necessary professional
qualifications.
Finally, accounts of meritocratic systems typically high­
light procedural mechanisms for ensuring qualifications
and merit over political connections. They include the lim­
ited involvement of executive politicians in hiring and firing
senior bureaucrats (Dahlström and Lapuente 2017) and the
use of formal examinations for entering the civil service at
lower levels (Meyer-Sahling, Mikkelsen, and Schuster 2021;
Oliveros and Schuster 2018). Our research design focuses on
variations in appointee attributes while keeping the appoint­
ment procedure constant.
The independent variables in our model are the appointees’
expertise and career backgrounds. When it comes to expertise,
we follow Lewis (2007) and use “specialist” as shorthand for
senior bureaucrats with specialist knowledge related to their
organization’s mission and “generalist” for senior bureaucrats
without such expertise (see also Krause and O’Connell 2019).
More specifically, we assume that stakeholders have distinct
views related to the qualities expected of the actors involved
in policymaking (cf. Riccucci, Van Ryzin, and Lavena 2014;
Schmidt and Wood 2019). As already mentioned, experi­
mental studies of the politicized US federal bureaucracy show
that citizens’ trust in government (Hollibaugh 2016) and
specific agencies (Rogowski 2020) is associated with their
perceptions of bureaucratic leaders’ mission-related expertise.
While former studies often investigate stakeholders’ diffuse
support for such public bodies, our research design directly
measures respondents’ assessment of administrative leaders’
qualifications for leading an agency. However, our main ex­
pectation is the same: stakeholders’ support of these leaders
and the agencies’ policies will suffer if respondents consider
administrative leaders to lack the specialist expertise required
for the job.
Moreover, meritocratic contexts should differ from
contexts in which politicians more frequently become senior
bureaucrats, as a political background is more likely to be
considered a poor qualification for a top bureaucrat posi­
tion. We use “former politician” for senior bureaucrats with
a background as a senior-level executive politician and “ca­
reer bureaucrat” for senior bureaucrats with a classic civil
service background. The appointment of a former politician
in a meritocratic context may raise doubts about whether po­
litical connections and partisan or personal loyalty trumped

When it comes to balancing professional autonomy and
political responsiveness, we conjecture that the legitimacy of
government is created at several stages of the policy process.
This includes perceptions about what goes on in the advisory
stage of policymaking, where scientific evidence, statistics,
and other factual knowledge are fed into the policy formula­
tion process. This is not only a matter of connecting outside
scientific experts to decision-makers; perhaps even more im­
portant is the ability and willingness of senior bureaucrats to
offer alternative policy options to political principals and to
speak up if political preferences run contrary to what experi­
ence and scientific studies suggest are the best policy options.
Therefore, the legitimacy of the government and that of public
policies depend on stakeholders having faith in the ability of
the government apparatus to provide political principals with
high-quality and independent policy advice.
A well-known dilemma for administrative leaders is bal­
ancing the competing norms of professional autonomy and
political responsiveness (Christensen and Opstrup 2018).
Agency heads, for example, will occasionally experience
policy experts in their organization recommending a different
policy than that preferred by the political principal. In a mer­
itocratic system, stakeholders may doubt that a politician-
turned-bureaucrat can and will stand up to political leadership
and speak truth to power. While long-term career officials can
offer frank and fearless advice (Savoie 2003), a former poli­
tician might plan for a return to politics, limiting the will to
stand up to their present (but perhaps temporary) masters. In
line with our argument, survey findings from Canadian civil
servants show that merit recruitment positively affects em­
ployee voice: “The more civil servants believe that merit re­
cruitment is high, the less they fear reprisal for expressing a
dissenting opinion to their superiors” (Cooper 2018, p. 721).
Likewise, senior bureaucrats’ attributes may also affect
stakeholders’ views on the implementation of government
policies. One common dilemma arises when a bureaucratic
organization, despite having recommended policy A and ad­
vised against policy B, is instructed by the political principal
to implement policy B. Whenever government agencies have
some autonomy in implementing public policy, there is a
scope for senior bureaucrats to push operational implemen­
tation closer to the option recommended by the same bureau­
cratic organization. In other words, bureaucratic discretion in
policy implementation creates an opportunity for bureaucrats
to sabotage their political principal’s policy preferences
(Brehm and Gates 1997). Again, senior bureaucrats must bal­
ance the competing norms of professional independence and
political responsiveness. However, compared to the provision
of policy advice, bureaucratic norms on how to handle this
situation are much clearer, as bureaucrats should follow po­
litical directions in policy implementation even if their profes­
sional advice has not been (fully) considered.
We expect respondents to consider expert appointees
to display lower levels of political responsiveness (and
thus higher levels of professional autonomy) compared to
generalist appointees, who lack in-depth expert knowledge
and clear policy preferences related to the agency’s mission.
Moreover, we expect former politicians to be perceived as
more responsive to political leaders than career bureaucrats.
For career bureaucrats, being politically responsive is one
concern that must be balanced with professional autonomy.
The latter concern may arguably be more easily trumped by
political considerations for former politicians. Across the
policy advisory and implementation stages, we hypothesize
the following:
H3: Former politicians are considered more politically re­
sponsive (and less professionally autonomous) than career
bureaucrats.
H4: Generalists are considered more politically responsive
(and less professionally autonomous) than specialists.
Turning to elite–citizen gaps, we assume that perceptions
about administrative leaders’ attributes and behaviors are
likely to vary between different respondent groups. What
constitutes the “elite” status obviously varies depending on
the context and the topic (Kertzer and Renshon 2022). For the
present study, we argue that stakeholders have varying levels
of knowledge and interest in a meritocratic system’s norms
for senior bureaucrats’ competencies and for politician–bu­
reaucrat relationships in policymaking. We assume that
elites—stakeholders in or closely related to government—will
usually pay attention to conformity and deviance vis-à-vis
established norms for the hiring and profile of administra­
tive leaders, partly out of “who’s who” curiosity (especially
in small countries) and partly out of concern for maintaining
the existing “bargain” between politics and administration.
In a meritocratic context, elite stakeholders can be assumed
to perceive administrative leader profiles deviating from the
ideal type, that is, a specialist career civil servant, as indica­
tive of a disturbance to what they consider to be appropriate
political-administrative relations and the appropriate role of
expert knowledge in policymaking. Contrarily, citizens argu­
ably are less aware of the meritocratic norms, and therefore,
less attentive to possible breaches. Furthermore, citizens may
be primarily concerned with the output of public policies, for
instance, the access to and quality of services, rather than the
characteristics of the responsible actors (Schmidt and Wood
2019).
The level of interest citizens take in administrative leaders’
attributes and behaviors should depend on the empirical
context. For instance, research on the appointment of cab­
inet ministers across Europe has found that citizens prefer
executives with mission-specific expertise and non-partisan
experts (“technocrats”) over career politicians as cabinet
ministers (Rojon et al. 2023; Vittori et al. 2023). These studies
do not include data on Norway. However, they show that
citizens’ preferences for technocratic ministers are weakest
in Denmark and Finland, which, like Norway, have strong
merit-based bureaucracies. In other words, respondents in
these countries do not necessarily believe that technocrats
make for better cabinet ministers.
Building on these findings, we expect that citizens in

expect that citizens will take less interest in whether and
how background characteristics in either politics or admin­

Research context
The empirical testing bed for our five hypotheses is a study of
various groups’ attitudes toward bureaucratic appointments
in Norway. We used a survey experiment in which we
manipulated the career background and professional exper­
tise of a hypothetic director (chief executive) of the Norwegian
Immigration Authority. Thus, we held constant several
factors that potentially affect attitudes toward bureaucratic
appointments, such as trust in the government, country-level
rules and norms related to bureaucratic appointments, policy
portfolios, and position-dependent role expectations. We pro­
vide details on the recruitment process for agency director
positions in the Appendix (Box A1). Importantly, the selection
procedure itself is not manipulated in our research design, un­
like in other studies (Meyer-Sahling, Mikkelsen, and Schuster
2021; Oliveros and Schuster 2018).
Studying appointments in a highly meritocratic context is
relevant for several reasons. First, this allows us to measure
the direct effect of appointee background on perceptions of
competence and responsiveness. Existing studies show that
the appointment procedure itself—merit-based or political—
is an important driver of bureaucrat respondents’ views on
important outcome variables, including bureaucratic task
performance (Batista 2023) and corrupt behavior (Oliveros
and Schuster 2018). By studying a meritocratic context, we
can hold the appointment procedure constant in our research
design. Second, while acknowledging that the politicization
of appointments is a widespread and possibly increasing
phenomenon internationally, there are a significant number
of countries where top-level bureaucratic appointments
are largely merit-based (Nistotskaya et al. 2021). Although
political connections will only play a very limited role in
hiring decisions in those contexts, there are still significant
differences in the backgrounds of top civil servants. This
includes the occasional hiring of individuals who have pre­
viously served in political positions, which deviates from the
ideal-typical separation of political and administrative careers
(Dahlström and Lapuente 2017).
Norway has about 175 state agencies. All agency heads
report to a parent ministry, and the principle of ministe­
rial responsibility is comparatively strong. The Immigration
Authority reports to the Ministry of Justice and Public
Security; it has a semi-autonomous position, with ministe­
rial interference legally limited for decisions pertaining to
individual cases. The organization is known to most citizens
due to its high media exposure. Its tasks include processing
applications for asylum, family immigration, and citizenship;
making decisions to expulse rejected applicants; ensuring
temporary domicile to asylum seekers; and advising the gov­
ernment about policymaking.
Although the perceived political influence on personnel
decisions is comparatively low in Norway (Nistotskaya
et al. 2021), a longitudinal study found that 14 percent of
Norwegian agency directors between 1980 and 2020 had
backgrounds as former politicians (Askim and Bach 2021).
Importantly, ex-politicians affiliated with the opposition
are appointed just as often as those affiliated with the gov­
ernment, suggesting that governments seek “political craft”
more than partisan-based loyalty when appointing former
politicians. However, politicians-turned-bureaucrats typically
have a hybrid rather than a pure political career background.
Overall, Norway can be seen as a meritocratic system in
which ex-politicians are sometimes appointed to senior bu­
reaucrat positions, whereas this practice is virtually absent in
other, even “purer,” meritocratic contexts, such as Denmark
(Binderkrantz et al. 2023).
Expectations concerning top Norwegian civil servants’
interactions with their ministers are codified in government
guidelines (Ministry of Local Government and Modernization
2019). Agency directors and other top civil servants are ex­
pected to act as “loyal and constructive partner for the min­
ister, while simultaneously safeguarding the party-political
neutrality” of bureaucracy (Ministry of Local Government
and Modernization 2019, p. 16). Importantly, top civil
servants, including agency directors, remain in office after a
change in the government (or minister) and are expected to
serve the incoming political masters as loyally as they did the
outgoing ones. When asked for policy advice, top civil servants
shall “present their assessments using their best professional
judgment, regardless of the minister’s party-politically pre­
ferred line of action” (Ministry of Local Government and
Modernization 2019, p. 16). However, once a political deci­
sion has been made, it is considered the bureaucracy’s duty to
loyally implement that decision and use its discretion in line
with the political decisions’ objectives.

Discussion
The
experimental
findings
largely
corroborated
our
hypotheses on perceptions of competence. Overall, the
respondents considered a former politician to be less qualified
than a career bureaucrat for an agency head position (H1)
and a generalist to be less qualified than a specialist (H2).
For appointees’ subject expertise, our findings are strikingly
similar to those of studies conducted in the US. Hollibaugh
(2016) found the highest levels of perceived competence for
appointees with mission-specific expertise, irrespective of
whether an appointee is a career bureaucrat or a former poli­
tician. Likewise, Rogowski (2020, p. 46) showed that “survey
respondents expressed significantly lower levels of confidence
in bureaucratic institutions when bureaucrats were described
as having lower levels of policy expertise.”
Regarding appointees’ career backgrounds and perceived
competence, experimental evidence from the US suggests
that few citizens would raise an eyebrow upon hearing that
a bureaucratic leader had been recruited from the polit­
ical arena (Hollibaugh 2016). In Norway, where this prac­
tice is much less common (Askim and Bach 2021), more
would likely react. Nevertheless, senior bureaucrats being
former politicians is apparently a relatively modest concern
in Norway—one driven by strong treatment effects among
administrators (see below). What really matters for perceived
competence in Norway, as in the United States, is that senior
bureaucrats have specialist expertise.
Our study goes beyond prior experimental assessments of
the effects of appointments in two important respects. First,
we study not only senior bureaucrats’ competence but also
their behaviors and decisions, as suggested by Rogowski
(2020, p. 46). We studied perceptions of how appointees
handle administrative dilemmas in the advisory and imple­
mentation stages of policymaking. These outcome variables
measure the political responsiveness of senior bureaucrats,
which is a key concern in scholarship regarding the polit­
ical control of bureaucracy. We hypothesized that former
politicians would be considered more politically responsive
than career bureaucrats (H3) and that generalists would
be considered more politically responsive than specialists
(H4). Both hypotheses received support, although the results
differed between the outcome variables.
Treatment effects were somewhat weaker for the ad­
ministrative dilemma outcomes than for the qualification
outcomes. This is hardly surprising. Reflecting on the con­
nection between a person’s stylized CV and their perceived
qualification for a position is relatively easy—easier, at least,
than reflecting on the connection between a person’s CV and
their task performance once embedded in the organization.
That said, the difference in treatment effects suggests that,

Concerning the different dilemmas, the treatment effects

for the implementation stage; stakeholders trust that bureau­
cratic leaders, irrespective of their background or type of ex­
pertise, will know better than to sabotage the decisions of
their political principals.
Second, our study goes beyond prior research by comparing
treatment effects between citizens and elites. Based on the as­
sumption that citizens have less knowledge of and interest
in norms and practices inside the government than elites, we
hypothesized that treatment effects would be stronger for
elite respondents than for citizen respondents for all outcome
variables (H5). This hypothesis was not supported, although
we found distinct patterns that merit further attention.
For the first deviance from the meritocratic ideal, the hiring
of an administrative leader without mission-specific exper­
tise, the differences between respondent groups are small.
Given the high-trust empirical context, we expected that cit­
izens would be less concerned about administrative leaders’
profiles deviating from this aspect of the meritocratic ideal
than elites would. Instead, our citizen respondents share the
elite respondents’ concerns about bureaucratic competence
when an agency head lacks subject area expertise. In fact, we
found a stronger effect of this expertise treatment among citi­
zens than among elite respondents. This suggests that political
principals, who might see some benefits in horizontal recruit­
ment, for example, to curb sectarian parochialism (‘t Hart
and Wille 2006), should consider the potential costs in terms
of citizens” negative views on competence—and a potential
loss in public support—when hiring administrative leaders
without mission-specific expertise.
The picture is more nuanced for the second deviance from
the meritocratic ideal: the hiring of a former politician. We
observed no notable group differences for the administrative
dilemma outcomes, but competence evaluations differed in
ways we did not anticipate. While appointing a former poli­
tician to a senior bureaucratic post is considered unproblem­
atic by citizens and representatives, as far as the appointee’s
level of qualification is concerned, such a decision is clearly
frowned upon by administrators. If we assume that politicians
are cognizant that most voters are indifferent to this issue,
this can help explain why former politicians are occasionally
hired into senior administrative roles. Bureaucrats may voice
misgivings, but there will be no electoral penalty.
For competence evaluations of administrative leaders
who used to be politicians, we thus observe a gap be­
tween administrators and other respondent subgroups, not
the expected elite–citizen gap. This finding could reflect
administrators being particularly concerned about the chal­
lenge that hiring former politicians represents to the merito­
cratic ideal. They may also be negative about hiring former
politicians for self-interested reasons, as they see their own ca­
reer prospects endangered by external hirings. The latter ex­
planation resonates with the demotivating effects of replacing
career bureaucrats with political appointees at the top of US
federal agencies (Lewis 2008). Future research should inves­
tigate whether administrators’ skepticism toward external
hirings is driven by their dedication to the meritocratic ideal
or by their own career ambitions.
As a note of caution, the sample of representatives in our
study was more heterogeneous than would be considered
ideal. Only a very select group of politicians, consisting of
cabinet and junior ministers, have direct personal experi­
ence with the scenario in the survey vignette, that is, the gov­
ernance relationship between a government ministry and a
subordinate agency. Our panel of representatives primarily
contained politicians from the local and regional levels of
government. Some of the results concerning representatives
might, therefore, have been different if we had access to a
homogenous panel of national political executives. However,
the limited number of individuals with experience in poli­
tics at the national level places clear constraints on the feasi­
bility of conducting experimental studies. More importantly,
subnational levels of government in Norway have political–
administrative governance relations that resemble those at the
national level, and the political parties are mostly the same
at both levels. The respondents in our panel of representa­
tives therefore have experience and are embedded in political
cultures which makes them a good proxy for national execu­
tive politicians.
To build cumulative knowledge about the impact of con­
text and to facilitate comparison, O’Toole and Meier (2017)
encouraged scholars to specify the internal and external char­
acteristics of the organizations under study. Consider, there­
fore, that our experiment described a hypothetical leader for
a high-profile and politically salient agency. Respondents,
be they elites or citizens, might have reacted differently to
a scenario within a less salient sector. A more context-free
treatment could have solved this problem, yet we prioritized
ensuring a sufficient level of experimental realism to ensure
that the respondents truly engaged in the experimental task
(Mutz 2021). Although immigration is highly salient, the
Immigration Authority in Norway is not an extreme choice—
unlike, for example, a central bank or pharmaceutical regu­
lator, where task-specific expertise is evidently required. Not
using an extreme case strengthens the external validity of our
experimental findings.
As for external characteristics, consider that the study is
based on an experiment in Norway, a country whose govern­
ment bureaucracy values specialist expertise more than those
in, for example, France or the UK, where far more top civil
servants have generalist and multidisciplinary backgrounds
(Mangset 2017). The strong observed effect of the expertise
treatment, that is, the hypothetical agency director lacking
mission-specific expertise, might not be replicable in coun­
tries where administrative leaders’ legitimacy to a smaller
extent is dependent on their having “skills on par with the
professions that they manage” (O’Toole and Meier 2017,
p. 18). However, comparable studies from more politicized
contexts show that citizens generally prefer technocratic
politicians (Lavezzolo, Ramiro, and Fernández-Vázquez
2021; Rojon et al. 2023; Vittori et al. 2023) and bureaucrats
with mission-related expertise (Hollibaugh 2016; Rogowski
2020). We expand the insights from these studies, as our
results suggest that citizens are not necessarily skeptical of
agency leaders with political experience. Rather, it is their
lack of professional expertise that people react most strongly
to.
As for the “former politician” treatment, we consider
Norway neither a most likely nor a least likely case for

stakeholders to be skeptical about senior bureaucrats being
former politicians. As mentioned, a pattern of a considerable
minority of Norwegian agency heads being former politicians
has persisted for decades, without noticeable controversy
(Askim and Bach 2021). As a further external condition, con­
sider that Norway’s political system is unitary and centralized
and that its public sector is well-resourced. Bureaucrats’ more
negative competence assessment for an agency head being a
former politician might not be replicable in a more fragmented
political system with a less prosperous public sector. In such
a context, there might be more support from within to have
a former politician at the head of a government agency, as
this background would equip them with the political skills
needed to maneuver and secure political and financial sup­
port (O’Toole and Meier 2017, p. 8). To incrementally build
systematic knowledge about the factors affecting competence
and political responsiveness in bureaucratic appointments,
future experimental research should elaborate on the opinion
effects of expertise and career background across policy
sectors and systems to test the robustness of our findings and
broaden systematic knowledge about context-specific vari­
ations. Rather than replicating this study’s design, however,
scholars should make adaptations to ensure that the experi­
ment depicts a realistic scenario and that the treatments cap­
ture context-specific points of contestation.
Finally, although political responsiveness and adminis­
trative autonomy are opposite poles in the administrative
dilemma scenarios used in this study, there are arguably nu­
merous other scenarios in which they are compatible or even
complementary. A political principal might be unhappy with
a sycophantic senior bureaucrat, and many politicians will
understand that their long-term success is best served by
senior bureaucrats speaking truth to power based on their
professional autonomy. However, in any decision-making
process in a democratic system, there comes a point when
the political principal says the time for deliberations and
counterarguments is over, and a decision must be made
(Christensen and Opstrup 2018). At that point, which can
obviously be depicted differently than we have done in this
study, political responsiveness and administrative autonomy
are opposite poles for senior bureaucrats.
Conclusion
This article used a unique paired survey experiment with
administrators, representatives, and citizens in Norway
to study how the background characteristics of senior
bureaucrats affect perceptions of their competence and ex­
pectations about their handling of administrative dilemmas.
This article finds positive effects on competence evaluations
when appointees conform to the traditional meritocratic
ideal, that is, when they are specialists rather than generalists
and—to a smaller extent—when they are career bureaucrats
rather than former politicians. The effects on political respon­
siveness outcomes are mixed, indicating that respondents ex­
pect that organizational elements will dampen any effect of
an individual leader on policy advisory and implementation
processes.
Treatment effects differ between respondent groups on
some outcome dimensions, although not typically along
the hypothesized elite–citizen divide. Most importantly, we
find clear differences in treatment effects between citizens
and administrators, indicating a mismatch between popular
perceptions of bureaucracy and administrators’ own insider
views. In particular, our finding regarding citizens’ indiffer­
ence to the competence effects of hiring former politicians
stands in contrast to observational research, showing that
hiring career bureaucrats has positive effects on performance
and—more generally—good governance (Dahlström and
Lapuente 2017; Lewis 2008).
To be sure, the relevant outcome variable directly measures
competence rather than the quality of services provided or
control of corruption, which are common indicators of good
governance. That said, an important implication of our study
is that appointing former politicians to leadership positions
can ensure good governance, in the sense of having qualified
individuals, as long as there is no trade-off with candidates’
professional expertise. This interpretation is consistent with
research showing that the negative effects on performance
due to political appointees in managing positions are caused
by these appointees having lower levels of mission-related ex­
pertise (Gallo and Lewis 2012).
Citizens are not indifferent about appointee attributes,
however. They care about senior bureaucrats’ expertise, pre­
ferring mission-related expertise over generalist expertise.
Our findings, therefore, demonstrate the potential of bu­
reaucratic appointments to either strengthen or undermine
stakeholders’ perceptions of good governance, understood
as a government with competent bureaucrats who dare to
“speak truth to power” and who loyally implement author­
itative political decisions. All our respondent groups are
most aligned with this understanding of good governance
when career civil servants with task-specific expertise are
selected for top bureaucratic posts. Therefore, if observers
are correct in projecting an international trend moving re­
cruitment practices away from the classic meritocratic ideal
(‘t Hart and Wille 2006; Bourgault and Van Dorpe 2013;
Institute for Government 2022; Trangbæk 2023), a decline
in stakeholders” beliefs about good governance is a likely
consequence.
Finally, our research has policy implications. We draw the
lesson that governments should clearly communicate the pro­
fessional experience of bureaucratic appointees who have
been in politics to prevent stakeholders from focusing solely
on potential negatives. A more formalized innovation would
be to expand the conception of relevant qualifications by
making it explicit in regulations and vacancy announcements
that for selected agency head positions, knowledge of
the inner workings of the machine room of government is
considered a meriting qualification—if not a core qualifica­
tion, then a supplementary one that can tip the balance be­
tween candidates who rank equally in terms of the more
classic merits. Although political nous can be incorporated
into the explicit deliberation about candidates’ qualifications,
instead of being an “unspeakable” part of the opaque consid­
eration of “personal fit” to the position, it should not replace
lacking managerial or technical qualifications.
If knowledge of the inner workings of politics were an ex­
plicit selection criterion, it might help bureaucrats to accept
ex-politicians as their leaders. Such an innovation would also
be good news for politicians who worry about their employ­
ability in the government sector—if they can manage to ac­
cumulate a fair amount of extra-political experience, they
should be fine. For small countries, avoiding the ostracism of
former politicians has an additional benefit—it enlarges the
recruitment pool for top administrative positions.

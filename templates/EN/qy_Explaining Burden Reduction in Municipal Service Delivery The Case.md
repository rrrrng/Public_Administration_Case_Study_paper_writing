ABSTRACT
Administrative burdens are deliberate policy choices, as governments can proactively design rules and procedures to shape
burdens experienced by citizens. While existing research has examined the effects of burden reduction strategies, less is known
about the factors driving burden reduction actions at the local level. This paper investigates why city governments adopt stream-
lining ordinances to reduce burdens in permit service delivery, focusing on the electric vehicle charging station permitting
process. Using data from 482 California cities between 2015 and 2023, we find that cities with stronger fiscal capacity, more
climate-­concerned, and whiter populations are more likely to institutionalize burden reduction practices in streamlining ordi-
nances. Contrary to expectations, neither mass political ideology nor political leader (mayoral) partisanship has a significant
impact on municipal permitting reforms. Our findings demonstrate how capacity constraints, distributive politics, and policy-­
specific constituency preferences jointly shape local governments' willingness to reduce burdens in public service delivery.
1   |   Introduction
The complexity inherent in regulation and program design cre-
ates barriers to compliance across many policy areas. Public ad-
ministration scholars have traditionally studied such challenges
through the concept of red tape (Bozeman 1993; Bozeman 2000;
Pandey and Bretschneider  1997; Pandey and Scott  2002).
Recently, this line of inquiry has converged with the adminis-
trative burden literature, which defines burden as “an individu-
al's experience of a policy implementation as onerous” (Burden
et al. 2012, 746).
Moynihan et  al.  (2015) propose to measure burdens by the
tangible and intangible costs individuals face when engaging
with government programs, including learning costs, compli-
ance costs, and psychological costs. Building upon their work,
most empirical research has examined burdens through the
perspective of individual citizens. Individual characteristics
such as health status and race/ethnicity have been shown to
explain variation in how burdens are experienced (Barnes and
Henly  2018; Chudnovsky and Peeters  2021; Bell et  al.  2023).
Studies further demonstrate that individuals interpret and re-
spond to burdens differently across populations (Masood and
Nisar 2021). Moreover, individual burden experiences can have
significant policy and political implications, as they influence
whether and to what extent people participate in government
programs (Fox et al. 2023), and more broadly, how people engage
in political realms (Keiser and Miller 2020; Zhu et al. 2024).
Recent scholarship increasingly acknowledges the politically
constructed nature of administrative burden, that is, burden cre-
ation, distribution and reduction are deliberate political choices
(Moynihan et al. 2015; Herd et al. 2023). Formal and intentional
burdens can stem from intentional design of administrative

1.1   |   Burden Reduction as Intentional State Action
Burden reduction refers to “state action that directly or indi-
rectly reduces administrative burdens” (Benish et al. 2024; 350).
Governments can use a range of measures to effectively lower
burdens. For instance, unified and simplified program applica-
tion processes have been shown to reduce administrative bur-
den for applicants, which in turn increases program take-­up in
Medicaid (Herd et al. 2013; Fox et al. 2020; Camillo 2021) and
reduces job candidate drop-­off in police recruitment (Linos
and Riesch  2020). Reduced document requirements lowered
burdens in COVID-­19 small business relief fund application
(Bell, Kappes, et al. 2024), while online interviews and appoint-
ments improved WIC participants' experiences (Barnes and
Petry 2021). Similarly, the adoption of categorical eligibility has
been associated with higher enrollment and greater program ef-
ficiency in the Low-­Income Home Energy Assistance Program
(Graff 2025).
While this body of scholarly work demonstrates the benefits of
burden reduction strategies, less attention has been paid to bur-
den reduction as a policymaking tool and as an intentional state
action situated within the broader political context (Moynihan
et al. 2015; Herd et al. 2023). In this sense, burden reduction is
not only a managerial or technical adjustment but also a pol-
icy decision that reflects government priorities, constraints, and
governance logics. In addition, to purposefully alter burdens
in administrative processes, governments often need to design
and adopt administrative rules and regulations (Moynihan
et al. 2016; Peeters 2020). These formal rules, falling between
two extensively studied streams of literature—policy formation
and administrative behavior, are overlooked and understudied
(Moynihan et al. 2016).
In practice, governments increasingly use burden reduction
not only to enhance efficiency but also to pursue broader pol-
icy goals. For example, Canada's Red Tape Reduction Act (2012)
aims to attract business investment by reducing administra-
tive burdens. Within this context, a key question that warrants
greater attention is when and why governments choose to
adopt burden reduction actions. Existing research in this area
has largely relied on in-­depth case studies of a single jurisdic-
tion's burden reduction choices. Herd et al. (2013), for instance,
use interviews and documentation review to narrate how a
Democratic administration in the state of Wisconsin sought to
reduce burdens in Medicaid implementation. Some other stud-
ies focus on individual traits of political leaders. Using survey
and observational data with Danish local politicians, Baekgaard
et  al.  (2021) find that politicians who are right-­wing or those
without a personal experience of receiving welfare benefits are
more burden tolerant. While these studies consistently highlight
the importance of political preferences, they leave open import-
ant questions about the broader patterns of burden reduction
choices by local policy makers in the United States, and factors
shaping these patterns.
The main reason for this gap is both empirical and methodologi-
cal: there is often a lack of comparable measures and data of bur-
den reduction strategies across jurisdictions to allow for large-­N
analyses. As a result, rigorous quantitative research on why pol-
icymakers decide to increase or reduce burdens remains scarce.
Moynihan et al. (2016)'s cross-­sectional study of state variation
in Medicaid application procedures is one of the few exceptions.
They find that states experienced continuous Democratic party
control tend to have lower administrative burdens (Moynihan
et al. 2016). Building on and extending their work, this paper
examines burden reduction efforts in municipal permit service
provision. We focus specifically on California cities’ decisions
to streamline the electric vehicle charger permitting process.
Using a longitudinal dataset covering 482 California cities from
2015 to 2023, we answer the following research question: what
factors drive cities’ decisions to reduce burdens in the electric
vehicle charger permitting process?
Results of the event history analysis provide strong evidence
that local governments' decisions to reduce burdens in permit
service delivery are shaped by governmental capacity and com-
munity demographic characteristics. Specifically, cities with
stronger fiscal capacity and whiter constituents are more likely
to adopt ordinances that institutionalize streamlined permitting
practices. This finding highlights disparities in burden distribu-
tion across communities in municipal permit service provision,
reflecting differences in both resource constraints and con-
structed images of target populations. Complementary cross-­
sectional analyses using Heckman selection and fractional logit
models further corroborate this finding. The results show a neg-
ative association between administrative ease in the permitting
process and the proportion of minority residents.
While prior research emphasizes the role of partisan control and
political ideology in shaping burden levels in state and federal
programs (Moynihan et al. 2016; Lavertu et al. 2013), our results
suggest a more nuanced effect of local political preferences. We
find that neither mass political ideology nor mayoral partisan-
ship has a significant impact on municipal permitting reforms.
Instead, cities with more climate-­concerned constituents are
more likely to streamline the electric vehicle charger permitting
process. This suggests that streamlined permitting is framed
and enacted as a targeted climate strategy that resonates with
public demand for more local mitigation actions, rather than

This paper makes several contributions to the existing literature.
It advances the administrative burden literature by shifting
the analytical focus from individuals’ experiences to govern-
ments’ policy making choices. Our paper responds to Moynihan
et al.'s (2016) call to examine burden reduction as a tool of policy
design. By coding administrative rules outlined in municipal
streamlining ordinances and constructing an administrative
ease index, we link formal policy design choices to the level of
burdens citizens are likely to experience. This approach demon-
strates that local governments actively shape burdens not only
through implementation, but also through deliberate policy for-
mation and design.
In addition, our study broadens the scope of administrative bur-
den research in two important ways. First, while prior studies
have primarily examined the political economy of burdens in
the context of citizen access to social and political rights (Burden
et al. 2012; Moynihan et al. 2016; Heinrich et al. 2022), we an-
alyze burden reduction in local permit service provision, an
overlooked yet important policy area that shapes interactions
between citizens and local governments. The design of per-
mitting processes not only directly affects citizens’ perceptions
of local government responsiveness and capacity, but also in-
fluences the speed of clean technology diffusion (e.g., electric
vehicle chargers and solar PV). Our paper hence broadens the
substantive policy areas in which burdens are studied. Second,
we address the scarcity of quantitative evidence on why policy-
makers choose to increase or reduce burdens. Leveraging a novel
longitudinal dataset of 482 California cities from 2015 to 2023,
we provide empirical evidence on the local-­level determinants
of burden reduction efforts. In doing so, our analysis extends
Moynihan et al.'s (2016) cross-­sectional study of state Medicaid
application procedures, offering a quantitative, city-­level assess-
ment of the conditions under which governments pursue burden
reduction in public service delivery.
In the following sections, we first describe the empirical con-
text of our research, hypotheses about factors driving municipal
burden reduction decisions, and data and research design. We
then present results of our analysis. We conclude this article by
discussing theoretical contributions and policy implications of
our research findings.
2   |   Empirical Context: Streamlined EVCS
Permitting in California
In recent years, the expansion of electric vehicles (EV) and their
supporting charging infrastructure has become a critical policy
goal in California. With the rapid EV deployment, the local per-
mitting process for EV charging stations (EVCS) has emerged as
a major bottleneck (Hickerson and Goldsmith 2019). To install a
residential or commercial EV charger, property owners are typi-
cally required to obtain a building permit. This process ensures
that installations comply with local safety codes, zoning regula-
tions, and electrical standards. Permit applications are usually
submitted to the city or county building department, where staff
review the proposed installation and issue the permit.
Applying for and obtaining a permit for EV charger installation
can be a time-­consuming task. Property owners can apply for
permits by themselves or work with contractors to file permits.
Between 2010 and 2024, local governments in California re-
ceived more than 96,957 EVCS permit applications, averaging
over 6000 per year since 2018 (Shovels, Inc 2025). The average
approval time is 25 days, and it is not uncommon for applicants
to spend several months to secure a permit (Shovels, Inc 2025).
In recognition of the need to accelerate EV infrastructure de-
ployment, the California legislature adopted Assembly Bill 1236
(AB1236) in October 2015. AB1236 requires local governments
to adopt an ordinance that creates expedited and streamlined
processes for EVCS permit application and review, with the ex-
plicit goal of removing obstacles and minimizing costs for both
public and private EVCS permits and facilitating EVCS installa-
tions (see Appendix A1 for a detailed summary of AB1236 legis-
lative goals, language and key requirements).
EVCS permitting in California provides an ideal empirical case
for analyzing drivers of burden reduction decisions. First, unlike
many policy areas where burden reduction occurs informally or
sporadically, AB1236 explicitly mandates local governments to
streamline administrative processes in pursuit of widely shared
sustainability goals. It offers a rare opportunity to observe city
burden reduction decisions through identifiable and formal poli-
cies that shape burdens in citizen-­state interactions. Second, this
state mandate provides neither financial incentives to encourage
streamlining practices nor penalties to punish noncompliance.
Cities are encouraged to comply through the adoption of model
practices outlined in a state-­issued guidebook,1 while having
discretion over the design of their own streamlining efforts. This
allows us to study the variation of burden reduction across cities.
3   |   Factors Driving Burden Reduction in
Municipal Permit Service Provision
Government adoption of burden reduction actions is rarely po-
litically neutral. Instead, it is part of the political economy of
public organizations, reflecting the priorities, constraints, and
incentives that local policymakers face (Peeters  2020). In the
following sections, we discuss three hypotheses about factors
influencing cities' burden reduction decisions.
3.1   |   Governmental Capacity
The existence and degree of administrative burdens reflect a
government's ability to operate in accessible, simple and respect-
ful ways (Herd and Moynihan 2018). A lack of governmental ca-
pacity can cause implementation failures and generate burdens
(Herd and Moynihan 2018; Peeters 2020). When shifting bur-
dens from individuals to the state, administrative capacity plays
a key role in determining the success of those administrative
reforms (Herd et al. 2013; Heinrich 2016). For instance, Aiken
et al. (2023) find that staff capacity constrained the adoption of
streamlined application platforms in emergency housing assis-
tance programs. Similarly, Pierce and Moulton (2023) show that
streamlining the application process for a foreclosure preven-
tion program without sufficiently expanding state capacity may
simply substitute one form of administrative burden for another.

From the policy design and implementation perspective, trans-
lating a state mandate into local policy prescriptions requires
significant commitment of personnel, technical expertise, and
financial resources (Walker  1969). The financial burden is
especially high when mandates are unfunded, meaning local
governments must shoulder the full financial cost of com-
pliance (Kelly  1994; Grossback  2002). A state streamlining
mandate, such as AB1236, can exacerbate these challenges
by requiring local building officials to adopt new permitting
procedures. These can include transitioning from manual,
paper-­based processes to automated online systems and pro-
viding written deficiency notices to applicants. Implementing
those changes typically requires substantial technical ca-
pacity and investment (e.g., skilled IT staff or stand-­alone IT
division), and additional bureaucratic training and platform
optimization (Shi and Wang 2025), all of which are more ac-
cessible to governments with abundant resources and capacity
(Lampe 2017; Shahi et al. 2019; Chen and Gant 2001). Thus,
we expect that cities with greater governmental capacity are
better positioned to adopt and implement burden reduction
actions in permit service provision.
H1.  Cities with greater governmental capacity are more likely to
reduce burdens in permit service provision.
3.2   |   Local Political Preferences
Politics play a central role in both the creation and reduction
of administrative burdens (Moynihan et al. 2015). Existing re-
search shows that partisan control and ideology shape burdens
in welfare and social policy settings. For example, at the U.S.
state level, Democratic control is associated with less burden-
some Medicaid enrollment procedures (Moynihan et al. 2016).
State administration led by a Democratic governor is also more
likely to reduce burdens for social welfare programs (Herd
et al. 2013). Similarly, local politicians' ideology explains burden
tolerance: right-­wing politicians in Danish cities are found to
be more burden tolerant in the social policy setting (Baekgaard
et al. 2021).
In the U.S. subnational context, local policymaking differs
from the state level in that administrative rules are not en-
acted through legislatures but through city councils. Early
scholarship in local politics suggests political ideology and
partisanship have limited influence on local service pro-
vision, as local government decisions are driven mostly by
pragmatic considerations rather than ideological motivations
(Hirsch 1995; Bel and Fageda 2007; Oliver et al. 2012). More
recent research challenges this view, showing that city policy
outcomes (e.g., spending decisions) are responsive to citizen
opinion and partisanship (Hajnal and Trounstine 2010; Gerber
and Hopkins 2011; Tausanovitch and Warshaw 2014; Einstein
and Kogan 2016).
If burden reduction was understood as a deliberate policy de-
sign choice, it is reasonable to expect that city policy makers
take constituents' political preferences into account when de-
ciding whether to streamline permitting or not. One relevant
dimension of local political preference concerns regulatory ef-
ficiency and deregulation. Since AB1236 is designed to reduce
costs and delays associated with permitting, constituents who
value government efficiency and deregulation may be more
likely to support streamlined permitting. In addition, stream-
lined and expedited permitting also signals less restrictive land
use practices. Prior research shows that Democratic communi-
ties often support more constrained land use regulations to miti-
gate environmental externalities and protect public safety (Kang
and Feoick 2006). Republicans tend to favor deregulation and
reduced land use control (Coggburn 2001; Cooper et al. 2008;
Bjørnskov and Potrafke  2013). Given that permitting aims to
protect public health and safety, we expect cities with more con-
servative electorates to be more likely to streamline the EVCS
permitting process.
A second dimension is public attitudes toward climate change.
At its core, AB1236 and municipal streamlined EVCS permit-
ting are embedded within the state's broader climate and de-
carbonization agenda. Citizens who believe in climate change
may view streamlining as a legitimate and necessary climate
mitigation measure. On the contrary, policymakers opposed to
a policy will support burdens that limit access to policy benefits
(Herd and Moynihan 2018). In communities where public cli-
mate awareness is low, city policymakers may be less motivated
to comply fully with the state mandate, opting for noncompli-
ance or bureaucratic delay.
H2a.  Cities with more conservative constituencies are more
likely to reduce burdens in permit service provision.
H2b.  Cities are more likely to reduce burdens in permit service
provision when local public opinion favors climate action.
3.3   |   Demographic Characteristics
of Target Groups
The theory of social construction posits that policy design
reflects policymakers' perceptions of the political power
and deservingness of different social groups (Schneider and
Ingram 1993). Groups constructed as politically powerful and
socially deserving are more likely to receive benefits, whereas
groups perceived as less deserving or politically weak are more
likely to experience sanctions and burdens (Schneider and
Ingram 1993).
Welfare scholars have operationalized deservingness through
the CARIN criteria—control, attitude, reciprocity, identity and
need (van Oorschot 2000). Among these dimensions, identity re-
fers to the perceived proximity between a target group and one's
own social grouping (van Oorschot 2000). Recent scholarship
argues that the CARIN framework can be extended beyond wel-
fare policy to specify Schneider and Ingram's notion of deserv-
ingness in broader policy contexts (Crabtree and Wehde 2023;
Blum and Kuhlmann 2025). Group identities such as race, eth-
nicity, and socioeconomic status are closely tied to social con-
struction and deservingness of target populations, which have
important implications for policy design. For instance, federal
low-­income homeownership policy has been shown to allocate
benefits to private mortgage lenders while shifting burdens onto
low-­income homeowners (Drew 2013). Similarly, in both edu-
cation and environmental policy domains, more punitive and

In administrative contexts, research demonstrates that politi-

Many have further documented the prevalence of racialized
administrative burdens, showing that racial minorities face

Building on the literature, we argue that local governments'
decisions to actively reduce administrative burdens are condi-
tioned by the constructed images of the target population. In
permit service delivery, local policy makers have discretion in
designing administrative rules and routines (e.g., streamlining
measures) that can either increase or reduce burdens for permit
applicants. When the anticipated beneficiaries of streamlined
permitting procedures are perceived as politically powerful
or deserving, policy makers may be more willing to invest in
burden-­reducing reforms. In the case of EVCS permitting, these
constituencies are often more affluent and predominantly white
residents who are more likely to adopt EV and home charging
infrastructure (Lee et al. 2025). Therefore, we expect that de-
mographic characteristics of the target population, particularly
income and racial composition, are associated with local gov-
ernments' burden reduction decisions.
H3.  Cities are more likely to reduce burdens in permit service
provision when their constituencies are more affluent and have a
larger white population.
4   |   Data and Methods
4.1   |   Dependent Variables: City Burden Reduction
Actions
We measure cities' burden reduction actions using two vari-
ables. The first variable is the adoption of a municipal stream-
lining ordinance. The state mandate AB1236 requires each local
government to adopt an ordinance that creates an expedited,
streamlined permitting process for EVCS. This ordinance out-
lines administrative rules and practices governing the stream-
lined EVCS permit application and review process. It is a formal
policy decision collectively made by city council members, with
input from the public and stakeholders. Thus, the streamlining
ordinance represents local governments' intentional actions to
reduce burdens in permit service provision. Furthermore, in our
review of ordinances, we find that the streamlining practices
embedded in ordinance policy design vary across cities. These
formal administrative rules directly shape the ease of citizen
access to public services and their experiences interacting with
local governments, hence provide a basis for measuring bur-
dens embedded in ordinance designs. Therefore, we constructed
a composite index to capture the level of administrative ease
based on the bundle of streamlining practices adopted by cities.
Below, we discuss the approach and data sources we use to con-
struct these two variables.
4.1.1   |   Streamlining Ordinance Adoption
We construct a dataset of EVCS permit streamlining ordinances
for all counties and municipalities in the state of California by
reviewing county and municipal codes and websites. The adop-
tion year of each ordinance was collected from the municipal
code website or via direct contact of the city clerk. Among the
482 cities, 336 cities2 have adopted a streamlining ordinance
as of January 2024, with over half of cities adopting their or-
dinance before 2021 (see a breakdown of municipal ordinance
adoptions by year in Table 1, and adoption rates, cumulative
adopters, and new adopters by year in Figure 1). Figure 1 in-
dicates that following the enactment of the state mandate
(AB1236), the proportion of cities having adopted an ordinance
follows an S-­shaped pattern over time, with a slower adoption
rate at the beginning, then accelerating, and finally slowing
down again. Based on this dataset, we construct a binary vari-
able measuring whether a city has adopted an EVCS streamlin-
ing ordinance in a given year.
4.1.2   |   Administrative Ease Index
We measure the administrative ease of the EVCS permitting
process by assessing how municipal streamlining practices af-
fect the “learning, psychological, and compliance costs that
citizens experience in their interactions with the government”
(Herd and Moynihan 2018, 22). To do so, we first manually re-
viewed the policy content of ordinances to identify and code
streamlining practices. Five types of practices emerged as par-
ticularly critical in shaping burdens in the EVCS permitting
process, including an online checklist, electronic submission, ad-
ministrative approval, not subject to association approval, and a
correction notice.

feedback in individual-­state interactions; hence, they are com-

both make it easier to meet the administrative requirements;
hence, they are simplifying strategies. Requiring administrative
approval when all requirements are met accelerates the admin-
istrative process and is an example of an expediting strategy.

4.2   |   Independent Variables
We use budgetary balance as a proxy for governmental capacity,
which is measured by the percentage of general budget surpluses
or deficits to general revenues (Wei 2022). Cities with a larger bud-
getary balance have more resources for new policy adoption and
implementation. We collected data for city revenue and expendi-
ture from the California State Controller's Office and then com-
puted budgetary balance values for all cities from 2015 to 2023.
We use the city level percentage of Democratic votes in the most
recent presidential election to measure the political ideology of
the mass public and overall public preference on government
efficiency. This data was obtained from the California State
Portal. Public support for climate action is operationalized by the
estimated percentage of the population who think global warm-
ing is happening. Data for this variable were collected from the
Yale program on climate change communication (YCOM).
Lastly, we use percentage of minority population and median
household income as two measures of community demographic
characteristics. Five-­year average estimates for percentage of mi-
nority and median household income for years 2014 and 2023 were
collected from the Census Bureau American Community Survey.
Median household income was inflation adjusted to 2022 dollars.
Minority and income data for 2015–2022 were interpolated.
4.3   |   Control Variables

and economic features of a city. We measure rurality using

4.4   |   Empirical Approach
The main goal of this research is to determine what factors drive
burden reduction at the local level. To accomplish this goal, we
estimate two sets of models.
4.4.1   |   Event History Analysis
The first set analyzes a binary time-­series cross-­sections dataset
using an event history analysis (EHA) approach, specifically, a
probit model that accounts for duration dependency (Jones and
Branton 2005). The EHA approach has been widely employed to
understand determinants of policy adoption and diffusion (Berry
and Berry 1990; Shipan and Volden 2008; Nicholson-­Crotty 2009;
McDonald and Gabrini 2014). Compared to other cross-­sectional
regression models, EHA is advantageous in examining the occur-
rence of an event of interest within a population of entities across
time, accounting for both the exact time at which an event oc-
curred and changes in both time-­varying and invariant covariates
(Lacy 2015). The dependent variable used in the EHA is whether
a city has officially adopted a municipal ordinance that creates
an expedited and streamlined permitting process for EVCS in
a given year. This variable is coded as 0 when no ordinance is
adopted. In the year a city adopts an ordinance, this variable is
coded as 1, and this city will be removed from the dataset in the
following years as it is no longer at risk of ordinance adoption.
The full sample used in the EHA includes 482 cities in California
from 2015 to 2023, and 336 cities adopted ordinances within this
time. The total number of observations is 2937 city-­year. Spline
function of the duration time (number of years to ordinance
adoption since 2015) is used in the analysis to control for time
dependence. The assumption is that duration has a linear effect
on the probability of an event occurring between the knots. As
robustness checks, we compare the AIC score of this model with
those of others using different duration dependency specifica-
tions (see Appendices A5 and A6), and the model with linear
spline function has the lowest AIC score. Standard errors are
clustered by city-­year to account for heteroskedasticity and
correlation across observations. All independent variables are
lagged by 1 year.
4.4.2   |   Heckman Selection Model
Because ordinance design, particularly the streamlining prac-
tices adopted by cities, varies across jurisdictions, we estimate a
Heckman selection model using the two-­step estimation method
to further examine the determinants of administrative ease in
the EVCS permitting process. Selection bias can arise when
there are unobserved characteristics that make cities more likely
to adopt a streamlining ordinance and affect the level of admin-
istrative ease. The Heckman selection model estimates the level
of administrative ease that a city would have if it had adopted
an ordinance. The dependent variable is the administrative ease
index we constructed using factor analysis. The first step, or the
selection equation, is run on the full sample of all California cit-
ies using a probit model. We include all independent variables
we use in the event history analysis. The second step, or the con-
ditional equation, is estimated using the subsample of cities that
have adopted an ordinance. This stage also includes the inverse
Mills ratio obtained from the first equation to control for selec-
tion into the subsample. The second equation is estimated using
an ordinary least squares (OLS) model. To satisfy the exclusion
restrictions, we exclude the two regional diffusion variables in
the second equation: the percentage of cities with ordinances
and whether the county has adopted an ordinance. Since cities
adopt their streamlining practices in different years, we con-
struct a cross-­sectional dataset that aligns each city with the
year immediately prior to ordinance adoption. For cities with-
out an ordinance, we use data from the latest year in our study
period, 2023. The resulting dataset for the Heckman selection
model includes 482 cities in the selection stage and 336 cities in
the second stage.

The EHA results in Table 5 support Hypothesis 2b and suggest
the importance of local political preferences in shaping munic-

change, city officials are more likely to reduce administrative

The democratic vote share variable is negative and statistically
significant in the baseline model (Model (2)). This aligns with
our Hypothesis 2a that more conservative leaning constituen-
cies increase the probability of ordinance adoption. However,
the significant effect of democratic vote share disappears in
Model (5) once community demographic characteristics are con-
trolled for. Combined with the significant effect of local climate
beliefs, it suggests that the political ideology of the mass public
may not be the primary driver of ordinance adoption. Instead,
adoption is more directly determined by the issue-­specific public
demand for climate actions. In other words, streamlining EVCS
permitting tends to be viewed by local governments less as a
deregulation initiative tied to partisan ideology, but more as a
targeted climate policy that responds to public demand for more
local mitigation actions.
There is some evidence for the impact of community demo-
graphic characteristics (Hypothesis  3). The estimated coeffi-
cients of median income are positive and significant in Models
(4) and (5), suggesting higher-­income communities are more
likely to adopt burden reduction practices. However, this re-
lationship weakens and becomes statistically insignificant in
Model (6) once local climate opinion is controlled for. This shift
indicates that part of the positive effect of income may reflect
underlying climate attitudinal differences across communities,
as wealthier jurisdictions often have stronger climate beliefs.
Percent minority is significant and negative in Models (4), (5),
and (6), suggesting that cities with larger proportions of minority
populations are less likely to adopt a streamlining ordinance.
Changing percent minority from its minimum to maximum
(from 0.8% to 91.2%) reduces the predicted probability of ordi-
nance adoption from 0.1270 to 0.0288. In other words, cities with
predominantly white populations are about four times more
likely to streamline EVCS permitting compared to cities with
predominantly minority populations. This percent minority

for climate beliefs and democratic vote share. This provides
strong support for Hypothesis 3, suggesting that local burden
reduction decisions are responsive to the racial composition of
constituencies. Cities serving majority white communities are
more likely to reduce burdens in the permitting process.
As an additional robustness check on local political preferences,
we examine the role of elected local officials' political ideol-
ogy (Democratic mayor) using a subsample of 86 large cities
for which mayoral partisanship data are available. These data

model, mayoral party identification is statistically insignificant.
This suggests that the political ideology of the mayors does not
explain the adoption of burden reduction measures.
5.2   |   Explaining Administrative Ease
Table  7 presents results from the Heckman selection model.
The estimate of lambda is 0.308 and statistically insignificant
at the 0.05 level, suggesting no existence of selection bias. In the
first stage of the model, higher income, whiter, and larger cities
are more likely to adopt streamlining ordinances. Climate be-
liefs are positively but only weakly associated with ordinance
adoption, while budgetary balance is not statistically significant.
These results differ from the event history analysis because of
the cross-­sectional design of the Heckman model, which exam-
ines the snapshot of time for cities.
Conditional on ordinance adoption, neither budgetary balance
nor local climate beliefs significantly influence the adminis-
trative ease of the permitting process. Percent minority is neg-
atively and significantly associated with administrative ease.
This suggests that conditional on ordinance adoption, cities with
a larger share of minority population tend to design ordinances
that result in higher administrative burdens in the permitting
process. This finding is consistent with recent scholarship on ra-
cialized administrative burden (Jang-­Trettien and Bolger 2024;
Ray et al. 2023). Even when ordinances are formally adopted,
their design is associated with more burdensome permitting in
cities serving larger minority constituencies.
As a robustness check, we estimate both OLS and fractional logit
models on the subsample of 336 cities with ordinance adoption.
The dependent variable of the OLS model is the administrative
ease index. Given that the administrative ease index is skewed
to the right, we also estimate a fractional logit model using the
min–max scaled administrative ease score, which falls between
0 and 1. Results are consistent across specifications: percent mi-
nority remains negatively associated with administrative ease,
reaching significance at p < 0.05 in the fractional logit model
and at p < 0.10 in OLS. Taken together, the results provide ev-
idence that administrative burden is further racialized in the
design of local streamlining ordinances. This reinforces the
argument that burden reduction is not a neutral, technocratic
exercise but one embedded within local political and social con-
texts (Moynihan et al. 2015).

6   |   Conclusion
This study advances the administrative burden literature
by shifting analytical attention from citizens' experiences of
burdens to the policy design choices made by policymakers.
Whereas much prior research emphasizes how burdens emerge
in frontline service delivery and government communication,
we examine burden creation and reduction in policy design. By
systematically reviewing and coding the administrative rules
governing electric vehicle charger permitting across California
cities, we demonstrate that municipal governments can delib-
erately shape administrative burdens in routine citizen-­state
encounters through the design and adoption of local regulatory
procedures.
We further contribute to the literature by developing an ad-
ministrative ease index that operationalizes burdens based on
the design features of municipal streamlining ordinances. This
measure captures how formal policy content shapes the learn-
ing, compliance, and psychological costs citizens are likely to
face, thereby extending existing approaches that rely primar-
ily on self-­reported experiences. By linking policy design to
anticipated burden exposure, the index provides a systematic
way to study administrative burdens upstream, at the point of

the literature to examine burden reduction efforts through the
lens of policy formation and design (Moynihan et al. 2016) and
helps establish a research agenda at the intersection of policy
design and administrative burden.
We also build upon the policy adoption and local politics lit-
erature to explain why local governments choose to adopt
burden reduction measures. Our findings suggest that burden
reduction through the adoption of streamlining ordinances is a
deliberate and intentional policy action exercised within insti-
tutional and political contexts. In particular, we find that cit-
ies with stronger fiscal capacity are more likely to proactively
reduce burdens for citizens. This finding reaffirms Herd and
Moynihan (2018)'s claim that administrative burden is fun-
damentally a function of administrative capacity. Burden re-
duction strategies that aim to limit administrative barriers for
citizens may shift work and costs onto governments, requiring
investments in new systems and staff time. Cities facing fis-
cal stress or staffing shortages may therefore be less likely to
engage in burden reduction without external support for ca-
pacity building and infrastructure upgrades. For practitioners,
this highlights a critical but often overlooked lesson: reducing
burdens is costly for government. Policy makers designing bur-
den reduction strategies should anticipate these capacity and
resource requirements up front and incorporate adequate fi-
nancial and personnel support for smooth implementation.
Moreover, this study contributes to scholarship on equity in
public administration by showing that variation in adminis-
trative rule design can produce unequal burden distributions.
Our finding that greater burden reductions occur in whiter
communities is consistent with prior research documenting
the racialized distribution of administrative burdens (Jilke
et al. 2018; Ray et al. 2023). Local policymakers may be more
inclined to provide efficient and user-­friendly permitting ser-
vices when nonminority residents are the primary or perceived
beneficiaries. Therefore, streamlined permitting processes that
disproportionately benefit whiter communities may reinforce
existing inequalities in access to clean energy infrastructure
(Lee et al. 2025; Ermagun and Tian 2024).
Finally, this paper broadens the substantive policy areas in which
administrative burdens are studied. Existing research on admin-
istrative burdens has focused predominantly on state-­level wel-
fare programs. In this paper, we investigate burden reduction in
local permitting services, an understudied yet consequential do-
main that influences everyday citizen–government interactions.
The design of permitting processes and individual experiences
seeking permit approvals can influence citizens' perceptions
of local government and their decisions to adopt clean technol-
ogies. Understanding burden reduction at the local level hence
carries important implications beyond public administration
research. Our findings suggest that burden reduction should be
understood not only as a technocratic reform for administrative
efficiency but also as a deliberate policy design choice situated
within issue-­specific political contexts. When cities streamline
EVCS permitting, they signal the political salience of climate and
clean energy issues. These insights point to the need for future
research to study how the origins and consequences of burdens
may vary depending on the substantive policy issue areas.
There are a few limitations with this study. First, the gener-
alizability of our findings is limited. Our administrative ease
index illustrates how burdens embedded in policy design of
administrative rules can be systematically aggregated into
a measurable construct. However, its broader generaliz-
ability and applicability may be constrained by the inherent
complexity of local administrative practices and issue-­specific
policy contexts. Also, this research focuses on a single state,
and California is arguably one of the most politically liberal
states in the U.S. This can limit the extent to which our find-
ings generalize to other states and regions. Conducting a na-
tionwide study on local burden reduction actions remains
challenging due to the lack of comparable and identifiable
local policy data at the national scale. To our knowledge,
California is currently the only state with a mandate designed
to directly influence administrative burdens in local permit-
ting. Our sample includes all incorporated cities in California
and spans 9 years following the mandate's enactment, which
provides substantial variation across jurisdictions and over
time. This allows us to test our hypotheses and generate in-
sights that may inform research and practices beyond one sin-
gle state.
In addition, our analysis does not incorporate variables that di-
rectly capture features of the local administrative apparatus.
Although the budgetary balance variable serves as a proxy for
governmental resources and capacity, it does not account for or-
ganizational structure, human capital, or institutional continuity.
While most California cities operate under a council-­manager
form of government, the structure, staffing, and expertise within
city building departments may play a critical role in shaping per-
mitting service delivery. Future research could develop a com-
posite index to capture these dimensions of the administrative
apparatus and to evaluate its impact on administrative burden
reduction.
Methodologically, our Heckman selection model uses a cross-­
sectional research design, which is constrained in its ability to
control for unobserved factors that are correlated with ordinance
adoption and administrative ease. Even though it addresses the
concern of cities selecting into the ordinance adoption treatment
group, our results may suffer from omitted variable bias. The
cross-­sectional design is also unable to capture the changes in
key independent variables over time, which may influence both
ordinance adoption and administrative ease. As a result, the
Heckman estimates reflect associations at a single point of ob-
servation rather than dynamic causal processes and should be
interpreted with caution.

Abstract
Top managers hold a significant position in the functioning of public organizations. While existing studies have predominantly investigated the
impacts of individual characteristics of top managers on public organizational outcomes, limited attention has been given to heterogeneity, es­
pecially nondemographic heterogeneity, among them. This study focuses on the impact of language dissimilarity among top managers in public
organizations and proposes that the dissimilarity can negatively influence their communication and social identification, leading to a decrease in
organizational performance. Based on economic growth and managers’ biographical data across cities in China (N = 4,700), our two-way fixed
effects model finds that the language dissimilarity between the two top managers in a city government has a negative impact on the economic
growth rate of the city. This impact does not diminish as the two managers work together over a long period, and the ethnic dissimilarity be­
tween them can amplify the negative impact. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of the top management team’s heterogeneity in driving the
performance of public organizations.
Key words: top managers; language dissimilarity; panel data analysis; organizational performance.
Introduction
Leadership plays a pivotal role in both private and public
organizations. The upper echelons theory suggests that in­
dividual characteristics of top managers can significantly
affect organizational processes, decisions, and outcomes
(Hambrick and Mason 1984). This insight has been increas­
ingly acknowledged within public administration literature
(Huang and Villadsen 2023), suggesting that top managers
in public organizations are not merely mechanistic executors
of policies and rules as predicted by the bureaucratic theory
(Weber 1978). Their personal traits can significantly impact
various organizational outcomes (Anessi-Pessina and Sicilia
2020; Boyne et al. 2011; Damanpour and Schneider 2006;
Villadsen 2011).
However, focusing solely on the impact of individual char­
acteristics does not fully cover the topic of top management. In
practice, top management is often described as a collective en­
deavor, involving collaboration and coordination of multiple
key individuals, commonly known as top management teams
(TMTs) (Finkelstein and Hambrick 1990). Although few in
number, public administration scholars have studied TMTs,
particularly examining demographic heterogeneity and its
effects on organizational outcomes (Huang and Villadsen
2023). TMTs are not merely collections of demographically
diverse managers. They are also involved in complex, inter­
active relationships (Leach and Lowndes 2007). Despite this,
little is known about how specific heterogeneity shapes inter­
personal dynamics in TMTs, such as language dissimilarity,
may influence public organizational outcomes (Huang and
Villadsen 2023). Accordingly, this study investigates the im­
pact of language dissimilarity within TMTs on organizational
performance.
Language serves as the fundamental mechanism for cate­
gorization, contemplation, and articulation of perceptions
of reality, thereby constituting one of the strongest bonds
among human beings (Lenneberg 1967). As ethnic minorities
and immigrants increasingly enter the public sector work­
force (Ding and Riccucci 2023), linguistic heterogeneity
within public organizations has emerged as a notable phe­
nomenon. For instance, Switzerland, with its four national
languages, faces ongoing challenges in ensuring equitable
representation and effective communication across linguistic
groups (Ritz and Alfes 2018). Canada’s commitment to bi­
lingualism necessitates careful consideration of language pro­
ficiency in public service appointments and service delivery
(Heller 2006). In India, the multitude of official languages
and regional dialects presents complexity for public admin­
istration, impacting everything from policy implementation
to citizen engagement (Annamalai 2001). Beyond national
governments, transnational organizations like the United
Nations, with their diverse staff from across the globe, also
need to navigate the complexities of multilingual communi­
cation and collaboration to achieve their mandates (Ammon
2006; McEntee-Atalianis 2016). Supplementary Appendix C
provides more cases related to the challenges of dialect usage
within the public sector across the globe.

language dissimilarity in public administration, research on

scarce. Particularly, within TMTs, language dissimilarity
among top managers may have impacts on organizational

managers may lead to communication frictions or intergroup

may lead to a high level of information elaboration or more

making it an open question requiring empirical investigation.
We examine the question by focusing on the two-person
TMTs in Chinese city governments. Based on socioeconomic
statistics and city managers’ biographic data of Chinese cities
from 2005 to 2019, we find that the language dissimilarity
between the two top managers significantly reduces a city’s
economic growth rate, the most important performance indi­
cator for Chinese local governments. Critically, this impact is
plausibly causal as the hometown avoidance rule for China’s
local leader appointments provides quasi-exogenous varia­
tion in top manager language dissimilarity. This impact does
not diminish as the two managers work together over a long
period, but the ethnic dissimilarity between them amplifies
the negative impact. We also reveal that infrastructure invest­
ment and housing investment, two areas controlled by the city
government and particularly requiring cooperation efficiency
between top managers, can be the channels through which
their language dissimilarity lowers economic performance.
The study has contributions in the following aspects. Firstly,
it contributes to the literature of top managers within public
organizations. By investigating the impact of language dissim­
ilarity among top managers, the study results highlight the
significance of TMT’s nondemographic features in influencing
organizational outcomes, complementing prior research fo­
cusing more on individual or demographic characteristics
(Huang and Villadsen 2023). Second, this study speaks to the
literature of heterogeneity among employees in public organ­
izations. Extant research focuses more on heterogeneity of
demographical variables, such as ethnicity, gender, or nation­
ality among general employees (Ding and Riccucci 2023). The
influence of language dissimilarity (heterogeneity influencing
interpersonal dynamics) within TMTs on organizational per­
formance remains largely unexplored in the literature and our
study might fill the gap. We embed our analysis within the
Categorization-Elaboration Model (CEM) to disentangle the
complex mechanisms through which heterogeneity influences
organizational outcomes, addressing calls in the literature for
theory-driven research on heterogeneity (Ding and Riccucci
2023). From a broader perspective, our study also contributes
to the “linguistic turn” in public administration (Hand and
Catlaw 2019; Ritz and Alfes 2018) by providing rigorous em­
pirical evidence on the tangible consequences of language dy­
namics in public organizations.
Literature review
Top managers in public organizations
Public administration scholarship increasingly recognizes
the pivotal role of top managers in shaping organizational
outcomes. Foundational work by O’Toole and Meier (1999)
directly challenged the traditional bureaucratic view by
demonstrating that management is not merely an administra­
tive function, but a critical factor influencing public organiza­
tional outcomes. They established that management matters
and that variations in managerial quality have tangible effects
on performance. However, as “management” itself is an ab­
stract concept, quantifying its contributions to organizational
outcomes is quite challenging (O’Toole and Meier 1999).
Consequently, a significant stream of research has focused
on examining observable manager attributes as proxies for
managerial quality. For instance, Meier, O’Toole, and Goerdel
(2006) explored gender as management capital, demonstrating
how demographic characteristics of managers can influence
program performance. Similarly, Avellaneda (2009) focused
on mayoral quality in terms of education and expertise and
linked these attributes to local financial performance.
More recently, public administration research has further
enriched its theoretical framework by integrating insights
from the upper echelons theory, arguing that top managers’
personal characteristics, including values and cognitive
styles (reflected in observable characteristics such as gender,
age, education, etc.), shape their interpretations of informa­
tion and subsequent decision-making processes (Huang and
Villadsen 2023). Recent empirical studies have underscored
the impact of top public managers’ demographic characteris­
tics, such as gender (Krøtel, Ashworth, and Villadsen 2019),
ethnicity (O’Flynn and Mau 2014), age (Anessi-Pessina and
Sicilia 2020), and educational background (Damanpour and
Schneider 2009), and linked these characteristics to important
organizational outcomes such as organizational performance
(Boyne et al. 2011), innovation (Damanpour and Schneider
2006), accounting manipulation (Anessi-Pessina and Sicilia
2020), and policy diffusion (Villadsen 2011).
Furthermore, the field has moved beyond a singular focus
on individual managers to recognize that leadership is often
a collective endeavor and that the actions and interactions
among TMT members can significantly influence public or­
ganizational outcomes (Huang and Villadsen 2023). Prior
studies have primarily focused on TMT demographic com­
position and heterogeneity (Andrews 2023; Opstrup and
Villadsen 2015; Wang, Ma, and Christensen 2024). A limited
number of studies have attempted to understand the inter­
personal processes within TMTs, including internal and ex­
ternal relationships, leadership style variations, and turnover
rate (Boyne et al. 2011; Leach and Lowndes 2007; Villadsen
2011). However, there has been a noticeable lack of atten­
tion to language issues among top managers in public ad­
ministration research. Considering that language is the basis
of team collaboration (Neeley 2013), this gap deserves more
investigation.
Heterogeneity in public organizations
Employee heterogeneity in public organizations has attracted
the attention of public administration scholars (Ding and
Riccucci 2023). While prior literature often uses “heteroge­
neity” and “diversity” interchangeably (Harrison and Klein
2007), our study adopts “heterogeneity” throughout the
paper as a generic, team-level concept referring to the dis­
tribution of differences among members regarding a spe­
cific attribute, such as gender, tenure, ethnicity, or language.
To provide conceptual clarity and address different forms
of heterogeneity, we draw upon the framework proposed
by Harrison and Klein (2007), which distinguishes between

“separation” and “variety” as distinct types of heterogeneity
(their third type, “disparity,” is irrelevant; see Supplementary
Appendix D). The choice between these conceptualizations is
not absolute. It depends on the research question and context,
particularly whether the focus is on the categorical nature of
differences (variety) or the degree of distance among members
(separation).
Specifically, according to Harrison and Klein (2007), sep­
aration reflects differences in magnitude along a continuous
attribute. It is characterized by measurable distances be­
tween team members based on the level of an attribute, such
as age, positive affect, or organizational commitment, and
teams differ in the extent to which their individual members
are close along the attribute. In contrast, variety is hetero­
geneity based on categorical differences along a nominal at­
tribute (Harrison and Klein 2007). Variety exists when team
members are differentiated by distinct categories, focusing on
the distribution of members across categories like gender, ed­
ucational discipline, or ethnicity (as distinct groups). Teams
differ in the extent to which their members are evenly spread
across all the categories of the attribute. Prior public adminis­
tration research has predominantly focused on employees’ va­
riety, highlighting its benefits (Andrews and Ashworth 2015;
Compton and Meier 2016), drawbacks (Owens and Kukla-
Acevedo 2012; Ritz and Alfes 2018), and mixed effects (Ding
and Riccucci 2023).
Although most of the literature emphasizes the heteroge­
neity of general employees, a few studies have begun to focus
on the heterogeneity of TMTs in public organizations. Similar
to studies on general employees, these studies also focus
mainly on a variety of specific demographic attributes among
top managers (Andrews 2023; Opstrup and Villadsen 2015;
Wang, Ma, and Christensen 2024). While contributing val­
uable insights, these studies often overlooked other impor­
tant dimensions of attributes, such as cognitive, functional,
and linguistic differences, which are difficult to measure but
may significantly influence team dynamics and organizational
outcomes (Ritz and Alfes 2018). Furthermore, separation in
public TMTs also remains under-investigated. Inconsistent
findings on TMT variety, e.g., gender diversity (variety)
showing both positive (Opstrup and Villadsen 2015) and neg­
ligible effects (Wang, Ma, and Christensen 2024), underscore
the need for deeper exploration into different types of het­
erogeneity, especially the less-studied area of separation, like
language dissimilarity.
Our study operationalizes language dissimilarity as a form
of separation, focusing on the measurable language distance
among top managers. Specifically, we define language dis­
tance based on the hierarchical structure of a language tree
to capture the quantitative distance between dialects (Jacques
and List 2019; Wurm et al. 1987). While “separation” is not
commonly used in public administration literature, we use
“language dissimilarity” for clarity and consistency.
The role of language in public administration
The recent “linguistic turn” in social sciences underscores
language not merely as a communication tool, but as a fun­
damental force shaping social interaction (Alvesson and
Kärreman 2000; Ginsburgh and Weber 2020), especially as
a marker of social identity (Gumperz 1982). This perspec­
tive, rooted in symbolic interactionism and discourse theory,
recognizes language as constitutive of social practices and
power dynamics (Foucault and Nazzaro 1972; Mead 2015).
While still a nascent field of inquiry, public administration
scholarship is actively examining the multifaceted role of lan­
guage in the public sector through diverse analytical lenses
(Hand and Catlaw 2019; Ritz and Alfes 2018). These emerging
perspectives collectively highlight the crucial linguistic dimen­
sion within public sectors, moving beyond purely functional
views of communication to consider language’s broader soci­
etal and organizational impacts.
These analytical lenses can be categorized into three
approaches. The first approach regards language as institu­
tional architecture and examines how formal language policy
instruments shape public service delivery or socioeconomic
outcomes. For example, Kolisnichenko and Rosenbaum
(2009) found that the government of Ukraine used language
policies as a tool to address ethnic minority demands through
the public education system. Laitin and Ramachandran (2016)
demonstrated that national language policies significantly in­
fluence human capital development in Africa. The second ap­
proach regards language as bureaucratic traits and focuses
on the individual linguistic attributes of public servants,
examining how language diversity (variety) and proficiency
among public employees impact organizational dynamics.
For example, Ritz and Alfes (2018) found that language di­
versity (variety) at the unit level negatively influences public
employees’ attachment to their jobs. The third approach
views language as administrative skills and investigates the
strategic use of language in administrative practices. For
instance, Eckhard and Friedrich (2024) documented that
bureaucrats’ relational communication, rather than informa­
tional communication, improves citizen satisfaction during
public encounters.
Our study engages with the expanding field of language
issues in public administration. Specifically, we focus on lan­
guage dissimilarity as a critical dimension of TMT heteroge­
neity. While existing literature acknowledges language’s role in
general team dynamics and individual employee experiences
(Ritz and Alfes 2018), the impact of language dissimilarity
within public sector TMTs on objective organizational per­
formance remains a significant gap.
Theory and hypotheses
Top managers’ language dissimilarity and public
organizational performance
We use the CEM to analyze the impact of top managers’ lan­
guage dissimilarity on public organizational performance
(Ding and Riccucci 2023; Van Knippenberg, De Dreu, and
Homan 2004). The CEM explains how organizational het­
erogeneity shapes performance through two fundamental
processes: social categorization and information elaboration.
Applying this lens, we propose that language dissimilarity
within TMTs, a salient dimension of heterogeneity, undermines
organizational performance by negatively impacting both so­
cial categorization and information elaboration.
From a social categorization perspective, language serves
as a primary marker of social identity and group member­
ship. Drawing upon social identity theory (Ashforth and
Mael 1989), individuals naturally categorize themselves and
others into social groups, often based on salient cultural cues.
Language, as the very foundation of culture and communi­
cation, stands as one of the most immediate and impactful

cues for this categorization process (Gumperz 1982). Shared
language fosters a sense of “in-group” belonging, signaling
shared values, understandings, and a common worldview.
Conversely, language dissimilarity highlights “out-group”
status, creating a social distance between individuals (Tajfel
1982). The in-group preference can lead to more positive in­
terpersonal dynamics, smoother collaboration, and a greater
willingness to cooperate in an organization. In contrast, lan­
guage dissimilarity can trigger unconscious biases and reduce
social identification, leading to decreased trust, increased
social distance, and ultimately, hindering organizational
performance.
While the information elaboration process in CEM
emphasizes the potential benefits of heterogeneity through
communication, in the context of language dissimilarity, the
very foundation of effective information elaboration, i.e.,
shared linguistic understanding, is compromised (Ding and
Riccucci 2023; Van Knippenberg, De Dreu, and Homan
2004). Specifically, a higher level of language dissimilarity
among top managers can significantly increase information
concealment and asymmetry, thereby facilitating less efficient
information exchange. This means that the benefits of social
or cultural heterogeneity cannot be realized through effective
communication. Rather, decreased communication efficiency
can negatively contribute to organizational performance. For
instance, Piekkari, Oxelheim, and Randøy (2015) found that
linguistic dissimilarity between the chairman and the man­
aging director often results in less substantive discussions
in a company. Additionally, increased language dissimilarity
can lead to more errors or misunderstandings. For instance,
Elfenbein (2007) documented that dialect is a vital channel
for expressing emotions and the lack of a shared linguistic
foundation can hinder the development of a unified cultural
philosophy and foster misunderstandings within management
teams.
From the perspectives of both social categorization and in­
formation elaboration, the influence of language dissimilarity
can be particularly salient in public organizations. Specifically,
in the highly complex environment of public management,
where consensus on major strategic initiatives like infrastruc­
ture investments and welfare programs is critical, insufficient
mutual trust and social cohesion caused by language dissimi­
larity can impede the reconciliation of divergent views, cause
distrust-driven resistance, and ultimately lengthen decision
cycles or erode decision quality (Hart and Wille 2006; Song et
al. 2025). Meanwhile, the communication friction can mani­
fest as prolonged debates over policy details or a greater risk
of misinterpreting complex directives, ultimately undermining
the effectiveness of public organizations (Garnett, Marlowe,
and Pandey 2008; Leijerholt, Biedenbach, and Hultén 2022).
Hence, we propose Hypothesis 1.
Hypothesis 1: Language dissimilarity among top managers
negatively influences public organizational performance.
The moderating effect of co-tenure length
The CEM suggests that the appropriate range of certain con­
textual factors can alleviate the potential negative effects of
heterogeneity and promote its overall positive effects (Ding
and Riccucci 2023). Based on this, we propose that the length
of co-tenure between these leaders may moderate this effect.
From the social categorization perspective, familiarity gained
through working together enhances the predictability of each
other’s behavior in the TMT and fosters a stronger sense of
team cohesion (Uzzi 2018). This cohesion can overcome so­
cial identity barriers imposed by language dissimilarity, as
managers begin to identify more with their shared goals and
experiences than with their linguistic backgrounds (Chatman
and Flynn 2001). The shared experiences and challenges faced
over time further strengthen this team identity, enhancing mu­
tual trust and commitment to organizational goals and re­
ducing the negative effects of language dissimilarity on social
identification.
Regarding the information elaboration mechanism, ex­
tended co-tenure provides managers with opportunities to de­
velop mutual understanding and adapt to each other’s accents,
thereby reducing misunderstandings and enhancing coordi­
nation (Reagans, Argote, and Brooks 2005). In addition, as
managers work together over time, they build interpersonal
trust and establish shared norms, which facilitate smoother
communication despite initial language dissimilarity (Gulati
1995). Thus, this adaptation process can mitigate the adverse
impact of language dissimilarity on organizational perfor­
mance by improving the efficiency of information exchange
and reducing the likelihood of conflict.
Based on the above-mentioned theoretical arguments, we
propose Hypothesis 2.
Hypothesis 2: The length of co-tenure among top managers
mitigates the negative relationship between language dis­
similarity and public organizational performance.
The moderating effect of ethnic dissimilarity
Ethnic dissimilarity is also an important factor influencing
social categorization (Gumperz 1982; Tajfel 1982) and has
garnered attention in public administration scholars (Ding
and Riccucci 2023). Crucially, ethnicity and language are
deeply intertwined dimensions. For many groups, language is
a primary marker of ethnic identity, reflecting shared history,
values, and worldview (Gumperz 1982). How do ethnic and
language dissimilarity together impact organizational perfor­
mance? Do they crowd each other out or operate in tandem
to produce a reinforcement effect? These questions warrant
further investigation. Based on the CEM framework, we pro­
pose that ethnic dissimilarity amplifies the negative impact of
language dissimilarity on organizational performance.
First, ethnic dissimilarity can intensify the adverse effects of
language dissimilarity through the social categorization mech­
anism. Workers in public sectors tend to stereotype and form
judgments about their colleagues from different groups, in­
cluding distinctions based on language, gender, and ethnicity
(Bertram, Bouwman, and Tummers 2022). Social categoriza­
tion and stereotypes undergo a process of self-reinforcement
through the accumulation of supportive evidence. When
individuals encounter multiple pieces of evidence that clas­
sify another person as belonging to an out-group, their social
categorization tends to become more entrenched (Schneider
2005). Hence, when language dissimilarity is coupled with
ethnic dissimilarity, preexisting ethnic stereotypes may be
more readily activated, further hindering mutual identifica­
tion, trust, and willingness to cooperate. This will negatively
influence performance.
Second, the presence of ethnic dissimilarity can further
undermine communication efficiency already strained by

norms can vary across ethnic groups. When top managers

cultural context (Gudykunst and Kim 1992). This amplified
miscommunication and misunderstanding can further under­

Based on the above-mentioned theoretical arguments, we
propose Hypothesis 3.
Hypothesis 3: Ethnic dissimilarity amplifies the negative
effect of top managers’ language dissimilarity on organiza­

Context
We test the above hypotheses in the context of city (prefec­
ture) governments in China for the following reasons. First,
the combination of China’s linguistic heterogeneity and the
cities’ dual-leader system allows us to construct our key inde­
pendent variable: top manager language dissimilarity. Second,
significant fiscal autonomy and inter-city competition mean
that economic growth serves as a crucial and measurable per­
formance indicator for these governments (Du and Yi 2022;
Li et al. 2019). Third, the city level offers a suitable sample
size for analysis, unlike the limited number of provinces or
the data-scarce county level, aligning with established prac­
tice in public administration research (Du and Yi 2022; Tang,
Wang, and Yi 2023). Despite leadership variation, these city
governments operate under the same national institutional
framework, also creating a relatively controlled setting for
analysis.
TMTs in Chinese cities: the dual-leader system
The term “top management team” refers to the relatively small
group of most influential executives at the apex of an organ­
ization (Finkelstein and Hambrick 1990). In China’s local
governments, the TMT is clearly and easily delineated, as each
governmental body is led by two key officials: the communist
party secretary and the chief administrative officer (Landry
2008). The Chinese local government hierarchy is structured
across four levels: province, city (prefecture), county, and town­
ship. Our study focuses on the city level, where the govern­
mental leadership is bifurcated between the party secretary
and the mayor. Nominally, the party secretary commands the
political and ideological direction, holding responsibility for
personnel affairs and ensuring the party’s paramountcy in ad­
ministration. Meanwhile, the mayor takes responsibility for
day-to-day administrative operations, driving economic de­
velopment, public services, and policy implementation. These
positions, holding equivalent rank, forge a TMT driving local
government operations (Landry 2008).
In practice, Chinese city governments rely on a collective
decision-making process that underscores the critical team­
work between the mayor and party secretary. City policies
are decided by the City Party Standing Committee (typically
nine to eleven members), headed by the party secretary, with
the mayor as the second-ranking member. Given their signif­
icant decision-making roles within the committee, their col­
laboration is indispensable for advancing major city policy
initiatives.
Crucially, the traditional political-administrative dichotomy
can be misleading when applied to the party secretary and
mayor in the Chinese context (Rosenbloom 2008). They are
not neatly divided into a political leader and an administrative
official, respectively. Instead, both are fundamentally adminis­
trative officials, a point underscored by their selection process.
Neither position is elected through popular vote; both are ap­
pointed by higher authorities (Landry 2008). This appoint­
ment process is driven by a complex set of considerations,
including political loyalty, individual competence, and rele­
vant experience (Choi 2012; Li and Zhou 2005). To our best
knowledge, there is no evidence to suggest dialect compat­
ibility is a deliberate criterion. Furthermore, the hometown
avoidance rule, which prohibits officials from serving as
mayor or party secretary in their home city, introduces quasi-
exogenous variation that is plausibly unrelated to local eco­
nomic trends, thereby enhancing identification.
In sum, the party secretary and the mayor are primarily
responsible for implementing policies mandated by the upper
authority, focusing on administrative efficiency, economic
development, and adherence to national directives. Their ac­
countability is directed upward to the appointing authorities,
and their career progression depends on meeting performance
targets rather than on electoral support from constituents.
Their roles emphasize administrative management over po­
litical representation, distinguishing them from traditional
politicians who are elected to represent the interests of their
constituents and have greater autonomy in policy-making
(Landry 2008). Together, they function as a unique two-
person TMT (Rutherford 2016).

Dialect heterogeneity in China
The Chinese language (branch) is the common language of
China, but due to the vastness of the country’s territory, the
pronunciation of Chinese in different regions varies signifi­
cantly. Generally, the Chinese language can be categorized
into ten groups, with Mandarin occupying the largest share,
followed by Jing, Wu, Min, Hakka, Yue, Xiang, Gan, Hui, and
Ping. It should be noted that Mandarin is defined as a super-
dialect group and others are defined as dialect groups. Under
super-dialect groups, there are dialect groups and under dia­
lect groups there are subdialect groups, contributing to the
linguistic heterogeneity within the Chinese language (Chen
1999; Lavely 2000). In addition, apart from the Chinese lan­
guage branch, the Tibeto-Burman, Mongolian, Turkic, and
Kam-Tai language branches are also used in certain minority
ethnic regions. Figure 2 displays the linguistic relationships
and geographic distribution among the various languages
(dialects) in China.
China’s official language and lingua franca is standard
Mandarin or Beijing Mandarin (Li 2006). Nevertheless, local
dialects still play a pivotal role in everyday communication
across China (Li and Yao 2024). Two official surveys from
2006 and 2010 reveal that about 80 percent of Chinese people
continue to primarily use local dialects for communication,
especially within their households (Zhang 2022). In addition,
reports from Chinese media outlets have noted a marked rise
in the utilization of dialects within working environments,
including retailing, public transport, and local television
programs, indicating that the relevance of dialects in contem­
porary settings remains undiminished (Zhang 2022).
Chinese dialects display profound variations, even to the
point of mutual unintelligibility. An important reason is that
the Chinese language is a tonal language, meaning the same
syllable can have different meanings with different tones used
(corresponding to different Chinese characters). Tones vary
significantly across dialects, and exposure to dialects can
interfere with the perception of various tones (Wiener and
Ito 2015). As a result, native dialects can distort tones and
cause listeners to misinterpret syllables in standard Mandarin
(Beijing Mandarin). These factors contribute to the differences
among dialects and allow us to define the distance between
them based on the structure of dialect classification, enabling
a quantitative measurement of language dissimilarity (Liu,
Xu, and Xiao 2015).
It is important to emphasize that while officials typically
strive to speak standard Mandarin in their work, their pro­
nunciation and grammar are often influenced by their na­
tive dialects, resulting in distinctive accents that may affect

Data and method
Data and variables
The context of our study is the city-level governments in
China. The study period is from 2005 to 2019. During this
period, economic development has been the core objective
of Chinese city governments, and relevant data is readily ac­
cessible. The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact
on governmental behavior and operations after 2019. We
use several data sources to construct a dataset for the em­
pirical study. First, we manually collect the biographical in­
formation on mayors and party secretaries of all cities for
the study period. It includes the demographic variables and
career information for each position. In cases where multiple
individuals held the same position in a city for a given year,
we select the person with the longest tenure within the year.
Second, we construct a dataset that includes the dialectical
distances between all pairs of counties across China. Utilizing
this dataset, along with the birthplace data of city leaders, we
calculate the language dissimilarity of top managers in each
city for every year. Third, following Wu et al. (2021), we use
the satellite nighttime light data to construct the dataset of
actual economic growth rates for cities in China. Other soci­
oeconomic indicators are collected from statistical yearbooks
or official reports. Finally, we construct a city-year panel
dataset for 323 cities (out of a total of 333 prefecture-level
jurisdictions in China, with the exclusion of 10 cities due to
missing data) over the period from 2005 to 2019.
Dependent variable.
Our core dependent variable is organizational perfor­
mance of a city government. However, defining public or­
ganizational performance is a challenging issue. The goals
of public organizations are inherently multiple, ambiguous,
and even contradictory, and there is no single performance
measure that fits the eight primary purposes of management
(to evaluate, control, budget, motivate, promote, celebrate,
learn, and improve) (Behn 2003). In other words, every per­
formance metric has its limitation, and public managers
should think carefully about the managerial purposes that
these metrics (e.g., economic growth, financial performance,
efficiency, effectiveness, and other domain-specific perfor­
mance) are meant to serve and how they can be effectively
applied (Boyne et al. 2011).
In the context of this study, we use the economic growth rate
to measure a city government’s organizational performance.
This method is practical and straightforward, capturing how
well a city government has managed its resources and fostered
a favorable business environment. Globally, economic devel­
opment is one of the core objectives for both national and
local governments (Eisinger 1988). This indicator is particu­
larly proper for China’s local governments. Over the past few
decades, economic growth has been a significant source of
legitimacy for the Chinese government and a primary focus
of work for local officials (Holbig and Gilley 2010). The
upper-level authority evaluated local leaders based on their
jurisdictional economic success, making economic growth
rates a key factor in promotions (Li et al. 2019; Li and Zhou
2005). Hence, it is reasonable to use a city’s economic growth
rate as the indicator for organizational performance of city
governments in China.
While public administration research often uses offi­
cial GDP growth to proxy government economic perfor­
mance (Im 2010; Ma 2016), this approach has been viewed
as problematic in the Chinese context by existing studies.
The performance-based cadre evaluation system in China
prioritizes regional GDP growth for promotions of local
leaders, who are also directly responsible for and in control
of local economic statistics. As a result, the reliability of of­
ficial GDP statistics, especially at the local level, has been
questioned in the literature. Many studies have documented
possible issues related to the accuracy of GDP figures reported
by local governments in China (Cai et al. 2022; Chen, Qiao,
and Zhu 2021; Zeng and Zhou 2024). Therefore, to capture
genuine economic performance, we do not use government-
published GDP growth rates. Instead, we utilize economic
growth rates from satellite nighttime light data as our primary
dependent variable. Nighttime light brightness is a robust and
increasingly validated proxy for GDP, and its growth rate is
a recognized proxy for GDP growth in economic literature
(Henderson, Storeygard, and Weil 2012), especially for the
Chinese context (Chen, Qiao, and Zhu 2021; Zeng and Zhou
2024). Public administration scholars have also used satel­
lite data as a proxy for local government performance, across
both economic and environmental domains, in response to
the problem of data manipulation within the public sector
(Cai et al. 2022; Du and Yi 2022; Li, Chen, and Peng 2023;
Tang, Wang, and Yi 2023).
The most widely used nighttime light data in social sci­
ence research are derived from the Defense Meteorological
Satellite Program (DMSP) and the Visible Infrared Imaging
Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) (Wu et al. 2021). DMSP offers
data from 1992 to 2013, whereas VIIRS covers the period
from 2012 onwards. The remote sensing literature has pro­
vided mature methods for cleaning, processing, and merging
the two datasets, facilitating the derivation of annual average
nighttime light brightness for specific regions (Elvidge et al.
2021). Based on Wu et al. (2021), we integrate these two data
sources to construct a panel dataset depicting the nighttime
light growth rates across Chinese cities from 2005 to 2019.
Our satellite-based economic growth measure is significantly
correlated with official statistics for GDP growth (β = .377,
P < .001), suggesting that the two indicators capture broadly
similar economic trends. Because official GDP figures may
themselves contain measurement error, we view this correla­
tion as supportive, but not definitive, evidence of the satellite
measure’s validity.
Independent variable.
Our core independent variable is the language dissimilarity
between the party secretary and the mayor. While the specific
dialects spoken by these two leaders are not directly observ­
able, we use the primary dialect spoken in their birthplace
as a proxy to estimate their language dissimilarity. Linguistic
theories posit that the critical period for human language ac­
quisition begins at the age of two and concludes at the ages
of twelve and thirteen (Lenneberg 1967). Thus, it is reason­
able to assume that an individual’s dialectal characteristics
are shaped by the dialect predominant in their birthplace.

It should be noted that this assumption may be challenged
by population mobility. However, in our context, all leaders
in the dataset were born and raised before the 1980s. The av­
erage birth years of the party secretary and mayor are 1959
and 1961, respectively. The youngest leader was born in 1976.
During the period of their growth, population movement in
China was very limited due to policy restrictions (Chan and
Zhang 1999). Thus, the influence of population mobility
is minimal. We also demonstrate in robustness checks (see
Supplementary Appendix B, Table B6) that neither education
level nor age significantly moderates the impact of language
dissimilarity on performance, implying the influence of dialect
is substantial.
The calculation of language dissimilarity between the party
secretary and the mayor is as follows. First, we identify the
county of birth for each city leader from the collected bio­
graphical information. Second, based on the Language Atlas
of China (Institute of Linguistics and Chinese Academy of
Social Sciences et al. 2012; Wurm et al. 1987), we determine
the dialect code for each county. This code is at the subdialect
group level, which is part of a hierarchical linguistic struc­
ture that also includes dialect groups, super-dialect groups,
and language branches, ranked from lower to higher levels.
In cases where a county has more than one subdialect group,
we selected the code corresponding to the predominant one.
Third, the language distance (dissimilarity) between the two
subdialect groups is calculated according to the following
rules: if the two counties belong to the same subdialect group,
their distance is zero; if they belong to the same dialect group
but different subdialect groups, their distance is one; if they
belong to the same super-dialect group but different dialect
groups, their distance is two; if they belong to the same lan­
guage branch but different super-dialect groups, their distance
is three; and if they belong to different language branches,
their distance is four. In the baseline regression, we treat the
independent variable as a continuous variable, while the ro­
bustness test also reports consistent results of treating it as an
ordinal variable. See Supplementary Figure B1 in Appendix B.
Table 1 details the distribution of language distance be­
tween city leaders in our sample. A language gap is evident
for most mayor-party secretary pairings, with distances of
one, two, three, and four observed in 19.8 percent, 26.8 per­
cent, 30.0 percent, and 2.9 percent of cases, respectively. In
20.4 percent of observations, the mayor and party secretary
share the same subdialect. Language dissimilarity changes fre­
quently over time. In our sample, 92 percent of cities (299
of 323) experienced at least one change in language dissim­
ilarity during the 2005–2019 period. On average, cities ex­
perienced 2.860 changes in language dissimilarity over this
period, providing sufficient variation for empirical anal­
ysis. Supplementary Figure E1 in Appendix E presents the
temporal trends of language distance between city leaders for
all cities in our sample.
Other variables.
Our key moderators include the co-tenure length and ethnic
dissimilarity between the party secretary and the mayor. The
co-tenure length refers to the duration that the two leaders
have worked together in their respective roles. It can be easily
calculated from the biographical database. The ethnic dissim­
ilarity between the party secretary and the mayor can also
be derived from the database. China recognizes 56 ethnic
groups, with each individual belonging to a specific group.
We use a dummy variable to denote the ethnic dissimilarity. If
the two leaders are from the same ethnic group, it was coded
as zero. If not, it was coded as one. Table 2 shows summary
statistics of all variables.
Regression specification
We test the three hypotheses using a two-way fixed effects
model. The baseline regression specification is given in equa­
tion (1):
Economic performancei,t = β × Language dissimilarityi,t
+ δ × Xi,t + ηi + λt + εi,t
(1)
In equation (1), i and t index city and year, respectively. The
dependent variable is Economic performancei,t representing
the annual growth rate of nighttime light brightness for city i
in year t. The independent variable is Language dissimilarityi,t,
representing the language dissimilarity between the party sec­
retary and the mayor of city i in year t. Xi,t is a vector of
control variables including gender, age, education level, and
tenure years of the two leaders respectively. ηi is the city-fixed
effects. λt is the year-fixed effects. εct is the error term. This
approach allows us to control for unobserved, time-invariant
city-specific heterogeneity through city-fixed effects (ηi),
capturing factors like geography and stable cultural norms.
Year-fixed effects (λt) further account for common time trends
and nationwide shocks affecting all cities. The model is par­
ticularly suitable for our research question as it focuses on
the within-city relationship between changes in top managers’
language dissimilarity and economic performance over time,
mitigating concerns of omitted variable bias.
As discussed above, the hometown avoidance appointment
system for local leaders in China offers quasi-exogeneity in
language dissimilarity. Based on this, we interpret β as the
estimated impact of language dissimilarity between the two
city leaders on economic performance. If β is significantly
negative, Hypothesis 1 is supported. Then, we add variables
of Co-tenure length and Ethnic dissimilarity into equation (1)
as moderators for the main effect to test Hypotheses 2 and 3.

Robustness of the main results
We rigorously validate our core findings through several
checks. Our empirical strategy relies on the quasi-exogeneity of
city managers’ language dissimilarity. While higher authorities
might consider personal traits in appointments, strategically
manipulating language dissimilarity seems unlikely. Indeed,
Table 3 supports this: including city manager characteristics
and provincial leaders’ fixed effects minimally impacts the
coefficients of language dissimilarity, underscoring its inde­
pendent effect on economic performance. Furthermore, we
directly test whether provincial authorities adjust language
dissimilarity based on prior GDP growth. As Supplementary
Appendix B, Table B1 shows, the regression coefficient for
prior GDP growth is near zero and statistically insignificant,
solidifying our independent variable’s relative exogeneity.
Second, employing nighttime light growth as a proxy for
economic growth may introduce errors. Prior research has
pointed out that in some places where population density is
too sparse, satellite nighttime light data do not reflect the true
economic growth (Gibson et al. 2021). To ensure the accuracy
of our dependent variable, we exclude leagues, autonomous
regions or provincially administered areas that cover 49.9
percent of China’s land area but only 6.7 percent of China’s
resident population in the 2010 census (Gibson et al. 2021),
and redo the estimation. Regression results based on this sub­
sample are shown in Supplementary Appendix B, Table B2.
The impact of top managers’ language dissimilarity on eco­
nomic growth is still significantly negative.
Third, we can observe that the economic growth rates
measured by satellite nighttime light data exhibit extreme
values (as shown in Table 2), which might lead some to ques­
tion whether our empirical results are driven by these outliers.
Therefore, we conduct a two-tailed trimming at the 1 per­
cent level on the dependent variable to mitigate the impact of
extreme values. The estimation result based on the trimmed
sample is presented in Supplementary Appendix B, Table B3.
It shows that the coefficient of the independent variable re­
mains significantly negative, further supporting the robust­
ness of our main results.

Infrastructure, housing, and business attraction as
channels
The above empirical results have demonstrated that the lan­
guage dissimilarity among top managers can negatively im­
pact overall economic performance. To delve deeper into the
mechanisms, we now examine how language dissimilarity
specifically influences aspects of city governance that are
directly within the control of top managers and are crucial
for driving economic growth. We focus on three potential
channels: infrastructure investment amount (Infrastructure
investment), housing investment amount (Housing invest­
ment), and business attraction (New companies). Our theory,
based on the CEM, posits that language dissimilarity hinders
TMT communication and social identification. For strategic
initiatives like infrastructure and housing investment, and in­
tense business attraction in Chinese cities, effective collabo­
ration between party secretary and mayor is vital. Language
friction leads to less efficient decision-making, manifesting as
information asymmetry, slower processes, higher costs, and
reduced trust. This ultimately results in fewer investment
projects initiated or businesses attracted.
The first indicator refers to annual investments in roads
and bridges, railways and public transportation, water
supply, gas, heating, drainage, flood control, green spaces,
sanitation, waste management, etc. During our study period,
infrastructure investments have consistently served as a cru­
cial approach for stimulating economic growth and have
been directly led by local governments (Rogoff and Yang
2024). The high budgets associated with these infrastruc­
ture projects necessitate that relevant decisions require effec­
tive collaboration and communication among top managers.
Hence, this indicator can be used as a channel variable, and
we expect that Language dissimilarity has a negative impact
on Infrastructure investment. The regression result is given in
Model 1 of Table 5, where the coefficient of Language dissim­
ilarity is negative and significant, supporting infrastructure
investment as a channel.

our research period, China’s real estate industry experienced

significantly influenced by government policies and serve as
a tool for economic stimulus (Rogoff and Yang 2024). Real

factor that often entails significant distribution of interests.

secretaries may directly participate in project decision-making
(Chen and Kung 2019). Hence, this indicator can be used as
another channel variable, and we expect that Language dis­

Discussion and conclusions
Based on the CEM framework and using a long-term panel
dataset at the city level in China, this study finds that the lan­
guage dissimilarity between the two top managers in a city
government has a negative impact on the economic growth
rate of the city. This impact does not diminish as the two
managers work together over a long period. The ethnic dis­
similarity between them can amplify the negative impact.
These results have both theoretical and practical implications.
Theoretical contributions
Our study contributes to the literature in several ways. First,
it contributes to the literature of top managers in public ad­
ministration. Based on a comprehensive literature review,
Huang and Villadsen (2023) provide a framework where top
public managers are part of a system that reacts to internal
and external inputs and carries out operational processes to
achieve organizational goals. Our study reveals that language
dissimilarity acts as a potential barrier to social categoriza­
tion and collaboration among top managers and impedes
organizational performance, adding comprehensive evidence
for the framework. It also highlights the differing impacts be­
tween nondemographic and demographic heterogeneity on
organizational performance (Opstrup and Villadsen 2015).
Furthermore, we find that co-tenure length (which may lead
to team cohesion) does not mitigate this effect, indicating that
the influence of language is substantial (Ritz and Alfes 2018).
Our robustness checks further reveal that the age gap and
gender difference between the top managers do not moderate
the language dissimilarity effect (see Supplementary Table B9
in Appendix B), further indicating the uniqueness of language
dissimilarity.
Second, this study speaks to the literature of the heteroge­
neity effect in public organizations. Prior research has prima­
rily focused on the effects of variety among general employees
in public organizations (Ding and Riccucci 2023). A few
studies have also examined the variety of TMTs in public
organizations, mainly focusing on gender, and have mostly
reported nonnegative effects (Opstrup and Villadsen 2015;
Wang, Ma, and Christensen 2024). Our study contributes to
this literature by providing empirical evidence on the effects
of separation (dissimilarity) among top managers. We find
the effect of language dissimilarity on organizational perfor­
mance to be negative, which aligns with previous assessments
on the impact of separation in general management literature
(Harrison and Klein 2007). In addition, while language dissim­
ilarity among public employees has been mentioned in prior
research, the outcome variables are related to individual-level
perceptions (Ritz and Alfes 2018). Our study is the first in the
field of public administration to link language dissimilarity
among top managers with objective performance indicators.
Our study also contributes to the framework of the CEM
(Ding and Riccucci 2023; Van Knippenberg, De Dreu, and
Homan 2004). We find that when language itself serves as
a source of heterogeneity, its impact is likely to be signifi­
cantly negative. This aligns with the CEM, which proposes
that heterogeneity can promote organizational performance
only after undergoing sufficient information elaboration (Van
Knippenberg, De Dreu, and Homan 2004). The coefficient
of the interaction term between language dissimilarity and
ethnic dissimilarity further supports this point. When these

Practical implications
Our study also has implications for practitioners. The negative
impact of language dissimilarity presents a notable contrast to
mainstream understanding of heterogeneity (diversity), which
often emphasizes its positive outcomes in public sectors (Ding
and Riccucci 2023; Opstrup and Villadsen 2015). While het­
erogeneity is undoubtedly valuable for equity, representation,
and broader perspectives, our results suggest that practitioners
should adopt a more balanced approach, considering not only
diversity’s potential benefits but also the practical difficulties
in ensuring that diverse teams can function effectively, partic­
ularly when communication and social cohesion are affected.
Moving forward, it is crucial to implement strategies to mit­
igate the negative effects of language dissimilarity in public
sector TMTs. Given that co-tenure length alone appears in­
sufficient to overcome these barriers, deliberate interventions
are necessary. These interventions could include proactive
language and cross-cultural communication training for top
managers, the provision of professional translation and inter­
pretation services, and the implementation of structured com­
munication protocols to minimize misunderstandings.
Finally, while language dissimilarity might pose challenges
for economic performance, language-diverse TMTs could be
advantageous for achieving other critical public service goals.
To maximize overall organizational success, managers should
actively seek to leverage the potential strengths of language-
diverse teams in areas such as fostering innovation, improving
social responsiveness, and promoting equity and inclusion,
rather than solely focusing on economic outcomes.
Limitations and future work
This study has some limitations. First, our use of birthplace di­
alect as a proxy for current language dissimilarity introduces
potential measurement error. However, this bias is likely con­
servative, potentially underestimating the negative effect, be­
cause officials are more likely to adopt Standard Mandarin
over time, potentially reducing the measured dissimilarity
compared to their native dialect influence. Furthermore,
as shown in Supplementary Appendix B, Table B6, the ro­
bustness check indicates that education level and age do
not moderate the effect of language dissimilarity, implying
that the influence of native dialect is persistent. Second, our
operationalization of language dissimilarity, while systemat­
ically derived from established linguistic classifications and
reflecting structural dialectal distances, does not directly
measure communication intelligibility. Future research should
try to incorporate quantitative intelligibility assessments for
a more nuanced understanding of the impact of language
dissimilarity. Third, while economic growth is a relevant per­
formance indicator for Chinese city governments and many
governments globally (Eisinger 1988; Li and Zhou 2005), it
is an imperfect proxy for overall public organizational perfor­
mance. To mitigate this, we examined channel variables like
infrastructure investment, housing investment, and business
attraction, demonstrating language dissimilarity’s impact on
government-controlled aspects. Of course, we recognize that
language dissimilarity may have different impacts on other
performance indicators, which should be a focus for future
research.
Finally, the context and generalizability of our study war­
rant further elaboration. Although China’s political system
differs from that of Western countries (as discussed above, the
city’s party secretary and mayor function together as a TMT),
our hypothesis development relies on the CEM, which does
not involve political factors. Moreover, key variables such
as language dissimilarity, ethnic dissimilarity, and co-tenure
length are common in public organizations worldwide.
Therefore, our research findings should possess a certain de­
gree of generalizability. From another perspective, the Chinese
context provides us with an opportunity to conduct rigorous
empirical tests for the hypotheses. The hometown avoidance
appointment system for local leaders in China offers quasi-
exogeneity in language dissimilarity, supporting more reliable
quantitative analysis. Regarding the unique two-person struc­
ture, it is straightforward to define language dissimilarity in
teams with more than two members: for dyads (N = 2), it is
the single pairwise distance; for larger teams (N > 2), it is the
mean of all pairwise distances. The number of members in
TMT does not change our theoretical hypotheses, but rele­
vant empirical testing falls beyond the scope of this paper.
We call for future research to provide empirical results from
teams with larger memberships.

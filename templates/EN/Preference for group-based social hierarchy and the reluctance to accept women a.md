Abstract
While many public organizations have made notable strides to improve the representation of women at all ranks, women remain severely un­
derrepresented in law enforcement organizations. Research shows that a critical barrier to women’s integration into law enforcement is the
common perception among policemen that women are unsuited for police work. This study draws on Social Dominance Theory (SDT) to illumi­
nate the values and beliefs underlying policemen’s negative perceptions. Using multi-wave survey data and Ordinary Least Squares Regression
analyses, we examine the association between social dominance orientation (SDO), an individual difference variable that captures preference for
group-based social hierarchy, and officers’ assessment of women’s suitability for law enforcement. In line with existing evidence, our analyses
show that compared to policewomen, policemen report significantly more negative assessments of women’s suitability for law enforcement.
We also find that higher SDO officers report more negative assessments of women in law enforcement, and officers’ diversity value partially
mediates this relationship. These novel findings suggest that officers who desire to protect existing power dynamics are more likely to resist
organizational diversity efforts and hold more negative views about women’s suitability for law enforcement.
Key words: gender equity, diversity value, social dominance orientation, law enforcement.
Introduction
Studies in public administration (PA) have shown that women’s
inclusion in law enforcement promoted public trust and con­
fidence in the police (Riccucci, Van Ryzin, and Lavena, 2014),
improved officer performance during traffic stops (Shoub,
Stauffer, and Song, 2021), resulted in greater responsive­
ness to sexual violence reports (Meier and Nicholson-Crotty,
2006; Schuck, 2018), and led to fewer citizen complaints
and less reliance on coercive policing tactics (Ba et al., 2021;
Rabe-Hemp, 2017). However, law enforcement organizations
in the US remain dominated by men. Women constitute only
about 13 percent of local police officers and only about 6
percent of state law enforcement officers. Women are nearly
absent from the highest ranks, holding only about 3 percent
of leadership positions (Bureau of Justice Statistics, 2020).
Research has also shown that women’s participation in law
enforcement, particularly in state law enforcement agencies,
has stagnated over the last two decades. For instance, Yu and
Viswanath (2022) reported that 6.47 percent of state-sworn
personnel were women in 2000, which remained nearly un­
changed in 2016.
What explains the persistent underrepresentation of
women in law enforcement, even as many public organi­
zations have become more gender inclusive? Research that
explores women’s experiences in occupations dominated by
men, including law enforcement and the military, provides
important clues. Studies show that a primary barrier to
women’s full integration into policing is the common per­
ception among policemen that women are unsuited for po­
lice work (Hughes, 2011; Martin, 1980; Prokos and Padavic,
2002; Rabe-Hemp, 2017; Yu, 2015). This perception is
shaped by an occupational culture that has been described as
a cult of masculinity (Silvestri, 2017). Norms and practices
based on masculine stereotypes promote the perception that
policing is the natural domain of men (Acker, 1990; Prokos
and Padavic, 2002), which encourages and justifies hostility
towards women despite findings that little to no gender
differences exist in officer job performance (Kakar, 2002;
Morash and Greene, 1986). Similarly, Doan and Portillo’s
(2019) in-depth interviews with men and women officers in
the US military, an institution dominated by men and struc­
tured very similarly to US police organizations, clarify our
understanding of men’s perceptions of women’s suitability
for the military. Their study documents widespread nega­
tive views by men about women’s fitness for military roles
based on stereotyped assumptions about women’s biology
and behavior. The study also captures the stout masculine
organizational structures and processes that hinder women’s
acceptance and advancement. Doan and Portillo’s findings
extend long-standing research demonstrating the effects of
negative gendered stereotypes and how they shape organiza­
tional dynamics in public institutions dominated by men and
motivate the current study.
Our study seeks to clarify the values and beliefs underlying
the negative perception that women are unsuited to police
work. To this effort, we employ Social Dominance Theory
(SDT) (Sidanius et al., 2018), a multilevel framework used to
understand the formation and maintenance of group-based
inequities. SDT identifies structural (e.g., institutions, or­
ganizations) and individual factors that lead to inter-group
hierarchy in which dominant groups exert power over sub­
ordinate groups. According to this perspective, policing is a

Our study asks, “How does preference for group-based
social hierarchy relate to officers’ assessment of women’s
suitability for law enforcement?” We argue that compared
to officers reporting lower SDO, higher SDO officers are
more likely to consider women unsuitable for police work
to protect their dominant social status. We also contend that
compared to lower SDO officers, higher SDO officers are
less likely to value diversity in law enforcement. We expect
that officer diversity value will mediate the anticipated neg­
ative association between SDO and officers’ assessment of
women’s suitability in law enforcement. Analyses of multi-
wave survey data collected from a state-level police organiza­

a more negative assessment of women’s suitability for law
enforcement than policewomen. Consistent with our theoret­
ical expectations, we also find that higher SDO officers more
negatively assess women’s suitability for law enforcement
than lower SDO officers. Moreover, we find that higher SDO
officers report less support for diversity in law enforcement
and that officer diversity value partially mediates the negative
link between SDO and assessment of women’s suitability for
law enforcement.
These findings advance PA research in several important
ways. First, our results demonstrate the relevance of SDT as a
framework and SDO as a construct for advancing PA research
on social inequities. Emerging research examines the relation­
ship between personality traits and organizational outcomes
(cf, Anessi-Pessina and Sicilia, 2020; van Witteloostuijn,
Esteve, and Boyne, 2017). To the best of our knowledge, no
PA research has considered the influence of SDO on public
employees’ work attitudes related to diversity, equity, and
inclusion. Second, the study contributes to long-standing
PA research on gender (Baker and Hassan, 2021; Bishu and
Heckler, 2021; Dehart-Davis et al., 2020; Doan and Portillo,
2019; Guy, 2017; Hatmaker and Hassan, 2023; Nielsen,
2015; Potter and Volden, 2021; Smith et al., 2021; Stivers,
2000; Wilkins, 2007), moving past identity categories as an
explanation for persistent inequality in public work contexts
dominated by men. We empirically test and unpack the be­
lief systems underlying gender inequity in a public organ­
ization dominated by men. Our results also add to the PA
literature on gender diversity in law enforcement (Baker and
Hassan, 2021; Meier and Nicholson-Crotty, 2006; Riccucci,
Van Ryzin, and Lavena, 2014; Schuck, 2018; Shoub, Stauffer,
and Song, 2021; Stauffer, Song, and Shoub, 2022; Yu, 2022).
Finally, our findings have implications for diversity manage­
ment in police agencies. Specifically, the results suggest that
police managers seeking diversity as a mechanism to promote
equitable social outcomes must think beyond increasing rep­
resentation and consider structural interventions to trans­
form policing into a hierarchy-attenuating institution. Such a
project would require restructuring organizations to allocate
power and status more equitably.
Next, we summarize existing research on women in po­
lice organizations. Then, we describe SDT and develop
hypotheses about the influence of SDO on officers’ assessment
of women’s suitability for law enforcement and the mech­
anism underlying the relationship. After that, we describe our
sample, data collection procedures, and methods and present
our results. Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings
for theory and praxis.
Literature review
Studies in PA, policing, and feminist criminology pro­
vide insights into women’s experiences in law enforce­
ment, including persistent career barriers. Studies show that
policewomen are frequently sorted into gendered occupa­
tional roles, such as sexual violence units, juvenile justice, or
community relations, and remain almost entirely excluded
from more traditionally masculine roles (e.g., SWAT teams)
(Rabe-Hemp, 2017). Even as entry into feminized police roles
increases women’s representation, the lack of prestige associ­
ated with such roles impedes women’s inclusion and career
advancement (Rabe-Hemp, 2017). Moreover, the literature
suggests that the most significant barriers to women’s inte­
gration are pervasive negative attitudes and disrespect from
colleagues (Yu, 2015). In particular, past studies report that
policemen largely consider women to be unsuited for police
work (Hughes, 2011; Martin, 1980; Rabe-Hemp, 2017).
Research suggests that policemen’s negative perceptions
of women are shaped by an occupational culture frequently
described as a “cult of masculinity” (Silvestri, 2017, 290).
In our collective imagination, policing embodies hegemonic
White masculine ideals (Hunt, 1990; Steinþórsdóttir and
Pétursdóttir, 2022; Todak and Brown, 2019). Police officers
are portrayed as aggressive and athletic, even though most po­
lice work is sedentary and administrative (Rabe-Hemp, 2017).
Consequently, many policemen do not believe women belong
in law enforcement because they think women lack the phys­
ical and assertive traits that they perceive are necessary for ef­
fective police work. However, existing research shows little or
no gender differences in job performance, ­decision-making,
and policing practices (Kakar, 2002; Morash and Greene,
1986; Shoub, Stauffer, and Song, 2021).
The prevailing masculine logic drives formal and informal
occupational socialization processes. Police recruits enter
the academy with a culturally constructed crime-fighter
image in mind, and this hyper-masculine image is reinforced
through recruitment and training materials (Gascon and
Schaefer 2003, Prokos and Padavic 2002). A study by Prokos
and Padavic (2002) found that police academy training
exaggerated differences between men and women, excluded
and degraded women, and promoted masculinity as essential
to policing. The study concluded that socialization through
academy training relied on a type of masculinity that brands
women as unsuitable for policing.
Although there is general agreement that police culture
in the US endorses White masculinity and resists women’s
integration (Hughes, 2011; Sanders, Gouweloos, and
Langan, 2022; Todak and Brown, 2019), recent scholarship

Yu (2018) found that officers persisted in law enforcement,
motivated by their desire to show the men that they were just
as good.
Social dominance theory
SDT is a multitiered theoretical framework that accounts
for the reciprocal individual and structural mechanisms
that facilitate group-based social hierarchy (Sidanius and
Pratto, 1999; Sidanius et al., 2018). The theory suggests that
status and resources are unequally distributed across groups,
producing asymmetrical power structures that foster domi­
nant and inferior social groupings. According to this perspec­
tive, institutional and individual factors work in tandem and
contribute to the production and maintenance of group-based
hierarchy. Moreover, the acceptance of ideologies and myths
that legitimize group-based inequality is partly determined
by indiviual preferences for group-based social hierarchy, or
Social Dominance Orientation (SDO) (Pratto et al., 1994).
SDO is an important individual difference variable with
two related dimensions—dominance and anti-egalitarianism
(Pratto et al., 1994). The dominance dimension, which
denotes a belief that some social groups are naturally supe­
rior to others, aligns with greater acceptance of aggressive
and punitive treatments towards subordinated groups. The
anti-egalitarianism dimension reflects a desire for structural
inequality by resisting policies and programs designed to help
less powerful groups, such as social welfare, foreign aid, af­
firmative action, and acceptance of immigrants and refugees
(Sidanius et al., 2018).
Research shows that, over time, SDO is a stable psycholog­
ical orientation (Sidanius et al., 2018). While the origins of
SDO are yet to be fully understood, it is thought to be both a
cause and an effect of hierarchal social structuring (Sidanius et
al., 2018). Recent research suggests that SDO may be a partly
heritable personality trait and shaped by social-environmental
conditions (Kleppestø et al., 2019). Studies in organizational
settings indicate that socialization processes can influence
the development of anti-egalitarian values and beliefs, in­
cluding SDO. A longitudinal study by Nicol, Charbonneau,
and Boies (2007) showed that military cadets’ SDO scores
increased over four years of training. Another longitudinal
study showed a reciprocal relationship between SDO, occu­
pational sorting, and socialization (Zubielevitch et al., 2022),
suggesting that although high SDO individuals were more
likely to ­self-select into hierarchy-enhancing occupations
(such as policing and the military),1 occupational socializa­
tion, organizational experiences, and contextual characteris­
tics may amplify or dim SDO’s effects.
SDT posits that institutions, including occupations and
organizations, are instrumental in maintaining group-based
inequalities because they determine the allocation of re­
sources in society (Sidanius et al., 2018). This perspective is
consistent with critical organization theories that suggest that
organizations are gendered (Acker, 1990) and rarely race-
neutral (Portillo, Humphrey, and Bearfield, 2022a, 2022b).
According to SDT, policing is a hierarchy-enhancing institu­
tion (Sidanius et al., 1994) because it represents the interests
of the dominant social group (Murphy et al., 2022). The his­
tory of policing in the US is “deeply implicated in the repro­
duction of racial schemas related to black criminality” (Ray,
Ortiz, and Nash, 2018, 4) and can be traced back to slave
patrols (Alexander, 2010). After the Civil War, the police en­
forced discriminatory policies that created and maintained
the unequal social standing of minoritized groups (Alexander,
2010). In the U.S., White men have always dominated law
enforcement; many police departments did not employ
people of color and women until the mid-1950s (Murphy et
al., 2022; Ray, Ortiz, and Nash, 2018). Research also shows
police officers report higher SDO than the general popula­
tion (Sidianius et al., 1994). However, although policing is a
hierarchy-enhancing occupation (Sidianius et al., 1994), there
is likely to be considerable individual variation in preferences

As noted previously, SDO has been shown to predict a
wide range of inter-group attitudes, including sexism, racism,
xenophobia, and public support for punitive criminal jus­
tice policies and outcomes (Sidanius et al., 2018). However,
most studies have focused on SDO in the general population,
with few examining the influence of SDO on public sector
employees’ work attitudes and behavior. Linking survey and
administrative data, Swencionis et al. (2021) found that higher
SDO was associated with more frequent use of force by White
police officers in the U.S. Van Droogenbroeck et al. (2022)
found that high SDO officers considered misconduct by other
officers less serious, and high SDO officers were less likely to
report peer misconduct to their superiors. Using survey data,
another recent study by Davis et al. (2023) found a negative
association between SDO and frontline police officers’ sup­

including procedural fairness, respecting the rights of citizens,
and police accountability. Finally, a recent study found that
SDO predicted endorsement of masculine norms among the
Canadian Armed Forces (Deng, Nicol, and Ralph, 2023).
While these findings underscore the relevance of SDO in PA
and policing scholarship, more research is needed, particu­
larly on the implications of SDO for workforce diversity in
public agencies.
Research model and hypotheses
Figure 1 depicts the research model and the three hypotheses
tested in this study. As we outlined previously, studies in po­
licing, feminist criminology, and PA have shown that a pri­
mary barrier to women’s integration into law enforcement
is policemen’s pervasive negative attitudes and disrespect
towards policewomen (Hunt 1990; Martin 1980; Rabe-
Hemp 2009; Yu 2015.) Moreover, despite studies showing
little or no gender difference in officer behavior or perfor­
mance (Kakar, 2002; Rabe-Hemp, 2017; Schoub et al.,
2018), the literature on women’s experiences in law enforce­
ment consistently reports policemen’s belief that women are
unsuited to the work (Hughes, 2011; Martin, 1980; Prokos
and Padavic, 2002; Rabe-Hemp, 2017). The literature also
suggests that policemen’s negative perceptions are at least
partially explained by an occupational culture that promotes
pervasive stereotypical perceptions that women lack neces­
sary physical and agentic traits necessary to be effective law
enforcement officers (Doan and Portillo, 2019), leading to a
hostile workplace for women (Prokos and Padavic, 2002; Yu,
2015).
We contend that these findings can be informed by
incorporating a social dominance perspective. As policing
is a hierarchy-enhancing institution comprised mostly of
members of the dominant group (i.e., White men), women
who enter policing may be perceived as a threat to men’s so­
cial status, prompting officers with higher SDO to invoke gen­
dered legitimizing myths to justify hostile and discriminatory
treatment towards women to protect the status quo. There is
evidence that SDO positively correlates with sexism (Austin
and Jackson, 2019), particularly hostile sexism and support
for traditional gender roles (Sidanius et al., 2018). Research
also shows that higher SDO individuals harbor beliefs that
women are incapable of successfully working outside the
home (Christopher and Wojda, 2008; Pratto et al., 1994)
and are inappropriate for leadership and managerial roles
(Manganelli, Bobbio, and Canova, 2012). Notably, research
shows that holding context constant, men report higher levels
of SDO than women (Sidanius et al., 2018). Pratto, Stallworth,
and Sidanius (1997) suggest that men’s higher SDO motivates
them to be more supportive of traditional gender roles to pro­
tect their dominant social status and power. Following these
studies, we expect officers with higher SDO to have more neg­
ative views about women’s suitability for policing, driven by a
desire to protect men’s dominant position and maintain gen­
dered social structures. Accordingly, the first hypothesis that
we test is as follows:
Hypothesis 1: Officers reporting higher SDO will more
negatively assess women’s suitability for law enforcement
than officers with lower SDO.
We investigate the role of officer diversity value to shed light
on the mechanisms underlying the expected negative associa­
tion between SDO and officer assessments of women’s suita­
bility for law enforcement. Research exploring the conditions
necessary to facilitate an inclusive organizational climate
suggests that employees’ beliefs and attitudes about diver­
sity directly affect outcomes related to diversity and inclu­
sion (Ely and Thomas, 2001). The psychological construct
“pro-diversity belief” is defined as “beliefs individuals hold
about how group composition affects group function, i.e.,
whether individuals perceive diversity as beneficial, detri­
mental, or neutral for the group functioning” (Van Dick et
al., 2008, 1467). Van Knippenberg et al. (2004) argue that
pro-diversity beliefs may reduce inter-group conflict by
intervening in processes of subgroup categorization that

produce identity threat and conflict. According to Homan

assess diverse group members as each possessing individual
differences rather than defining them based on their subgroup
membership (e.g., based on gender, race, religion, sexual ori­
entation, or ethnicity). Consequently, in diverse workgroups,
higher pro-diversity values may diminish identity threats and
promote more positive attitudes among dominant group
members about subgroups, facilitating the conditions con­
ducive to improved teamwork, problem-solving, and group
performance (Meyer, 2017). Accordingly, officers with
higher pro-diversity values will likely provide more positive
assessments of women’s suitability for law enforcement.
Due to the impact of pro-diversity beliefs on critical work­
group and organizational outcomes, factors that cultivate or
thwart such beliefs and values are particularly interesting. Van
Knippenberg et al. (2004) suggest that such beliefs are more
likely to be fostered through positive previous experiences with
diverse group members. Flynn (2005) argues that individual
difference variables and personality traits likely play an es­
sential role in developing pro-diversity values and beliefs and
calls for further exploration of diversity belief antecedents. We
contend that SDO has a negative association with employee
diversity value. Extensive evidence demonstrates that higher
SDO is associated with lower individual support for equity-
promoting public policies (e.g., immigration, same-sex mar­
riage, and affirmative action) likely to enhance organizational
demographic diversity (Sidanius et al., 2018). Further, higher
SDO individuals may be more likely to contest organizational
diversity efforts because such strategies may draw attention
to legitimizing myths about salient subordinate groups and
evoke hostile emotional responses, motivating discrimination
against those groups (Lee, 2023).
As law enforcement is a hierarchy-enhancing institution,
some subordinate group members may report high SDO
(Sidanius et al., 2018). For example, higher SDO officers
who are women may resist diversity efforts because they sup­
port the subordination of their group vis à vis White men,
including by resisting the advancement of women into leader­
ship, even as they fight bias to build and advance their careers.
Nevertheless, as diversity is central to law enforcement values
and missions, including the organization that is the focus of
the current research, cultivating representative organizations
is identified as one of policing’s most significant challenges
(Farrell and Barao, 2023; Stergioulis, 2018). Research shows
that police managers and line-level officers generally report
support for diversity because it facilitates public trust (Farrell
and Barao, 2023) and support for the police (Stergioulis,
2018). However, studies on officers’ perceptions of diversity
add important nuance, complicating these findings. Several
studies found that White policemen opposed diversity efforts
(Wieslander, 2018) and reported perceived favoritism towards
non-majority officers (Farrell and Barao, 2023; Loftus, 2008).
Notably, Farrell and Barao (2023) found that regardless of
identity, officers reported support for creating an organiza­
tion demographically reflective of the communities they serve,
but they disagreed with or resisted organizational diversity
efforts and implementation.
Incorporating pro-diversity beliefs into analyses of the gen­
dered dynamics of law enforcement may provide important
insight into officer responses to organizational diversification
efforts. As noted previously, policing is a hierarchy-enhancing
institution that attracts individuals who prefer group-based
social hierarchy (Sidanius et al., 1994). Because higher SDO
officers prefer social hierarchy, they are less likely to value
demographic diversity within law enforcement, they are more
likely to consider that women do not belong in law enforce­
ment. This argument suggests that officer diversity value will
mediate the connection between SDO and officers’ assessment
of women’s suitability for law enforcement. Accordingly, the
second and third hypotheses that we propose and test are as
follows:
Hypothesis 2: Officers reporting higher SDO will value
diversity in law enforcement less than officers reporting
lower SDO.
Hypothesis 3: Officers’ diversity value will mediate the
negative association between SDO and their assessment of
women’s suitability for law enforcement.
Data and measures
Sample and procedures
Data for our study come from multi-wave surveys
administered to all sworn personnel working in a large state-
level law enforcement organization in the Midwest region
of the U.S. At the beginning of the study, the organization
had 2,508 total employees, 1,711 sworn officers, and 797 ad­
ministrative/professional employees. Our analysis excludes
administrative employees because the study focuses on law
enforcement officers. Approximately 88 percent of the 1,711
sworn officers were White, and 91 percent were men. The law
enforcement officers worked in units geographically distrib­
uted throughout the state.

the second 2019 survey. We matched the responses from
both surveys using a unique identifier. After listwise dele­
tion of responses with missing values, our analyses of the
2019 sample included 675 matched surveys/responses. The
responding officers were 91 percent men, 86 percent White,
and 60 percent ranked line-level/patrol officers, which closely
matched the demographic composition of the agency’s sworn
personnel in 2019. The average age of the respondents was
39.11 years, and the average tenure in the organization was
12.45 years.
As indicated in Table 1, we distributed a third survey in
August 2021. This survey included the 2019 survey items on
women’s suitability for law enforcement and diversity climate
and three new items on officer diversity value. Additionally,
the survey included questions related to the COVID-19 pan­
demic, community protests, job-related stress, and leadership
practices. The 2021 survey administration process closely re­
flected that of 2019. We used an updated personnel list with
emails provided by the agency to distribute a recruitment
email to the organization’s 1,498 sworn officers, followed
by the survey one week later. Officers had about a month
to complete the survey. We sent multiple reminder emails to
the officers in the agency to boost the survey response rate.
In total, 748 officers (49.9 percent) completed the survey,
of which 88 percent were White, 92 percent were men, and
54 percent were line-level officers. The average age of the
respondents was 39.66 years, and the average tenure was
14.08 years. After listwise deletion of responses with missing
values, our final multivariate regression model included 481
officers who completed both the 2019 surveys and the 2021
survey.
Measures
Our dependent variable, officer assessment of women’s suit­
ability for women in law enforcement, is measured using
three items from the 2019 and 2021 surveys. The items are
“Women are as capable as men of handling the duties of law
enforcement,” “Law enforcement is not a suitable occupation
for women” (reverse coded), and “A woman can be just as
good a police officer as a man.” We adapted the items used by
Chu (2018) and measured responses using a five-point Likert-
type scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The
measure’s internal reliability coefficients (Cronbach alphas)
are 0.86 and 0.81 for the 2019 and 2021 samples, respectively.
We averaged the responses to these three items to create a
compositive scale for each of the years surveyed, with a higher
value indicating a more positive assessment and a lower value
indicating a more negative assessment of women’s suitability
for law enforcement.
The key predictor variable, SDO, is measured with five
items from the first 2019 survey. The items come from a
scale developed by Pratto et al. (1994), asked respondents
to indicate how much they favor or oppose (1 = strongly op­
pose; 5 = strongly favor) each idea: “An ideal society requires
some groups to be on top and others to be on the bottom,”
“Groups at the bottom are just as deserving as groups at the
top” (reverse coded), “Group equality should not be our pri­
mary goal,” “It is unjust to try to make groups equal,” and
“We should do what we can to equalize conditions for dif­
ferent groups” (reverse coded). The Cronbach’s alpha for this
measure is 0.73. We averaged the items to create a composite
scale score, with a higher score indicating a higher SDO and a
stronger preference for group-based hierarchy.
As noted earlier, the mediator variable, diversity value,
is measured with three items from the 2021 survey. The
items were adapted from Kauff et al.’s (2019) validated Pro-
Diversity Beliefs Scale (PDBS). Officers were asked how much
they agreed or disagreed, on a five-point scale, with the fol­
lowing statements: “A law enforcement organization that
is diverse functions better than one that is not diverse,” “A
law enforcement organization with a high degree of diversity
is better able to tackle new problems,” and “A diverse law
enforcement organization can overcome societal challenges
better than one that is not diverse.” The Cronbach alpha for
this measure is 0.90. We averaged the items to create a com­
posite scale score, with a higher score indicating greater pro-
diversity beliefs in law enforcement.
To isolate the relationship between SDO and officer assess­
ment of the suitability of women for law enforcement, we
control for officer perceptions of their organization’s diversity
climate. Research shows that perceptions of diversity climate
may influence employee assessment of organizational diver­
sity (Van Dick et al., 2008). It may also be related to officer
preference for group-based hierarchy, as well as officer diver­
sity value. Diversity climate is measured with five items from
the 2019 and 2021 surveys. The items, which were taken from
a community diversity climate scale developed by Ragins et
al. (2012), are “This organization welcomes employees of
different races and ethnicities,” “Racial or ethnic minority
employees are NOT respected at this organization” (reverse
coded), “This organization fosters a positive work climate
for women employees,” “Employees of different races and
ethnicities would like to work in this organization,” and “This
organization is a model for valuing diversity.” All items use a
Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).
The Cronbach alphas for the measures are 0.79 and 0.72 for
the 2019 and 2021 samples, respectively. We calculated the
average of the responses to these items to create a composite
scale, with a higher score indicating perceptions of a work cli­
mate more supportive of diversity and inclusion.

organization tenure2 in the analysis. We obtained data for

Confirmatory factor analysis
We conducted confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) to assess the
construct validity of the study measures. Table 2 displays the
standardized factor loadings obtained from the CFAs for
the 2019 and 2021 samples. All scale items have statistically
significant factor loadings (p < .01) for their latent constructs.
Although standardized factor loading for two SDO items was
below 0.50 (0.41 and 0.43), we included them in the analysis
because their exclusion would artificially improve the fit of the
measurement model. The fit indices obtained from the CFA

CFI = 0.94, and TLI = 0.93. Additionally, the CFA results also

enforcement items. As shown in Table 2, one diversity climate

exclusion would artificially improve the fit of the measure­

The fit indices obtained from the CFA results of the 2021
model are χ2 = 147.64 (41), RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 0.96, and
TLI = 0.95. None of the intercorrelations between any pairs

that the data fit both the measurement models well and that
the measures have adequate convergent and discriminant
validity.

Regression analysis
The law enforcement officers who responded to our survey
work in posts or units that are dispersed throughout the state.
The nested structure of the data violates the independence
assumption necessary for Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) re­
gression analysis. Theoretically, officers working in the same
unit may be more alike in their assessment of women’s suit­
ability for law enforcement than officers working in other
units. Hence, we need to account for any variability in the
outcome variable at the unit level, which can be accom­
plished by including unit fixed effects in the OLS regression
models (McNeish and Kelley, 2019). Alternatively, we can es­
timate Multilevel or Hierarchical Linear Models (Bryk and
Raudenbush, 1987) to account for the unit-level variation in
officers’ assessment of women’s suitability for law enforce­
ment. We estimated both Multilevel and OLS regression
models with unit fixed effects and found that the results of
both analyses are very similar. Because we are primarily inter­
ested in the effects of SDO, an individual difference variable,
we present the results of OLS regression models as our main
results. As noted above, all our OLS models included unit
fixed effects, and we used robust standard errors to assess the
statistical significance of the estimated regression coefficients.

Discussion and conclusion
The current study aimed to understand policemen’s reluctance
to accept women as equals in law enforcement. Understanding
the sources of policemen’s views that women are unsuited to
police work or do not belong in law enforcement is important
because such views are likely to be associated with discrim­
inatory behaviors and organizational cultures that alienate
women, undermine their sense of belonging, impede their
professional development and advancement, or lead to them
exiting the profession altogether (Hughes, 2011; Yu, 2015).
Consequently, negative assessments of policewomen may
contribute to the persistent exclusion of women in policing.
Our survey data enabled us to identify how officer character­
istics, preferences for group-based social hierarchy, and diver­
sity value related to officer assessments of women’s suitability
for law enforcement. In doing so, we located specific barriers
to women’s full integration into police across its ranks.
Our results show that while most officers considered women
suitable for law enforcement, officers who are men report
more negative assessments of women’s suitability for law en­
forcement than officers who are women. This finding reflects
extant research, which shows that men’s disrespect creates a
negative work culture that undermines women’s acceptance,
performance, and career development (Hughes, 2011; Prokos
and Padavic, 2002; Rabe-Hemp, 2017; Yu, 2015). The re­
sistance to women appears to stem from a perceived threat
that women may alter distinctively masculine cultural norms
(Hughes, 2011; Rabe-Hemp, 2017).
Our analysis shows a consistent negative association be­
tween SDO and officers’ assessment of the suitability of
women in law enforcement. While this finding reflects prior
research linking high SDO to sexism and adherence to other
legitimizing myths associated with the subordination of
women (Sidanius et al., 2018), to our knowledge, no prior
study in PA has established this relationship. Our results also
demonstrate that a key mechanism underlying the relationship
between SDO and negative assessment of women’s suitability
for law enforcement is officer diversity value. These findings
advance PA research on gender diversity (Bishu and Heckler,
2021; Dehart-Davis et al., 2020; Doan and Portillo, 2019;
Guy, 2017; Hatmaker and Hassan, 2023; Nielsen, 2015;
Potter and Volden, 2021; Smith et al., 2021; Stivers, 2000;
Wilkins, 2007) by moving past reliance on social identity to
explain persistent inequality in organizations dominated by
men by empirically examining the underlying belief systems
that create and maintain gender inequity. The results also
add to the growing PA literature on gender diversity in law
enforcement (Baker and Hassan, 2021; Meier and Nicholson-
Crotty, 2006; Riccucci, Van Ryzin, and Lavena, 2014; Schuck,
2018; Shoub, Stauffer, and Song, 2021; Stauffer, Song, and
Shoub, 2022; Yu and Viswanath, 2022). Overall, the results
suggest that higher SDO officers are more susceptible to
status threats in the presence of outgroup members and or­
ganizational diversity. Such efforts make legitimizing myths
and negative stereotypes about marginalized groups sa­
lient (Sidanius et al., 2018) and evoke negative emotional
responses motivating discriminatory behaviors (Lee, 2023).
Consequently, such officers are likely to actively discourage
and resist efforts towards gender inclusion.
Our findings potentially complicate police reform
approaches that predominantly rely on increasing the num­
bers of women and individuals from other marginalized
groups to advance organizational equity. Because policing is
considered a hierarchy-enhancing institution (Sidanius et al.,
2018), high SDO individuals, including women and members
of minoritized groups, may enter policing because of its so­
cial stratifying impact. Lee (2023) draws on explorations of
women in hierarchy-enhancing professions (those dominated
by men) and argues that women who excelled held higher
SDO levels, meaning they supported the hegemonic status of
the dominant group. As such, women may experience intense
discrimination as they seek to build their careers while simul­
taneously discriminating against other women by defining
them with negative gender stereotypes. In doing so, women
maintain the hierarchal status quo and reinforce their same-
gender group’s subordination. Evidence of Lee’s argument is
found in feminist criminology studies that show little unity
among policewomen, policewomen distancing themselves
from one another, and a desire by policewomen to align
with men (Dodge and Pogrebin, 2001; Rabe-Hemp, 2017;
Saunders et al., 2022). Furthermore, Umphress et al. (2008)
suggest that women with higher SDO might be more likely to
discriminate against women peers in the workplace because
they prefer to associate with the dominant group.
Law enforcement managers seeking organizational di­
versity to promote equitable social outcomes may need to
think beyond a primary reliance on increasing representative
numbers and consider other strategies. For example, stra­
tegically recruiting community-oriented officers who have
egalitarian values may help law enforcement organizations
change organizational cultural norms related to diversity,
equity, and inclusion, as well as officers’ mindsets about po­
licing. Sidanius et al. (2018) also call for more research on
the structural components of organizations that unequally
allocate power across social groups. Moving beyond a sole
focus on individuals to include organizational processes and
structural change may be a promising approach (Saunders et
al., 2022). For example, several studies provide preliminary
evidence that effective performance evaluation and greater
performance monitoring may influence high SDO individuals
to adopt some egalitarian practices (Umphress et al., 2008).
Our finding that higher education did not influence officer
diversity value or their support for women in law enforce­
ment contradicts earlier research that found higher educa­
tion correlated with more egalitarian attitudes (Brewster and
Padavic, 2000). While colleges and universities are generally
considered hierarchy-attenuating, specific courses of study
may be either hierarchy-enhancing or hierarchy-attenuating
and more likely to attract students with corresponding SDO
levels (Sidanius et al., 2018). As such, police officers may

select hierarchy-enhancing courses. Sidanius et al. (2006)
found that regardless of major, men reported significantly
higher SDO than women across their college careers. This
helps explain policemen’s lower levels of support for women
in nontraditional occupational roles, including policing.
Importantly, education duration dampened SDO expression
but not necessarily individual SDO levels. These findings
support our argument about the limitations of organiza­
tional diversity strategies heavily focused on individual-level
interventions.
While our study informs PA research and practice, it has
some limitations. As we use survey data collected at three
points, our results are unlikely to be influenced by common
method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Nevertheless, the
responses relied on self-reports from participants and fo­
cused on sensitive topics, which might result in some response
bias motivated by a desire to protect their organization and
their image as police officers. Future research should con­
sider using a list experiment to elicit accurate information
from officers about diversity and their assessment of women
in law enforcement. Additionally, because our study used a
cross-sectional design, we cannot infer any causal connections
between the study variables. Future research should consider
using a longitudinal design to examine how socialization
affects SDO and whether SDO changes in different stages of a
police officer’s career. A qualitative research design may pro­
vide important insights into how socialization and SDO work
in tandem to shape officers’ work attitudes and behaviors.
Future research should also consider studying the influence
of SDO in other public sector organizations dominated by
men, such as the military and fire departments. Additionally,
PA scholars should consider investigating gender-integration
mechanisms necessary to facilitate gender parity in public
organizations dominated by men. Finally, our study did not
distinguish between women with different gender and racial
identities. Future research should take an intersectional ap­
proach, as gender parity and inclusion in police and other
public organizations dominated by men cannot be fully
achieved without understanding the differing and complex
challenges that women of color, lesbians, and women with
diverse gender presentations face.

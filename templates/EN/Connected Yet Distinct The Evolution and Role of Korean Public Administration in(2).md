ABSTRACT
Over the past seven decades, South Korea has developed a distinctive trajectory in its public administration (PA) through bal-
ancing the domains of research, education, and engagement with government. Our analysis shows that it embodies a connected
yet distinct character, closely linked to Western administrative science and global PA scholarship, yet continually reshaped by
Korea's bureaucratic culture, Confucian legacies, and pragmatic governance needs. To map this evolution systematically, we
examine the entire Korean PA ecosystem by (1) analyzing 4447 scholarly articles published in four leading domestic and inter-
national journals over the past two decades, (2) reviewing PA curricula across Korean universities, and (3) tracing government-­
funded research projects and faculty appointments to senior public positions over the same period. The findings suggest that the
strong institutional linkages between research, education, and government have enhanced Korea's administrative capacity and
responsiveness, while also highlighting the need for greater intellectual independence and theoretical inquiry.
1   |   Introduction
Over the past seven decades, public administration (PA) in South
Korea1 has evolved in tandem with profound political, economic,
and social transformations nationwide (Chung 2007; Jung 2014;
Park et  al.  2016; Raadschelders  2009). From post-­war recon-
struction to the modern-­day knowledge-­based state, Korean PA
has developed into a mature discipline that effectively integrates
theory and practice. Its evolution embodies Korea's “connected
yet distinct” identity—shaped by Western administrative sci-
ence and global PA scholarship, yet deeply rooted in Confucian
traditions, a centralized bureaucratic culture, and a pragmatic
orientation toward government service and civil servant train-
ing. As such, Korean PA offers a distinctive case for understand-
ing how a national administrative system can absorb global
ideas while preserving its indigenous institutional foundations
and scholarly traditions.
Building on this historical trajectory, this study presents the
first comprehensive analysis of PA in South Korea that sys-
tematically integrates three interrelated domains: (1) research
trends and methodologies, (2) PA education and civil service
training, and (3) the institutional linkages between PA schol-
arship and government practice. While previous studies have
provided snapshots of Korean PA by examining research pat-
terns (e.g., Choi and Park 2011; Eun 2016; Jeon 2016; Kim 2016;
Kwon 2016; Lee 2016a, Lee 2016b; Moon et al. 2014; Park 2008;
Park  2016; Park and Shim  2012), educational developments
(e.g., Cho  2005; Kim and Kim  2016; Kim and Myeong  2014;
Lee and Ryu 2016; Moon et al. 2023; Park 2008), or historical

To address these limitations, this study provides a holistic and
systematic understanding of Korean PA—how it has evolved,
how it operates across research, education, and government,
and the directions it should take. To this end, this study ad-

PA evolved, and how do these patterns compare with global

To answer these research questions, we analyze 4447 scholarly
articles published between 2005 and 2024 in leading domestic
journals (i.e., Korean Public Administration Review [KPAR],
Korean Journal of Policy Studies [KJPS]) and international jour-
nals (i.e., Public Administration Review [PAR], Journal of Public
Administration Research and Theory [JPART]) to identify topical
and methodological trends; review the curricula of all Korean
universities offering PA programs to evaluate their structure
and linkage with national civil service examinations as well as
in-­service training systems; and examine government-­funded
PA research projects and faculty appointments to senior govern-
ment positions to uncover the embedded relationship between
scholarship and governance.2 Taken together, these analyses
reveal the distinctive yet connected character of Korean PA in
bridging theory and practice.
2   |   Historical Trajectory of Korean PA
The historical trajectory of Korean PA can be understood
through a set of theoretical lenses, including institutional
path dependence (Pierson 2000), reflected in Confucian bu-
reaucratic traditions; policy diffusion (Berry and Berry 1990),
through the adoption of Western models of modern PA during
periods of state-­building and reform; and paradigmatic
change (Hall 1993), as Korean PA has undergone major shifts
in response to democratization, economic crises, and evolving
citizen demands.
The intellectual origins of Korean PA trace back to the Joseon
Dynasty (1392–1897), when Confucianism shaped a hierarchi-
cal and morally grounded bureaucracy governed by the civil ser-
vice examination system (gwa-­geo), which served as a principal
avenue to prestige and upward social mobility (Kim 2012; Moon
et al. 2023). By the late seventeenth century, however, the prac-
tical learning (Sil-­hak) movement had begun advocating prag-
matic and people-­oriented governance (Kim 2012). One of its
most influential thinkers, Jung Yak-­yong, in his Mokmin Simseo
(Book on Governing the People 1821), articulated a systematic
manual for local and general administration—including per-
sonnel management, finance, justice, and welfare—establish-
ing an indigenous foundation for later administrative thought
(Kim 2012).
It was in the mid-­1950s, under the strong influence of the
United States, that modern PA emerged in South Korea as a
distinct academic discipline. Prior to this transplantation, edu-
cation in the field had been largely grounded in Japanese-­style
administrative law and other legal studies (Kim 2012; Moon
et al. 2023). In particular, the Minnesota Project (1954–1962)
played a pivotal role in shaping the development of PA in Korea
by dispatching students and professors to the University of
Minnesota to prepare for government reforms and strengthen
Korea's higher education system (Moon et al. 2023). During
this period, Korean PA became firmly established in sev-
eral ways: by founding the Graduate School of Public
Administration (GSPA) at Seoul National University in 1959;
by establishing the Korean Public Administration Research
Forum in 1956 (later renamed the Korean Association for
Public Administration [KAPA] in 1961); and by including PA
as a required subject in the Higher Civil Service Examination
in 1959 (Kim 2012; Kim and Myeong 2014).
In the 1960s and 1970s, the Park Chung-­hee administration
(1961–1979) vigorously pursued rapid national economic growth,
and PA served as the intellectual engine of the developmental
state (Jung 2014; Kim 2012; Moon et al. 2023). During this pe-
riod, PA served both as a vehicle for government modernization
and a training ground for technocratic elites who implemented
economic planning and development strategies. Consequently,
the fields of development and comparative administration flour-
ished, and universities broadened their course offerings in these
areas until their prominence began to decline after the 1990s
(Kim 2012).
Evidence for Practice
•	 Research: Korean PA research has achieved significant
quantitative growth and methodological sophistica-
tion domestically and internationally. Yet, this pro-
gress has reinforced dependence on Anglo-­American
theories and methods, limiting contextual relevance
and hindering indigenous perspectives.
•	 Education: Korean PA education has closely aligned
with governmental and student needs. Undergraduate
programs train future civil servants, while graduate
programs enhance incumbent capacity. This system
has ensured a steady supply of competent officials but
also constrained intellectual diversity and pedagogi-
cal innovation.
•	 Government linkages: Korean PA academia and
government maintain a long-­standing partnership
through government-­funded projects and the ap-
pointment of PA scholars to senior posts. However,
this relationship remains largely consultative, posi-
tioning PA in a supporting rather than leading role in
governance.
•	 Future direction: Korean PA is well positioned to move
beyond a responsive, government-­centered model
toward generative distinctiveness—advancing con-
textualized theory, inquiry-­driven education, and co-
produced governance that enables the discipline not
only to support administration but to lead it.

Between the 1980s and 1990s, Korean PA underwent a
major paradigm shift. Rapid economic growth gave rise to
increasingly complex social problems, while diverse social
demands—suppressed under the military regime—erupted
amid the country's democratization movement (Kang  2008;
Kim  2012; Moon et  al.  2023). Against this unsettled back-
drop, public policy emerged as a key instrument for manag-
ing increasing social complexity, supported by the growing
influence of economics-­based quantitative methodologies.
Accordingly, universities introduced public policy courses
and began recruiting faculty members specializing in the field
(Moon et al. 2023). Throughout the 1990s, with the inaugu-
ration of the first two civilian governments (Kim Young-­sam
in 1993 and Kim Dae-­jung in 1998), civil society grew stron-
ger, and governance studies began to attract scholarly atten-
tion. The implementation of full-­scale local autonomy in 1995
further expanded research on local politics and local govern-
ment management. The end of the decade was marked by
the Asian financial crisis of 1997, which spurred New Public
Management (NPM) reforms emphasizing efficiency and
performance-­based accountability (Kim 2012).
In the 2000s and onward, the governance and NPM discourses
that had emerged in the late 1990s became firmly embedded
in administrative practice and academic research. The Roh
Moo-­hyun administration (2003–2008), for example, empha-
sized participatory governance, promoting citizen engage-
ment and transparency in public decision-­making. Alongside
these developments, the rapid advancement of information
and communication technology (ICT) spurred growing inter-
est in e-­government as part of broader public sector reforms
(Kim  2012). During this period, research on NPM-­related
themes expanded considerably; however, since the 2010s,
scholarly attention has gradually shifted toward post-­NPM
agendas, particularly publicness, social enterprises, delibera-
tive processes, quality of life, and safety (Baik and Park 2022).
Following the COVID-­19 pandemic, the scope of research has
further diversified to encompass studies on human resource
and organizational change, crisis management, welfare and
economic policies, and the experiences of vulnerable groups,
offering practical insights for the post-­pandemic administra-
tive landscape (Kim and Kim 2022).
In sum, Korean PA has long been shaped by close connectivity
among research, education, and government, enhancing admin-
istrative responsiveness and problem-­solving capacity. Yet this
strength has also constrained theoretical autonomy and critical
inquiry through an overriding emphasis on policy relevance.
The next stage of Korean PA thus lies not in loosening its ties to
government, but in strengthening the quality of this connectiv-
ity by sustaining theoretically mature and independent scholar-
ship alongside engagement with practice.
3   |   Section 1: Evolution of Korean PA Scholarship
(2005–2024)
In Section  1, we trace the evolutionary development of
Korean PA research within the global scholarly landscape.
Specifically, we address three interrelated questions: (a) how
research themes in Korean PA have shifted over time; (b)
how methodological approaches have evolved in response to
these thematic changes; and (c) how the global presence and
research patterns of Korean scholars are reflected in leading
international journals. To this end, our analysis draws on four
leading journals identified earlier—KPAR, KJPS, PAR, and
JPART—which together represent the core scholarly platform
of Korean and global PA.3
3.1   |   Trends in PA Research Themes
We systematically analyzed research themes extracted from the
titles and abstracts of all articles published in four major PA jour-
nals between 2005 and 2024 (4447 articles in total).4 Detailed
classification criteria are provided in Supporting Information:
Table A2.
Table 1 presents the domestic distribution of research themes by
5-­year period. Across both KPAR and KJPS, public management
(KPAR 41.7%; KJPS 39.3%) and PA theory (KPAR 31.4%; KJPS
25.3%) dominated research, together accounting for roughly
70% of publications. It is also notable that human resource man-
agement (HRM) and public finance and economics ranked next
(HRM: KPAR 6.5%; KJPS 8.1%; public finance and economics:
KPAR 8.1%; KJPS 10.4%), further contributing to the dominant
portion of research output.
From 2005 to 2014, normative and institutional studies empha-
sizing administrative accountability, public ethics, and institu-
tional reform prevailed, reflecting a state-­centered paradigm
(Choi and Park 2011; Kwon 2016; Park and Shim 2012). Between
2015 and 2019, research shifted toward organizational behavior,
performance, HRM, leadership, and civic engagement, signal-
ing a transition from administrative efficiency to citizen partic-
ipation after democratization (Eun 2016; Jeon 2016; Park 2016).
Since 2020, themes such as social equity, e-­government, digital
innovation, and governance have become more prevalent, align-
ing with Korea's digital transformation and a growing emphasis
on inclusive governance (Kim and Kim 2016; Park et al. 2016;
Raadschelders 2009).
Table 2 presents the global distribution of research themes by 5-­
year period. PAR and JPART exhibit similar core distributions—
public management (PAR 41.3%; JPART 40.6%) and PA theory
(PAR 29.2%; JPART 41.1%). However, their thematic diversity
is broader and more evenly distributed across HRM, public fi-
nance and economics, e-­government and digital innovation,
and citizen participation and governance, whereas Korean PA
places relatively greater emphasis on HRM and public finance
and economics. Notably, both Korean and global PA devote lim-
ited attention to global or comparative administration, reveal-
ing an imbalanced epistemic landscape in the field worldwide
(Bhuiyan and Perry 2024).

(see Table 11). By contrast, graduate programs primarily serve
in-­service officials, emphasizing capacity building and profes-
sional specialization.
To meet strong demand for civil service careers, Korean PA cur-
ricula are closely aligned with national civil service examina-
tions across Grades 5, 7, and 9.7 These exams involve multi-­stage
selection processes combining aptitude tests (PSAT), written ex-
aminations, and interviews, with subject requirements varying
by rank and job series. As a result, undergraduate PA programs
commonly include courses in administrative law, PA, economics,
and political science aligned with these examination subjects.
Table 12 illustrates broader curricular trends across Korean PA
programs. Many departments also offer PSAT-­oriented courses
and separate exam-­preparation programs to support students
pursuing public service careers.
At the graduate level, Korean PA programs primarily aim to
strengthen the professional competencies of in-­service officials.
Although they also help cultivate researchers, doctoral training
has traditionally depended on US PA programs for advanced ac-
ademic development. Consequently, Korean graduate education
in PA tends to be largely practice-­oriented, focusing on capacity
building for current public officials.
This orientation is exemplified by the prevalence of specialized
(professional) graduate schools, contracted departments, and
evening programs. As shown in Table 7, in 2025, there were 320
PA graduate programs nationwide, of which 148 (46.3%) oper-
ated in the evening, reflecting their accessibility for working
professionals. Among these evening programs, 139 (93.9%) were
specialized professional programs designed for practitioners.
Additionally, among the 26 contracted departments across all
fields, 18 (69.2%) were tailored to the needs of specified public
organizations.
Many PA graduate programs also offer short, practice-­focused
courses for incumbent officials. For instance, Seoul National
University's GSPA operates the Digital Platform Government
Innovation Program for executives in central government,
which is designed to strengthen understanding of big data and
artificial intelligence in support of the national digital-­platform
government initiative.8 Together, these features underscore the
applied and practitioner-­centered character of Korean PA grad-
uate education.

5.2   |   Ministerial Appointments of PA Faculty
We compiled data on ministers' former occupations over the past
20 years (2005–2025) using web-­based searches. A distinctive
feature of ministerial appointments in Korea is the diversity of
occupational backgrounds.
As shown in Table 17, career civil servants account for the larg-
est share of ministers over the past two decades (34%), followed
by politicians (29.8%). Academics also constitute a substantial
proportion of appointments (23.5%). Within this group, profes-
sors of PA make up 2.5% of all ministers, but when considered
within the academic category alone, PA accounts for 10.7%. This
indicates that PA scholars have played a particularly influential
role in government by directly assuming leadership positions
within ministries. This trend was especially pronounced during
the Lee Myung-­bak administration, which appointed three pro-
fessors in PA as Ministers.
This pattern is striking in comparative perspective. For ex-
ample, Japan, a neighboring country with a parliamentary
system, fills its cabinet exclusively with politicians drawn
from the majority party. Even in the United States, which
shares the presidential system with South Korea, it is rare for
academics—let alone PA scholars—to be appointed as minis-
ters. The Korean case thus highlights a distinctive integration
of academic expertise into the highest levels of government,
underscoring the unique role of PA scholarship in shaping
governance (for further details, see Supporting Information:
Appendix 3).
6   |   Discussion
6.1   |   Research: From Adoption to Contribution

achieved remarkable quantitative growth and methodological
sophistication in research. Our analysis shows that this prog-
ress is evident not only domestically but also internationally.
Empirical studies have expanded rapidly, and research has
been highly responsive to societal and governmental agendas,
demonstrating strong practical relevance.
However, as Jung (2016) pointed out, Korean PA research faces
two distinctive limitations: (1) a lack of relevance to real adminis-
trative and societal contexts, and (2) insufficient scientific rigor.
The adoption of US-­based PA frameworks, theories, and meth-
odologies substantially contributed to Korea's modernization,

tion. Bhuiyan and Perry (2024) identified geographical, meth-

global PA. These characteristics are also evident in the trajectory
of Korean PA, which has developed largely within this global
structure of epistemic dependence. Moreover, an overreliance
on empiricism has limited theoretical creativity, as much of the
research has remained at the level of correlation analysis, re-
vealing a persistent weakness in causal inference (Jung 2016).
This tendency is also reflected in the relatively limited use of
qualitative research.

largely unfolded within a “catch-­up paradigm,” characterized
by reactive rather than pioneering societal and administrative
agendas. This does not mean that Korean PA has lacked internal
reflection. Since the 1990s, scholars have examined its identity
and relevance, but the impact of these discussions has been un-
even due to institutional pressures for policy responsiveness and
short-­term empirical research. The next stage for Korean PA
should, therefore, shift from adoption to contribution in knowl-
edge production—advancing empirically grounded yet theoret-
ically generative research that will help expand the epistemic
map of global PA.
6.2   |   Education: From Preparation to Inquiry
In Korea, PA education has been institutionally well established
and has grown in response to strong societal demand. As an inde-
pendent discipline distinct from law and political science, PA has
maintained its high competitiveness within the social sciences.
This growth has been particularly prominent at the undergrad-
uate level, where PA remains closely tied to civil-­service recruit-
ment. The structure of PA curricula has long been aligned with
civil-­service examinations, and universities have been proactive
in responding to students' career needs. At the graduate level, PA
programs are primarily designed to meet the needs of in-­service
officials and working professionals—particularly through profes-
sional, evening, or contract-­based tracks—which have helped en-
hance practical administrative capacities within the bureaucracy.
The strong institutional linkage between universities and the civil
service has thus ensured a steady supply of competent public em-
ployees, thus sustaining PA's labor-­market relevance.

curricula remain heavily oriented toward preparing future civil

environment has reduced students to passive test-­takers rather
than inquisitive learners, fostering standardized pedagogy and
discouraging creative problem-­solving and analytical reasoning
(Lee 2010; Park 2008).
At the same time, the prevalence of evening, professional, and
contracted graduate programs demonstrates Korea's success
in institutionalizing practice-­oriented upskilling of incumbent
officials—a model that other countries could adapt while safe-

the recent expansion of interdisciplinary programs—linking PA
with data science or science and technology (S&T) policy—sug-

cross-­domain competencies.
The next stage of Korean PA education, therefore, should move
from preparation to inquiry—transforming its pedagogical
model to cultivate civic responsibility, ethical judgment, and
critical thinking. Universities should design curricula that em-
phasize problem-­based and experiential learning, enabling stu-
dents to engage with real-­world governance challenges and to
reclaim PA's original mission—not merely to prepare test-­takers
or administrators, but to nurture intellectual explorers and re-
flective practitioners who address societal problems, foster civic
leadership, and advance public values.
6.3   |   Government Linkage: From Consultancy to
Coproduction
In Korea, a strong institutional linkage has been established be-
tween academia and government. This close relationship is re-
flected in the growing number and scale of government-­funded
research projects commissioned out to major PA academic asso-
ciations. Much of this collaboration, however, occurs through
noncompetitive contracts and central government commissions,
revealing an insular and highly centralized government-­driven
research structure. The linkage is also evident in the frequent
appointment of PA professors to high-­level government po-
sitions—such as ministers, vice ministers, or senior advis-
ers—which illustrates a distinctive pattern in which academic
expertise directly informs policymaking.
Nevertheless, this strong government–academic connection has
produced mixed results. The linkage is primarily manifested
in the form of consultancy rather than through a broad and
independent scholarly ecosystem. Indeed, many Korean schol-
ars have long criticized this close relationship for creating de-
pendence on government-­commissioned projects and limiting
the field's capacity to lead rather than merely support admin-
istrative practice (Jung 2016; Kang 2008). This overreliance on
government contracts has also shaped how public officials en-
gage with research outputs. For example, Park's (2007) survey
showed that civil servants rarely referred to academic books or
journal articles in their actual work but perceived consulting
and commissioned reports as more useful. This consultancy-­
oriented structure has fostered a proliferation of short-­term,
government-­led studies, which in turn have weakened theoreti-
cal depth and academic independence. In many cases, graduate
students have been mobilized as research labor in these projects,
further blurring the boundary between academic training and
contract work (Kang 2008).
The next stage of government linkage in Korean PA should
move from consultancy to coproduction. According to Buick
et  al.'s  (2016) Typology of Scholar–Practitioner Coproduction
(p. 37), Korean PA currently exhibits high levels of interaction
between scholars and practitioners; yet this interaction remains
primarily shaped by a “commission-­and-­oversight” model, in
which practitioners initiate studies and direct research agendas.
To build a healthier relationship and respond more effectively to
governmental demands, this pattern should evolve into a genu-
ine partnership in which practitioners are engaged at every stage
of the research process—thereby enhancing both the sustainabil-
ity and practical utilization of research outcomes (Armstrong
and Alsop 2010; Buick et al. 2016).
In doing so, as Kang (2008) emphasized, a key prerequisite is
the intellectual honesty and moral integrity of scholars who re-
sist the temptation to overlook inconvenient truths in pursuit of
governmental favor. Government linkage should also be broad-
ened beyond the state to include civil society, the market, and
international organizations, enabling PA in Korea to address not
only short-­term administrative challenges but also longer-­term
societal transformations (Lee 2010).
7   |   Conclusion
The evolution of Korean PA reveals a trajectory that is deeply
connected yet distinct: connected to global paradigms of admin-
istrative science and Western scholarly traditions; distinct in how
it has been reshaped through Korea's own bureaucratic culture,
Confucian legacies, and pragmatic government needs. Across
the dimensions of research, education, and government linkage,
Korean PA demonstrates institutional adaptability and maturity.
These features have facilitated rapid administrative development
and responsiveness to societal demands. At the same time, the
Korean experience offers a broader lesson for global and compar-
ative PA: strong institutional coupling between academia, educa-
tion, and government can effectively build administrative capacity,
but overreliance on Western academic paradigms, government-­
centered education, and commissioned research may constrain
intellectual autonomy and theoretical innovation.
Moving forward, Korean PA should transform these con-
nections into generative distinctiveness. As Bhuiyan and
Perry  (2024) have called for the expansion of the epistemic
map of global PA, Korea should proactively shape the disci-
pline's future through contextualized theorizing, inquisitive
and critical education, and governance grounded in genuine
partnership between scholars and practitioners. At the same
time, PA in Korea must seek a balanced identity—one that
reconciles its multiple roles as a science, a craft, an art, and

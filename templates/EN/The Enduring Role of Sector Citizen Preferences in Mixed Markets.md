Abstract
What role does sector play in citizens’ perceptions of products or services in mixed-market settings? Where governments compete with
for-profit and nonprofit vendors, or when governments partner or contract with private-sector providers, do the public and nonprofit sectors
have an advantage over for-profit providers? Do the public and nonprofit sectors have an advantage over for-profit providers? Using choice-based
conjoint analysis with a nationally representative paid consumer panel, we examine the relevance of sector to consumers and compare it other
signals of quality; namely, price, third-party certifications, and consumer ratings. Of these, subjects are most sensitive to information from con­
sumer ratings. Regarding sector, we find that subjects generally prefer nonprofit to government providers and government to for-profit providers.
Sector is most relevant to consumers in low-information environments when the quality of a product or service is otherwise unclear. We ex­
tend theory by proposing four possible mechanisms for the differential value of sector to consumers (process quality, product quality, expertise
quality, and moral quality).
Scholars have long sought to define the role of the public
sector and the origin of its authority (e.g., Bozeman 1988;
Rosenbloom 2016), as well as to distinguish the practice
of public management from that of private management
(Allison 1980; Baldwin 1987; Rainey and Bozeman 2000).
Whether the focus is on the distinctiveness of the public sec­
tor (Bozeman 1988; Rainey 2009, 2020; Rainey et al. 1976),
the unique values of the public sector (Bozeman 2002), or the
characteristics of publicness that may inhabit organizations
(Bozeman and Bretschneider 1994; Moulton and Eckerd
2012), the differentiation between government activity and
private sector activity has been an important topic of research
and theory in the field.
However, contributions to theory have mostly focused on
the perspectives of administrators (e.g., Perry and Rainey
1988) rather than the experience of citizens—to whom public
administrators are ultimately accountable (Farwell et al. 2019;
Pegnato 1997; Smith and Huntsman 1997; Thomas 2013; van
de Walle 2018; Van Slyke and Roch 2004). Although the legal
theory that traditionally forms the basis of our understanding
of the sectors provides valuable insight (e.g., Bozeman 1988;
Rosenbloom 2016), consumer perceptions of sector also have
important implications for public management. In this art­
icle, we therefore ask whether sector is salient to citizens in
their role as consumers of services in mixed markets where
governments compete with private for-profit and nonprofit
providers.
Our analyses show that citizens prefer nonprofit providers
over government providers and government providers over
for-profit providers for mixed market goods. Sector becomes
particularly salient in low information environments when
little additional information is available about the quality of
a good or service. Sector is not the only characteristic that
could potentially appeal to consumers, and other signals are
increasingly common in an age when information is imme­
diately available to consumers via the internet. We examine
third-party certifications, consumer ratings, and price along­
side sector. We find that the most direct measure of product
quality—consumer ratings—is especially important to con­
sumers. Even in the presence of consumer ratings, however,
sector and other indirect indicators of quality also affect con­
sumer preferences.
The Role of Public and Nonprofit
Organizations
Government and nonprofit organizations have been theorized
to exist in part to remedy information problems associated
with nonprivate goods (Olson 1965). There are two situations
in which information problems are manifest. The first informa­
tion problem results when the good is nonexhaustible, meaning
that use of the good by one person does not diminish the quality
or quantity of the good. This is a characteristic shared by both
public goods and club goods, and the benefit of such goods to
individual consumers is often difficult to quantify (Olson 1965;
Ostrom 2009; Witesman 2016). Governments address the dis­
tributional problems associated with these goods by requiring
citizens to pay for them through taxes or by using redistribu­
tive policy—including granting nonprofit tax benefits—to en­
sure the availability of certain goods in the marketplace.
The second information problem occurs when the payer
(donor or taxpayer) is not the same person consuming a good
(client or consumer). In such cases, there is no direct quality
information from consumption that is transmitted to the

moral hazard in which a provider could continue to extract
payment from donors and/or taxpayers without providing
sufficient quality to consumers. A profit motive would in­
centivize this type of behavior, and for this reason, nonprofit
organizations are prohibited from distributing profits to
shareholders. Hansmann (1980, 1981) theorized that this
prohibition increases the likelihood of organizational invest­
ments in quality rather than profits. Traditionally, govern­
ments have operated under similar constraints and for similar
reasons (Rainey 2009), and both government and nonprofits
have been widely viewed as solutions to information asym­
metries in the marketplace (Nelson and Krashinsky 1973;
Smith and Lipsky 1993; Weisbrod 1988).
Government and nonprofit organizational forms—both ab­
sent the profit motive—are thus believed by scholars to en­
courage organizations to invest in the quality of the products
and services they provide. Our question is whether this theory
translates to direct consumer preferences for sector at a time
when third-party certifications, user ratings, and price sig­
nals may sufficiently bridge the informational divide between
payers and consumers.
In addition to economic views of sector differences, there
are more normative theories about the public benefit of gov­
ernment and nonprofit organizations. Bozeman (2002) sug­
gests that the private sector’s focus on economic efficiency
and profit maximization can create social value problems
even in the absence of formal market failures (Bozeman
2002). In this view, government and nonprofit organiza­
tions have a responsibility to embody public values that
are not typically associated with the norms of the private
sector (Jørgensen and Bozeman 2007). For example, they
have been theorized to provide products and services pre­
ferred by minority constituencies even as market mechan­
isms favor majority consumer preferences, creating greater
equity in the mixed marketplace (McGuire and Olson 1996;
Young 2000).
Public values are prominent in describing the importance
of the public and nonprofit sectors, which are described
as having different ethos (Rayner et al. 2011), motivations
(Perry and Wise 1990; Wright 2001), values (Jorgensen and
Bozeman 2007, Witesman and Walters 2014) and concep­
tions of value (Meynhardt 2009). Whether these normative
views of the sector extend to sector advantage from the per­
spective of citizen consumers remains an open question.
The Distinctiveness of Public and Private
Organizations
The answer to whether the sector of the provider is associ­
ated with consumer preferences is complicated by evolving
notions of the distinctiveness of organizations across the sec­
tors. Distinctions between the public and private sector are
at the heart of public administration theory (Bozeman and
Bretschneider 1994; Rainey 2009), and scholars have de­
voted considerable effort to understanding how organizations
across the sectors are similar to and different from each other
(Bozeman and Moulton 2011; Bromley and Meyer 2017;
Eikenberry and Kluver 2004; Moulton and Eckerd 2012).
Much of public and nonprofit sector theory was written at
a time when the specific products and services provided by
public and private entities were largely distinct (Rainey et al.
1976). However, significant shifts have occurred in the last
half century that have brought into question the continued
relevance of assertions about sector differences.
First, the privatization movement (Osborne and Gaebler
1992; Box 1999; Kettl 2002; McLaughlin et al. 2002) in­
volved private sector actors in government provision in un­
precedented ways, distributing the provision of traditionally
public services across the public, private, and nonprofit sec­
tors (Brown and Potoski 2003; Brudney et al. 2005; Hefetz
and Warner 2004; Van Slyke 2003). Although many public
administration scholars decried the “hollowing” of gov­
ernment (Milward and Provan 2000), the undermining of
public values (Bozeman 2002), or the weakening of the so­
cial contract (Blanchard et al. 1998), in the wake of the New
Public Management movement, contracting, organizational
hybridity, and cross-sector partnerships and collaborations
have become part of the new normal for public governance
(Salamon 2002).
A second shift is the rise of the mixed-market economy. For-
profit entities have entered the marketplace for prosocial goods,
competing directly with government and nonprofit providers
by offering similar goods and services in a competitive environ­
ment (Marvel 2015; Kopel et al. 2014; Marwell and McInerney
2005; Brown and Slivinski 2018; Ben-Ner 2002). The result of
both developments is that public administration scholars must
now take seriously a reality in which public administration is
multisectoral and the demands of citizen-customers are para­
mount but paradoxical (Blanchard et al. 1998; Fountain 2001;
Pegnato 1997; Smith and Huntsman 1997; Thomas 2013).
These shifts have resulted in a blurring of the sectors that
has brought into question whether sector distinctions are
practically meaningful in an increasingly isomorphic organ­
izational landscape (Dees and Anderson 2003, Bromley and
Meyer 2017). The question of a dimensional “publicness”
that can cross sector boundaries has also been theorized
(Bozeman and Bretschneider 1994; Bozeman and Moulton
2011). Empirical research seeking to objectively observe
sector distinctions in terms of organizational performance
has found relatively thin evidence of objective performance
differences between public, nonprofit, and for-profit pro­
viders (e.g., Heinrich 2000; Cabin et al. 2014; Witesman
and Fernandez 2013; Ben-Ner et al. 2018; Morris and
Helburn 2000), though this literature is largely limited
to those spaces (such as child care, health care, and job
training programs) where mixed markets are robust (Schiff
and Weisbrod 1991).
There is varied evidence on how consumers view sector dis­
tinctions. In mixed-market settings, some research suggests
that consumers may be unaware of the organizational form of
the service provider or of the functional differences between
legal forms (Schlesinger et al. 2004; Handy et al. 2010). Yet,
sector seems to have some meaning to consumers (Aaker et al
2010; Hvidman 2019; Marvel 2015; Van den Bekerom et al.
2021) as well as some practitioners (Child et al. 2015, 2016).
Research also indicates that when consumers believe that they
are receiving services from nonprofit organizations, their as­
sessment of the quality of these services increases (van Slyke
and Roch 2004; Child and Witesman 2019), even though
many consumers know little about what nonprofit status ac­
tually means (Handy et al. 2010; Aaker et al. 2010). Indeed,
prior scholars have shown that citizen evaluation of public
organizations can suffer from sector-linked biases (Marvel
2015, 2016; Schlesinger et al. 2004; van den Bekerom et al.
2021; Meier et al. 2019).

Why a Citizen Perspective on Sector Matters
The citizen perspective is important for the day-to-day prac­
tice of public management. Empirical research suggests that
a customer service orientation has a positive impact on
the performance and work attitudes of public employees
(Paarlberg 2007). By extension, the ability to better antici­
pate the needs of citizens is an important aspect of public
management. Sector-based preferences are thus highly rele­
vant at a time when nonprofit, for-profit, and public entities
frequently compete, contract, and collaborate in mixed-
markets where citizens have multiple options and therefore
influence the consumption of a good (and the success of a
provider) through their choices (Bel et al. 2014; Boris and
Steuerle 2006; Considine 2003; Handy et al. 2010; Marwell
and McInerney 2005; Weisbrod 1997). Because collabor­
ation, contracting, and other consumer-driven market prac­
tices have become common, it is now incumbent on public
managers to acknowledge the citizen’s perspective when
considering or entering into relationships with third-party
entities, such as businesses and nonprofits. Consumer-
conscious administrators will do so with an eye toward how
those decisions will be perceived by citizens. When citizens
are also customers (Blanchard et al. 1998; Thomas, 2013),
the success or failure of a publicly supported service could
very much hinge on how citizens perceive it, not only its ob­
jective merits (Van Slyke and Roch 2004).
Consumer choice is an important dynamic in the structure
and function of markets, and patterns of consumer psych­
ology affect not only how consumers perceive and participate
in markets, but also how producers position their products
and services (Thaler 1980). Although these issues of con­
sumer psychology, perception, and market interaction are
perhaps most robustly considered and applied in the private
sector, they are also relevant to the public and nonprofit sec­
tors, where unique marketing approaches may be rooted in
sector differences as much as in firm or product-level differ­
ences (Fine 2017, Wright et al. 2012).
There is evidence that government administrators and
their vendor partners are interested in how the public per­
ceives their services, and they spend time monitoring col­
laborative arrangements (Koliba et al. 2011; Silvia, 2018a)
and collecting feedback (Brown and Potoski 2004; Brown
et al. 2006; DeHoog and Salamon 2002; Kettl 2002). But
very little of the public management scholarship examines
the value to public managers of studying public opinion
broadly (Van Slyke et al. 2005), let alone specifically how
the public regards the choice of vendors, even though the
principle of citizen preference and empowerment is cen­
tral to the privatization hypothesis (Osborne and Gaebler
1992). More often, the insights we do have about seeking
collaborative or contracted partners comes from the per­
spectives of public managers and/or vendors (Graddy and
Chen 2009; Silvia 2018b; Van Slyke 2003; Witesman and
Fernandez 2013), where governments are deemed to favor
nonprofits over for-profit contracting partners (Prentice
and Brudney 2015; Van Slyke 2009).
Sector in the Context of Other Signals
Many of the debates about the relevance of sector predate
public use of the internet, which has made other information
as widely available as incorporation status—and could be
used to judge, a priori, the likely quality of goods and services.
In our study, we examine perspectives regarding the vendor’s
sector alongside additional signals that citizens could poten­
tially receive before being in a position to make a first-hand
judgment of quality. These include third-party certifications,
consumer ratings, and price. Sector, like third-party certifica­
tions and price, is an indirect signal of product quality in that
it does not specifically provide information about the con­
sumer experience with a good or service. On the other hand,
consumer ratings provide a more direct quality signal.
Organizational Sector
We have already cited a useful literature on sector distinctive­
ness broadly. Here we note the scholarship that examines why
preferences for particular sectors might exist. Some research
suggests that nonprofits are seen as more trustworthy than
for-profits because the law prohibits them from enriching
shareholders (Hansmann 1980; Nelson and Krashinsky
1973; Prentice and Brudney 2015). Whether it is actually
the case that nonprofits are more trustworthy is debatable
(compare Permut 1981 with Farwell et al. 2019, Handy et al.
2010, and Schlesinger et al. 2004), but the idea has remained
a pervasive one (see, e.g., Frumkin 2005). Other scholarship
investigates perceptions of government, sometimes in com­
parison to private companies and/or nonprofits (Edelman
2021; Hvidman 2019; Hvidman and Andersen 2016; Marvel
2015; O’Neill 2009; Poister and Henry 1994). Some scholars
find that citizens view government service provision as ineffi­
cient, inflexible, wasteful, and inferior to what is provided by
private sector firms (Marvel 2016), but others have refuted
these assertions (van den Bekerom et al. 2021). It is clear,
however, that sector is a consideration in contracting deci­
sions and public-private partnership (Weißmüller and Vogel
2021) and affects public perceptions of public services (Berg
and Johansson 2020).
Third-Party Certifications
One type of information frequently made available to con­
sumers—and one that could render organizational status less
salient (Needleman 2001)—comes in the form of labels asso­
ciated with field-level certifications. As part of a broad class
of new regulatory forms that decenter the authority of state
agencies and rely instead on standards and market competi­
tion to motivate desirable behavior (Schneiberg and Bartley
2008, Brunjes 2020), certification and labeling initiatives have
become an established way of communicating to consumers
that products meet a level of quality that the consumer ex­
pects (Becker 2018).
The purpose of certification and labeling, as with similar
initiatives, is to make key pieces of information transparent
to consumers, who are then empowered to make informed
decisions based on their preferences. The information is
often transmitted through the placement of a label or seal
on product packaging and promotional material, which is
typically ensured by third-party agents. Increasingly, such
third-party certifications include reviews of empirical out­
come evaluation data with specific evidentiary standards
(e.g., Blueprints certification, Pew-MacArthur Results First
Initiative, and Bridgespan’s What Works Marketplace). Field-
specific certifications, such those found in the fair trade, for­
estry, electronics, and other industries, are now commonplace
(Raynolds 2009; Stork and Woodilla 2008).

Whether certifications are direct or indirect quality sig­
nals depends to a large extent on the certification standards
themselves. Certifications vary widely on the rigor of their
standards and how vigorously participants are monitored.
Certification and labeling systems can be ineffective (Willems
et al. 2017) or break down (Child 2015). Although some may
explicitly suggest a level of product quality, others focus more
on ethical practices and procedural concerns. Competing cer­
tifications and labels could serve to confuse or desensitize
consumers, thus reducing their effectiveness. It is therefore
not clear whether certifications and labels would effectively
replace the signal that organizational sector provides.
User Ratings
In “reputation star societies” (Willems et al. 2019, Xu 2020),
user ratings have become an important mechanism for com­
municating information about products and the organizations
that make and sell them. If potential consumers understand
how former consumers regard the quality of services, then
user ratings could reduce the importance that citizens place
on organizational sector. Although others’ ratings of product
quality may not directly reflect the utility a new consumer
would receive from a product, these signals are nonetheless
more direct indicators of consumer experience than are sector,
third-party certifications, and price.
User ratings have been democratized such that consumers,
not just experts, contribute to evaluation processes (see
Jøsang et al. 2007 for a review). User rating systems are some­
times referred to as an element of Web 2.0 (Madden and Fox
2006)—a moniker that draws attention to an updated version
of internet experience in which data are shared in collabora­
tive and participatory ways. In a matter of a few keystrokes,
customers can broadcast their purchasing and consumption
experiences to anyone with an internet connection, enabling
potential consumers to learn how actual consumers rate quite
literally any good available (or its provider), from bookstores
and libraries to homeless shelters and hospitals.
Although user ratings have become ubiquitous in the age
of the internet (De Langhe et al. 2015; Smith and Anderson
2016), it is possible that consumers discount them. They may
not, therefore, have a clear effect on consumer preferences.
Pew polling shows that even though most Americans at least
sometimes check online reviews before making a purchase,
they are split on whether they find reviews trustworthy (Smith
and Anderson 2016). Maybe this is for good reason, since
user ratings tend not to provide accurate information about
the objective quality of products for sale (De Langhe et al.
2015). It is an open question, then, how consumers value user
ratings in comparison to other potential signifiers of quality,
such as sector.
Price Signals
Finally, the price of a good is a ubiquitous and important po­
tential signal of either quality (associated with higher prices)
or value (associated with lower prices) (Hirshleifer 2015;
Oh 2000). Market signals may not actually reflect consumer
preferences, but rather be used to direct them (Porter 2008).
Even so, in a mixed-market setting with other available in­
formation, we would expect price signals to be relevant to
consumers. All other quality signals being equal, consumers
would rationally be expected to select the less expensive good
in order to enhance their sense of value. Although pricing
is not the focus of our study, it is an important control and
reference point regarding citizen perceptions of quality and
indicates, to some extent, a willingness to pay for the other
quality signals introduced in the study.
Methods
Data for this study come from a survey in which subjects were
asked to select among similar products that varied according
to different characteristics, such as whether they were pro­
duced by a government, nonprofit, or for-profit, and whether
they were certified by third parties or had received positive
user ratings. We use choice-based conjoint (CBC) analysis
as our empirical approach to understand their preferences.
Furthermore, we rely on Sawtooth Software’s discrete choice
modeling software to estimate the importance of attributes
and the utility of attribute levels using hierarchical Bayes
methods. In a CBC study, the analyst presents subjects with
alternatives possessing different combinations of characteris­
tics, and subjects decide which combination of characteristics
they prefer most given the options presented. Each subject
repeats the task of selecting their preferred alternative sev­
eral times, with the combinations of possible characteristics
varying systematically. These data are then used to discern
patterns in consumer preferences. See online supplementary
appendix A.
Procedure
As is typical in a CBC study, we first presented the subjects
with a glossary of the key terms that they would see in the
survey. See online supplementary appendix B. We then pre­
sented subjects with a scenario and the description of four
products with different levels for each attribute, whose pro­
files we listed next to each other on one survey page for easy
comparison. See online supplementary appendix C. In this
study, the attributes for each product included the sector of
the organization providing the product, the cost, the cus­
tomer rating, and the whether it was certified. Levels are the
values for each attribute. For the sector attribute, for ex­
ample, the levels were government, nonprofit, for-profit, and
unknown. We asked the subject to select the most desirable
option of the four profiles. After examining the choices, a
subject would identify which product they would purchase,
and then the exercise would repeat 11 more times using the
same scenario but with products with different levels for
each attribute. The subject’s role in all of the scenarios is
that of a paying (i.e., “purchasing”) consumer, not a volun­
teer or donor.

Similar to approaches used in general marketing studies (e.g.,
Cai and Aguilar 2013), we focus here on perceptions of or­
dinary people prior to direct experience with the vendor in
question. We assigned subjects randomly to one of two
scenarios, purchasing a health test or purchasing museum
admissions tickets,1 with 274 and 255 subjects assigned to

Measuring Quality Signal Attributes
Along with the organizational type, we varied the product
profiles based on three other attributes: the certifica­
tion status of the organization providing the product, the
product’s customer rating, and its cost (see table 1). We in­
dicated the organization’s certification status with a logo or
seal. The organizations either had or did not have a cer­
tification listed. The logo, signifying a hypothetical certifi­
cation body, verifies that the providing organization meets
high standards. We indicated the customer rating with stars
ranging from one to five, with five stars for the highest
rating. Subjects could also receive a “No ratings informa­
tion available” description, in which case the profile did not
display any stars. Finally, the cost of the good or service in
all scenarios was between $10 and $15 and varied in incre­
ments of $1.
Survey Instrument
Besides the conjoint questions, the survey included a study
information page, four test questions about the sharing
economy (intended to obfuscate the specific purpose of the
study and reduce desirability bias and any novelty and dis­
ruption effects [Shadish et al. 2002]), two attention questions,
and a short battery of demographic questions (age, gender,
race, marital status, education, employment status, income,
political leanings, and work history).
Subjects
A total of 529 paid subjects participated. All were part of
a nationally representative panel managed by Centiment, a
survey panel company, which sets proprietary compensation
rates. Centiment incorporates security and fraud algorithms
to ensure that only unique panelists participate in the survey.
They also employ verification processes to confirm the loca­
tion of respondents and the accuracy of their user profile data,
and they seek to balance panels across key demographics.
See online supplementary appendix E. Use of a survey panel
company to manage subject selection allows us to draw con­
clusions based on a sample similar to that to which a typ­
ical company would consider as a representative consumer
sample.
Analytic Strategy
We used Sawtooth’s proprietary, built-in Analysis and
Simulator tools to conduct initial analyses. Supplementary
analyses and graphics were generated using Excel and R.
Analysis proceeded in the following way.
First, we estimated the part-worth utilities, or the influence
of individual attribute levels on the decision. This allows us to
state, for example, the extent to which nonprofit status mat­
ters compared with government or for-profit status. For re­
porting purposes, the reference level for utility is the average
across all levels of the part-worths. The part-worths are zero-
centered for each attribute such that the average part-worth
score for each attribute is set to 0, resulting in some part-
worths that are positive and some that are negative. Although
negative part-worths are less desirable than positive ones, a
negative part-worth does not mean that the level is undesir­
able or unacceptable. Part-worth scores serve as the basis for
the analysis of conjoint analysis results.
Second, we use the part-worth utilities to estimate attri­
bute importance scores. Whereas the part-worth estimates
indicate, for example, the added utility provided by a two-
star rating over a one-star rating, the attribute importance
scores provide information regarding the relative importance
of each attribute overall—organizational sector, certification
status, ratings, and cost—on the subjects’ decisions. Attribute
importance scores are estimated by calculating part-worth
utilities for each attribute level for each subject. The range in
part-worth utilities for each attribute is then determined (the
highest part-worth utility at a certain level of the given attri­
bute minus the lowest part-worth utility at a certain level of
the given attribute) before computing each attribute’s utility
range as a percentage of the combined possible utility, which
are then averaged across the dataset (Orme 2014). The re­
sulting percentage is an attribute’s importance. Attribute im­
portance scores, therefore, can be interpreted as the average
percent of the total variation in utility attributable to the par­
ticular attribute.

Third, we use the part-worth utilities to estimate the prob­
ability that a subject will make a purchasing decision at
specified attribute levels. Because part-worth scores are zero-
centered by attribute, part-worth scores for one attribute
cannot be compared with the scores of the other attributes
because of the difference in scaling. Therefore, we transform
part-worth utilities into preference shares using a market
simulator. Market simulator results are easier to interpret be­
cause they are on a zero to one hundred scale, and have ratio
scale properties (Orme 2014). We present these results graph­
ically, with each plotted point representing the probability
that subjects would purchase the product at the indicated
levels of the attributes.
Finally, we regressed the cost levels on the average utilities
for each cost level to calculate the willingness to pay for each
attribute level in order to create a linear interpolation of a
continuous covariate.
Results
Part-Worth Utilities and Preference Shares
In both scenarios, subjects found nonprofit organizations
more attractive than governments, and governments more at­
tractive than either for-profits or organizations whose sector
is unknown. This can be seen by comparing (within each
scenario) the average part-worth utility estimates for each or­
ganization type in table 2, where the average part-worth is
more positive for nonprofits than they are for governments,
for-profits, or organizations with an unknown organizational
sector. In the health test scenario, for instance, the average
part-worth utility associated with nonprofit is 0.66, compared

Attribute Importance
Whereas the part-worth utility estimates allow us to evaluate
how subjects valued the different levels within each attribute
(comparing, for example, preferences for a health test pro­
duced by a nonprofit organization rather than a government
one), attribute importance estimates indicate how important
the attribute is in comparison to other attributes (e.g., the ex­
tent to which organizational sector matters compared with
price). Table 3 shows the average attribute importance esti­
mates for each of the four attributes in each scenario. As seen
by the high values in each scenario, customer ratings stand
out as the most important attribute. They alone account for
roughly half of the estimated variation in utility in each scen­
ario (from 49.86% in the museum ticket scenario to 55.16%
in the health test scenario).
Sector, on the other hand, accounts for between 13.72%
(health test scenario) and 18.47% (museum ticket scenario)
of the variation in utility. These results suggest that although
organizational sector matters to some degree, other attributes
are equally or more important. Even in the health test scen­
ario, where the stakes are high for consumers (who have a
vested interest in ensuring that they receive reliable informa­
tion), subjects apparently prefer customer ratings and certifi­
cations to organizational sector when deciding which test to
purchase.

Willingness to Pay
Finally, we analyzed the marginal willingness to pay (MWTP),
which is the amount of money that an individual would be
willing to pay for a particular feature of the service being
offered (see table 4). A negative MWTP value indicates that
the provider would need to reduce the price of the service in
order to compensate for the perceived inferiority of that fea­
ture. A positive MWTP, on the other hand, is the estimated
premium that respondents would pay for a service with that
feature.
The results of the willingness to pay analysis show that
MWTP for one- and two-star products were negative,
meaning that providers would need to lower their prices for
these services when compared with services whose ratings
were unknown in order to compensate for the poor ratings.

Discussion and Implications
Based on the results reported above, we find that sector
boundaries apparently matter to consumers, especially
when other quality signals are absent. Nonprofit organ­
izations in particular are preferred by customers, even
when other information is available. Our data are con­
sistent with the expectation that consumers believe they
will receive at least some added utility from purchasing
services from nonprofit organizations in comparison to
other types of organizations, and that citizens still value
government—even in the face of privatization and pos­
sible antigovernment bias.
There are longstanding cultural tropes that may affect con­
sumer perceptions of quality—some based in evidence, and
some not—that could be driving consumers towards one
sector over others. Widespread perceptions exist, for example,
that nonprofit organizations operate for the common good
(Aaker et al. 2010) and that businesses are efficient in ways
that public agencies are not (Hvidman 2019; Hvidman and
Anderson 2016). Some consumers may have a pro-nonprofit
bias or an anti-public sector bias that operates beyond a ra­
tional calculus about information asymmetries and trust­
worthiness (see Marvel 2015, 2016; but see also Child and
Witesman 2019 and Hodgkinson et al. 2017). We did not find
evidence of a pro-business sentiment since private service pro­
vision was never preferred over nonprofit or government pro­
vision. Nor did we find evidence for anti-government bias in
the context of our study, at least in comparison with private
for-profits.
The museum scenario underscores the importance of con­
text in understanding sector perceptions. In that scenario,
nonprofits have a significant advantage over organizations in
other sectors, even when no certifications are available and
users give it one-star only. This suggests that consumers fa­
vored nonprofits not only in the absence of information, but
also when there was evidence that quality was actually low
(based on the one-star rating). A pro-nonprofit bias, if unwar­
ranted, may not serve beneficiaries or contracting organiza­
tions well, and public administrators should prioritize direct
and objective quality signals over potentially biased percep­
tions based on sector. The health scenario, on the other hand,
does not show the same pattern, suggesting that sector prefer­
ences may be particularly sensitive to contextual factors.
Four Possible Mechanisms of Sector Preference
Public and nonprofit theory suggests that the absence of a
profit motive should enhance public trust in government and
nonprofit institutions when direct quality signals are obscure.
Although our study does not directly address the mechanisms
by which subjects determine preference for government and
nonprofit providers over for-profit forms, we believe that the
empirical evidence from our study suggests (1) that consumers
prefer direct quality signals (such as consumer ratings) to in­
direct quality signals, (2) that consumers consider sector in
consumption decisions in a manner similar to other indirect
quality signals such as price and third-party certification, and
(3) that in the absence of other information, sector becomes
a particularly important factor for making consumption deci­
sions in a mixed market.
Although the absence of a profit motive explains the differ­
ence between public and nonprofit institutions and for-profit
ones, it does not explain the reason why nonprofits are pre­
ferred to government providers. This suggests that the absence
of profit motive is an insufficient explanation for sector-based
consumer preferences and that a deeper examination of the
specific relationship between sector and perceptions of quality
should be further examined.
We posit four interrelated possibilities for consumer pref­
erence for public and nonprofit providers that are derived
from contemporary public administration literatures. Each is
rooted in quality signals that status as a public or nonprofit
organization may provide.
First, when there is an inability to observe the process
of creating a product and/or experience using the product
prior to purchase, consumers may prefer process quality sig­
nals (Ben-Ner and Ren 2015; Hansmann 1980). Public or
nonprofit status could provide a process-related quality signal
because government processes include public-oriented norms
and public oversight through notice and comment procedures

Second, when determination of the quality of a product
is highly subjective, we might expect public or nonprofit
status to provide a product quality signal because a com­
mitment to the public interest would suggest a motive to in­
vest in the quality of products or services even when there
is no marginal profit benefit to doing so (Hansmann 1980;
Olson 1965; Bozeman 2002). Empirical studies on sector-
based performance outcomes demonstrate mixed results (e.g.,
Witesman and Fernandez 2013), but this belief may yet shape
the perceptions of consumers (see Van de Walle 2018; Bodem-
Schroetgens and Becker 2020).
Third, consumers may expect government or nonprofit
organizations to provide higher expertise quality resulting
from historical production or provision of a type of good or
service in a particular sector. Fine arts, human services, and
educational programs, for example, have a large nonprofit
presence and a long history in that sector. Consumers may
associate greater quality with nonprofit programs in these
areas because they perceive a higher level of expertise in
that area from the sector generally, or are more likely to
be aware of organizations providing similar services in
the nonprofit sector. We suspect that a pro-nonprofit bias
could be especially prevalent in contexts where nonprofits
are doing the kind of work that consumers expect of them
(such as organizing a museum rather than selling a health
test). The bias could be salient if the nonprofit organiza­
tion is located in the “right” category or if other types of
organizations are seen as illegitimate (Zuckerman 1999).
This could help explain the modest differences between the
results in our scenarios.
Another perspective suggests that consumers may also ex­
pect sector-based expertise quality because public or nonprofit
experts are motivated, at least to some extent, by mission
alignment and prefer to avoid situations where their expertise
would be captured for private gain rather than public benefit
(Bullock et al. 2015; Wiig 2002; Wright 2001).
Fourth, in cases where consumers seek to signal their per­
sonal values by supporting specific types of organizations
that align with their preferences, government or nonprofit
status may signal increased moral quality because, as
Hansmann argues, they are symbolically “invested with an
ethical quality that places them beyond mere utilitarian con­
cerns” (1980, 898; see also Child et al. 2015; Bozeman 2002;
Horton 2006; Jørgensen and Bozeman 2007). For instance,
consumers might prefer a nonprofit organization because
they believe that it is morally superior to a for-profit com­
petitor due to its prosocial mission even if the products are of
similar quality. In such cases, the value signaling that accom­
panies support of public, private, or nonprofit organizational
forms may be important even in the presence of direct quality
information about the product or service (Child et al. 2015;
Seemann et al. 2014).
Implications for Intersector Practice
When competing in a mixed-market space, our findings of a
persistent consumer preference hierarchy suggests that sector
signaling may provide an advantage to government entities
over for-profit entities in certain contexts. Our findings also
suggest, however, that nonprofit provision might be preferred
by citizens over government solutions. Although citizen pref­
erences are only one part of making government provision
decisions, accountability to citizens (Farwell et al. 2019;
Pegnato 1997; Smith and Huntsman 1997; Thomas 2013;
van de Walle 2018; Van Slyke and Roch 2004) suggests that
governments operating in mixed markets should be clear
about their rationale for competing with private sector pro­
viders since mixed markets challenge the notion of private
market failure (Schiflett and Zey 1990, Schiff and Weisbrod
1991, Kopel et al. 2014). Rationales offered by public ad­
ministration research include addressing distributional needs
for particular types of goods (Witesman 2016, Olson 1965;
Weimer and Vining, 2017) or addressing public value failure
(Bozeman 2002).
Our findings suggest that citizens prefer government
provision over for-profit providers. This would extend to
contracting with for-profit firms, despite pressures toward
privatization (Hefetz and Warner 2004). Given prior re­
search and theory that suggests certain advantages for gov­
ernment provision over private provision (Milward and
Provan 2000; Bozeman 2002; Rainey 2009), our findings
on citizen preferences further suggest an important role for
direct government provision of public services rather than
provision by private for-profit contractors. However, our
findings also suggest that consumers may prefer nonprofit
organizations to government ones, suggesting that in cases
when contracting is appropriate (see Brown and Potoski
2005; Brudney et al. 2005; McGuire and Silvia 2010; Van
Slyke 2007; Witesman and Fernandez 2013), sector might
be a valid consideration in identifying qualified contrac­
tors. Contracting governments may want to select nonprofit
providers over for-profit providers not only because of
goal alignment (Van Slyke 2007; Witesman and Fernandez
2013), but also because citizen preferences may lean in favor
of nonprofit service provision.
Contract or collaboration decisions often occur before a
product or service is provided. In these cases, ratings and cer­
tification may not yet be available or applied to the products
and services in question. The quality of some public services
is also difficult to know in any objective sense (Hansmann,
1980; Weimer and Vining, 2017), and consumers in our study
demonstrated reliance on sector in the absence of other in­
formation. A potential contracting partner’s sector may be
the only characteristic known by the public manager or con­
sumers. In such circumstances, partnering with a nonprofit or
government may be advisable.
Though organizational form is important at the margins,
our findings show that organizational sector pales in com­
parison to consumer ratings. Thus, although sector persist­
ently matters to consumers, its effect is not a substitute for
direct indications of product quality and should not be the
primary consideration in contracting decisions. Consumer
ratings are simply a more direct proxy for consumer percep­
tions of quality, and consumers appear to care more about
the cost and quality of their services than they do the spe­
cific provider. In response to citizen preferences, government
should pay the greatest attention to cost and quality.
Of all the variables we studied that public administrators
can manipulate, price might be the easiest. Our findings sug­
gest, however, that it is among the least important of the at­
tributes. To be sure, this could be because our prices were

Limitations and Future Directions
We acknowledge that surveys are artificial representations of
reality and, furthermore, that we cannot make claims based
on these data regarding whether citizens would actually
know whether the organizations from which they purchase
goods and services are nonprofit, for-profit, or government
entities (or hybrids). We necessarily provided that informa­
tion in order to measure preferences for each (but see Handy
et al. 2010; Permut 1981). Also, in order to reduce cognitive
load, we limited the number of possible attributes on which
we varied respondents’ selections. Others could have been
considered.
Future work could examine the assumption that customer
ratings are verifiable, equitable, and an accurate source of
information about citizen preferences. To that end, scholar­
ship is needed to understand how governments can confirm
that ratings are authentic (Zhu et al. 2020) prior to making
service delivery decisions based upon them. Additionally,
studies could be conducted to find ways to verify that cus­
tomer ratings, and the comments that often accompany
them, are accurate representations of citizens’ true prefer­
ences. Future studies might also examine the extent to which
citizens agree or disagree with public management scholars
regarding when and if government services should be privat­
ized or offered in mixed market settings. Variations across
national contexts are sure to exist and should be explored.
Consumer expectations, objective measures of product
quality, and purchaser proximity to the producer could also
be manipulated. Finally, although we purposefully selected
scenarios that represented two broad mixed-market sub­
sectors, future studies could examine additional scenarios
and attributes to understand how they shape consumer pref­
erences. Other direct measures of quality could be studied
in the future. For example, outcome evaluation data shows
promise as a direct quality metric for effectiveness of public
services (Meier et al. 2019).

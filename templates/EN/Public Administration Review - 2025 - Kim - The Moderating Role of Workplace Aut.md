ABSTRACT
Corruption research highlights the importance of organizational and individual correlates, such as corruption control strate-
gies or public employee attitudes. In this study, we integrate these research streams by examining whether the effectiveness of
two common organizational approaches to controlling corruption—value-­oriented strategies, which emphasize ethical decision-­
making, and compliance-­oriented strategies, which rely on monitoring, audits, and punishment—depends on perceptions of
workplace autonomy. To explore this, we use novel administrative data on corruption, along with codes of ethics and survey
data from 1235 career public servants across 33 South Korean ministries. Findings suggest greater perceived autonomy is asso-
ciated with greater corruption tolerance in organizational contexts where compliance-­oriented corruption control strategies are
prevalent. Conversely, greater perceived autonomy is associated with lesser corruption tolerance when value-­oriented corruption
control strategies are prevalent. These findings contribute to administrative corruption research by illustrating how individual
perceptions of their workplace shape the efficacy of organizational corruption control strategies.
1   |   Introduction
Corruption presents formidable challenges to public organiza-
tions globally, especially in light of its adverse implications for
good governance outcomes (Mungiu-­Pippidi 2023). For example,
studies have shown that corruption negatively affects outcomes
such as trust in government (Morris and Klesner 2010), fiscal
performance (Connolly 2007), and the efficiency of administra-
tive processes (Shleifer and Vishny 1993). Despite the significant
challenges administrative corruption poses for public bureau-
cracies, the complex nature of such behavior frequently hinders
efforts to curb it (Bandura 1999; Darley 2005).
Conventionally, studies that attempt to explain why public
employees engage in acts of corruption have focused on orga-
nizational factors such as the use of technology (Kalesnikaite
et al. 2023), culture (Campbell and Göritz 2014), or punishments
(Lindstedt and Naurin  2010). More recently, scholars have
started to investigate individual-­level determinants of corrup-
tion (for an overview, see Weißmüller and Zuber  2023). This
line of research offers novel insight into how factors such as
public employees' perception of their work environment (Zhang
et al. 2019), values (Malagueño et al. 2019) or public service mo-
tivation (Gans-­Morse et al. 2022) are associated with corruption.
These findings on individual attitudes and perceptions are im-
portant because they offer a more comprehensive understanding
of corruption by complementing the longstanding emphasis on
organizational determinants.
While research on individual and organizational level cor-
relates of corruption helps explain why corruption occurs, an
important limitation of this body of research is that it tends to

Addressing this issue is important not only because it offers
insight into factors that shape the performance of widely used
anti-­corruption tools, but also because it touches on a more
fundamental tension in public administration, popularized
by the Friedrich-­Finer debate, between the importance of ef-
forts to foster bureaucratic expertise and discretion on the
one hand and the need for robust external democratic control
mechanisms on the other. This tension is echoed by Afonso and
Nelson  (2025) who demonstrate that government structures
emphasizing professionalism and administrative discretion can
support anti-­corruption efforts relative to those emphasizing
oversight and accountability. As emphasized in the Friedrich-­
Finer debate, the challenge lies in striking a balance between
allowing public administrators the autonomy to exercise their
professional judgment and expertise, while simultaneously
maintaining sufficient oversight to prevent abuse of power and
ensure adherence to democratic principles. Thus, by exploring
how public employee perceptions of workplace autonomy shape
the performance of anti-­corruption interventions, our findings
also offer a novel perspective on how efforts to strengthen the
accountability of public organizations are influenced by public
employee attitudes and perceptions.
To address our research objective, we focus on two common
organizational corruption control strategies. The first strat-
egy, which is compliance oriented, seeks to discourage corrup-
tion by increasing the perceived cost of engaging in corrupt
acts through the use of monitoring, auditing, and punishment
mechanisms. This strategy can be seen as a strong form of ex-
ternal accountability that constrains bureaucrats' professional
judgment and expertise. The second strategy, which focuses
on values, attempts to deter corruption by communicating or-
ganizational values and ethical decision-­making practices to
public employees, typically through the development of dis-
tinctive organizational codes of ethics. This approach comple-
ments efforts to foster bureaucratic expertise and discretion,
as it aims to guide decision-­making rather than strictly control
behavior.
Bridging theories of organizational control (Ouchi 1979) and
self-­determination (Ryan and Deci 2002), we argue that the
effectiveness of two commonly used corruption control strat-
egies is shaped by public employees' perceptions of workplace
autonomy. Specifically, we propose that employees who per-
ceive greater autonomy respond more positively to value-­
oriented approaches that emphasize ethical decision-­making,
while those who perceive less autonomy are more responsive to
compliance-­oriented strategies that focus on monitoring and
punishment. Organizational control theory provides a valu-
able framework for this research, as it helps identify and ana-
lyze the mechanisms that drive the effectiveness of different
corruption control approaches. Our focus on workplace auton-
omy is informed by self-­determination theory (SDT), which
suggests that individuals with a higher sense of autonomy feel
more empowered to act according to their beliefs and values
(Sousa et al. 2012). This theory highlights how the efficacy of
control mechanisms may vary across individuals. Central to
our argument is the idea that a mismatch between corruption
control strategies and employees' perceptions of autonomy can
lead to greater tolerance for corruption.
An important challenge confronting empirical scholarship
on corruption concerns measurement. Much of the exist-
ing research on corruption relies on perceptual or behavioral
measures. However, a major problem with this measurement
approach is that it can systematically underestimate the preva-
lence of corruption (Rubin and Whitford 2008). For this reason,
we draw on an alternative measurement strategy that focuses
on public employee corruption tolerance, which is defined as
“the extent to which people tolerate acts of corruption” (Gong
and Wang 2013, 517). A desirable property of this approach to
measuring corruption is that it tracks closely with actual levels
of corruption (Schweitzer et al. 2004; Warburton 2013). To this
end, understanding an individual's attitude toward corruption
provides crucial insights into their likelihood to engage in cor-
rupt activities.

2   |   Two Approaches to Corruption Control
Managerial practices play an important role in establish-
ing an ethical work environment for any organization (Bellé
and Cantarelli  2017). Organizational control theory (OCT)
offers a framework for understanding how managerial strat-
egies can guide, motivate, and cultivate desirable behaviors
from employees to achieve organizational goals, such as
combating corruption (Long et  al.  2002; Sitkin et  al.  2020;
Courtright et al. 2017). From the OCT framework, two gen-
eral approaches to fostering a work environment that deters
corruption are advanced, one that focuses on compliance and
the second that focuses on professional values (Paine  1994;
Weaver et al. 1999a, 1999b).
The compliance-­oriented corruption control strategy, in-
fluenced by rational choice theory (Mishra  2006; Rose-­
Ackerman 2013) and deterrence theory (Becker 1968), operates
on the premise that individuals are utilitarian in their decision-­
making and primarily driven by self-­interest. This approach
emphasizes careful monitoring of employee behavior and
penalties for deviating from acceptable behaviors (Butterfield
et  al.  2005). For instance, Gneezy  (2005) demonstrates how
punishments deter deception, while Zoghbi-­Manrique-­de-­
Lara (2011) explores the impact of monitoring and sanction-
ing on employees' compliance with organizational priorities.
By using managerial interventions of monitoring and punish-
ment to manipulate perceived incentives to engage in corrupt
acts, this strategy aims to align public employee behavior with
organizational goals by increasing the likelihood of detection
and anticipated costs of corruption while reducing expected
benefits (Cameron et al. 2009).
The value-­oriented corruption control strategy differs from
the compliance strategy in that it assumes individual decision-­
making is guided by a desire to uphold professional values
(Paine 1994). From this perspective, organizations can exert
control over employee behaviors not only by influencing
perceptions of risk associated with engaging in corrupt acts
(i.e., monitoring, auditing, and punishment mechanisms),
but also by developing and communicating professional val-
ues (Paine  1994; Weaver et  al.  1999a, 1999b). To encourage
ethical decision-­making, organizations rely on interventions
such as codes of ethics that link organizational ethical stan-
dards and professional norms to desired behaviors (Huberts
et  al.  2014; Tyler and Blader  2005; Green and Weber  1997;
Sunstein 2016). For example, Somers (2001) explores how cor-
porations' use of employee codes of ethics shaped the extent
to which employees prioritized the well-­being of the organiza-
tion when making decisions. Relatedly, Erwin (2011) explores
how the quality of organizational codes of ethics correlates
with corporate social responsibility performance. In sum, a
second prominent approach to controlling employee behavior
is through the use of value-­oriented strategies that attempt to
persuade public employees to behave ethically by appealing to
professional norms and expertise, rather than by attempting
to alter perceived risks of engaging in certain behaviors and
avoiding others.
Both corruption control strategies are widely used and find
justification from prominent theories on individual decision-­
making. However, there is limited empirical evidence on their
relationship to public employee attitudes toward engaging in
corruption (Gans-­Morse et al. 2018). This lack of evidence high-
lights a need to explore the efficacy of these different strategies,
as well as the mechanisms that contribute to their success. As
we argue below, psychological features of public employees,
and in particular their perceptions of workplace autonomy,
provide useful insight for formulating expectations as to how
these strategies work.
3   |   Hypothesis: Does the Efficacy of Corruption
Control Strategies Depend on Public Employee
Autonomy Perceptions?
Compliance oriented strategies attempt to dissuade public em-
ployees from engaging in acts of corruption through the use
of managerial interventions, namely, monitoring, audits, and
punishments, while value-­oriented strategies attempt to do
so by appealing to professional values and expertise. Each of
these strategies makes tacit, yet strong assumptions about pub-
lic employee decision-­making, with the compliance-­oriented
approach assuming that public employees are utilitarian
decision-­makers who focus on personal benefits, while the
value-­oriented approach assumes public employee decision-­
making is guided by a desire to uphold professional standards.
Yet, as a wealth of research has shown, factors that shape
decision-­making in organizational contexts are complex and
multi-­dimensional (Downs 1966; Keiser 2010; Krause 1994).
This means that factors that motivate one employee to act a
certain way may motivate a different employee to act a differ-
ent way (Frey 1997; Frey and Jegen 2001). That is, the effec-
tiveness of corruption control strategies may differ from one
public employee to the next. We argue that a public employee's
perceptions of workplace autonomy can help explain some of
this employee-­level variation and therefore improve our ability

3.1   |   Hypothesis 1: How Do Perceptions
of Workplace Autonomy Relate to Corruption
Tolerance?
SDT, a theoretical framework from psychology, is often used to
understand the causes and consequences of individuals' percep-
tions of autonomy (Deci and Ryan 1985; Ryan and Deci 2000).
Autonomy refers to the freedom an individual has to make de-
cisions and regulate their behavior based on their professional
norms and values (Carver and Scheier 2000). It is linked to de-
sirable outcomes such as improved work motivation (Langfred
and Moye 2004) and performance (Yu 2023). Conversely, indi-
viduals who perceive less workplace autonomy are shown to be
more influenced by tangible rewards and punishments (Sousa
et al. 2012).
Building on these insights, studies have shown public em-
ployee perceptions of workplace autonomy play an important
role in shaping their attitudes toward corruption. For example,
Zhang et al. (2019) found that public employees who perceived
greater autonomy exhibited greater job satisfaction and, in
turn, reported lower levels of corruptibility. Dhar (2016) un-
covers similar results. In their study, they show that greater
perceived workplace autonomy was positively associated with
perceptions of ethical leadership and employee intentions to
behave ethically themselves. Similarly, motivational theorists
argue that individuals who perceive greater workplace auton-
omy tend to use their professional discretion to promote the
best interests of the public (Arvanitis 2017). In other words,
public employees who report greater workplace autonomy are
also often shown to express an intention to use their profes-
sional discretion in prosocial ways.
We extend these arguments to propose that when public em-
ployees perceive they have the flexibility to use their profes-
sional expertise to avoid corrupt practices, they report lower
levels of corruption tolerance. This stems from the idea that
perceived autonomy gives public employees the flexibility to
use their beliefs and professional values to navigate ethically
challenging situations (Carver and Scheier 2000; Dhar 2016).
Greater perceived workplace autonomy empowers public
employees to resolve ethical dilemmas without feeling pres-
sured to engage in corrupt practices or adhere to organiza-
tional norms that may conflict with ethical standards (Zhang
et al. 2019). The confidence that comes from being trusted to
make decisions reinforces their commitment to ethical behav-
ior and reduces their tolerance for corruption.
With these points in mind, we predict that individuals who per-
ceive greater workplace autonomy will have lower levels of cor-
ruption tolerance.
H1.  Perceptions of workplace autonomy are inversely related to
corruption tolerance, such that individuals who perceive greater
workplace autonomy have lower levels of corruption tolerance,
and individuals who perceive less workplace autonomy have
higher levels of corruption tolerance.
3.2   |   Hypotheses 2 and 3: How Do Compliance
and Value-­Oriented Corruption Control Strategies
Relate to Corruption Tolerance?
While hypothesis 1 predicts that an organization's success in areas
such as cultivating expertise and discretion reduces corruption
tolerance, it is also important to examine how external control
mechanisms correlate with public employees' tolerance for cor-
ruption. OCT provides important insights into this question. OCT
argues that organizations use different managerial control strate-
gies to ensure employee performance aligns with organizational
objectives (Ouchi  1979). In general, the literature distinguishes
between two forms of control, with the first being coercive and the
second being autonomous (Baumann and Kuhl 2005). Coercive
forms of control try to pressure employees to comply with organi-
zational directives through the use of high powered rewards and
punishments (Jakobsen et al. 2018). Autonomous forms of control
attempt to encourage employees to behave in ways that are bene-
ficial to the organization by, for example, explaining how certain
behaviors will benefit the organization or society. In the context of
public management, examples of coercive forms of control include
pay-­for-­performance (Weibel et  al.  2010), naming and shaming
(Bevan and Wilson 2013), and threats of termination (An 2019).
Examples of autonomous forms of external control include men-
toring (Bozeman and Feeney 2009), efforts to align organizational
goals with individual priorities (Miao et al. 2018), and highlighting
the positive impact of public service (Honig 2021).
Research shows that coercive forms of external control can
backfire and end up encouraging the very behavior they are try-
ing to discourage. For example, Bicchieri et al. (2021) show that
in contexts where collaboration is difficult, efforts to use pun-
ishments to induce collaboration can actually weaken intentions
to collaborate. Raihani and Bshary (2019) show that the use of
punishments to deter a given behavior can raise awareness of
said behavior and, in doing so, tacitly encourage individuals
to engage in the very behavior that is being punished. Finally,
Muthukrishna et al. (2017) show that in certain contexts the use
of punishments to deter corruption can encourage individuals
to engage in it. Conversely, previous research has found that
autonomous forms of external control are generally effective at
encouraging employees to behave in ways that advance organi-
zational objectives. For example, Lee and Jin (2023) drew on a
sample of South Korean civil servants to demonstrate how ef-
forts to create learning opportunities for employees contributed
to greater knowledge sharing and innovative behavior. Similarly,
Favero et al. (2014) show that providing performance feedback
and encouraging employee participation in decision-­making sig-
nificantly improved the performance of public schools.
In the context of corruption control strategies, both compliance-­
oriented and value-­oriented approaches can be viewed as
external controls aiming to align individual behavior with or-
ganizational goals (Kinley and Ben-­Hur  2015). Compliance-­
oriented strategies, given their reliance on monitoring, audits,
and punishment, correspond to a coercive form of external

Given the evidence that coercive forms of external control fre-

intend to encourage, we predict this strategy will be positively
associated with corruption tolerance. That is, we predict that
the use of compliance-­oriented corruption control strategies will
be positively related to public employees' tolerance for corrup-

demonstrating various forms of autonomous external control ef-

values and align their behavior with organizational objectives.
Given this robust relationship, we predict that the use of value-­

of autonomous external control, will be associated with lower
levels of public employee corruption tolerance. In other words,
we predict an inverse relationship between value-­oriented cor-
ruption deterrence strategies and public employee corruption
tolerance.
H2.  On average, compliance-­oriented organizational control is
positively associated with corruption tolerance.
H3.  On average, value-­oriented corruption control is negatively
associated with corruption tolerance.
3.3   |   Hypotheses 4 and 5: Does the Efficacy
of Corruption Control Strategies Depend on Public
Employee Perceptions of Autonomy?
Earlier, we discussed a longstanding tension in public admin-
istration popularized by the Friedrich-­Finer debate, between
the importance of efforts to cultivate bureaucratic expertise and
discretion on the one hand, and the need for external oversight
on the other. Applying these insights, our first three hypothe-
ses develop predictions about how public employee corruption
tolerance is associated with the different perspectives in this
debate—perceptions of workplace autonomy on the one hand,
and organizational control over employee behavior, through the
implementation of anti-­corruption strategies, on the other.
However, the relationship between corruption control strate-
gies and corruption tolerance is likely moderated by public em-
ployees' perceptions of workplace autonomy (cf. Corduneanu
et al. 2020). Greater perceived autonomy is linked to employees
exercising more discretion and integrating professional norms
into their decision-­making (Gottlieb et al. 2021). According to
SDT (Ryan and Deci 2000), perceived autonomy fosters deeper
cognitive engagement with ethical guidelines, enhances align-
ment between personal and organizational values, and promotes
intrinsic motivation for ethical behavior. Employees with higher
perceived autonomy are more likely to develop stronger ethical
decision-­making skills and take proactive steps to maintain in-
tegrity. Consequently, these employees are more responsive to
value-­oriented corruption control measures, such as organiza-
tional codes of ethics, as they feel empowered to reflect on and
apply these values in their daily work (Curren and Ryan 2020).
For instance, Güntert  (2015) found that employees with
greater perceived autonomy were more inclined to internalize
organizational values when evaluating their own and their col-
leagues' behaviors. Similarly, Ryan and Deci (2017) demonstrate
that individuals with higher autonomy are more likely to use
rules and professional norms as guiding principles for accept-
able behavior. Therefore, it follows that employees who perceive
greater autonomy will be more influenced by the values embed-
ded in codes of ethics and more likely to incorporate these values
into their professional conduct (cf. Deci et al. 1994). This leads to
the following hypothesis:
H4.  The negative relationship between value-­oriented corrup-
tion control and corruption tolerance will be stronger among pub-
lic employees who perceive greater workplace autonomy.
For employees who perceive less workplace autonomy,
compliance-­oriented control measures are likely to be more ef-
fective in reducing corruption tolerance. These individuals tend
to adopt a utilitarian approach to decision-­making, focusing
on external rewards and punishments rather than internalized
professional norms, as they have limited ability to exercise in-
dependent judgment (Hagger and Chatzisarantis  2011). With
their autonomy constrained, they rely heavily on organizational
rules to determine acceptable behavior, making them more
sensitive to the risks of material penalties for non-­compliance
(Deci et al. 1989; Ryan and Deci 2017). For example, Schreurs
et al. (2014) found that employees with lower perceived auton-
omy responded more positively to coercive control methods,
such as pay-­for-­performance schemes, than those with higher
autonomy. Similarly, Gagné and Deci (2005) and Kuvaas (2006)
demonstrate that workers with low autonomy are more likely
to be influenced by external controls and monitoring. As these
findings suggest, compliance-­oriented strategies—emphasizing
monitoring, audits, and punishments—are more effective for
employees who depend on external controls to guide behavior,
as they have less reliance on internal values. Therefore, public
employees with low perceived autonomy are more likely to ad-
just their actions in response to external cues, particularly in en-
vironments where compliance-­oriented controls signal the costs
and consequences of corruption.
H5.  The relationship between compliance-­oriented corruption
control and corruption tolerance is weakest among individuals
who perceive lower levels of workplace autonomy.
4   |   Measurement, Data, and Analytical Approach
4.1   |   Measurement
4.1.1   |   Dependent Variable
The dependent variable for this study is corruption tolerance,
which examines how accepting an individual is of a “deviant be-
havior associated with a particular motivation, namely that of
private gain at public expense” (Friedrich 1989, 15) within an or-
ganization. Common examples include bribery, fraud, or misuse
of authority. To measure corruption tolerance, we use public em-
ployee survey data. As mentioned earlier, corruption tolerance
has been shown to be positively correlated with actual levels of
corruption (Schweitzer et al. 2004; Warburton 2013). Previous
research exploring public employee attitudes toward corruption

tend to use a mix of survey items that probe perceived accept-
ability of corruption in specific scenarios and more general atti-
tudes toward the severity of corruption. For example, Wang and
Sun (2016) ask civil servants to share their thoughts on whether
they believe it is acceptable for someone to provide gifts to public
employees in exchange for personal favors, as well as whether
they believe most people would use their position to advance
their own best interests. Similarly, Gong and Wang (2013) in-
clude items evaluating acceptability of specific types of behav-

gauging perceptions on the social prevalence of corruption.
Zhang et al. (2019) also employ a mix of items addressing both
general attitudes toward corruption and the acceptability of cor-

beliefs about the ethical vulnerability or accountability of peers
and the government system as measure of general attitudes and
two items of acceptability of specific behaviors. Drawing on
this measurement strategy, we developed a three-­item index
that gauges respondents' corruption tolerance using a 5-­point
Likert scale, where a score of 1 denotes low tolerance while 5
indicates a high tolerance for corruption. The items as well as
measures of internal validity can be found in Appendix B. As
can be seen from the descriptive statistics outlined in Table 1,
on average, participants fall just above the midpoint of our 5-­
point scale, which is interesting in that it indicates that public
employees, on average, are moderately tolerant of corruption.
Zhang et al. (2019) find similar levels of bureaucrat acceptance
of corruption using a sample of Taiwanese public officials.
4.1.2   |   Independent Variables
This study uses three key independent variables: compliance-­
oriented corruption control strategies, value-­oriented corruption
control strategies, and public employee perceptions of workplace
autonomy.
Compliance-­oriented corruption control: For our measure of
compliance-­oriented control, we use data from the 2016 Anti-­
Corruption Initiative Assessment (AIA), which was carried
out by the South Korean Anti-­corruption and Civil Rights
Commission (ACRC). The ACRC operates independently of
any government ministry and reports directly to the President,
which ensures the impartiality of the assessment process. The
AIA collects data from public institutions, examining how ef-
fective their management practices are at mitigating corrup-
tion risks, enforcing internal control systems, and handling
corruption-­related incidents. Based on the results of the assess-
ment, ministries are placed into one of five tiers, with the first
tier indicating the anti-­corruption measures taken by a partic-
ular ministry are highly effective and tier five indicating they
are ineffective. The tier a ministry belongs to is what we use to
measure compliance-­oriented corruption control. This tier rat-
ing is an apt measure of compliance-­oriented corruption control
because it captures the spectrum of managerial interventions
a ministry employs, namely monitoring, audits, and punish-
ments, to ensure that employees are complying with organiza-
tional mandates to prevent corruption. For example, a ministry
placed in the first tier would have demonstrated the effective
implementation of anti-­corruption protocols, such as regular au-
dits, monitoring, and strict enforcement of penalties for corrupt
activities. Cumulatively, these measures reflect the extent to
which managerial interventions are used to shape employee
behaviors to actively compel employees to behave in ways that
align with institutional goals of reducing corruption risks. By
relying on these tiers, we are able to quantify how effectively an
organization promotes compliance with corruption prevention
strategies (see Appendix C for a model result of an alternative
measure of compliance-­oriented corruption control).
Value-­oriented corruption control: As discussed earlier, value-­
oriented control strategies work by illustrating how professional
values translate into desired behaviors within a particular or-
ganizational context. Like the compliance-­oriented corruption
control strategy, this approach aims to exert control over public
employee behavior. However, unlike the compliance-­oriented
approach, which operates by shaping perceptions of risk and in-
centives associated with acts of corruption through the use of
tools such as monitoring, auditing, and punishment, the value-­
oriented control strategy emphasizes the use of values as a tool
for aligning employee behavior with organizational goals and
priorities. By fostering an environment where organizational val-
ues are internalized and promoted, value-­oriented controls can
enhance public employees' understanding of and commitment
to desired behaviors and reduce the likelihood of unethical prac-
tices. Therefore, the mechanism through which control is exer-
cised is fundamentally different, relying on internal rather than
external motivators to guide behavior. Focusing on the legal and
administrative context of South Korea, we use organizational
codes of ethics taken from 33 South Korean ministries. Codes of
ethics in the South Korean central government are specifically
designed to promote fair conduct and prevent corrupt behavior.
By addressing fair conduct, abuse of authority, and unethical
practices, they establish professional norms and values that pro-
mote corruption prevention and uphold integrity. Presidential
decree establishes a generic code of ethics that serves as a base-

Perceptions of workplace autonomy refer to the importance an
individual assigns to discretion and freedom to make meaningful
decisions regarding their work (Ryan and Deci 2000). Employee
perceptions of autonomy are heavily influenced by the extent
to which they believe a leader should shape the behavior and
decision-­making of subordinates (Breaugh 1985). To this end,
measures of an employee's perception of autonomy typically ask
employees about the extent to which they think it is important
for them to influence decision-­making processes and the work
of their organization (Jong and Ford 2021). Building on this, we
measure public employee perceptions of workplace autonomy
using a measure that is adapted from Treviño et al. (1998) In
their study, Treviño et al. measure employee beliefs that a su-
pervisor should establish a clear, one-­way chain of communica-
tion and command. Applying this measurement strategy to our
study, we developed a four-­item index that gauges respondents'
sense of autonomy using a 5-­point Likert scale. Data used to
measure perceptions of autonomy were taken from the public
employee survey data. The items as well as measures of inter-
nal validity can be found in Appendix B. As can be seen from
the descriptive statistics outlined in Table  1, the mean value
for perceptions of workplace autonomy is 3.06, which suggests
participants are slightly positive in terms of their perceptions of
workplace autonomy.
Finally, potential confounding influences were controlled for in
our analysis by accounting for background information about
respondents, such as rank, gender, age, job tenure, public ser-
vice motivation, and prosocial motivation. This data was also
obtained from the public employee survey data.
4.2   |   Data
The goal of this study is to explore the extent to which public
employee perceptions of workplace autonomy can help explain
the performance of institutional corruption control strategies.
To this end, we draw on two levels of data: organization-­level
data on corruption control strategies, and individual-­level data
on public employee perceptions and attitudes.
The individual-­level data used in this study come from a sur-
vey conducted by the Center for Government Competitiveness
(CGC) at Seoul National University between June 14 and August
29, 2016. The goal of CGC is to provide insight into steps gov-
ernments can take to improve different aspects of their national
competitiveness, ranging from efforts to combat corruption to
improving public employee morale. In service of this objective,
the CGC produces a ranking of national governments across the
world using a mixture of primary and secondary data. To this
end, the Center regularly collects survey data from government
ministries in South Korea to assess attitudes and perceptions
of the central government workforce. The survey data used in
this study come from one such effort. The sampling approach
employed a mixed-­method strategy, combining stratified and
purposive sampling to ensure representative and diverse partici-
pation from each ministry. A sample of 40 public employees was
drawn across each of the South Korean central government's
35 ministries. To administer the survey, Tablet Aided Personal
Interviews (TAPI) were used due to evidence that they help
improve the quality of survey data (Leisher 2014). Specifically,
TAPIs help to reduce errors that result during the survey ad-
ministration and data compilation phases of survey data col-
lection. Once data collection was complete, cases with missing
values were excluded. This resulted in dropping 79 observations.
Next, we screened out 80 responses that came from the Office
for Government Policy Coordination (OGPC) (n = 40) and the
National Police Agency (n = 40). The OGPC is a ministry level
organization that is distinct from other ministries in that its
primary purpose is to ensure coordination across South Korea's
remaining 34 ministries. Relatedly, this ministry also contains
an anti-­corruption agency, the Joint Anti-­corruption Prevention
Office, and typically includes a higher proportion of employees
on seconded appointments (public employees who are tempo-
rarily rotated to this ministry from a different ministry) when
compared to other ministries. For these reasons, it is likely that
public employees employed by this ministry have distinctive at-
titudes toward corruption and corruption tolerance when com-
pared to other ministries and are therefore dropped. Similar to
the OGPC, the National Police Agency was also excluded from
our analysis due to the central role this agency plays in efforts
to investigate and detect instances of government corruption.
Five observations were excluded because their job grade corre-
sponded to the senior executive service (SES). Members of the
SES were excluded because their responsibilities fundamentally
differ from other public employees. For example, in addition to
administrative responsibilities, these individuals also engage
in policymaking, serve as advisors to ministers, and liaise with
politicians (Yoon 2021; Kim and Im 2019). Finally, one observa-
tion was removed due to an anomaly in reported tenure—they
stated they worked as a public employee for 48 years, which is
nearly impossible given the mandatory retirement age for public
officials in South Korea is 60 years old. The resulting data set
consisted of 1235 responses. The average proportion of civil ser-
vants above grade 5 in the population was 47.4%, and in our col-
lected sample, this ratio was 40.2%.
For the organization-­level data, we incorporated official
information on the AIA ranking of each ministry and or-
ganizational codes of ethics to measure compliance and value-­
oriented control corruption strategies. As explained earlier,
our measure of compliance-­oriented control comes from the
2016 Anti-­Corruption Initiative Assessment. Specifically,
we use the tier each ministry was assigned. The ACRC does
not disclose individual scores for central ministries; instead,
it categorizes these scores into five tiers. The specific tier to
which each ministry is assigned is published annually. The
AIA ranks for the ministries in 2016 were collected from re-
ports available on the ACRC website. Data on organizational
codes of ethics were obtained by web-­scraping the Korean
Law Information Center website using the Selenium package
in Python. The Korean Law Information Center is a website

4.3   |   Analytical Approach
To model relationships between dependent and independent
variables, we use hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) with
random intercepts. This approach allows us to account for the
fact that the public employees included in our sample are nested
in distinct ministries and that there may be clustering effects
that result from the nested nature of our data (Hofmann 1997).
Specifically, in our study, 1235 civil servants are nested within
33 ministries. By empirically accounting for the nested nature of
observations, HLM allows for a cleaner partitioning of relation-
ships that are present between variables at different levels (e.g.,
organizational and individual levels) (Steel and Taras 2010). We
opted for random intercepts because public employee corrup-
tion tolerance may, on average, vary across ministries. In other
words, the random intercepts HLM allows us to better account
for the distinctive nature of each ministry when modeling re-
lationships between dependent and independent variables, as
well as for differences among employees working in different
ministries. Because corruption tolerance and perceptions of
workplace autonomy are measured using the same survey, we
also examined the results of a Harman's Single Factor Test. The
results demonstrate that no single factor accounts for more than
50% of the variance, thus limiting concerns of any relationship
between perceptions of autonomy and corruption tolerance
being positively biased.
5   |   Results
We begin by reviewing correlations between key variables. As
can be seen from the correlations in Table  1, perceptions of
workplace autonomy are strongly and inversely related to cor-
ruption tolerance (r = −0.291, p < 0.001). This suggests that pub-
lic employees who perceive greater autonomy tend to have lower
levels of corruption tolerance. This finding aligns with those
from previous research (Ryan and Deci  2000; Kish-­Gephart
et al. 2010; Treviño et al. 1999). There is a significant but weak
inverse relationship between the compliance-­oriented control
strategy and corruption tolerance (r = −0.091, p < 0.001). This
finding suggests that as the stringency of compliance-­oriented
controls increases, there is a slight reduction in corruption
tolerance among public employees. This relationship is con-
sistent with the findings of Raihani and Bshary  (2019) and
Muthukrishna et  al.  (2017). No significant relationships are
found between the value-­oriented control strategy and corrup-
tion tolerance. However, these results may be due to not using
HLM, as the correlations ignore the hierarchical structure of the
data, potentially overlooking nested relationships and leading to
biased estimates.
Turning to our HLM analysis, we use a stepwise approach to
estimating relationships between key independent variables
and our dependent variable, corruption tolerance. The results of
these analyses are presented in Table 2. The first model used is
a null model that only includes a constant term. By omitting in-
dependent and control variables, we are able to see how much of
the total variance in corruption tolerance is explained by differ-
ences at the ministry level using the intraclass correlation coeffi-
cient (ICC). Model 2 incorporates control variables, while Model
3 adds in individual and organizational level variables. Model 4
includes interaction terms between individual and organization
level independent variables. As is expected, with the addition
of new variables across each new model, we observe improve-
ments to model fit, as evidenced by log likelihood ratio tests and
declining Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian
Information Criterion (BIC).
From Model 1, using the ICC coefficient, we see that roughly 2.7%
of the total variation in corruption tolerance can be explained by
differences across ministries. In Model 3, we uncover a strong neg-
ative and statistically significant relationship between perceived
autonomy and corruption tolerance, implying public employees
who perceive greater workplace autonomy tend to be less tolerant
of corruption. We see no statistically significant relationship be-
tween both corruption strategies and corruption tolerance.
Our findings currently support hypothesis 1, but not hypothe-
ses 2 and 3. The lack of a statistically significant relationship
between either corruption control strategy and corruption tol-
erance is notable, as prior research has devoted much effort
to exploring how these tools relate to corruption measures.
However, as shown in Model 3, even when considering the
nested structure of our data, the relationship remains weak for
both value-­oriented and compliance-­oriented control strate-
gies. One interpretation of these findings is that neither compli-
ance-­ nor value-­based corruption control strategies are effective.
Alternatively, these strategies alone may have little explanatory
power regarding levels of corruption in public organizations.
Instead, their effectiveness may depend on individual-­level fac-
tors. Additionally, the non-­significant result could be linked to
the potential backfiring effects or mixed outcomes highlighted
in previous research on control strategies. Without considering
the conditions that trigger opposing effects, both compliance-­
and value-­oriented controls may have conflicting impacts,
which could explain the non-­significant results in Model 3.
To understand how the efficacy of corruption control strategies
varies according to public employee autonomy perceptions,
Model 4 incorporates two interaction terms, one that com-
bines the value-­oriented corruption control strategy with em-
ployee perceptions of autonomy and a second that combines the
compliance-­oriented corruption control strategy with employee
perceptions of autonomy. Interestingly, we observe that both in-
teraction terms are statistically significant, though the relation-
ships appear to move in opposite directions, as evidenced by the
coefficient signs. Specifically, the coefficient on the interaction
term combining value-­oriented control and perceptions of au-
tonomy is negative (r = −0.018, p < 0.05), whereas the interaction

6   |   Discussion
This study argued that the effectiveness of organizational
corruption control strategies is shaped by public employees'
perceptions of workplace autonomy. The analysis focuses on
public employees' corruption tolerance, a factor closely linked
to actual corruption levels within organizations (Schweitzer
et al. 2004; Warburton 2013). Our findings generally support
our hypotheses, indicating that public employees' perceptions
for workplace autonomy significantly moderate the relationship
between corruption control strategies and corruption tolerance.
Additionally, we observe that a mismatch between organiza-
tional corruption control strategies and public employees'

control strategies are associated with higher levels of corrup-

strategies appear ineffective among those who perceive greater

Before outlining the contributions of this study to theory and
practice, we review some limitations that pave the way for future
research. First, this study relies on cross-­sectional data, meaning
that we are unable to offer causal insight into the performance of
different corruption control strategies at increasing or decreasing
levels of public employee corruption tolerance. While the analysis
identifies an association between corruption control strategies and
corruption tolerance that is moderated by perceptions of autonomy,
it is important to acknowledge that further research employing al-
ternative research designs are needed to explore these correlations
that can be considered causal. To establish causality and resolve
issues of directionality, future research should utilize longitudinal
data and experimental methods to better isolate the causal effects
of the independent and moderating variables. Second, measures of
perceptions of workplace autonomy and corruption tolerance are
both taken from the same survey, creating the potential for com-
mon source bias. This concern notwithstanding, the underlying
factor structure of these variables, the results of a Harman's single
factor test, and emphasis on interaction terms suggest this issue
is unlikely to be a driving force behind our results. That said, the
validity of the insights we offer would be strengthened by future
research that relies on measures of perception of autonomy and
corruption tolerance using different surveys. Third, our measure
of corruption tolerance combines several survey items, including
questions related to the ethical vulnerability of peers and staff (e.g.,
most will engage in corruption if there is no possibility of being
caught) and a direct question about the likelihood of engaging in
corrupt acts (e.g., falsifying reimbursement information). While
this measurement approach aligns closely with prior research on
corruption tolerance and corruptibility (Wang and Sun 2016; Gong
and Wang 2013; Zhang et al. 2019), it is also important to acknowl-
edge the need for additional research that employs alternative mea-
surement approaches. To this end, future research should consider
developing alternative measures that distinguish between corrup-
tion tolerance and, for example, trust-­related constructs. Such ef-
forts would provide further clarity on the relationships between
corruption control strategies and behavioral outcomes. Fourth, we
focus on a single level of government, thereby raising questions
over the generalizability of our findings to state/provincial and
local governments. Thus, an interesting question to be explored by
future research is whether the patterns of relationships observed
here apply to other levels of government within a particular na-
tional setting. Additionally, our measure of value-­oriented corrup-
tion control offers one approach to capturing how organizations
may attempt to communicate organizational values and priorities
to employees, namely through a tailored code of ethics. To substan-
tiate the claims made in this paper, further research, drawing on
alternative measures of value-­oriented controls, is needed. Finally,
and related to this point, South Korea relies on a highly profession-
alized public bureaucracy, where civil servants regularly study for
years to pass civil service exams to join the government workforce
(Woo and Kim 2024). It is also important to explore these research
questions in contexts where civil service norms and practices are
different, as well as in contexts where the civil service may not be
as professionalized.
Despite these limitations, this study offers important contri-
butions to public administration research and practice. First,
our findings highlight the importance of accounting for indi-
vidual characteristics of public employees when considering
strategies to fight corruption. This insight resonates with the
long-­standing Friedrich-­Finer debates in public administra-
tion, which grappled with questions of bureaucratic discretion
versus strict adherence to rules. Our study adds nuance to this
debate by suggesting that the effectiveness of control strate-
gies may depend on individual employee perceptions of work-
place autonomy. Indeed, when testing hypotheses 2 and 3, our
empirical analysis finds no support for either corruption con-
trol strategy being significantly related to corruption, on av-
erage. That is, efforts to understand the implications of these
strategies for corruption will likely paint a misleading picture
if they do not also take into consideration characteristics of
the employees these strategies target. However, corruption
control strategies typically are implemented based on assump-
tions that overlook the heterogeneity of the public workforce,
a shortcoming that is particularly important given efforts to
increase the diversity of government employees around the
world. To this end, our findings establish an important base-
line from which future research can continue to build in order
to better understand how different features related to public
employees' attitudes and perceptions help shape the effective-
ness of anti-­corruption interventions.
The second contribution of this study is to shed light on factors
that contribute to the potential backfiring of anti-­corruption
initiatives. In keeping with past work, we find some evidence
of backfiring across two distinct corruption control strategies
(Muthukrishna et  al.  2017). However, our findings also build
upon previous research by documenting the conditions under
which such backfiring might occur. Specifically, as we illus-
trate, mismatches between organizational control mechanisms
and individual perceptions of workplace autonomy may play a
critical role in triggering the backfiring of anti-­corruption in-
terventions. Importantly, however, these findings should not be
interpreted as one strategy being more effective than the other at
controlling corruption, but rather that implementing a corrup-
tion control strategy without accounting for the perceptions and
organizational contexts of public employees can be detrimental.
To this end, when implementing a comprehensive corruption
control plan, public organizations should consider the diversity
of employee viewpoints and tailor their strategies accordingly.
Relatedly, given variation in public employee viewpoints and
attitudes, corruption control strategies may be most effective
when they are combined, with the particular emphasis on one
strategy or another determined according to the characteristics
of the organizational climate in which these strategies are im-
plemented in.
Building upon this point, the final implication of our findings
for practice is to illustrate the importance of corruption control
strategies accounting for other managerial interventions that are
being implemented concurrently. While interventions intending
to reduce corruption are a priority for any public organization,
these strategies are being implemented alongside a host of other

initiatives, many of which will serve the purpose of trying to
motivate and empower their employees. For example, public
organizations are shown to employ a range of organizational
management techniques targeting public employee autonomy
in hopes of fostering employee satisfaction, reducing turnover
intentions, improving innovation, or increasing public service
motivation (see Caillier  2016; Cesário and Magalhães  2017;
Demircioglu  2021). Acknowledging this, our findings suggest
that managerial interventions targeting corruption are likely
to be more effective when they complement organizational
initiatives that are used to motivate public employees. That is,
compliance-­oriented strategies may be most effective in cli-
mates where extrinsic rewards are emphasized, whereas value-­
based strategies may be most effective where intrinsic rewards
are emphasized.
7   |   Conclusion
This study bridges theories on organizational control and self-­
determination to explore the relationship between corruption
control strategies and corruption tolerance among public em-
ployees. Our findings suggest the efficacy of corruption con-
trol strategies varies significantly according to public employee
perceptions of workplace autonomy. That is, for public employ-
ees who perceive less workplace autonomy, our data suggest
compliance-­oriented controls appear to work best, whereas for
public employees who perceive greater workplace autonomy,
value-­oriented controls appear to work best. Cumulatively,
these findings suggest that corruption control strategies are
likely only successful to the extent that they resonate with
employee-­level workplace viewpoints and attitudes, as failure
to do so may result in unexpected, or even counterproductive,
outcomes.

In the main body of this article, we utilize AIA tiers as a measure of
compliance-­oriented control strategy. However, to ensure that our core
findings are robust to different measures, we assess the reproducibility
of results using the total number of severe punishments for corruption
as an alternative measure of compliance-­oriented control. The results
are substantively consistent with the main model, although the patterns
of the interaction effect between perceived autonomy and compliance-­
oriented control exhibit slight variations with this alternative measure.

AIA tiers, the negative slope describing the relationship between
compliance-­oriented control and corruption tolerance becomes less
steep as perceived autonomy increases. Conversely, when measured by

Abstract
Administrative burden research disproportionately examines micro-level burdens on clients claiming benefits from public agencies. Yet we
know little about meso-level burdens on third-party providers making up the submerged state—private actors working on behalf of a public pur-
pose—and what effect these burdens have on services. We draw on interviews, participant observation, and focus groups with substance-use
disorder service providers to map provider burdens and how they affect services that third parties offer. We supplement the provider perspec-
tive with data from clients and their families about their experience with services. We find that providers face significant administrative burdens
resulting from federal and state policy; that these burdens affect the quality of the services they are able to offer; and, ultimately, that burdens
on providers can trickle down to become burdens on clients. Our research has implications for how we understand administrative burdens, the
solutions best suited to reducing them, and the role of burdens as a form of hidden politics in the submerged state.

While the literature is conclusive about the negative effects of
burdens on clients, it has less to say about the effect of meso-
level burdens on administrators. Yet there is some evidence
that burdens on administrators affect programs and even
clients. The impact may be especially pronounced for burdens
on third-party service providers that make up the “submerged
state”—private actors who conduct activities serving a public
purpose (Mettler 2011, 4)—because these actors can exacer-
bate burdens or opt out of participating in public programs
to avoid them. The literature demonstrates that burdens on
clients influence participation in programs, but it has less to
contribute about how burdens on service providers affect
services and, ultimately, whether these burdens trickle down
to clients. We extend research on administrative burdens into
the submerged state, asking: What effect do provider burdens
have on the services they provide and clients they serve?
To answer this question, we examine the burdens imposed on
an important subset of administrators: third-party providers.
Private and nonprofit service providers that make up the
submerged state are the backbone of human services and an
important part of policy implementation in the United States
(Hacker 2002; Hackett 2017, 2019; Mettler 2010). Yet we
know little about the administrative burdens these providers
experience (but see Garboden et al. 2018; Willison 2021) and
the effect these burdens have on the ­services they offer and,
ultimately, clients’ experiences.

Administrative Burdens in the Submerged
State
Administrative burdens are defined as “an individual’s ex-
perience of policy implementation as onerous” (Burden et
al. 2012, 741). Certainly, the idea that government is un-
wieldy and unnecessarily bureaucratic is not new and is
captured elsewhere in the concepts of red tape, sludge, and
ordeals (Madsen, Mikkelsen, and Moynihan 2022). One
key feature of administrative burdens, compared with these
other concepts, is the emphasis on individual experience of
burdens rather than burdens (rules and processes) themselves
(Baekgaard and Tankink 2022; Madsen, Mikkelsen, and
Moynihan 2022). Administrative burden research has made
individual clients central to its study. Yet there is no reason ad-
ministrative burden research cannot “study public managers
or employees” and cannot include “private actors who help
implement policies” (Madsen, Mikkelsen, and Moynihan
2022, 382). Indeed, there are good theoretical reasons to in-
clude meso-level administrators who mediate between macro-
level policies and micro-level client experiences (see, e.g.,
Gofen, Sella, and Gassner 2019; Herd and Moynihan 2018;
Moffitt, O’Neill, and Cohen 2023). In the case of adminis-
trative burdens, research documents the costs that policies
impose on individuals, but we know less about the costs on
administrators, especially third-party providers, the effect
that burdens have on the services they provide or the burdens
that clients experience, and the implications for hidden poli-
tics of the submerged state.
Micro-level Client Burdens
The focus of the literature to date has been on micro-level
client burdens, or how policies and practices are experienced
by the target population. Moynihan, Herd, and Harvey
(2015) explain the three costs that burdens impose on
­citizens: learning costs to find out about program eligibility
and access; compliance costs—time, effort, and money—to
enroll, document, and respond to demands; and psycho-
logical costs, such as stigma, stress, or loss of autonomy, in
interactions with the state. Administrative burdens can serve
important functions such as reducing fraud, enhancing effi-
ciency, rationing scarce resources, and ensuring the integrity
of services (Doughty and Baehler 2020). But they can also
make it more difficult for clients to access programs and for
programs to succeed (Barnes 2020; Bhargava and Manoli
2015; Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021; Deshpande and Li
2019; Peeters 2020; Sommers et al. 2015). When burdens re-
sult in “administrative exclusion” (Brodkin and Majmundar
2010), or the inability to access needed programs, they can
have negative effects (beyond the program) on health and
well-being more generally (Heinrich 2016). Burdens are not
distributed evenly, and they exacerbate existing inequalities
(Barofsky et al. 2020; Bergman et al. 2020; Brodkin and
Majmundar 2010; Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021; Dynarski
and Scott-Clayton 2006; Heinrich et al. 2022; Keele,
Cubbison, and White 2021; Ray, Herd, and Moynihan 2023)
because they add extra costs and because the people who
are required to shoulder them often have fewer resources
(Barofsky et al. 2020; Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021; Olsen,
Kyhse-Andersen, and Moynihan 2022; Willison 2021).
Indeed, social, cultural, economic, and administrative cap-
ital—such as people’s understanding of bureaucratic rules
and structures—may account for individuals’ ability to nav-
igate burdens (Masood and Nisar 2021), such that clients
with the greatest access to capital will best be able to manage
burdens. Unfortunately, at the very time when individuals
need state assistance the most, they may be least capable of
navigating burdens (Christensen et al. 2020).
Because the literature disproportionately sees client-level
burdens, it identifies client-specific solutions to reduce these
burdens, such as more information, simplified procedures, and
minor assistance. Research demonstrates that simplifying in-
formation and enrollment can increase take up in the Earned
Income Tax Credit (Bhargava and Manoli 2015), Medicaid
and the Children’s Health Insurance Program (Fox, Stazyk,
and Feng 2020), childcare (Jenkins and Nguyen 2022), and
college applications and attendance (Bettinger et al. 2012;
Dynarski et al. 2021). Similarly, notifications and reminders
can increase compliance with municipal codes (Linos, Quan,
and Kirkman 2020) and recertification with the Supplemental
Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) (Lopoo, Heflin, and
Boskovski 2020). Additionally, reducing or eliminating doc-
umentation, attendance, and participation demands can
increase autonomy and decrease client stress (Baekgaard
et al. 2021). Sometimes research examines more resource-
intensive (but still client-specific) solutions, such as assis-
tance finding or accessing services, and shows that they are
effective in increasing participation in SNAP (Finkelstein and
Notowidigdo 2019) and the Affordable Care Act (Sommers
et al. 2015) and effective in reducing residential segrega-
tion in housing (Bergman et al. 2020). Likewise, additional
financial resources increase applications for naturalization
(Hainmueller et al. 2018). By seeing burdens at the client
level, the problems imposed by burdens and the solutions to
address them are necessarily truncated, focusing on simplified
procedures, education, and assistance for clients.

Meso-level Administrator Burdens
Yet there are also meso-level burdens on administrators. As
Burden et al. (2012, 741, 742) explain, “The burden of policy
implementation is embedded in the day-to-day existence of
the implementer” and it may require administrators to re-
spond with what they see as a “high level of resources.” For
example, Lemak, Alexander, and Campbell (2003) estimated
that, under increasingly complex requirements imposed by
the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, 1
hour of substance-use treatment therapy was associated with
approximately $60 of non-salary operating expenses. Because
of these high compliance costs, there is good reason to be-
lieve that these burdens may change behavior and affect the
services administrators provide. They may develop “escape
strategies” to avoid high compliance costs (Rich 2022), opt
out of participation in programs entirely (Willison 2021), or
pass burdens to clients. Mikkelsen, Madsen, and Baekgaard
(2023, 9) found, for example, that administrators’ chronic
stress led them to adopt coping strategies (e.g., rationing of
services, routinized counseling, and rigid rule enforcement)
that shifted “the share of administrative burdens. . .back onto
the client.” When layered on top of the micro-level burdens
experienced directly by clients, these meso-level burdens can
make accessing services even more challenging (Herd and
Moynihan 2018).
Third Parties in the Submerged State
In the United States, third-party providers play a crucial
role in policy implementation, especially in the American
welfare state. Scholars have shown how American govern-
ment relies on these private actors in what is described as
the hollow state (Agranoff and McGuire 1998; Milward
and Provan 2000, 2003; Peters 1994), hidden state (Howard
1993), shadow state (Gottschalk 2000), divided welfare state
(Hacker 2002; Klein 2003), and delegated state (Morgan and
Campbell 2011). Suzanne Mettler explains private actors are
a submerged state working on behalf of the state, by rules
set by the state, “to serve a public purpose” (Mettler 2011,
4). Therefore, third parties make concrete many government
goals (Light 1999, 2019; Pettijohn et al. 2013; Strach 2007,
2009; Willison 2021).
Meso-level burdens in the submerged state may be especially
problematic because third parties can exacerbate administra-
tive burdens for clients or they can opt out of service provi-
sion on behalf of government and because the work of the
submerged state undermines democratic accountability and
deepens hidden politics. First, third-party providers can exac-
erbate burdens on clients. According to Herd and Moynihan
(2018, 86), Medicaid administrators ask for increased doc-
umentation from third-party abortion providers, leading
these providers to “sometimes add their own layers of doc-
umentation for women that exceed those required by law.”
Furthermore, Carolyn Barnes (2020) demonstrates how
relying on voucher-based food programs to provide public
services creates another venue for burdens when clients try to
redeem their benefits. She shows how portability—restricting
clients to particular goods and services—and variation in
third-party providers’ quality and participation in programs
create redemption costs for clients, potentially making it
harder for clients to purchase food for which they are eligible.
Second, burdens may disincentivize third-party providers’
participation in public programs, reducing the availability of
services. In a study of urban housing vouchers, Garboden et
al. (2018) demonstrate that landlords weigh the costs of par-
ticipation (inspections and bureaucratic hurdles) against the
benefits of the guaranteed income from the tenants they will
have. In some housing markets (e.g., Baltimore) participa-
tion in these programs makes sense whereas in other housing
markets (e.g., Cleveland) it does not. In research on home-
less service provision, Charley Willison (2021, 157–62) shows
how under-resourced local nonprofit service providers have
a difficult time managing complex, time-consuming, and ex-
pensive administrative burdens in the Medicaid program, po-
tentially reducing the number of third-party providers willing
to participate in it. Both studies suggest that reducing admin-
istrative burdens may increase rates of third-party participa-
tion and enhance services for clients.
Third, reliance on the submerged state may expand state
capacity at the same time it attenuates the relationship be-
tween government expenditures and program delivery
(Hackett 2019; Milward and Provan 2000). Moynihan,
Herd, and Harvey (2015, 44) argue that administrative
burden is “a venue where politics plays out,” and a site of
“hidden politics,” largely invisible to the public and even
policymakers. Policymakers can sidestep legislative politics
and instead create time-consuming procedures that limit
benefits (Herd and Moynihan 2018; Moynihan, Herd, and
Rigby 2016). Because they are hard to see, hidden politics
allow policymakers to take actions that suit their political
purposes even if the broader public is opposed. In this way,
administrative burdens bypass accountability processes.
Hidden politics are even more acute in the submerged state,
which renders government invisible (Mettler 2011). Because
they have little awareness of government policies or their re-
distributive effects, clients may not realize they are enrolled in
a government program (Tallevi 2018). The submerged state
can skew “people’s understandings of the relevance of govern-
ment social policy” and make “it difficult for policy recipients
to engage in the political sphere as informed citizens” (Tallevi
2018, 139), though some populations, especially racially
marginalized groups, are very aware of government’s role in
their lives (Rosenthal 2021; Thurston 2015). Although the
submerged state literature focuses on elements of policy design
such as tax expenditures, deductions, credits, and payments

at micro-level burdens on clients, identifying a narrower set
of burdens and solutions to address them. Scholars of the
submerged state have examined the primary role that private
third parties play in American public policy. Bringing these
two literatures together can potentially extend the insights of
each. Third-party administrators experience burdens too. Yet
there is little research on the types of burdens they experience,
the effect of these burdens on the services they provide, and
the implications for hidden politics in the submerged state.
Data and Methods
To map the burdens on service providers and the effect of
these burdens on clients’ experience of programs, we look in
depth at third-party providers of SUD services in New York
State. We chose to examine SUD service provision in New
York State because services in New York, like the United
States more generally, are provided overwhelmingly by third
parties (OASAS n.d. “About”; SAMHSA 2021b, Table 2.2)
under extensive government regulation. SUD services are bur-
densome (Jaffe and O’Keeffe 2003), and policies overseeing
substance-use disorder are representative of other highly
regulated policy areas.
Policy Context
SUD service provision is heavily regulated by a combina-
tion of federal and state law enforcement and public health
agencies. Some of the most consequential agencies include the
federal Department of Justice’s Drug Enforcement Agency
(DEA), which regulates controlled substances—including
opioids—as well as medication-assisted treatment to treat
opioid-use disorder and the federal Department of Health
and Human Services’ Substance Abuse and Mental Health
Services Administration (SAMHSA), which oversees accred-
itation and certification for treatment providers. While the
broad contours of what may be provided, how, and to whom
is set by federal agencies, states also play an important role.
New York State has its own agency—the Office of Addiction
Services and Supports (OASAS)—dedicated to regulating
SUD service provision while the Office of Mental Health
(OMH) oversees mental health issues that often co-occur
with substance-use disorder. Comparatively, New York State
has a wealth of resources, including one of the largest drug-
treatment systems in the United States (SAMHSA 2022). Yet
even with its extensive infrastructure and laws in place, New
York’s opioid overdose death rate (24.7 deaths per 100,000
people in 2021) mirrors the US rate (24.5) as a whole (KFF
2021). SUD service providers follow prescriptive rules set by
agencies with different missions, different foci, and at dif-
ferent levels of government and they deal with clients who
choose their services or are mandated by courts or other
agencies to receive them.
Meso- and Micro-level Data
Providers operate under countless rules and regulations
across a host of agencies. We collected data from providers
(see also Barnes and Gennetian 2021) about the burdens
they face and the effect of those burdens on clients. First, we
conducted open-ended interviews with 92 nongovernmental
service providers in New York State, choosing providers rep-
resentative of geography (urban, suburban, and rural), size,
and type of services offered. We asked generally about what
problems interviewees encountered, what worked well for
them, what did not work well, and what they would like
policymakers to know (see Appendix A). Interviews lasted be-
tween 30 min and 2 hours. We asked for permission to audio
record. For those who did not want to be recorded, we took
handwritten notes. Second, we also collected data through
participant observation at eight opioid-related meetings
(three task forces, three statewide meetings, and two forums)
and seven tours of health facilities, where we took notes by
hand. Participant observation provided general context for
many of the statements we heard in interviews, allowing us to
see firsthand how providers interacted with others and how
policies in place were translated into concrete experiences for
clients. For example, we heard about facilities requirements
and were able to see what clients walked into when they tried
to access services. We conducted all participant observation
and most interviews in-person (some interviews were over
the phone due to distance or COVID-19) between November
30, 2017 and April 26, 2022. Third, to capture how unprece-
dented policy changes made during the COVID-19 pandemic
shaped the landscape and perceptions of burdens (see also
Baekgaard et al. 2021), we partnered with the Alcoholism and
Substance Abuse Providers of New York State (ASAP) to hold
eight focus groups with 78 providers (74 unique individuals)
in the summer of 2020. ASAP chose a representative sample
based on geography, size, and type of service (prevention,
treatment, and recovery). We asked participants about what
changed during the pandemic, how those changes affected
them, and what they would like to see in the future (see
Appendix B). With the suspension of many state and federal
policies, providers were able to reflect on what is possible and
desirable from government agencies to provide quality care to
clients (see also Barnes and Petry 2021). Focus groups were
conducted via Zoom between July 14 and August 4, 2020,
digitally recorded, and transcribed.

Ethical Considerations
All participants were informed that participation in this re-
search was voluntary, and they were given contact informa-
tion so they could withdraw at any time (one participant
did later withdraw from the study and all of their data were
deleted). The participant pool is representative of the loca-
tion, size, and type of SUD service organizations. Clients are
representative of race, age, and geography and not identified
by their given names. No participants were compensated.
Our fieldwork was approved by our institution’s Institutional
Review Board.
Analysis
The data collected for this article are part of a broader project
examining the challenges of the opioid epidemic, which is se-
quential and ongoing since 2017. The data reflect changes in
the perception and experience of the opioid epidemic as well
as regulatory changes made as a response to the COVID-19
pandemic (see also, Pérez-Chiqués, Strach, and Zuber 2021).
For this article, we draw from examples of meso-level burdens
on administrators, some of which changed (momentarily or
permanently as a result of COVID-19), but that nonetheless
illustrate the broad universe of burdens that operate on third-
party providers and the effect on services that clients receive.
All data (transcribed interviews and focus groups, par-
ticipant observation notes) were uploaded into Atlas.ti, a
qualitative analysis program. To center the perspective of
our interviewees, we coded the data inductively (Braun
and Clarke 2006; Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw 2011, chap. 6;
Haverland and Yanow 2012). Our first pass of open coding
generated the key theme of participants’ difficulties navigating
an array of burdens they encountered. We then proceeded to
do a focused coding, collecting systematic references to what
administrators found burdensome in SUD service provision.
We aggregated the burdens into analytical categories and
traced forward to the effects of those burdens on clients and
traced back to the policies that created them. We use these
data to identify micro-level burdens, meso-level burdens, and
trickle-down burdens. Although providers operate under
countless federal and state policies, the coding identified sev-
eral categories of burdens that were salient across different
locations, types of providers, and modes of data collection
(see Appendix C). Confidence in the validity of our results is
strengthened through a research design that generated data
about administrative burdens unprompted as well as through
intensive, long-term involvement with data collection, de-
tailed, rich data, and triangulation through different modes
of data collection and a wide variety of individuals (Maxwell
2012, chap. 6).
The unit of analysis is a statement: a short vignette, story, or
observation in a larger interview, observation, or focus group.
In the analysis that follows interviews transcripts are denoted
IT, interview notes (for people who did not want to be re-
corded) IN, participant observation PO for the eight meetings
and field notes FN for the seven tours, and specific codes
for the focus groups: FL = Frontline; R = Residential; OP =
Outpatient; P = Prevention; RY = Recovery; B = Business; H =
Harm Reduction; OTP = Opioid Treatment Program; and C =
Client. Each participant is given a unique numerical indicator.
Findings
Our coding reveals five categories of burdens, two associated
with service provision—treatment protocol and authorization
(who may provide services, under what conditions, and to
how many people)—and three with program and personnel
management (staffing, funding, facilities). Only one cate-
gory (treatment protocol) is a micro-level burden on clients
while all five categories are meso-level burdens on providers.
Looking only at micro-level burdens would understate the
true size, scope, and effect of burdens. We find that meso-
level administrator burdens in SUD services and program/per-
sonnel management affect the quality of services for clients.
What’s more, as figure 1 illustrates, some meso-level burdens
(treatment protocols, authorization, staffing, and facility
requirements during COVID-19) trickle down to become
burdens on clients, reducing services providers offer and ac-
cess for clients. In short, there is a broad landscape of burdens
that affect the client experience beyond micro-level burdens.
Meso-level burdens affect the services administrators provide
and may even trickle down to become burdens on clients,
adding compliance costs and diminishing the quality of care.
Below we detail the micro- and meso-level burdens and effects
for clients.
Micro-level Burdens on Clients—Treatment
Protocols
Clients
face
significant
micro-level
burdens
accessing
medication-assisted treatment, but they are only a fraction of
the overall effect that burdens have on them. The primary way
that substance-use disorder is addressed in the United States
is with medication-assisted treatment. Notably the two main
medications used for treatment, methadone and buprenorphine,
are Schedule II and Schedule III drugs (respectively) under the
federal Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. § 801). Prior to
COVID-19, federal regulations required in-person physical
exams before treatment, frequent toxicology screenings, and
daily visits to receive methadone (42 C.F.R. § 8.12). During
COVID, some of these very burdensome regulations were sus-
pended by federal and state agencies, including allowing treat-
ment with buprenorphine without an in-person physical exam;
relaxing toxicology expectations; and increasing discretion for
physicians about how often patients need to come for metha-
done (SAMHSA n.d.a).
Treatment protocols that require daily visits to a federally
certified OTP (the only places allowed to dispense metha-
done) for a dose of methadone create real burdens for clients,
who may not want to seek this medication even when it may
be the most effective therapy for them. “People often say
they hate methadone,” but, according to one provider, “they
hate the methadone delivery system, and they would actually
prefer methadone if they could get it prescribed in a more
patient-centered way” (IT207). Daily visits are burdensome
to clients who have other obligations. “The requirement of
coming in daily for dispensing that might be fine for someone
who’s brand new to treatment but for someone. . .that just
doesn’t need to be maintained for the length of time that

children and infants and you’re coming into the clinic every

adds costs for people with jobs and/or without transportation.
Beyond daily visits, federal regulations also require fre-
quent toxicology screening (at least eight times per year) for
medication-assisted treatment with methadone and advise
frequent screening for buprenorphine (SAMHSA 2021a).
Twenty-six states require more than eight screenings (Pew
2022), and courts that mandate treatment may require more
testing. Toxicology screening can be beneficial. One provider
explained that some clients like “the accountability piece,
and they like seeing their results” (OP30). Providers believe
when tests are used to determine treatment, they may be ef-
fective. But the number of tests they are required to perform
is often needless. One provider summed up how the courts
(for mandated clients) and agencies (e.g., for housing) re-
quest frequent toxicology screenings, yet “they have no clue
what the results mean many times. . . .[T]hey requested us
to do toxicology once a week, and I can do a toxicology five
times a week. It’s not going to change what I do. . . . [C]
ertainly it’s beneficial early on [in] treatment that it needs
to be done more frequently. But, you know, it needs to be
used as a clinical tool, not a punitive tool. And I think we’ve
been using it as a punitive tool” (OTP72; also HR61, OP27).
Toxicology screenings add compliance costs for individuals
seeking treatment because they have to come in person to be
tested and psychological costs for treating people punitively,
limiting their autonomy and stigmatizing their medical care.
Policies regulating medication distribution and drug
screenings create burdens on clients. They add compliance
costs that make services more difficult to get and include
psychological costs through stigma and loss of autonomy.
Although these micro-level client burdens make services more
challenging, they are a small portion of the overall effect that
burdens have on clients. In addition to micro-level burdens
on clients, there are also meso-level burdens on providers that
affect quality and accessibility of services for clients.
Meso-level Burdens on Providers and Trickle-Down
Burdens to Clients

focuses narrowly on burdens on clients, federal and state
policies burden providers too. With their origins in the

providers alone. Yet they also affect the quality and acces-
sibility of services providers offer and often trickle down
to become burdens on clients, adding compliance costs be-
cause of reduced services offered, increased wait times to get
services, and longer travel distances to receive services. In

Service provision includes treatment protocols that
are burdensome to providers. Strict regulations around
medication-assisted treatment and toxicology are time-con-

services that clients may need. While self-reporting may
be considered an accurate measure of drug consumption
(Shadloo et al. 2022; but see Folk et al. 2022), testing adds
compliance costs for providers, who feel they “shouldn’t
have to absorb the cost for drug screening,” which is “such
a big expense” (OTP75). Another provider echoed the senti-
ment, noting they “spend more on toxicology than we do on
treatment” (OTP74).
In addition to compliance costs of screening, these
regulations limit discretion, and add psychological costs of
stigma and loss of autonomy for providers. One provider
explained: “I think the. . .push for us to do testing has less
to do . . .with whether or not they believe the patient. It’s re-
ally how much they respect the opinion or the information
provided by the clinician. So I think that stigma follows not
only the clients and patients that we see, but I think they
don’t always believe us” (OP28). Even though providers are
professionals with extensive backgrounds in addiction serv-
ices, they do not feel they are treated that way. For providers,
tight regulations remove clinical discretion and trust,
replacing these with logistically difficult and stigmatizing
procedures.
Burdens of service provision also include provider author-
ization, or the rules and regulations around who can provide
services, under what conditions, and to how many people.
Although many people are authorized to prescribe opioids,
prior to 2023, only federally waivered medical personnel
could prescribe buprenorphine (21 U.S.C. § 823) and only
to a limited number of patients: 30, 100, or 275 depending
on the waiver (21 U.S.C. § 823). Under the Drug Addiction
Treatment Act of 2000 (DATA 2000), doctors could obtain a
waiver to prescribe buprenorphine with an additional 8 h of
training, while the Comprehensive Addiction and Recovery
Act of 2016 (CARA) expanded the waiver to nurses and
physician assistants with 24 h of training.1 Applying for a
waiver and requiring training create barriers for medical
personnel, making it harder to provide treatment. As a re-
sult, there are not enough professionals able to provide
medication-assisted treatment (SAMHSA 2020) and those
who do rarely prescribe to the maximum number of patients
(Thomas et al. 2017). “[W]hen you think about that from a
chronic disease perspective,” said one provider, “why would
we ever put something as the DATA 2000 waiver or the
CARA waiver in place to prevent health care professionals
from taking care of people in the best way we know how?”
He proceeded to describe the waivers as “the biggest bar-
rier” to primary care physicians “having the time” and
ability to treat patients “simply because they’re not gonna
dedicate that time to doing that. They’re not gonna go
and take all this required education. They’re not gonna
limit the number of people in their practice that they can
treat” (IT54). Because waivers were so burdensome to ob-
tain, fewer doctors were willing to invest the time. Another
provider explained, “When providers are so restricted, you
think they’re going to go out of their way unless they’re re-
ally mission driven” to obtain a waiver (IT197)? As a result,
fewer medical personnel were willing to get waivered, lim-
iting the services professionals provide and making it diffi-
cult for clients to access treatment.2

limit on the number of new facilities (Indiana) and a mor-

Because state rules dictate the type of staff required, when those
staff people are not available, facilities must limit the services
they provide even when there is room in the building and other
types of staff members available. As a result, providers create
waiting lists for clients, making it more difficult or even impos-
sible for some people who want medical care to receive it.
Furthermore, the state puts limits on how much providers can
pay staff and locks them into funding lines. Providers cannot
offer competitive salaries or increases. One explained, “we can’t
give them any more money because we’re sold into these lines,
salary lines, through OASAS. We can’t give people increases, so
we can’t keep staff. So the turnover is pretty high” (IT165).
Providers are losing their staff to government, hospitals,
and the service sector, which pay better. One provider noted,
“it’s hard to attract and retain staff” (B52) while another
explained, “I’m not losing those staff to another provider. I’m
losing them to like Best Buy, or someplace where they say, ‘I’m
not going to do this anymore. I’m not going to get paid. I can
get paid the same money and not get spit on or told I’m, you
know, a terrible person’” (B57). Perhaps unsurprisingly, given
these conditions, there is a nationwide shortage of more than
two million SUD professionals (SAMHSA 2020). For clients,
staff shortages make it hard to access services. As one health
provider noted, “You’re trying to call for a bed, there’s not
a nurse there, there’s not a doctor there. So you finally press
every button that you can to talk to someone, and they say,
‘Oh well, no, the nurse isn’t here, she’ll be in on Saturday’”
(IT24). If third-party organizations cannot find and retain
staff, they have to cut back on the quality of the services they
offer, on the scope of services they offer, and the hours serv-
ices are offered to the detriment of clients who want help.
In addition to staffing, there are also barriers to consistent,
predictable funding, which affects the quality of care clients re-
ceive. Providers describe the burden of trying to run a business
on the state’s deficit funding model, where the state provides
the last dollar needed. Under this model, providers who ac-
cept state dollars must take clients regardless of their ability
to pay. It also means, however, that providers cannot innovate
or pivot during crises. Providers who “run large programs. .
.at the behest and benefit of the state. . .cannot generate any
type of surplus or cash reserves,” which makes them “incred-
ibly vulnerable to any type of interruption of revenue. . .any
type of unplanned costs” (R10). The deficit funding model
means that if providers outperform expectations, by increased
billing for insured patients or obtaining more grants, for ex-
ample, the state (counterintuitively) takes back funding from
them. As a result, it is difficult for providers to respond to
routine change, let alone crises like COVID-19 (B57). As one
provider commented, “There’s no other business that would
work that way. The margin is just too thin” (B15).
The state will not fund certain types of services and does
not give providers flexibility on moving funding from one
purpose to another. A provider explained the financial dif-
ficulty running integrated services when funding is overseen
by siloed agencies on individual funding lines. If one part of
the provider’s practice is losing money and another part is
exceeding targets, the state will say “‘Oh, . . .you used up
all your contract dollars and you have a deficit here, but
over here you’re underspent so we’re going to take another
150 this year. . .’ Every year that I excel and do better, every
year you shave away my contract dollars” (B57). Providers
cannot use the savings by doing better in one part of their
client-facing operations to subsidize another part of their
client-facing operations that is doing poorly. The result is that
the state creates inflexibility, which hampers innovation and
limits services for clients (B57).

the patients need (OTP72). According to one provider, if

providers offer. They cannot innovate to try new solutions;

Providers are also limited in how their facilities are designed,
affecting quality of care and access (the latter especially during
COVID). State agencies have regulations and guidance about
how facility spaces are configured: the number of doors,
the width of hallways, and the size of rooms. One provider
explained how they are supposed to provide “one-stop shop-
ping” but they still need to have separate entrances for clients.
“I know a provider who had to spend $200,000 redoing a
building to add a separate entrance,” he noted, “because that
was required between state agencies” (B52). Space guidelines
also specify how wide hallways must be, and the guidelines call
for much narrower hallways than traditional health facilities,
which is “not conducive to an appropriate healthcare environ-
ment” (R10). Space guidelines further hold there should be 80
square feet per bed in single room occupancy settings and 60
square feet per bed in shared rooms (OASAS Part 814). “I’ve
been in jails with bigger cells,” said one provider (IN178).
Space limitations affect the quality of services clients receive.
One provider noted, “It doesn’t give people enough room to
have a therapeutic living environment, and it creates a substan-
tial health threat of any type, whether we have the flu or the
common cold going around” (R10).
Inpatient facilities are “built on economies of scale” (R18),
often mirroring dormitory style rooms with two, three, four
or more people confined to small spaces. These tight spaces
put clients at increased risk during the COVID-19 pandemic.
“I don’t care what anybody says,” explained one provider,
“the residential system in New York State, and probably
in the entire country . . . is not set up for social distancing”
(IT185). During the pandemic, providers had to slow down
admissions sometimes by 50% or more, limiting access to
care for “people who are struggling in our community” (R13).
One provider described the effect: “we weren’t serving the
group of people that we would normally serve” (R15). Space
guidelines and social distancing requirements combined with
a public health crisis limited access to people who needed help
the most.
Meso-level burdens affect the services administrators pro-
vide and, at times, trickle down to become burdens on clients,
adding compliance costs that make services difficult to access.
Examining micro-level burdens alone obscures the true scope
and effect of burdens on clients.
Trickle-Down Burdens in the Submerged State:
Client Perspectives
Unlike providers, who could often name the particular policy
that created burdens, clients experience burdens but did
not necessarily distinguish between the source of burdens—
whether from federal or state policy, from the substance-use
agency or the mental health agency, or from the conflicting
rules between different levels of government or different
agencies. One notable exception was a parent who, like many
of the clients we spoke with, described how difficult it was
to get help. Yet unlike other clients or family members, this
mother tied the burden back to meso-level treatment protocols
that required physicians to have a waiver to provide medica-
tion and limited how many patients they could see.
The idea that whatever the medication is to help [these]
people, there’s only certain doctors that can do it. When
my son was prescribed Suboxone. . .the doctor was over
an hour away. Now meanwhile, he has no car, no way
to get there, so who has to take him? Me! Drive an hour
away so he can get his prescription for Suboxone and then
come back another hour. And all of the rules that he has
to go through to get that medication to survive, and. . .the
doctors were only allowed to have 150 patients, because
there was a limit (IT31).3
This mother understands that burdens trickle down to her
son and also, as his primary source of transportation, to her.
Yet even though federal and state rules like the DATA 2000
waiver were burdensome, the vast majority of clients did
not identify the particular source of burden. Instead, clients
recognize providers are “understaffed by far. So it’s not a
matter of they can’t do it. It’s when they can get to it to do
it” (C66). Clients note the difficulty accessing services during
times when they are in crisis, but they rarely talk about the
rules and regulations governing substance-use treatment
more broadly. In this way, providers serve as intermediaries
between government policies or programs and clients.

Providers and clients both experience burdens. But, unlike
providers, clients did not often identify the source of burdens,
and therefore, they did not see the state working through
third-party providers (see also Smith and Lipsky 1993). For
clients, it did not matter where the burden came from, only
that it existed. Trickle-down burdens, then, affect services that
third-party providers offer, prevent clients from getting help,
and obscure the government’s role in providing SUD services.
Discussion
What can we learn by examining administrators’ admin-
istrative burdens? In this article, we studied a subset of
administrators, third-party providers, in a single policy area,
SUD services, and our findings suggest contributions to the
literature to understand administrative burdens, the solutions
appropriate to address them, and the hidden politics of the
submerged state. Yet the focus on one type of administrator in
a single policy area to build theory also leaves open extensions
for future research.
First, we find that meso-level burdens affect the serv-
ices that providers offer. Previous literature demonstrates
how burdens affect what administrators do (e.g., Heinrich
et al. 2022) and their support for public policy (Burden et
al. 2012). Our study shows burdens related to federal and
state policies not only added costs for providers, they also
affected the quality and availability of services to clients.
While research shows that administrative burdens on
clients can make services difficult or impossible for them
to access, we find that burdens on providers affect services
and can even trickle down to become burdens on clients,
exacerbating the burdens clients face and achieving the
same poor result. Our analysis of a single human-services
policy area addressing a marginalized population is sugges-
tive for similar kinds of human services policies that are
underfunded, overburdened, and reliant on third parties
(e.g., Barnes 2021; Willison 2021; Willison et al. 2021). Yet
the theory we have developed here is not limited to these
types of policies. Future research can examine the distri-
bution of burdens and benefits (Schneider and Ingram
1993) across policy areas: Do policies targeting advantaged
populations that rely on third-party providers similarly
have trickle-down burdens or even trickle-down benefits?
Research can also look at the difference between public-
and private-sector administrators: Do public policies simi-
larly burden public administrators, and what is the effect on
the services they provide?
Second, the difficulties clients have accessing SUD services
are well known, including lack of treatment facilities, having
to travel long distances, and inability or unwillingness to leave
children (Henrich et al. 2022). While client-specific solutions
to address administrative burdens commonly studied in the
literature—such as more information, simplified procedures,
minor assistance or even increased transportation support
(Heinrich et al. 2022)—are welcome, they are not the only
reasons that clients are unable to access SUD services. Instead,
meso-level administrator burdens create costs for providers,
affecting the quality of services and leading providers to limit
the hours they are open, reduce the number of clients they
serve, and decide that services are too cumbersome to provide
in some communities. The result is that clients do not receive
the highest standard of care and they have difficulty accessing
services when and where they need them.
To lighten the effect of burdens on clients, policymakers
may need to adjust the burdens placed on administrators. It
is a fine balance between policies that are designed to keep
clients safe and the burdens policies create that make it hard
for individuals to get the care they need when they have the
fewest resources to navigate them. In the case of SUD services,
protecting a marginalized and stigmatized population must
be weighed against what happens when quality of care suffers
or that population finds burdens difficult to surmount or is
actively turned away because the stakes are so high (see also
Heinrich 2016; Willison 2021). The effect of burdens is po-
tentially compounded in human services—like drug policy—
that depend disproportionately on third-party providers,
who may opt out from participating in public programs by
choice—because they can—or by necessity—because they
cannot meet the financial and reporting requirements re-
quired. Future research can further examine how policies that
reduce burdens on administrators affect clients. For example,
does increased staffing/funding or reduced administrative
reporting requirements lead to better services and reduced
burdens for clients? If so, under what circumstances is it more
effective to target scarce resources to reduce administrator
burdens or to reduce client burdens? Our study examined
the burdens originating in macro-level public policies, but
third-party agencies (hospitals, treatment providers, and
social service agencies) have their own potentially burden-
some rules they impose on top of federal and state policies.
Future research can examine third-party providers not only as
mediators between macro-level policy and micro-level client
experience but also as the source of administrative burdens
on clients (e.g., Barnes 2021; Brodkin 2007, 14).
Third, if existing research has documented administrative
burden as a site of hidden politics, administrative burdens in
the submerged state create politics that are doubly hidden.
Policymakers have two levers they can pull when they engage
in hidden politics. They can add/remove burdens on clients,
such as lengthy applications, extensive documentation, and
in-person interviews (Moynihan, Herd, and Rigby 2016), or
they can add/remove burdens on providers, such as onerous

billing requirements that are expensive for nonprofits to ad-
minister and low reimbursement rates that make it not worth
providers’ time to participate (Willison et al. 2021). Increasing
burdens on either clients or providers may lead to the same
result, fewer people accessing services, even if the pathway
burdens take is different.
The literature on hidden politics suggests that policymakers
are strategic in using burdens rather than more visible means
to address a policy (Herd and Moynihan 2018; Moynihan,
Herd, and Harvey 2015; Moynihan, Herd, and Rigby 2016).
Burdens may also be the result of benign neglect (Herd and
Moynihan 2018; Moynihan and Herd 2010; Peeters 2020).
Policymakers may be unaware how provider burdens af-
fect clients, and this, too, creates a form of hidden politics.
Policymakers can look at services and see no obvious barriers
preventing individuals from accessing them and blame clients,
branding them untruthful or unmotivated to access services.
For example, New York State eliminated many client-specific
burdens in SUD services, forbidding insurance companies
from requiring preauthorization for treatment and expanding
Medicaid so that no New Yorker can be denied treatment
because of inability to pay. As a result, some policymakers
believed that individuals who claimed to want services, but
were not receiving them, did not know how to access treat-
ment or were not being truthful in their efforts (Strach, Zuber,
and Pérez-Chiqués 2020).
The submerged state literature shows how the hidden na-
ture of services attenuates the relationship between govern-
ment and the people receiving services. As we found, most
clients know they experience burdens even when they do not
know where that burden comes from. Clients of programs
administered in the submerged state may not even know they
are participating in a public program at all. Although much
of the submerged state literature looks at the positive views
of government that individuals might have if they knew the
services that government provides that are often obscured
through policy design (e.g., Buell, Porter, and Norton 2021),
we show how there may be additional negative views of gov-
ernment performance in the third-party implementation of
public policies. Because the true nature of governments’ in-
volvement in SUD services is not readily apparent, individuals
do not necessarily hold government agencies and officials
accountable for things they should be held accountable for.
Understanding that hidden politics may take place at the
meso-level opens up opportunities to examine what types
of burdens policymakers place on providers, under what
circumstances, why, and with what effects. When do clients
name the source of the burden and understand who is respon-
sible for it? Under what circumstances would they be willing
to do something about it, and what do they do?
Finally, future research can examine the reasonableness of
burdens in substance-use disorder and other human services
provision. Although it is beyond the scope of the article to
analyze each of the burdens we have identified here, Doughty
and Baehler (2020) offer a five-point test to determine
whether burdens are acceptable or excessive that scholars can
apply in this and other areas. There may be good reasons the
governments have for policies that burden administrators and
clients, such as preventing diversion of medication, keeping
people in treatment safe, or providing services in a cost-ef-
fective manner. These reasons need to be weighed against
criticisms that the rules are unnecessarily bureaucratic,
harmful to clients, and in service to a broader racial agenda.
Madsen, Mikkelsen, and Moynihan (2022, 376) suggest that
researchers should “acknowledge the potential benefits of
frictions relative to their costs” to avoid blinding them “from
addressing frictions where benefits exist but do not exceed
costs or where costs more than justify the friction.”
Conclusion
Administrative burden research has demonstrated the effect
of micro-level burdens on clients. We find that providers face
significant administrative burdens resulting from federal and
state policy; that these burdens affect the quality of the serv-
ices they are able to offer; and, ultimately, that burdens on
providers can trickle down to become burdens on clients. Our
research has implications for how we understand adminis-
trative burdens, the solutions best suited to reducing them,
and the role of burdens as a form of hidden politics in the
submerged state.

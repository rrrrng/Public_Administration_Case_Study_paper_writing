Abstract
This study advances a deeper understanding of the antecedents and mediators of goal ambiguity within public organizations. Expanding upon
the established notion of the negative relationship between goal ambiguity and performance in public organizations, this study goes one step
further by exploring how exogenous shocks may exacerbate this adverse impact. Focusing on the unprecedented COVID-19 pandemic and
the subsequent responses taken by the South Korean government, this study aims to offer a comprehensive examination of the complex in­
terplay between organizational goals and performance during unforeseen disruptions. Drawing on data from the 2019 and 2020 Korean Public
Employee Viewpoint Survey with 6,552 respondents, this study employs a moderated multiple regression model to examine the moderating
role of exogenous shocks. The findings reveal that exogenous shocks can act as moderators, intensifying the detrimental effects of goal ambi­
guity on organizational performance. This study thus highlights the mutable nature of goals in public organizations, especially in times of crisis,
suggesting the necessity of understanding the intricate dynamics within a prevailing premise of the negative association of goal ambiguity and
organizational performance.
Key words: goal ambiguity; public management; organizational performance; South Korea; moderated multiple regression model.
Introduction
Grounded in a fundamental proposition of goal-setting
theory, which suggests that organizations’ goals significantly
contribute to organizational performance (Locke and Latham
1990, 2002), the importance of effective goal setting has
remained a major focus in the fields of public administration,
management, and political science across both public and pri­
vate sectors. In particular, the public sector is characterized by
its multiple, intangible, and vague goals compared to its pri­
vate counterparts (Boyne 2002; Rainey, Backoff, and Levine
1976), leading to increased attention within the realms of
public management on the role of set goals and their impact
on public organizations. A stream of existing research has
examined the impact of goal ambiguity or lack of goal clarity
in the public sector by investigating the influences of goal am­
biguity on organizational performance in general (e.g., Chun
and Rainey 2005a; Jung 2011), as well as in specific aspects,
including employees’ work motivation (e.g., Wright 2004),
public service motivation (e.g., Jung and Rainey 2011), job
satisfaction (e.g., Jung 2013; Wright and Pandey 2011), and
the turnover intention of public staff (e.g., Jung 2010, 2012).
While previous research has effectively emphasized the
role of goal ambiguity in the public sector by delineating the
distinctions between public and private organizations, com­
paratively less academic attention has been given to the dy­
namics of organizational goals, suggesting the possibility of
fluctuations in the degree of goal ambiguity and the resulting
organizational performance. An organizational goal is not
static but rather subject to various external influences that
can shape its formulation, prioritization, and modification
over time (Thompson and McEwen 1958). As Finer (1941)
notes, public administration primarily focuses on fulfilling
the expressed wants of the public, rather than their actual
needs. These wants can evolve over time and be influenced by
the environment surrounding the organizations. Unforeseen
exogenous shocks, such as natural disasters and economic
crises, can act as a crucial factor that perturbs the goals of
public organizations. Public policies and organizations face
challenges in adapting their goals in response to these shocks,
which necessitates a reassessment of which goals should be
prioritized, or whether additional goals should be incorpo­
rated along with the existing ones (Davidovitz, Cohen, and
Gofen 2021; Elston and Bel 2022). The increased multiplicity
of set goals implies greater organizational goal ambiguity
(OGA) caused by interpretational leeway, leading to negative
impacts on organizational performance (Chun and Rainey
2005a).
To address these issues, this study explores the effects of
goal ambiguity on organizational performance and how this
relationship is influenced by exogenous shocks, with a specific
focus on the unprecedented COVID-19 pandemic and the or­
ganizational outcomes resulting from the corresponding gov­
ernmental responses in South Korea. South Korea has been
recognized for its effective and proactive response to the

Theoretical background
The influence of goals on organizational performance has been
a central research topic within the discipline of organizational
management since the seminal work of Locke in 1968, in which
the concept of goal setting emerged as a foundational predictor
of job performance (Locke and Latham 2019; Wood et al.
2013). In goal-setting theory, organizational goals are defined
as “a desired state of affairs which the organizational attempts
to realize” (Etzioni 1964, p. 6). Thus, if clear perceptions of the
organizational goals are lacking, the possibility that members
experience confusion and misunderstand the envisioned future
state of their organization may increase. Conversely, those with
specific and challenging goals may outperform those with sub­
jectively interpretable goals. Specifically, proponents of goal-
setting theory, such as Locke and Latham (2019), insist that
the effectiveness of goals in fostering high-performance hinges
on their specificity and level of difficulty. Notably, goal diffi­
culty has emerged as a condition directly linked to task perfor­
mance, whereas goal specificity alone may not necessarily lead
to heightened performance.
In contrast to scholars in goal-setting theory who regard
goal specificity as a requisite for task performance, researchers
in public management have shifted their focus toward the sig­
nificance of goal ambiguity motivated by the acknowledgment
that public agencies encounter distinctive challenges triggered
by organizational goals that are inherently vague, numerous,
difficult to quantify, and sometimes conflicting compared to
their private counterparts (Rainey 2010; Rainey, Backoff,
and Levine 1976). Thus, the crux of the impact of goals on
task performance lies in examining whether the inherent
ambiguity present in public organizational goals serves as a
foundational determinant contributing to detrimental effects
on organizational performance (Chun and Rainey 2005a,
2006; Rainey 2010; Van der Hoek, Groeneveld, and Kuipers
2018). This underscores the unique contextual considerations
and challenges inherent in the public sector, necessitating a
nuanced understanding of the interplay between goal charac­
teristics and organizational performance.
Goal ambiguity in public organizations
Early research on public management regarding the meas­
urement of perceived goal ambiguity stemmed from the
exploration of its distinctiveness from private organizations.
Emerging as outcomes of political compromises necessitated
by conflicting interests within the public sector, a prevailing
consensus exists that the goals of public organizations are
intricate in nature (Lindblom 2018; Wildavsky 2017), with
conspicuous degrees of ambiguity, multiplicity, and mutual
conflict presenting inevitable challenges for governments
(Pandey and Rainey 2006; Rainey 2010; Rainey and Bozeman
2000; Rainey, Backoff, and Levine 1976).
An example of this complexity is the provision of public
and quasi-public goods. Governments are often tasked with
addressing market failures through the provision of public
goods (e.g., infrastructure, national defense, and environ­
mental services) that are nonexcludable and nonrivalrous,
making them unsuitable for provision by the private market.
Similarly, governments provide quasi-public goods (e.g.,
public health, police services, and education), which do not
fully exhibit the characteristics of pure public goods but de­
liver substantial societal benefits and involve externalities
that justify public involvement (Schneider 2021; Ward and
Deren 1991). Unlike their private counterparts, which prima­
rily aim to maximize profits or achieve tangible performance
metrics such as sales and market share, public organizations
face with less straightforward metrics for evaluating the pro­
vision of public and quasi-public goods. Moreover, the strong
influence of government authorities and political stakeholders
constrains their autonomy, making it more difficult to estab­
lish clear and well-defined goals for these goods (Rainey and
Jung 2015).
However, there remains inconclusive empirical evidence
supporting the notion that public organizations actu­
ally possess more ambiguous goals (Rainey and Bozeman
2000; Rainey, Pandey, and Bozeman 1995). Scholars have
endeavored to advance novel approaches for measuring
goal ambiguity in public organizations, positing that the
discrepancies in perceived ambiguity may arise from method­
ological challenges. Previous studies relied heavily on surveys
administered by public managers to assess “subjective” goal
ambiguity, a method susceptible to inaccuracies due to po­
tential misunderstandings about questionnaires, individual
dispositions, and other influencing factors (Pandey and
Rainey 2006; Rainey 2010).
In response to the aforementioned challenges, Chun and
Rainey (2005b) sought to address the limitations associated
with subjective measures of goal ambiguity by introducing
“objective” measures, conceptualized as “leeway for inter­
pretation.” Building on Feldman’s (1989) conceptualization
of ambiguity in public organizational decision-making as
the “state of having many ways of thinking about the same
circumstances or phenomena” (p. 5), they emphasize that
OGA is contingent upon the potential for multiple, divergent
interpretations (Chun and Rainey 2005b; Rainey 2010).
Furthermore, they suggest four distinct dimensions of goal
ambiguity: communication related to the reason for the exist­
ence of an organization (Mission Comprehension Ambiguity,
MGA), guidance for organizational activities (Directive Goal
Ambiguity, DGA), assessment of organizational performance
(Evaluative Goal Ambiguity, EGA), and determination of
goal priorities (Priority Goal Ambiguity, PGA). Specifically,
MGA pertains to the level of interpretive leeway allowed in
comprehending and communicating the organization’s mis­
sion, measured using the Gunning Fog Index as a tool to

Goal Ambiguity, Exogenous Shock, and
Organizational Performance
In this study, we posit that the relationship between OGA
and performance moderated by exogenous shocks. The un­
derlying rationale for this proposition is discussed in the sub­
sequent sections.
Organizational performance as a consequence of
goal ambiguity
Early scholars who identified goal ambiguity as an inherent
characteristic of public organizations also recognized its po­
tentially beneficial effects on organizational performance. For
instance, Lindblom (1959) argued that in contexts where di­
verse public values or goals lead to conflicts among citizens,
legislators, and public administrators, the latter are often
compelled to refrain from clarifying or prioritizing goals due
to the absence of a definitive standard of correctness. Instead
of pursuing precise objectives, administrators may focus on
identifying provisional means and ends that retain political
support, thereby advancing them incrementally through ten­
tative and politically feasible steps (Lindblom 1959; Rainey
and Jung 2015). Moreover, subsequent studies have suggested
that goal ambiguity can have beneficial effects on organiza­
tional performance by enhancing responsiveness to external
stakeholders’ expectations and mitigating the dysfunctions
associated with inappropriate goal specification, particularly
in the absence of mechanisms for goal validation and quality
control (Davis and Stazyk 2015; Rainey and Jung 2015;
Stazyk and Goerdel 2011).
However, aligned with goal-setting theory (Locke and
Latham 1990), which posits that more specific goals are asso­
ciated with improved outcomes, conventional understanding
posits that ambiguous goals contribute to diminished perfor­
mance. Locke and Latham’s (1990) goal-setting theory is built
based on approximately 400 studies as an inductive theory.
Through several meta-analyses on the effects of goals on
task performance, they found that people with specific, chal­
lenging goals reliably outperform those with “do-your-best”
goals because the latter type is interpreted too subjectively.
Ambiguous goals can lead to confusion regarding expecta­
tions, which undermines employee motivation and commit­
ment. When employees lack clarity about what is expected,
their efforts may become unfocused and less effective.
Since the 1990s, empirical investigation on the impact of
goal ambiguity on public organizational performance has
garnered substantial scholarly attention, becoming a pivotal
area of focus for numerous research endeavors (Chun and
Rainey 2005a; Jung 2014a, 2014c; Moynihan 2015; Rainey
2010; Song, Meier, and Amirkhanyan 2020). A notable contri­
bution to this domain was made by Chun and Rainey (2005a),
who revealed significant correlations between OGA and the
overall performance of public organizations by analyzing data
from the 2000 US Federal Employee Survey. Recent research
has further supported these findings by demonstrating the im­
pact of alleviating goal ambiguity through precise goal speci­
fication on organizational commitment, job involvement, and
work satisfaction (Andrews and Mostafa 2019; Jung 2014a,
2014c; Jung and Ritz 2014; Pandey and Wright 2006; Song,
Meier, and Amirkhanyan 2020).
Based on these scholarly efforts, a fundamental and widely
accepted belief in public administration is that the inherent
ambiguity in public organizational goals is a major factor
contributing to adverse effects on organizational performance
(Rainey 2010). While a subset of studies has raised concerns
that overly clarified or narrowly defined goals may some­
times result in dysfunctional efforts (e.g., Rainey and Jung
2015), it is generally acknowledged that, when goals are suf­
ficiently feasible and reasonable, clear organizational goals
tend to enhance performance (Locke and Latham 1990). This
perspective suggests an association between goal ambiguity
and weaker performance, leading us to propose the following
hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1: A negative relationship exists between organ­
izational goal ambiguity and organizational performance.
A fundamental premise associated with goal ambiguity in
public organizations is the widely held belief that the inherent
ambiguity in public organizational goals is a major factor
contributing to adverse effects on organizational performance
(Rainey 2010).
Moderating effects of exogenous shocks on the
goal ambiguity and organizational performance
relationship
Building on both theoretical and empirical expectations that
underscore the negative influence of goal ambiguity on organ­
izational performance, this study seeks to enhance our under­
standing of this relationship by incorporating the potential
moderating role of exogenous shocks. Exogenous shocks such
as wars, natural disasters, financial crises, and pandemics like
COVID-19, can be potent drivers of goal ambiguity in public
organizations. These shocks can trigger rapid and substantial
policy adjustments (Van der Heijden 2013), while simulta­
neously disrupting established standard operating procedures
for the delivery of public services (Shi et al. 2020).
As policies overlap and evolve rapidly, knowledge becomes
ephemeral, and the parameters of “reasonable” behavior be­
come increasingly ambiguous, subject to frequent redefinition
or lack of a clear definition altogether. In such an environ­
ment, public organizations struggle to identify best practices
due to the constant influx of new and poorly understood
goals emanating from decision-makers who are grappling
with limited data and uncertain responses to the shock (Shi
et al. 2020).
Furthermore, these unexpected events often trigger
disruptions, eroding service quality and hindering goal attain­
ment (Meier and O’Toole 2009). In response, public managers

The emphasis on exogenous shocks in this study aligns
with prior research emphasizing the significant influence
of external factors on the goal ambiguity of public organ­
izations. For example, Chun and Rainey (2005a, b) inves­
tigate how competing demands from constituencies and
external funding sources, such as financial publicness, af­
fect goal ambiguity. Similarly, Lee, Rainey, and Chun (2009)
provide compelling evidence that “salience” to external
stakeholders—defined as the degree of attention agencies
receive from the President, the Congress, and the media—
shapes the dimensions of goal ambiguity. These findings il­
lustrate the heightened susceptibility of public organizations
to external influences compared to private firms, stemming
from their institutional ties to governing entities, including
executive bodies, legislative institutions, and political
stakeholders (see also Davis and Stazyk 2015; Pandey and
Wright 2006; Rainey and Jung 2015).
Given these dynamics, it is reasonable to hypothesize that
government agencies’ goals would be especially sensitive to
a shock involving a public health crisis like COVID-19. For
instance, the South Korean government issued guidelines
to all public employees during the pandemic’s outbreak,
prioritizing health and safety above all other goals, thus
establishing a critical standard that superseded existing or­
ganizational objectives (Ministry of Personnel Management
2020). Public health emergencies impose not only immediate
operational disruptions but also rapidly shifting priorities
and overlapping mandates, further complicating goal clarity
and performance. Although empirical research examining
the moderating role of exogenous shocks in the relationship
between goal ambiguity and organizational performance re­
mains limited, the existing literature strongly suggests that
these shocks exacerbate the challenges of goal ambiguity in
public sector organizations. Consequently, we propose the
following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 2: Exogenous shocks exacerbate the negative
relationship between organizational goal ambiguity and or­
ganizational performance.
Methodology
Research area and period
This study explores South Korean public officials’ perceptions
of goal ambiguity and organizational performance during the
COVID-19 pandemic. South Korea reported its first COVID-
19 case on January 20, 2020, followed by its first COVID-19-
related death on February 20, 2020. The nation’s response
to the outbreak has been globally recognized as a model of
effective crisis management, inspiring policymakers world­
wide to reassess their own strategies (You 2020). However,
this success came at a significant cost to the public officials
tasked with executing these measures, who faced immense
challenges and made substantial sacrifices to sustain govern­
ment functions.
The
pandemic
profoundly
altered
the
roles
and
responsibilities of public officials, particularly those on the
front lines, such as street-level bureaucrats in public health
centers. These officials were confronted with unprecedented
challenges for which they were unprepared, including
conducting epidemiological investigations, monitoring and
reporting infection trends, managing testing records, pro­
viding financial support for COVID-19 tests, supervising
quarantined individuals, and reporting virus-related deaths.
In response to these new demands, many officials worked
tirelessly around the clock just to address immediate needs,
founding themselves overwhelmed.
Beyond these operational challenges, public health officials
had to navigate significant administrative hurdles, such as
processing newly allocated budgets, adhering to burdensome
procedural requirements, and meeting urgent data submission
requests from higher-level agencies. Role assignments, partic­
ularly in infection control, were often vague and incomplete,
forcing many officials to juggle newly assigned pandemic-
related duties alongside their preexisting responsibilities.
This imbalance in workload distribution further strained the
system.
The excessive additional workloads led to severe burnout
among many public officials. Stress levels were exacerbated
by inadequate manpower, unclear quarantine guidelines, and
the inability to balance work and personal life (Son, Yang,
and Park 2021). For example, one public official designated
as a cohort quarantine manager worked an average of 76.6
overtime hours per month, totaling 460 overtime hours in a
year. Tragically, this individual succumbed to extreme stress
and took his own life in May 2021.
The challenges were not limited to frontline public health
officials; other public employees not directly involved in
pandemic control also reported increased workloads (Hur
et al. 2022; Oh 2020). Recognizing these excessive admin­
istrative burdens, the South Korean government issued pan­
demic guidelines prioritizing health and safety above all other
objectives. These guidelines established a critical standard
that temporarily superseded existing organizational goals, re­
flecting the extraordinary demands due to the crisis (Ministry
of Personnel Management 2020). This unique context in
South Korea offers an ideal setting for examining the effects
of an exogenous shock on the relationship between goal am­
biguity and organizational performance.
To conduct this analysis, the study compared the year 2019
(pre-COVID-19) with the year 2020 (COVID-19), thereby
avoiding the confounding effects of political transitions in
South Korea. The Park Geun-hye administration, which was
initially scheduled to govern from 2013 to 2018, ended pre­
maturely in 2017 due to her impeachment. The Moon Jae-in
administration subsequently began in May 2017 and con­
tinued until May 2022. The literature widely acknowledges
that presidential influence on public administration is not
consistent over time (Crockett 2008; Potter 2016). To min­
imize the impact of such transitions, this study focused on
the midterm period of the Moon Jae-in administration,
which provides a politically stable context for evaluating
the relationship between goal ambiguity and organizational
performance.

Data and methods
To test the hypotheses, this study collected data from the
Korean Public Employee Viewpoint Survey (KPEV) for
the years 2019 and 2020, aiming to elucidate the impact of
the COVID-19 pandemic that commenced in December 2019.
The KPEV is an annual survey of public employees carried
out by the Korea Institute of Public Administration, which is a
national research institute established by Korean government,
and the results are utilized as a national statistic accredited by
South Korea’s National Statistical Office. Respondents for this
survey are public employees working in 46 central government
agencies and 17 regional metropolitan governments across
South Korea and a stratified sample of public employees was
asked about their perceptions and experiences on motivation,
leadership, work attitude, and job characteristics.
The 2019 data set contained 4,111 public employees while
that of 2020 contained 4,339 public employees. To analyze
the effects of goal ambiguity and the COVID-19 crisis as an
exogenous shock on public employees’ perception of organi­
zational performance using the two data sets, we preprocessed
the data using propensity score matching. Propensity score
matching is a statistical technique used to reduce selection bias
in observation studies. It works by estimating the conditional
probability—known as the propensity score—that a unit
receives a treatment based on observed characteristics. Units
with similar propensity scores from the treatment and com­
parison groups are then matched, creating a more balanced
comparison that helps control for confounding variables (Bai
2011). A comprehensive discussion of this methodology can
be found in Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983).
To address the confounding effects associated with
estimating treatment effects—specifically the impact of
COVID-19 in this study—this method was employed to en­
sure comparability between data sets containing different
individuals. By applying this approach, potential effects
arising from sample differences were controlled, making the
data sets more comparable (McFadden et al. 2022). To mini­
mize the influence of sample differences, the matching process
considered covariates such as gender, age, work experience,
and responsibility level as a public employee. Of the original
sample of 8,450 respondents before the matching, 1,898 (835
respondents from the 2019 data set and 1,063 respondents
from the 2020 data set) were excluded after the matching.
After removing the unmatched cases, the final sample size was
6,552 (3,276 respondents in each data set).
Based on the data selected after the propensity score
matching, we applied the Moderated Multiple Regression
model (MMR) to examine the moderating impact of
COVID-19 on public employees’ perceptions of OGA and
organizational performance. MMR is a well-established and
representative method for scrutinizing hypotheses related to
moderating effects (Aguinis et al. 2005).
Measures
Dependent variable: organizational performance perception.
This study measured organizational performance using
perceived performance as a proxy. While perceptions may not
always perfectly align with actual performance, they serve as
meaningful indicators of organizational effectiveness, partic­
ularly in contexts characterized by goal ambiguity. Numerous
prior studies in public administration research have estab­
lished perceived performance as a reliable proxy for actual
performance, emphasizing that perceptions significantly
influence organizational behavior, decision-making, and
outcomes (e.g., Choi and Rainey 2010; Moynihan and Pandey
2007; Pandey and Rainey 2006). The organizational perfor­
mance perception (OPP) as a dependent variable is measured
from three items in the KPEV: (1) “The organizational per­
formance has steadily improved”; (2) “The quality of organ­
izational performance has improved”; (3) “The expenditure
of the organization has reduced” (Cronbach’s alpha = .874).
All items measured as a 5-point Likert scale anchored from 1
(“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).
Independent variable: OGA.
The independent variable, OGA, was derived from four items
within the KPEV, following the framework established by
Chun and Rainey (2005b). Despite Chun and Rainey’s de­
velopment of the four objective OGA measures, Pandey and
Rainey’s (2006) recommendation to explore the relationships
between objective and subjective measures was heeded.
Consequently, while retaining the core concept of the meas­
ures, the OGA variable was adapted as a subjective measure
in line with subsequent studies (e.g., Jung 2014c; Song, Meier,
and Amirkhanyan 2020).
This study chose the items in the KPEV based on the
four dimensions suggested by Chun and Rainey (2005b):
(1) MGA: “I have clear understanding of the organization’s
goal”; (2) PGA: “The priorities among the organizational
goals are clear”; (3) DGA: “The organizational goals pro­
vide clear guideline for carrying out my duties”; (4) EGA: “I
can objectively evaluate the organizational goal attainments”
(Cronbach’s alpha = .877). All items were measured on
a 5-point Likert scale which had values ranging from 1
(“strongly agree”) to 5 (“strongly disagree”).
Moderating variable: COVID-19 as an exogenous shock.
The COVID-19 crisis is a notable example of an exogenous
shock within the realm of public administration and manage­
ment. It is approached as a turbulent problem, characterized
by its surprising, unpredictable, and uncertain nature, that
significantly impacts public management dynamics (Ansell,
Sørensen, and Torfing 2021). This study introduced a binary
variable representing the COVID-19 crisis, with the year 2019
denoted as pre-COVID-19 (0) and the year 2020 as post-
COVID-19 (1), serving as a proxy for the exogenous shock.
Control variables: age, gender, education, tenure, job rank,
organizational type.
Several demographic characteristics were controlled for re­
ducing their potential impacts on OPP including gender
(male = 0, female = 1), age (20–29 years = 1, 30–39 years = 2,
40–49 years = 3, 50 year or older = 4), work experience as
a public employee (less than 5 years = 5, 6–10 years = 2,
11–15 years = 3, 16–20 years = 4, 21–25 years = 5, more than
26 year = 6), responsibility level (level 1–4 = 1, level 5 = 2,
level 6–7 = 3, level 8–9 = 4), and education (high school = 1,
college = 2,
bachelor’s
degree = 3,
graduate
school = 4).
Additionally, we employed fixed effects of the affiliated agency
to control for a potential clustering effect within respondents
in an affiliated agency.

This study employed 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching
without replacement to ensure robust results. Propensity
score matching was conducted by estimating the propen­
sity score using a logistic regression with the covariates as
predictors and the time period (year 2019 = 0, year 2020 = 1)
as the outcome variable. The 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching
pairs each treated subject with the nearest comparator based
on propensity scores, making it one of the most commonly
used approaches in practice (Allan et al. 2020). This matching
ratio is preferred because matching a single treated unit to
multiple comparators can reduce precision by including sec­
ondary or tertiary matches of lower quality (Stuart 2010).
However, the 1:1 matching ratio carries a potential risk of
pairing a treated unit with a dissimilar comparator because
the matching continues even when the propensity scores show
substantial differences. This limitation highlights the impor­
tance of the maximum allowable distance between matched
subjects, commonly referred to as the caliper (Lunt 2014).
To enhance match quality in propensity score matching,
this study incorporated caliper width, a method used to im­
prove match quality in propensity score matching. Caliper
width refers to the maximum allowable difference in propen­
sity scores between matched units. A smaller caliper ensures
closer matches, reducing bias but potentially decreasing the
number of matched pairs. The choice of an appropriate cal­
iper depends on the relationship between the outcome vari­
able and the matching variables. A commonly recommended
threshold is 0.25 SD of the propensity score, as suggested by
Cochran and Rubin (1973), to achieve high-quality matches
(Raynor 1983).
This study used a caliper width of 0.01 SD to ensure ad­
equate balance on covariates. As shown in Table 1, the
differences in the covariates between the 2019 and 2020
data sets were not statistically significant after matching.
Furthermore, the covariates were well balanced, with all
standardized mean differences falling below 0.1, which is the
threshold commonly recommended to indicate sufficient bal­
ance (Stuart, Lee, and Leacy 2013; Zhang et al. 2019). These
results, presented in Table 2, suggest that the propensity score
model was appropriately specified.
A new data set for this study was determined with the
sample size of 6,552 after the matching, reduced from the
original sample of 8,450 respondents before the process.

dependent variables including before and after the matching.
The latent variables, OPP and OGA, were measured using a
summed average for each variable, ranging from 0 to 5.

OGA 4-item index. The results indicated that discriminant
validity for the latent variables exists: the root mean square
error of approximations (RMSEA) 0.086 (where values close

good fit); the goodness-of-fit index (GFI) was 0.998 (where
values close to 1 indicate a very good fit); Turker–Lewis index
(TLI) was 0.997 (where values close to 1 indicate a very good
fit). Cronbach’s alpha was applied to test the internal consist­
ency of the latent variables and results; the scores were above
0.8 indicating high reliability of the items for the OPP 3-item
index and OGA 4-item index.

The results indicate a negative impact of COVID-19 on the
relationship between PGA and OPP, influencing the level of
interpretive leeway in determining priorities among multiple
goals. Public organizations typically establish specific goals
over defined periods, such as every 1 or 5 years. However,
the advent of the pandemic introduced a new goal for or­
ganizations: to overcome the crisis (Rasul 2020). In line with
the argument presented by Chun and Rainey (2005b) that
an increased number of goals amplifies the ambiguity of goal
priorities, the emergence of COVID-19 as a new goal across
the public sector implies that this unexpected exogenous
event might exacerbate the relationship.
Furthermore, the findings suggest that the COVID-19 out­
break has reinforced the negative relationship between DGA
and OPP, influencing the interpretive leeway in translating
directives and guidelines for achieving goals. The pan­
demic, characterized as an exogenous shock, introduced
volatility, uncertainty, complexity, and ambiguity into or­
ganizational contexts (Murugan et al. 2020). To address
this crisis, governments worldwide have been compelled to
issue various guidelines aligned with the advisories of the
World Health Organization Given the crisis-driven nature
of decision-making, which often relies on intuition rather
than evidence (Ioannidis 2020; Miller 2018), the standards
or guidelines for managing the crisis inherently involve un­
certainty. This uncertainty necessitates administrative dis­
cretion for street-level bureaucrats (Fowler 2023), and some
individuals may not adhere to frequently changing rules due
to a lack of clear messaging and guidelines (Burton et al.
2023; SoleimanvandiAzar et al. 2021).
Nonetheless, the coefficients associated with the moderating
effects of MGA and EGA fail to attain statistical significance
(Model 2: β = −.01 at P = .510, Model 5: β = −.04 at P = .052).
These nonsignificant findings may potentially be attributed to
temporal dynamics. This study focuses on the immediate effects
of goal ambiguity in the context of an abrupt exogenous event.
Consequently, it is challenging to anticipate the impact of the
COVID-19 crisis on both MGA and EGA. MGA pertains to
the extent of interpretive latitude inherent in an organizational
mission, encompassing the faculties of comprehension, ex­
plication, and communication of the said mission. Likewise,
EGA encapsulates the degree of interpretive leeway embedded
in statements articulating organizational goals, specifically
in the context of evaluating progress toward the attainment
of missions (Chun and Rainey 2005b). Notably, the goals in
public organizations were established before the occurrence of
the crisis and remained unchanged during the research period.
Conclusion
Over the years, scholars in public administration have consist­
ently emphasized the distinct challenges posed by goal ambi­
guity in public organizations. These studies have shed light on
the causes and consequences of goal ambiguity, offering val­
uable conceptual frameworks and empirical findings that un­
derscore its significant impact on organizational performance.
However, as research on this topic has progressed, questions
remain regarding how goal ambiguity operates in varying
contexts and under changing conditions. This study aims to
contribute to this evolving body of knowledge by addressing
these gaps and advancing our understanding of goal ambi­
guity in public sector organizations. In particular, by using
data from a large-scale survey of South Korean government
officials, this study provides an opportunity to examine goal
ambiguity in a national context beyond the predominantly
studied US and European cases. The findings suggest that the
measures and theoretical constructs of goal ambiguity devel­
oped in prior research are applicable across diverse institu­
tional and cultural contexts, reinforcing the relevance of this
framework in understanding the challenges faced by public
organizations globally. By demonstrating that goal ambiguity
and its implications are not confined to specific settings, this
study contributes to a growing body of research seeking to
explore the universal nature of goal ambiguity and its effects
on organizational performance.
This study verifies the moderating effect of COVID-19 as
an exogenous unexpected sudden shock on the relationship
between OGA and organizational performance. The findings
from this study are expected to contribute to the empirical
research on goal ambiguity in the field of public adminis­
tration and management in several significant ways. First,
it advances our understanding of the dynamics of goal am­
biguity by adopting a multiyear analysis approach. Most
previous studies on this topic have employed a single-year
analysis, overlooking the changes in goal ambiguity over time
(e.g., Andrews and Mostafa 2019; Chun and Rainey 2005a;
Jung 2014a, 2014b; Song, Meier, and Amirkhanyan 2020).
This study examines the temporal effects of goal ambiguity
by comparing the periods before and during the exogenous
shock of the COVID-19 pandemic.
Furthermore, although theoretical attention to goal ambi­
guity has persisted for a considerable period of time, empir­
ical research in this area has been relatively limited. Despite
the growing body of empirical research examining the prop­
osition that goal ambiguity has a detrimental effect on or­
ganizational performance, most studies have focused on cases
within European countries and the United States, with lim­
ited research conducted in other regions (e.g., Andrews and
Mostafa 2019; Chun and Rainey 2005a; Jung 2014a, 2014b;
Jung and Ritz 2014; Song, Meier, and Amirkhanyan 2020;
Van der Hoek, Groeneveld, and Kuipers 2018). Therefore, the
analysis of the South Korean case has the potential to con­
tribute significantly to the expanding empirical evidence for
goal ambiguity theory.
This study also offers a more comprehensive and robust
approach for analyzing the relationship between OGA and
performance. To address the potential selection bias that may
arise from the dynamic analysis over multiple years, this study
utilizes propensity score matching. The inclusion of multiple
years in the analysis introduces the possibility of different
respondents in different time periods, which can lead to bi­
ased estimates of the relationships among the variables. By
applying propensity score matching in the multiple-year anal­
ysis, this study rigorously examines the relationship between
goal ambiguity and performance. This technique allows for
matching respondents across different time periods, effec­
tively reducing selection bias and accounting for the dynamic
nature of the variables involved (Bai 2011). This approach
enhances the validity and reliability of the findings and
provides a more comprehensive understanding of the rela­
tionship between goal ambiguity and performance over time.

Finally, this study introduces a novel approach for meas­
uring OGA. Traditionally, goal ambiguity has been measured
using two main approaches: the perceptual approach and the
objective approach. In the perceptual approach, goal ambi­
guity is measured through surveys based on the respondents’
perceptions (e.g., Campbell and Im 2016; Christensen et al.
2018; Moynihan 2015; Pandey and Wright 2006; Stazyk and
Goerdel 2011). On the other hand, the objective approach
measures goal ambiguity based on theoretical dimensions
using secondary data (e.g., Chun and Rainey 2005a; Jung
2014a, 2014b; Jung and Ritz 2014). The perceptual approach
has been criticized for its reliance on a limited set of survey
items that primarily capture subjective perceptions of goal
ambiguity. While these measures, such as those originally
developed by Rainey (1983), are widely used, the inherent
subjectivity associated with simple and direct questionnaires
constrains their ability to comprehensively assess the multi­
dimensional nature of goal ambiguity (Rainey and Bozeman
2000). In this study, we adopt the perceptual approach
but overcome its weaknesses by using items based on the
dimensions of the objective approach suggested by Chun
and Rainey (2005b). By integrating these two approaches,
this study improves the comprehensiveness the perceptual
measures and enhances the comparability of previous goal
ambiguity studies, offering an alternative methodology for
measuring future research in this area.

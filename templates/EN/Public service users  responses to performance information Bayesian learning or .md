Abstract
Although performance information is widely promoted to improve the accountability of public service provision, behavioral research has re­
vealed that motivated reasoning leads recipients to update their beliefs inaccurately. However, the reasoning processes of service users have
been largely neglected. We develop a theory of public service users’ motivated reasoning about performance information stemming from their
identification with the organization providing their services. We address a significant challenge to studying motivated reasoning—that widely
used existing research designs cannot rule out alternative cognitive explanations, especially Bayesian learning, such that existing findings could
be driven by strong prior beliefs rather than biased processing of new information. We use a research design incorporating Bayesian learning
as a benchmark to identify departures from accuracy motivated reasoning process. We assess the empirical implications of the theory using
a preregistered information provision experiment among parents with children using public schools. To assess their identity-based motivated
reasoning, we provide them with noisy, but true, performance information about their school. Overall, we find no evidence of directionally
motivated reasoning. Instead, parents change their beliefs in response to performance feedback in a way that largely reflects conservative
Bayesian learning. Performance reporting to service users is less vulnerable to motivational biases in this context than suggested by the general
literature on motivated reasoning. Furthermore, exploratory findings show that performance information can correct erroneous beliefs among
misinformed service users, suggesting that investment in reporting performance to service users is worthwhile to inform their beliefs and im­
prove accountability.
Key words: accountability; performance information; motivated reasoning; Bayesian learning.
Introduction
Disclosing information about the outcomes of public serv­
ices is promoted to improve the accountability of public
service providers to a range of stakeholders including citi­
zens, service users, public managers, and politicians (Behn
2003; Bovens 1998; James et al. 2020; Moynihan 2008; Van
De Walle and Van Dooren 2008). Informing stakeholders’
beliefs about performance enables public service providers
to be assessed—which is further facilitated by comparative
information about similar units (Barrows et al. 2016; James
2011; Olsen 2017). Informed recipients can more effec­
tively call providers to account, demanding explanations for
outcomes, requesting action, or sanctioning or rewarding
providers (Bovens 1998; James et al. 2020; Moynihan 2008).
Information is particularly important for the users of
public services. They need accurate beliefs to exercise
“voice” about performance (Dowding and John 2012;
Hirschman 1970) including praise or blame of providers,
and to inform voting where posts in provider organizations
are subject to election (Holbein 2016; Holbein and Hassell
2019). Information also helps users choose between service
providers where choice is available to them, including exit
from poorly performing providers (Dowding and John
2012; James et al. 2020). In aggregate, informed users’
service choices can further enhance accountability through
competitive pressures on providers to respond to users in
order to retain or expand usage (Chingos et al. 2012; Le
Grand 2007).
Disclosing performance information has, however, been
called into question because of motivational biases in how
information is interpreted and used (for reviews see Battaglio
et al. 2019; James et al. 2020). Motivated reasoning is evi­
dent such that, as well as a desire to arrive at accurate beliefs
about the state of the world (accuracy goals), reasoning can
be driven in a particular direction given ex ante directional
goals. These directional goals for reasoning arise because of
recipients’ desires to protect their salient identities, existing
beliefs, or values (Kahan 2016; Kunda 1990; Pennycook 2023;
Taber and Lodge 2006). Motivated reasoning is found for
citizens (Jilke 2018) and service users (Christensen 2018) as
well as public employees (Petersen et al. 2018) and politicians
(Baekgaard et al. 2019). Christensen (2018) identified users’
directional goals stemming from avoiding transaction costs
from redoing their choice of service provider. Service settings
of ongoing and close interaction between users and providers
further fuel motivated reasoning through feelings of attach­
ment and organizational identification with providers (Kahan
2016; Mael and Ashforth 1992). More generally, identity-
driven motivated reasoning among formal members of public
organizations makes them selectively skeptical toward neg­
ative information about their organization (Petersen et al.
2018). Highly attached service users may be unable to reason

These findings question the performance movement’s drive
to use information to enhance accountability. However,
motivated reasoning raises methodological challenges. Ziva
Kunda, in her seminal (1990) article, notes the problem “that
all research purported to demonstrate motivated reasoning
could be reinterpreted in entirely cognitive, non-motivational
terms” (p. 32). For instance, observing that Democrats and
Republicans have markedly different views after exposure to
the same information about policy performance could merely
reflect Bayesian updating from initially large differences
in prior beliefs rather than biased processing of the new
piece of information (Coppock 2023). Although studies of
motivated reasoning should be designed to rule out Bayesian
explanations of results this is rarely the case (Tappin et al.
2020). There is a need for methodological innovation in the
assessment of bias in performance information processing.
We develop a test of service users’ motivated reasoning in
their belief updating about service performance that is able
to rule out Bayesian updating as the underlying explanation
(Möbius et al. 2022; Zimmermann 2020). To do this, we in­
troduce and implement a novel Bayesian updating benchmark
in the study of public performance. In a, preregistered, exper­
iment we give service users true performance information and
examine their belief adjustment against a benchmark of un­
biased Bayesian updating computed for each participant. The
benchmark shows how each participant’s beliefs should re­
spond to the information if they are perfectly Bayesian—that
is, updating their beliefs according to Bayes’ Rule. This com­
putation is possible since we elicit participants’ prior beliefs
and because the performance information in the experiment
is assigned according to a probabilistic but known process;
essentially analogous to participants drawing a selection of
information from an urn containing multiple pieces of in­
formation (​Möbius et al. 2022; Zimmermann 2020).
Our study of motivated reasoning among service users
draws on the identity-protective cognition model (Kahan
2013, 2016; Kahan et al. 2017; Pennycook 2023). Users’
identifying with their service provider makes negative in­
formation about the provider seem inconvenient and dis­
turbing to them, causing such information to be treated
with asymmetric skepticism compared to positive informa­
tion (see also Petersen et al. 2018). We evaluate the empirical
implications of this model in three ways. First, we test for
overall asymmetric belief updating among service users to as­
sess selective skepticism toward negative feedback about their
own provider. Second, we test the moderating role of organ­
izational identification, which we measure in users directly
before the information intervention, to see if bias increases
with users’ identification with their provider. Third, to bol­
ster our findings, we test if these results are specific to service
users and not driven by general features of cognition in this
context; we repeat the experiment in a placebo version where
the performance information is related to a service provider
different to the respondent’s own provider.
The first section of this article draws out the implications
of identity-protective motivated reasoning for users of public
services and develops the identity-protective motivated rea­
soning model with its Bayesian benchmark. The second sec­
tion describes the research design and the empirical context
for the study in which we recruited 1,159 parents and carers
of children from 44 randomly sampled schools in Denmark.
We exposed these users to actual information about their
school’s relative performance and analyzed their belief adjust­
ment. The third section sets out the results which show that
motivated reasoning was not evident in these service users.
Instead, belief updating largely reflects conservative Bayesian
updating, both for users with high and low levels of organ­
izational identification, and for service users and nonusers
in the placebo version of the experiment. The final section
sets out limitations of the study and the implications of our
findings. Despite previous evidence of motivational biases
in reasoning about information, we find that performance
information systems can be highly effective in changing the
beliefs of public service users. Exploratory findings further
suggest that performance information has the potential to
“debias” beliefs that were erroneous at the baseline. We sug­
gest that regimes for reporting performance information can
be an important and effective part of the information en­
vironment of service users, helping them hold providers to
account.
Motivated reasoning about performance
information
Motivated reasoning is “a tendency to find arguments in
favor of conclusions we want to be believe to be stronger
than arguments for conclusions we do not want to believe”
(Kunda 1990). The theory comes from cognitive science and,
while not the only way of considering reasoning (see Clark
2013; Hawkins 2021), has provided valuable insights into
responses to information disclosing good or bad perfor­
mance—for instance, by members of the organization being
measured (e.g., Petersen et al. 2018). Motivated reasoning
theory distinguishes between two types of motivation in rea­
soning and the inherent tensions between them in many deci­
sion making contexts.
First, accuracy motivation in reasoning has the goal to
achieve approximately true beliefs about the world. Accuracy-
motivated reasoning is generally taken to be normatively
appropriate, and it is often modeled as a Bayesian learning
process (Hahn and Harris 2014). In this process, people are
receptive to new evidence, which leads them to adjust their
previously held beliefs (prior beliefs) to construct new and
updated beliefs (posterior beliefs) (Baron 2006). When rea­
soning is dominated by accuracy motives, beliefs incremen­
tally approach the true state of the world as additional pieces
of evidence accrue.
Second, and in contrast, “directional motivated” reasoning
entails processing information in order to arrive at specific
conclusions. In directional reasoning, people are motivated
to be more responsive to information guiding them in that
direction (Kunda 1990; Taber and Lodge 2006). For instance,
public employees may be more receptive to performance in­
formation about their organization showing positive rather
than negative performance (Petersen et al. 2018). Directional
reasoning can also be caused by avoiding threats to existing
beliefs, self-image or salient identities—especially organiza­
tional or political affiliations or vested interests (​Baekgaard
et al. 2019; Baekgaard and Serritzlew 2016; ​James and Van
Ryzin 2017; Petersen 2020; Taber and Lodge 2006). For ex­
ample, politically motivated reasoning about climate change
information comes from social identity, social consensus,

Motivated reasoning factors vary by context and

in particular contexts. Directional reasoning has been found
for public performance information across a range of actors
that suggest particular sources of motivated reasoning in this
context for politicians (e.g., Baekgaard et al. 2019; Lerusse
and Van de Walle 2022; Nielsen and Moynihan 2017), public
employees (e.g., Jakobsen and Petersen 2021; ​Petersen et al.
2018), citizens (e.g., James and Van Ryzin 2017), and service
users—in the case of university students (Christensen 2018).
Citizens’ motivated reasoning about public services has
primarily examined partisanship and political attitudes as
drivers of directional reasoning. Citizen-centered research has
examined assignment of responsibility and blame (​Jilke and
Baekgaard 2020; Porumbescu et al. 2022), citizen satisfac­
tion (​Jilke 2018), support for local environmental programs
(Deslatte 2019), ratings of the evidential strength of perfor­
mance information (​Baekgaard et al. 2020; ​James and Van
Ryzin 2017), and interpretations of performance information
about private compared to public organizations (​Baekgaard
and Serritzlew 2016) in research that has been replicated (​
Brogaard, Madsen, and Petersen 2024). Citizens have been
compared to politicians in their ability correctly to interpret
performance information (​Christensen and Moynihan 2020).
In a rare study of service users specifically, Christensen (2018)
studied regret avoidance motivated reasoning in relation to
goal (re)prioritization in response to hypothetical information
about poor performance of the service user’s chosen provider.
Motivated reasoning, however, has boundary conditions;
James and Van Ryzin (2017) found that citizens’ ratings of
the strength of performance information as evidence about
a public program in the US differed according to the party
political identification of those reasoning about information.
Those supporting a party generally hostile to the program
(Republicans) rated positive performance information less
strongly as evidence about its performance (​James and Van
Ryzin 2017). However, they also found that directional rea­
soning was reduced when people were encouraged to think
less about their political party identification and more about
their need for the policy program before processing the in­
formation. Moreover, individuals’ updating of beliefs re­
garding their own, personal, performance, has found mixed
results about responses to information (​Zimmermann 2020,
p. 349). This is somewhat surprising, given that personal per­
formance arguably is a more likely case of directional motives
than the organization-level performance typically studied in
public administration (Nielsen and Moynihan 2017; Petersen
et al. 2018; ​van der Voet 2023).
To help make sense of existing findings about motivated
reasoning we tackle three potential sources of inconsistency.
First, we study beliefs rather than evaluative attitudes, en­
abling a stronger test of the presence of bias, since we can
establish a clear benchmark (Bayesian learning) for belief
updating. Second, we build an identity-protective cognition
model of motivated reasoning grounded in the degree of or­
ganizational identification as an important moderator of
motivated reasoning (see also Petersen 2020; Petersen et al.
2018). Such moderators may drive some of the inconsistency
in previous findings (Kahan 2016; Kahan et al. 2017). Third,
we assess both users and nonusers—who we do not expect
to exhibit directional motives—to probe whether our results
are driven by status as service user or if they reflect general
features of cognition and belief updating.
To identify and gauge the impact of motivational bias, we
establish a Bayesian benchmark for belief updating at the
level of the individual service user (Hill 2017; Tappin et al.
2020). Despite a large existing literature on biases in public
administration (for reviews see Battaglio et al. 2019; ​James et
al. 2020), explicit use of Bayesian updating as a benchmark
has not been implemented in previous empirical studies. Some
partial exceptions include work applying a Bayesian frame­
work to build a theoretical model of managers’ identification
of performance gaps and subsequent prioritization (​Meier
et al. 2015). Andersen and Nielsen also applied a Bayesian
learning theory to study teachers’ responses to information
on student performance, assessing teachers’ prioritizing of ef­
fort in the light of test results (2020). This work, however, did
“not test the individual components of the model, that is, the
prior beliefs and the updating of beliefs based on new informa­
tion” (p. 429), and accordingly did not compute individual-
level learning benchmarks from Bayes rule1 (e.g., Möbius et
al. 2022; Zimmerman 2020). Thus, while Bayesian arguments
have entered the debate on behavioral public service perfor­
mance, motivated reasoning and other types of biased in­
formation processing have not previously been assessed with
explicit empirical reference to Bayesian learning. We adopt a
theory driven research design that allows us explicitly to in­
corporate Bayesian learning (​Eil and Rao 2011; Möbius et al.
2022; Zimmermann 2020).
Modeling service users’ reasoning in belief
adjustment from information
We set out a Bayesian learning process as a benchmark model
for unbiased responses to information. The benchmark model
assumes that service users utilize Bayes’ rule when adjusting
their factual beliefs in response to performance information
about their service provider2 (e.g., Eil and Rao 2011; Hill 2017;
Möbius et al. 2022; Zimmermann 2020). Beliefs are widely
researched, for example, standard economic theories under­
stand choices as a combination of four factors: preferences,
constraints, information, and beliefs, and studying the effects
of information provision on recipients’ beliefs is an impor­
tant topic (see ​Haaland, Roth and Wohlfart 2023). Focusing
directly on beliefs about performance gives a clearer measure
of belief adjustment than using proxies about beliefs such
as users’ evaluative attitudes (e.g., the satisfaction with the
provider) or their behavioral intentions (e.g., propensity to
exit the provider) (Andersen and Hjortskov 2016; ​Damgaard
and Nielsen 2020). Beliefs about performance, in turn, are a
component part of users’ attitudes and inform their decision

In accuracy-motivated updating, at the baseline, service
users’ prior performance-beliefs may be erroneous, but as
the service users encounter true information, their posterior
beliefs gradually approach the true state of the world—for
instance, in recognizing that a school ranks low nationally. A
way of modeling reasoning dominated by accuracy motives
is to assume it follows Bayes’ rule (Coppock 2023; Little
2022; Tappin et al. 2020). Hence, service users that are purely
accuracy motivated change their beliefs in the light of new
evidence in a Bayesian manner. Standard micro-economic
models generally assume Bayesian belief updating and thus
pure accuracy motivated reasoning is a standard assumption
in canonical rational choice models (e.g., Rabin 2002).
Accuracy-driven reasoning may, however, be supplanted
by directional motives resulting in an asymmetric, biased, re­
sponse to information. Our model of service users’ motivated
reasoning draws on the identity-protective cognition model
(Kahan 2016; ​Kahan et al. 2017). Individuals’ identities or
group loyalties—in our case as users of a specific service pro­
vider—constitute a salient bundle that generates directional
motives. Directional motives drive more credence in informa­
tion putting their affiliated organization in a positive light
compared to information showing their organization in a
negative light (​Eil and Rao 2011; Hill 2017; Möbius et al.
2022; Zimmermann 2020). Hence, positive information is
processed in a Bayesian manner, while negative information is
processed in a selectively more conservative Bayesian manner
reflecting selective skepticism toward negative information.
Negative information results in less belief adjustment than
predicted by Bayes’ Rule and also less belief adjustment than
would be the result of receiving positive information (Little
2022). Overall, the model holds that service users’ motivated
beliefs (overall beliefs) will be the result of a mixture of accu­
racy motives (prompting Bayesian reasoning) and directional
motives (prompting asymmetric conservatism).
We focus on organizational identification as a driver of
directional reasoning among service users. Here, identifica­
tion is broadly construed as a perception of oneness with
or belongingness to a group (​Mael and Ashforth 1992) and
negative information about the group is often seen as an
identity-threat to the individual group member (Bayes et al.
2020; ​Kahan 2016). Service users’ involvement in the pro­
vision of a service is an important driver of organizational
identification with the service provider and, in turn, identity-
protective-cognition. They engage in coproduction, defined as
“a mixing of produce efforts of regular producer [employees]
and consumer producers [service users]” (Parks et al. 1981,
p. 1002, words in square brackets added by us). For ex­
ample, in public schooling, this involvement includes helping
their child with their homework, interacting with the child
in ways that affect educational outcomes, or participating
in workdays at the school (Honingh et al. 2020). Poor per­
formance outcomes from coproduced public services reflect
negatively on the efforts of coproducing service users. This
reaction is similar to individual performance (​Köszegi 2006;
Zimmerman 2020) and employee’s responses to their own
organization’s performance (Petersen et al. 2018). Hence,
identity-protective directional motives limit service users’
ability to form accurate beliefs about their own service pro­
vider, leading to our first hypothesis:
H1: Service users adjust their beliefs about the performance
of their service provider asymmetrically with positive per­
formance feedback generating more belief adjustment than
negative performance feedback.
This primary hypothesis is an expectation of positivity bias
in the domain of identity-relevant performance feedback
(Möbius et al. 2022; Zimmermann 2020). At first sight,
this hypothesis may seem inconsistent with negativity bias
in human cognition with negative stimuli eliciting stronger
responses than positive stimuli across many—if not most—
settings (​Baumeister et al. 2001; Cobb and Kuklinski 1997; ​
Fiske 1980; ​Rozin and Royzman 2001). Negativity bias has
also been found specifically in citizens’ responses to perfor­
mance information (James 2011); as a factor affecting citi­
zens’ assessments of public organizations’ performance (​
van den Bekerom, van der Voet and Christensen 2021);
public managers’ responses to information (Hong 2019)
and politicians’ attribution of managers’ responsibility for
outcomes following the receipt of performance information (​
Nielsen and Moynihan 2017).
However, negativity bias should not necessarily be expected
in all contexts. Sometimes “positivity offsetting” can lead to
more positive than negative beliefs arising from information,
as has been found in non-partisan local governments for
some services (Deslatte 2020). Where service users respond
to information about their service, rather than citizens in
general, it is not obvious that reasoning will be dominated
by negativity (​Altay et al. 2023; Soroka and Krupnikov
2021). People tend to hold positive views about themselves
and their “in-groups” (​Bérnabou and Tirole 2002; Mercier
2017; Stanovich et al. 2013) and selectively disregard neg­
ative information that is self- or identity-relevant (Möbius
et al. 2022; Zimmermann 2020). More generally, citizens’
expectations have been found to be positive in a range of
policy domains (Osmundsen 2017; Soroka 2014) and people
are more likely to share positive information than negative
(Bright 2016; ​Stubbersfield et al. 2019). Similarly, public
managers are more inclined to broadcast positive relative to
negative performance information (Mikkelsen, Petersen, and
Bjørnholt 2022).
Misplaced optimism can also drive negativity bias through
disappointment from overly optimistic initial expectations
or prior beliefs, an apparent paradox Soroka summarizes
as “optimism (a positive reference point) is at the heart of
negativity bias” (2014, p. 17). From a Bayesian perspec­
tive, the apparent negativity bias is then an artifact of mis­
placed optimism—that is, biased priors—in a specific domain
(Niven 2000; ​Osmundsen 2017; ​Soroka 2014). We can as­
sess whether negativity bias persists when taking Bayesian
updating into account (i.e., how people should have updated
had they been Bayesian). Furthermore, in the case of finding
negativity bias, we can assess if it is driven by being selec­
tively conservative for positive information or is an over
adjustment to negative information (in comparison to the
Bayesian benchmark).

directionally motivated reasoning increases with the degree of
identification (Kahan 2016; Kahan et al. 2017). Service users’
identification varies according to the extent they engage in co­
production or connect themselves to the social groups associ­
ated with their provider. We measure service users’ organization

Previous studies have noted the need to study service users
specifically (Brown 2007; Christensen 2018; Damgaard and
Nielsen 2020), but the bulk of research has been on citizens
in general (e.g., ​Baekgaard et al. 2020; James 2011; Olsen
2017; ​van den Bekerom et al. 2021). Differences between
service users and citizens have been much less studied than
differences between citizens and politicians (Christensen and
Moynihan 2020). We compare users and nonusers to assess if
the results for our main hypothesis—expected asymmetrically
stronger responses to positive information—are specific to
service users’ responses to performance feedback about their
own provider (with which they identify) or if, alternatively,
the asymmetry is a more general phenomenon that emerges
in other situations as well—contexts where the identity-
protective motives are absent.
Because users’ directional reasoning comes from their iden­
tity as active use of a service we do not expect asymmetric
responses to performance feedback among individuals who
are not active users of the particular service provider they are
given information about. Thus, as a form of placebo test of
the empirical implications of the model, we formulate our last
hypothesis as an expectation of symmetric learning among
nonusers who are given information about a school that they
are not personally a user of. We thereby use this last hypothesis
to assess if any asymmetry identified in relation to H1 and H2
are caused by general features of cognition rather than being
a consequence of being a service user of a particular provider.
H3: Nonusers adjust their beliefs about the performance
of a service provider symmetrically with positive perfor­
mance feedback generating as much belief adjustment as
negative performance feedback.
Methods
We developed an experimental design with a learning task
focusing on changes in beliefs about service performance
in response to performance information to estimate direc­
tional tendencies in belief adjustment and to compare this
adjustment with a Bayesian benchmark. We preregistered
our hypotheses about the asymmetric impacts of positive
and negative performance feedback on beliefs (​https://doi.
org/10.17605/OSF.IO/2VZYX) and the study was approved
by the research ethics committee at the University of Southern
Denmark (case no. 21/24,565).
Our theoretical framework about learning from perfor­
mance information is general to all public service settings.
However, the directional goals should be most prevalent in
settings that encourage service users’ organizational identi­
fication with their provider, especially where: the interaction
between a service provider and users is of an intensity and
duration that can foster identification rather than a service
that a user only occasionally experiences; services have mean­
ingful opportunities for users to coproduce to foster a sense of
ownership for service outcomes; there is measurement of per­
formance and feedback is engaging and meaningful to service
users, for example, involving comparisons between providers.
Many public services occur in such contexts especially in
areas of health, education, and social care services.
The participants in the experiment were users of schools in
their roles as carers of children enrolled in local Danish public
schools. The children are enrolled in the year they become
6 years old. The service is ongoing and typically stays with
the same school for ten years throughout grades 0–9, after
which they can pursue upper secondary education or voca­
tional training. Parents are important coproducers and assist
reading and homework in general (Jakobsen 2013: Jakobsen
and Andersen 2013), affecting their children’s education in
the long term (Davis and Ostrom 1991; ​Honingh et al. 2020).
School performance information is available both about
their own school’s and other schools’ performance (Andersen
2008; ​Nielsen 2014), as is the case for many public services
across a wide set of jurisdictions (Behn 2003; James et al.
2020; Moynihan 2008; ​Van De Walle and Van Dooren 2008).
Experimental design and participants
We exogenously manipulate the positive or negative content
of information—that is, the information’s valence—in a field
context and do not need to resort to deceiving participants in
the experiment. Many previous studies estimate the heteroge­
nous effects of information by disclosing high versus low per­
formance for participants who are nested in organizational
units that have these different levels of performance (e.g.,
Hansen and Nielsen 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; van der Voet
2023). In these studies, organization-nested participants (e.g.,
employees) are randomly assigned to either information about
performance at the level of the organization (treatment group)
or no information (control group). The effect estimates from
organizations labeled by the researcher as “high” or “low”
performing are compared to identify valence-specific effects—
for instance, negativity bias (Nielsen and Baekgaard 2017) or
asymmetric acceptance (Petersen et al. 2018). However, be­
cause these designs do not randomize the valence of informa­
tion, they are threatened by confounding. How participants
respond to information in high and low performing organiza­
tions may be driven by other factors than the valence of the
information (Dafoe et al. 2018). For instance, if individuals
in low performing organizations are systematically less in­
formed about organizational performance ex ante compared
to individuals in high performing organizations, the news­
worthiness of the information is larger on average for low
performing organizations leading to a larger information ef­
fect for these participants, which could be misinterpreted as
a negativity bias (Haaland et al. 2023). In sum, the canonical
approach to studying performance information effects in field
contexts struggles to identify information effects driven by
valence since valence is not exogenously manipulated.
We resolved this methodological problem by devel­
oping an experimental design adapted from that utilized
by ​Zimmermann (2020). We elicited participants’ prior
performance-related beliefs, which enabled us to gauge sys­
tematic differences in prior beliefs between high- and low-
performing organizations (​Haaland et al. 2023). This first step
of the procedure controls for individuals’ prior beliefs. Causal
identification of valence is fully achieved in the next step,
where we randomized information valence for individuals
within the individual organizations using a feedback proce­
dure with noisy performance information.

Measures
Our dependent variable used measurements of a respondent’s
belief that a school ranks top 50 percent nationwide before
(prior beliefs) and after (posterior beliefs) seeing the perfor­
mance information feedback. In task 1 the school in question
was their own school, and in task 2 (for the nonuser con­
text) the school was another school. We elicited beliefs about
school ranking using an adapted and translated version of
the measure from Zimmermann (2020) through the question
“I think the probability that [school name] is ranked in the
upper (better) half nationwide is ….” Respondents answered
using a slider ranging from 0 to 100 with the descriptions:
“‘0’ means, that you are completely certain, that the school is
not ranked in the upper half of the 840 schools (i.e., ‘0 percent
chance’), while ‘100’ means, that you are completely certain,
that the school is ranked in the upper half of the 840 schools
(i.e., ‘100 percent chance’).”3
From these prior and posterior beliefs, we generated
the measure of belief adjustment in two steps. First, we
subtracted the prior from the posterior to get a raw measure
of the change in belief that the school ranked in the top 50
percent of schools nationwide. Here, the sign reflects the di­
rection of the belief change, with positive values representing
increased subjective certainty and negative values representing
decreased subjective certainty, where the difference from zero
represented the extent of belief adjustment. The distribution
of the data after the first step is depicted in figure 2 panel A
for the main experiment, which also indicates the directional
impact of negative feedback compared to positive feedback.
In standardized effect terms, this raw difference in group
means corresponds to a Cohen’s d of 0.95, 95% CI [0.83;
1.08]. This estimate should solely be interpreted in descriptive
terms and, also descriptively, we note that participants were
better informed after receiving the feedback despite its noisy
nature. Specifically, 50.7 percent had mostly correct beliefs
after seeing the noisy performance feedback about their own
school, compared to 45.3 percent before. Here “mostly cor­
rect beliefs” refer to parents (correctly) placing a greater than
50 percent probability of their school ranking in the top half
or bottom half. Most participants who held incorrect beliefs

We also elicited prior beliefs not only to generate the data
for the dependent variable, belief adjustment, but also to
calculate the Bayesian posterior—the adjustment each re­
spondent should have performed upon seeing the data had
their belief adjustment been fully Bayesian. To increase the
precision of this estimate, we also elicited subjective beliefs
for the school being ranked in specific quartiles (lowest quar­
tile, 2nd lowest quartile, 2nd highest quartile, highest quar­

being ranked top half, using an adapted version of the same
belief elicitation question. This allowed us to predict the
Bayesian posterior for both learning tasks (user and nonuser)
for each participant according to Bayes’ rule, on the basis of
their priors, the feedback they received, and the likelihood
of the feedback, which can be formally expressed as follows:

Discussion and conclusion
Motivated reasoning is less evident for service users’ responses
to performance feedback about their own service provider
than has been found in many other contexts for citizens, public
mangers, politicians, and public employees. Moreover, this re­
sult holds even for service users who identify strongly with
their provider. The findings suggest good prospects for public
service users to learn from information and to form accurate
beliefs about performance that help facilitate providers’ ac­
countability to users. The information is useful for their choice
of provider and to inform users’ voice to providers about

The findings are much more positive for accountability than

of studies about information processing by other groups—
where many studies have found biased responses to information
(Battaglio et al. 2019; James et al. 2020). Instead, users’ learning
in response to performance information approximates conserv­
ative Bayesian learning with them updating their beliefs—but
not as much as predicted by Bayesian learning. This finding
is in line with the broader literature on belief formation (Hill
2017; Möbius et al. 2022; Zimmerman 2020) and findings that
citizens change their perceptions of performance when given
performance information (​Baekgaard and Serritzlew 2016;
Barrows et al. 2016; James 2011; ​Olsen 2017; van den Bekerom
et al. 2021). The results are further consistent with effects of
performance reporting systems on frontline workers in schools
(Andersen and Nielsen 2020).
The difference from previous research that found motivated
reasoning seems explicable, at least in part, by differences
between types of recipients of performance information and
differences in their institutional contexts. For politicians,
Baekgaard et al. (2019) found not only motivated reasoning
but also that adding more evidence increased the influence of
prior attitudes on their reasoning. However, politicians are not
as directly affected by outcomes as users of a particular service.
Politicians also face pressures to create political narratives
about performance that force them to defend a coherent posi­
tion despite evidence about performance to the contrary.
For public employees, Petersen (2020) found that employees
receiving poor performance scores tended not to recall the score
given to them, indicating that they either ignored or dismissed
the information. The performance information may have been
perceived as being more directly about the workers themselves
than parents perceived their information as being about parents
in our case. Institutional contexts with very clear lines of func­
tional responsibility linking political principals with perfor­
mance outcomes may also generate a strong partisan lens for
citizens’ assessing performance, triggering partisan-motivated
reasoning (Jilke and Baekgaard 2020).
Our exploratory analysis suggests some negativity bias
in users’ responses to feedback about their own provider
which persists even when we control for how respondents
should have updated had they been Bayesian learners. This
finding is consistent with previous evidence about negativity
bias in responses to performance information (Battaglio et
al. 2019; Deslatte 2020; van den Bekerom et al. 2021). Our
Bayesian benchmark reveals that the apparent negativity bias
is not caused by an overreaction compared to what would
be predicted by Bayes’ rule. Instead, users’ responses to both
positive and negative feedback are conservative compared to
Bayes’ rule, but responses to negative feedback are somewhat
less conservative. Accordingly, previously documented nega­
tivity bias may be the result of selective conservativism rather
than selective overreactions. The drivers of conservativism—
both selective and general—is an interesting avenue of future
research. We do not find negativity bias when respondents
react to information about a different service provider to their
own (study 2). One potential explanation is that negative
stimuli increase attention (i.e., particular attention to nega­
tivity), but that the content of the stimuli must be sufficiently
salient before this mechanism is triggered—for instance, by
being about one’s own service provider (Pratto and John
1991; ​Rozin and Royzman 2001).
We find that although directional motives did not appear
to play a big role in the adjustment of beliefs within the
experiment, our participants’ prior beliefs were overly op­
timistic. This finding is in line with a broader literature on
misperceptions showing that although baseline beliefs are
erroneous—consistent with individual-level biased cogni­
tion—people tend to respond appropriately to correctional
information (Pennycook 2023; Tapping and Gadsby 2019).
Misperceptions are widespread across many areas of policy,
but rather than individual-level cognitive biases the litera­
ture has suggested that individuals’ surrounding information
environment may be the culprit (Pennycook 2023). Moving
beyond our study to the broader context of public services
and service users, managers may play an important role in
shaping such a biased information environment over time by
being more likely to share positive performance information
than negative, including to people beyond their organization’s
boundaries (Mikkelsen et al. 2022).

continuous, incremental, and ongoing process which gradually
shifts service users’ beliefs regardless of their level of organiza­
tional identification. However, the ways that users’ and others’
beliefs about performance emerge in the first place, and the
roles of public managers, frontline employees and the service
users themselves play in shaping the information environment
are important topics for future research. Relatedly, theories of
“active inference” focused on how humans and other biolog­
ical organisms interactively engage with their environments and
adaptively control their action-perception loops to solicit desired
sensory perceptions for particular purposes (​Parr, Pezzulo, and
Friston 2022) may be relevant. Reasoning about performance
information is influenced by broader purposes that may not al­
ways place a premium on forming accurate beliefs.
Future research should assess the generalizability of finding
across contexts both where users’ identification with their
providers is strong and where it is less evident. Where there is
only sporadic use of the service, or distance in interaction be­
tween user and provider, there may be less identification and
less potential for identity-based motivated reasoning in the first
place. Users may have less interest in paying attention to in­
formation in such contexts, especially for less salient services.
Where having inaccurate beliefs is less consequential, other
motives such as expressing a political identity may promote
motivated reasoning. This difference is consistent with James
and Van Ryzin’s (2017) finding that citizens primed to think
politically exhibited more partisan-motivated reasoning about
performance information than those primed to think about
their own need for services. Future research should consider dif­

informs users’ evaluative attitudes and, in turn, has behavioral
consequences including exit from service providers or voice
about performance, including voting (Dowding and John 2012;
Holbein 2016). However, future research should extend the
findings to examine these downstream outcomes. That there is
no directional processing at the first stage of belief formation is
important in itself and suggests that there should not be effects
that flow downstream. In this way, the findings generate re­
search questions for further empirical study of the consequences
of disclosing performance information for accountability.

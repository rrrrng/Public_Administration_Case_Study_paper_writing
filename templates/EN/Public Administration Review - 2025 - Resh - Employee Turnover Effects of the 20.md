ABSTRACT
As shutdowns have increased in frequency and length for the US federal government, such dysfunction may impact the federal
government's ability to retain talent. In December of 2018 through January of 2019, the US federal government and its work-
force endured the longest shutdown in its history. However, we know little about that shutdown's effects on the US federal civil
service labor market. In this paper, we examine the intervening impact of the 2018–2019 government shutdown on personnel
separations. Through the collection of data sources, we assess the shutdown's impact using two-­way fixed effects difference-­
in-­differences models of both whether an agency was shut down and whether the percentage of workers who were temporarily
furloughed in an agency could adequately capture the “intensity” of that shutdown. Results indicate that the 2019 shutdown im-
pacted separations and that it did so in distinct ways across different cohorts of employees in affected agencies. This work helps
inform those who study or practice strategic workforce development about the impacts of political events on labor dynamics.
1   |   Introduction
Over the last four decades, United States federal government
shutdowns have become progressively more numerous and
acute. These events entail the federal government curbing agency
activities, discontinuing non-­essential operations, furloughing
non-­essential workers, and maintaining essential functions. As
of January 2025, the United States has experienced 21 govern-
ment shutdowns. Notable examples occurred during President
Reagan's two administrations, including the 1981 shutdown
and the 1986 shutdown that furloughed around 500,000 federal
employees, and the 1995 to 1996 shutdown during President
Clinton's first administration, lasting 21 days. The most recent
shutdowns occurred during President Trump's administration,
with the latter one extending for 35 days from December 2018
through January 2019, the longest in history (Resh et al. 2024).
Government shutdowns produce wide-­ranging impacts—the
Congressional Budget Office estimates that the five-­week partial
shutdown between December 2018 and January 2019 delayed $18
billion in federal spending and lowered the real GDP of the first
quarter of 2019 by $8 billion (Congressional Budget Office 2019).
There are various reasons why government shutdowns can occur,
including strategic disagreements on contentious issues, limited
or expired authority for the Treasury to borrow, and the nature of
how the Congressional Budget Act was designed (Meyers 1997).
Examples abound for the real impacts shutdowns have on indi-
viduals across jurisdictions, demographics, and policies. For exam-
ple, due to the gap in federal appropriations, the public services
suspended as a function of the 2018–2019 shutdown negatively
affected healthcare for vulnerable populations (Tobey et al. 2020),
SNAP benefits were suspended for hundreds of thousands of peo-
ple (Gosliner et al. 2020), commodity prices increase (Goyal and

In terms of internal effects of shutdowns, there is also evidence

political shock to the workforce, we know little about the extent to
which shutdowns might impact employee exits from either their
agency or the government completely in comparison to agencies

Herpfer et al. (2024) show impacts from the 2013 shutdown among
employees who were furloughed versus those who were not within
the same agencies. Their work is essential in understanding the
mechanisms of intrinsic motivations and how they translate to
exits as a function of these political shocks. Nonetheless, they are
very much an exception to scholarship across public administra-
tion, political science, and labor economics on the impacts of shut-
downs on federal employees and the government's human capital
capacity. The fact is that we know little about the effects of govern-
ment shutdowns on both the supply and demand sides of the US
federal civil service labor market and very little on the impact of
the most recent shutdown of 2018 to 2019.
As well, while Herpfer et al. (2024) provide compelling evidence on
the effect shutdowns have on furloughed employees, they examine
these effects on a government-­wide shutdown by comparing the
labor market behaviors of individuals in agencies with more than
50% of their workforce furloughed versus those below that thresh-
old. It is difficult to establish a comparative basis of the 2013 shut-
down across agencies because all agencies, except the Securities
Exchange Commission (SEC) were shut down in that particular
instance. In this paper, we exploit the comparative basis that the
2018–2019 shutdown provides as an intervention that shut down
some agencies but not others due to the timing of appropriations.
We use that comparative basis to understand how the shutdown
impacted the civil service labor market in terms of existing, inter-
nal workforce supply by examining employee separations across
agencies as a distinct function of the shutdown.
Importantly, all separations from a federal government orga-
nization are not the same. Political principals may reorganize
agencies in reaction to a shutdown. Such reorganizations can
take the form of mass transfers of talent to other agencies or re-
ductions in force (RIF) (Dennis Jr 1983). Employees may leave
through their own discretion by means of transferring out of an
agency to another agency within the federal government, vari-
ous forms of retirement, or by exiting the federal government by
resignation. Each form of employee turnover impacts an agency
as a loss of existing capacity in different ways, and each form
may be inspired differently by the impetus of a government shut-
down. However, we know little about the heterogeneous effect of
shutdowns on different forms of employee outflow.
Indeed, little research has been conducted on how political envi-
ronments affect employee turnover. Existing studies have mainly
focused on how electoral cycles and presidential transitions in-
fluenced turnover among civil servants and political appoin-
tees (Bolton et al. 2021; Doherty et al. 2019; Richardson 2019;
Lewis and Richardson 2021; Limbocker and Richardson 2023).
Building on these studies—which demonstrate how politics af-
fect managerial capacity—the innovation of our study comes
from examining more deeply how discrete political events influ-
ence different types of (actual) turnover.
In sum, government shutdowns might have immense influence
on public personnel management and strategic human resource
planning. In this study, we analyze how employee separations
were affected by the U.S. federal government shutdown of 2018–
2019. We examine the effects of that shutdown on turnover dy-
namics of the federal labor market using (among other data) U.S.
Office of Personnel Management's (OPM) Enterprise Human
Resource Integration (EHRI) data of several million federal em-
ployee observations from 2005 to 2022. We find that the 2019
shutdown led to an increase in separations across agencies over-
all and in distinct ways across different employee cohorts. We
expect that an understanding of these effects will contribute
to bureaucratic politics and public administration scholarship
along with providing important insights for practitioners.

2.2   |   Voluntary Versus Nonvoluntary Turnover
Employee turnover can be broadly categorized into two types—
voluntary and nonvoluntary—based on whether the employee or
the employer initiates the decision to terminate the employment
relationship (Shaw et al. 1998; Grissom et al. 2016). Existing re-
search has found that these two types of turnover are affected
by different factors and are likely to have different consequences
(Grissom et al. 2016). For example, nonvoluntary turnover gen-
erally means that employers initiate the termination of the em-
ployment relationship. Typical reasons include unsatisfactory
employee performance or layoff due to acquisition and merger.
Voluntary turnover, on the other hand, reflects employees'
initiation of the termination of the employment relationship.
Voluntary turnover is likely a result of employees' balancing
of the desirability of the current job, or “push” factors, and the
availability of alternatives, or “pull” factors (Shaw et al. 1998; T.
W. Lee and Mitchell 1994). For example, if employees' salaries
increasingly fall behind what peer organizations offer to their
employees, and there are abundant external opportunities, some
employees may consider leaving their current positions. In this
case, the “pull” and “push” factors work in the same direction
to drive the employees to leave. While it is often believed that
nonvoluntary turnover such as dismissing low-­performing em-
ployees could be beneficial for organizations, voluntary turn-
over often causes the loss of institutional knowledge and skilled
workers, which could negatively affect organizational perfor-
mance (Wang and Sun 2020; Lee 2018; An 2019).
In the context of federal employee turnover, voluntary turn-
over includes three major forms: retirement, employees quit-
ting their jobs, and individual transfer to other agencies. Quit
means “voluntary resignation by an employee, abandoning one's
position, joining the military, or failing to return from military
furlough” (U.S. Office of Personnel Management 2014a, 2014b).
Retirement means employees meet minimum or reduced age and
service requirements for optional retirement, or they are found
to be disabled and meet the requirement for disabled retirement.
Individual transfers refer to individual employees transferring
to another agency with a break in service of 3 days or less.
In addition, this study considers three major forms of nonvol-
untary turnover: mass transfers, RIFs, and terminations or
removals. Mass transfer happens when a group of employees
are transferred to another agency because of the reassignment
of government functions to another agency. Reduction in force
means “employee separation resulting from lack of work or
funds, abolition of position or agency, or personnel ceiling re-
strictions” (U.S. Office of Personnel Management 2014a, 2014b).
Termination means “termination of a nonpermanent employee
due to expiration of appointment, work, or funds” (U.S. Office
of Personnel Management 2014a, 2014b).1 Similar to voluntary
and nonvoluntary turnover in other contexts, reasons leading to
turnover vary in the federal government. Moreover, due to the
fuzzy nature of employee separations, many nonvoluntary sepa-
rations could be passively voluntary. For instance, terminations
or removals also include those who resign upon receiving notice
of action based on performance or misconduct. Indeed, the very
reason for underperformance may be instances of “quiet quit-
ting” (Scheyett 2023). For OPM's explanation of different types
of separations, see Appendix A.
2.3   |   Differential Impacts of Government
Shutdowns on Different Types of Employee
Turnover
Government shutdowns cause a large number of federal employ-
ees to be furloughed or required to work without pay during the
period of shutdown, which temporarily but greatly reduces fed-
eral employees' income, leaving them to scramble to cover their
expenses (Baker and Yannelis 2017). Though they usually get
back pay when the shutdown is over, the uncertainties increase
stress and lower morale, potentially causing federal employees
to look to the private sector or retire (Jarvis 2014; Ali 2019). Its
effect on different types of turnover, however, could be different.
First, a government shutdown is a clear sign of political dysfunc-
tion that can act as an external shock to federal employees (Resh
et al. 2024). The turnover research in business management has
suggested that such a “shock” is “a very distinguishable event
that jars employees toward deliberate judgments about their
jobs” (Lee and Mitchell 1994, 60) and can play an important role
in employees' decision to leave (Morrell et al. 2004). Shocks can
prompt an employee to reconsider her organizational attach-
ment because they challenge the employee's goals or values.
Moreover, shocks can trigger an employee's pre-­existing plan to
look for other job opportunities (Lee et al. 1999).
In our research context, shutdowns dampen the career outlook
for career civil servants and may hasten exit by marketable
mid-­career or senior employees through the diminished value
of their organizational “investment.” Ali  (2019), for instance,

career prospects in the federal government, especially if they
have marketable skills that are in demand in the broader labor
market. Moreover, the disruptions caused by government shut-
downs hurt the morale of federal employees and pose financial
and psychological stress on employees, leading employees to
seek a way out.
On the other hand, a government shutdown could produce some
unintended financial consequences that pull civil servants to
stay. In a detailed study of the income changes and consump-
tion behaviors of civil servants who experienced the 2013 gov-
ernment shutdown, Baker and Yannelis  (2017) found that
furloughed federal employees actually increased their savings
compared with those that were not furloughed, which put them
in a better financial position coming out of the shutdown. One
important reason is that the shutdown only temporarily reduced
employees' income (civil servants received back pay after the
shutdown); and during the shutdown period, furloughed work-
ers increased home production (e.g., cooking at home rather
than dining out; opting out of child daycare services). Therefore,
it is important to consider that changes in income and consump-
tion could mean that agencies with a larger proportion of fur-
loughed workers may experience lower turnover rates after the
shutdown because of their employees' relatively better financial
conditions (compared with those that had a smaller portion of
furloughed workers).
Second, government shutdowns can influence nonvoluntary
turnover in the forms of RIF, termination, and mass transfer.
Shutdowns may allow managers to exploit performance man-
agement systems toward more dismissals for performance rea-
sons. The reorganization of government functions in reaction to
perceived or found capacity losses due to a shutdown may cause
mass transfers. Agencies might update their contingency plans
by transferring employees to exempted or excepted positions to
continue what a given principal identifies as essential functions
of the federal government. Even during a lapse of appropriations,
essential government functions must be continued, mainly by
exempted and excepted employees.2 Exempted employees “are
those who perform activities funded with budget authority that
remains available despite the lapse in appropriations, such as
multiple-­year or no-­year carryover balances,” and excepted em-
ployees “are those who perform activities pursuant to a statutory
authority that expressly authorizes an agency to enter into an
obligation in advance of an appropriation, or to address emer-
gencies involving the safety of human life or the protection of
property.” (Government Accountability Office 2020, 6–7).
In the Department of Homeland Security, for example, exempted
positions are those related to the conduct of foreign relations es-
sential to national security. These activities are to be continued
despite a lapse in appropriations. The 2019 shutdown lasted lon-
ger than many administrators had initially anticipated, and as
it dragged on, they had to add more positions as exempted or
excepted. This involved not only recalling employees back to
work but also transferring additional employees to these posi-
tions since these functions were conducted without the normal
cooperation from other parts of the agencies.
The problems in staffing exempted and excepted positions
during the shutdown and the potential for more prolonged shut-
downs in the future create a sense of urgency to update contin-
gency plans for shutdowns. As we use as a critical data source
for our analysis below, the Office of Management and Budget
(OMB) has long-­standing guidance that each federal agency
should make contingency plans for short and prolonged govern-
ment shutdowns (Office of Management and Budget 2022). After
the shutdown, the Government Accountability Office (GAO)
conducted a review of contingency plans in 2020 (GAO 2020).
Agencies that experienced the shutdown and its associated
chaos may be more motivated to clearly define exempted and
excepted positions and staff those positions properly to maintain
these essential functions. Agencies thus may need to transfer
more employees to these positions to ensure sufficient human
resources to continue essential operations independently when
other parts of the agencies ceased operations had there been an-
other prolonged shutdown. We may thus observe an increase in
the mass transfer of employees, particularly in the agencies that
experienced the 2019 shutdown.
2.4   |   Differential Impacts of Government
Shutdowns on Different Types of Employees
We argue that the 2019 shutdown led to an increase in voluntary
separations among federal employees. This notion is supported
by research demonstrating how workplace instability drives
employees to seek more stable employment. When government
operations halt, employees may experience delayed paychecks,
temporary job insecurity, and significant workplace disruption.
Herpfer et al. (2024) provide compelling evidence that employ-
ees exposed to relatively high agency furlough rates during the
2013 shutdown were more likely to leave in the subsequent year,
underscoring the negative impact of disrupted motivation and
financial uncertainty on job retention. This aligns with findings
by Griffeth et al. (2000) that job instability and dissatisfaction
are significant predictors of turnover intentions. Shutdowns
therefore act as catalysts for federal employees, especially those
with skills transferable to the private sector, to pursue more sta-
ble employment opportunities outside the government.
Herpfer et al. (2024) also show that the impact of the 2013 shut-
down on voluntary separations varied significantly by age.
Their study documents that younger employees, who often
have better outside options and value career advancement,
are more likely to quit following a shutdown. Meanwhile,
older employees—particularly those vested in pension bene-
fits—are more likely to retire but at lower levels than their
younger counterparts' method of exit. Griffeth et  al. (2000)
meta-­analysis further supports this perspective. They find
that longer-­tenured employees are more likely to remain with
an organization due to accumulated benefits, loyalty, and a
sense of obligation. Thus, one could posit that employees with
shorter service histories are more likely to voluntarily quit
during shutdowns, as they have less to lose by seeking alter-
native employment. Less tenured federal employees, more
sensitive to career instability, may be less willing to endure
shutdowns than their longer-­tenured counterparts, who may
have stronger financial incentives to stay until retirement

Employees with shorter tenures, who have less investment in

turnover rates following a shutdown. As Griffeth et al. (2000)
noted, tenure strengthens organizational commitment, making
long-­tenured employees more resilient to temporary disruptions.
Long-­tenured employees (having accrued significant benefits,
institutional knowledge, and loyalty to the agency) may weigh
these factors against the uncertainties of leaving (Moynihan and
Pandey 2007).
Contractual status, as well, should influence the impact of shut-
downs on voluntary separations of nonpermanent or contin-
gent employees especially. These employees may face a greater
degree of job precarity and, therefore, interpret shutdowns as
signals of expendability, leading to higher voluntary separation
rates. In turn, voluntary exit may be diminished among per-
manent employees who have stronger job protections and more
entrenched incentives, like retirement benefits, that make them
more likely to remain in their roles despite the instability caused
by shutdowns. At the same time, permanent employees tend to
have these positions as a function of organizational need, which
portends that they may also be more competitive in the general
labor market and may choose to exit based on the uncertainty or
diminished morale that a shutdown creates (Resh et al. 2024).
Hierarchical level might also affect the likelihood of voluntary
separations. Herpfer et al. (2024) find that lower-­grade employ-
ees, who typically earn less and are more financially vulnerable,
have higher separation rates following the 2013 shutdown com-
pared to higher-­grade employees with greater financial security.
Lower-­grade employees, facing immediate financial challenges
due to furloughs, may be more likely to seek alternative employ-
ment, while those in higher grades, with more stability, may be
better able to tolerate temporary pay disruptions. At the same
time, these lower-­grade employees may have fewer external
market options than those who have higher professional status.
Nonvoluntary separations, as well, might increase as a function
of the shutdown. Agencies may increasingly rely on contracted
private firms following the shutdowns to fill the gaps left by
departing employees. This increased reliance on contract labor
might lead agencies to streamline their workforce in response to
budget constraints, potentially increasing nonvoluntary separa-
tions among nonpermanent employees, who have fewer protec-
tions than permanent staff. As well, nonpermanent employees
should be more vulnerable to nonvoluntary separations during
fiscal crises like shutdowns, as agencies prioritize retaining per-
manent staff essential to their core functions.
In conclusion, the contention that government shutdowns lead
to increased voluntary and nonvoluntary separations within
the federal workforce is supported by the empirical research
on turnover and public sector motivation, but open questions
remain as to the relative impact that such events might have
across different employee cohorts. Shutdowns create conditions
of instability that could amplify the likelihood of separations,
but the impacts might vary by employee demographics and job
classifications. These propositions underscore the need to ad-
dress the adverse effects of shutdowns on federal employment
stability and to develop policy interventions aimed at minimiz-
ing workforce disruptions during fiscal impasses.
3   |   Data and Method
3.1   |   Data
We leverage the Office of Personnel Management's (OPM)
Enterprise Human Resource Integration (EHRI) personnel
data, which provides us detailed information about separations
that spans from 2005 to the 3rd quarter of 2022. OPM began to
update this data on a quarterly basis since 2016, while previ-
ous data (periods between 2005 and 2015) were updated on a
yearly basis. OPM classifies various types of separations such as
transfers-­out (both individual and mass transfers), quits, retire-
ments (voluntary, early out, disability, and other), reductions-­
in-­force (RIF), termination, death, and other separations.
Employee personnel data comes from the Employment Cube,
which has been updated on a quarterly basis since 2017.
We account for the potential intensity of the shutdown inter-
vention through the ratio of employees who were furloughed in
each agency. We verified each agency's furlough ratio through
multiple sources, including Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)
requests to each respective agency, government reports, and
published agency contingency plans (see https://​www.​white​
house.​gov/​omb/​infor​matio​n-­​for-­​agenc​ies/​agenc​y-­​conti​ngenc​
y-­​plans/​). Identification of agencies that were subjected to the
shutdown was verified by multiple sources, including reports
from the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, the
Executive Office of the President, GAO, and individual contact
with data managers within these agencies (see Appendix B for
list of agencies and Appendix C for FOIA request procedures).
Finally, we subset our samples by three salient individual-­level
subject attributes: contractual status (permanent or nonper-
manent), length of federal service, and hierarchical general
schedule (GS) status. Descriptive statistics for our measures as
compared across both the control and the intervention of the bi-
nary intervention are available in Appendix D.
We focus our analysis on overall separations, voluntary sepa-
rations, and nonvoluntary separations. Within those general
categories, we focus on quits and voluntary retirements for vol-
untary separations, as they comprise most of the voluntary types
of separations and are the most salient. Quits show people leav-
ing government service before retirement, which is perhaps the
most worrisome sign and, unlike individual transfers, quitting
allows the person to fully escape the threat of future shutdowns.
Voluntary retirements are useful to analyze, particularly among
the most tenured and older employees, as it is easier to retire
than to quit for those who meet retirement qualifications.

To examine the effect of the 2018–2019 shutdown during the
Trump administration, we exploit separations data on both
pre-­ and post-­intervention observations across our units of anal-
ysis. This provides an ideal setting for two-­way fixed effects
difference-­in-­differences (TWFE DiD)3 analyses because we
can take advantage of the fact that many agencies are affected by
the shutdown (treatment), while many others were not subjected
to the shutdown (control). We argue that agencies that were di-
rectly affected by the shutdown would show a systematically
different turnover pattern compared to the pre-­intervention data
and the control groups. That is, we have both time and groups to
compare, suggesting a DiD approach as the identification strat-
egy. We compare a treatment group consisting of agencies that
were subject to the shutdown and a control group consisting of
agencies that were not affected by the shutdown. Importantly,
we additionally model our intervention as varying in intensity
based on the percentage of furloughed workers. So, we run
models that use both a binary intervention (shutdown versus
non-­shutdown) and a continuous (the extent of furloughed em-
ployees versus non-­furloughed).

3.2   |   Hypotheses
Our models are run on overall employee samples and specific
subsamples. Our expectations about particular types of separa-
tions accord with our prior discussion above:
H1.  Federal agencies that experienced shutdowns have higher
overall employee separation rates compared to agencies that re-
mained operational.
H2.  The increase in turnover rates is positively correlated with
the proportion of employees furloughed during the shutdown.
H3.  Shutdowns lead to an increase in voluntary separations
among federal employees.
H3a.  The impact of shutdowns on types of voluntary separa-
tions varies by employee length of service.
H3a1.  Shutdowns will lead to higher voluntary retirements by
longer tenured employees.
H3a2.  Shutdowns lead to higher quits by less tenured
employees.
H3c.  The impact of shutdowns on voluntary separations varies
by contractual status (permanent vs. nonpermanent).
H3d.  The impact of shutdowns on voluntary separations varies
by hierarchical level (higher vs. lower general schedule).
H4.  Shutdowns lead to an increase in nonvoluntary separa-
tions among federal employees.
H4a.  The impact of shutdowns on nonvoluntary separations
varies by contractual status (permanent vs. nonpermanent).

vulnerability of non-­permanent employees to both voluntary and
nonvoluntary separations during fiscal crises.

Government shutdowns have increasingly become a hallmark
of U.S. politics, carrying significant repercussions for the econ-

increasing occurrence, our understanding of how these shut-
downs impact the operational capacity of federal agencies and
the dynamics within the civil service labor market remains

Utilizing a longitudinal approach through a variety of

government shutdown in the last 40 years to assess its impact
on employee separations across federal agencies. The shutdown
led to an overall increase in separations, with total separations
rising by 0.532 percentage points compared to non-­shutdown
agencies, a 19% increase relative to the mean separation rate
(2.81%). This increase encompassed both voluntary and nonvol-
untary exits, though the effects varied significantly by type and
employee subgroup.
Voluntary separations increased in shutdown agencies by 0.285
percentage points relative to non-­shutdown agencies (12% over
the mean of 2.29%), driven largely by resignations (quits), which
rose by 0.172 percentage points (17% over the mean of 1.02%).
These findings highlight the sensitivity of less-­tenured employ-
ees and those in lower-­grade positions to workplace instability,
as predicted in H3a2. Nonvoluntary separations, while showing

Contrary to some expectations, the analysis found no simple
linear relationship between the percentage of furloughed em-
ployees and separation rates. This suggests that the effects of
furloughs on turnover may depend on factors beyond the sheer
scale of furloughs, such as peer dynamics or perceived job inse-
curity. Agencies with higher furlough rates may inadvertently
create clearer expectations around roles, mitigating some nega-
tive effects on employee retention. More work should be done to
understand these dynamics at both individual and aggregated

Our use of DiD models offers robust insights but also comes with
limitations. First, the assumption of parallel trends between af-
fected and unaffected agencies may not fully capture unobserved
variables, such as agency-­specific policies or external economic
shifts. Second, the quantitative approach, while illuminating
patterns in separations, cannot capture the complex motivations
behind employees' decisions to leave. For instance, qualitative
factors such as diminished intrinsic motivation or feelings of
disconnect from meaningful work during a shutdown remain
unquantified. Third, our analysis primarily captures immedi-
ate and short-­term effects of shutdowns; longer-­term impacts,
such as delayed retirements or subsequent waves of resignations,
warrant further investigation.
To address these limitations, future research should incorporate
qualitative perspectives, including interviews or ethnographic
studies with affected employees. Such approaches could provide
deeper insights into the mechanisms driving separations, such
as morale, peer influence, or the perceived erosion of federal job
stability. Additionally, longitudinal studies that explore lagged
effects of shutdowns on turnover and agency performance could
enhance our understanding of their long-­term consequences.
6   |   Conclusion
Our findings underscore the broader human capital challenges
that government shutdowns pose, suggesting that such events
may lead to a loss of talent and experience due to increased
separations. Shutdowns particularly destabilize the workforce
through voluntary exits among less-­tenured and lower-­grade
employees, as well as heightened nonvoluntary separations
among non-­permanent staff. These trends can exacerbate ex-
isting vulnerabilities within agencies and compromise institu-
tional knowledge and technical expertise.
The impacts extend beyond immediate turnover. Shutdowns
can hinder the recruitment of skilled employees, as the prom-
ise of stability—a key competitive advantage of federal jobs—is
undermined. Prospective employees may view shutdowns as
indicative of broader instability, deterring talent from entering
public service. For current employees, intrinsic motivation is
tested during shutdowns, with diminished engagement and mo-
rale contributing to higher separations. These disruptions have
tangible consequences for agency innovation and productivity,
as research shows that turnover correlates with reduced cre-
ative outputs and increased reliance on high-­cost contractors to
bridge staffing gaps.
Strategic workforce planning is essential to mitigate these chal-
lenges. Agencies must prioritize retention strategies for at-­risk
groups, including targeted support during crises and post-­
shutdown recovery programs to rebuild morale. Recruitment
efforts should emphasize the meaningful work and public ser-
vice mission of federal employment, countering the negative
perceptions of instability caused by shutdowns. Policymakers
must also explore ways to insulate agencies from the disruptive
effects of political gridlock, ensuring continuity in public service
delivery and workforce stability. By addressing these challenges,
we can strengthen the resilience of the federal workforce in the
face of future political uncertainties.

Abstract
Policy is not only made by street-level bureaucrats at the frontlines. It is also made by their superiors—street-level managers—who set the or-
ganizational conditions through which street-level bureaucrats act. Although scholars have documented how street-level bureaucrats cope with
the pressures of their work by, for instance, breaking or bending rules, the question of how street-level managers cope with the pressures of
their own work has received less attention. Drawing from ethnographic data of a network of publicly funded health centers in the Midwestern
US, I show how street-level managers use an interaction ritual with role distance to cope. Role distance is mobilized when the person uses com-
municative expressions such as laughter or cries of frustration to convey a critical distance from what her organizational role prescribes. Based
on classic sociological insights, I posit that role distance can function as follows. It can help managers preserve self by allowing them to define
their putatively “more-human self” from their work, create a feeling of collectiveness as they orient themselves to the shared frustrations yet
obligations that their role engenders, which enables them to coordinate on carrying out tasks, even those that rub against their preferences and
well-intentions. Taken together, I suggest that role distance can offer a coping function, which enables them to hold in abeyance individual and
collective responsibility for the decisions they make. I then highlight the benefits and unintended consequences of role distance and posit what
academics and practitioners can do to ensure that street-level managers use role distance toward more productive ends.
Key words: interaction rituals, street-level bureaucracy, ethnographic methods, street-level management, performance management.
Street-level managers—the vast category of workers ranging
from the bottom tier of management to the Chief Executive
Officer (Gassner and Gofen 2018)—co-make public policy.
They set the strategies, allocate resources, and distribute the
rewards and responsibilities that shape the organizational
conditions through which policies are made (Evans 2015,
2016; Hupe and Keiser 2019). Despite their importance, the
professional class of managers hold a dubious position in pop-
ular and academic press. According to David Graeber (2018)
in his best-selling book, Bullshit Jobs, a Theory, managers
do bullshit jobs because they create extra work when none
is needed and supervise people who do not require supervi-
sion. Even Lipsky (2010) typecasts managers in Street Level
Bureaucracy, claiming that because street-level managers have
the narrow aims of meeting agencies’ objectives and enforcing
compliance to policy, they “restrict [street-level] workers’ dis-
cretion in order to secure results” (p. 19).
In contrast to such popular and academic renderings, this
article attempts to contextualize street-level managers’ plight.
After all, street-level managers face contradictory demands
from regulatory and organizational imperatives, and profes-
sional and personal norms, just like street-level bureaucrats.
Yet unlike street-level bureaucrats, where bending or
breaking rules or routinizing or rationalizing services are
widely understood as coping mechanisms (Lipsky 2010;
Tummers et al. 2015), we are only beginning to understand
how street-level managers cope with the demands of their
work (e.g., see Gassner and Gofen 2018; Hupe and Keiser
2019; Klemsdal, Andreassen, and Breit 2022).
Understanding how street-level managers cope requires
contextualizing their actions in everyday organizational
life. Thus, this article presents data from an organizational
ethnography of a network of large, publicly funded health
centers in the Midwestern US. It examines managers’ attempts
to get their workers to execute a performance measure or,
more precisely, click boxes in the electronic medical record,
a digital record of patients’ health records, for the ostensive
purpose of demonstrating clinical quality to a federal health
bureau. How did they cope with enforcing a task that even
they critiqued?
In this article, I suggest that an interaction ritual with
role distance may have helped. An interaction ritual refers
to the patterned behaviors between people that are shaped,
though not determined, by a structure. And often, that struc-
ture is shaped by roles. A role refers to the tasks, norms,
and behaviors that are expected of a person in a social pos-
ition in a given social relationship. Role distance occurs
when a person uses expressions such as laughter, jokes,
and rationalizing sentences to convey a critical distance
from what one’s role prescribes (Coser 1966; Goffman
1972/1961). Based on sociological insights on interaction
rituals, I posit that role distance can help managers cope by
functioning as follows (Collins 2005; Goffman 1982, 1983).
It can preserve self by allowing the manager to define her pu-
tatively “more-human self” in contradistinction to her pro-
fessional self, create a feeling of collectiveness as managers’
bodies and words comport in synchronicity with the shared
frustrations yet obligations that their role engenders, which

This article enriches the street-level literature by returning to
a classic concern—how well-meaning street-level bureaucrats
cope with their demands of their work—and extends it to a
recent development in the literature, the role of street-level
managers in co-making policies (Evans 2016; Gassner and
Gofen 2018; Hupe and Keiser 2019; Klemsdal, Andreassen,
and Breit 2022). Two contributions are made to the latter lit-
erature. First, we know that street-level managers use practical
strategies such as accommodating or adapting policies to local
contingencies to cope (Gassner and Gofen 2018; Klemsdal,
Andreassen, and Breit 2022). We also know that they use psy-
chological processes such as bringing discordant cognitions into
congruence to cope (Pandey and Welch 2005). In this article, I
layer the practical and the psychological with the sociological
to show a social way to cope: through an interaction ritual
with role distance. Second, unlike other coping strategies where
managers try to resolve or solve the contradictory demands of
their work through, for instance, bringing those contradictions
into congruence, this article offers a coping strategy that
permits those contradictions to linger, unresolved. We are
therefore reminded that street-level organizations are filled
with contradictions, many of which do not get rationalized
into logical order through plans, policies, or conversation.
From this, I lay out the benefits and unintended consequences
of role distance, then offer some recommendations on what we
can do to ensure that street-level managers use role distance
toward more productive ends.
Literature review
The street-level literature commenced with a provocative
claim: the discretionary acts of street-level bureaucrats, the
people who routinely interact with clients in their day-to-day
work, can cumulate to make policies (Lipsky 2010). Since
then, the literature has developed to keep abreast of public
administration reforms. It has been recontextualized from an
“old” managerialism based on governments’ command and
control over workers’ production of inputs to a “new” man-
agerialism whereby private organizations, acting as the de-
livery arm of the state, are “steered” through the imposition
of mandates requiring organizations to report performance
against pregiven goals. This shift has prompted scholars to
widen their gaze from examining how policies get made at the
frontline to how policies get made in organizations (Brodkin
2011, 2012; Dias and Maynard-Moody 2006; Sandfort 2000;
Soss, Fording, and Schram 2011).
Amid this development, the street-level manager has
emerged as a crucial co-maker of policies (Evans 2015, 2016;
Hupe and Keiser 2019). Transformed by the rhetoric of new
managerialism, managers are responsible for overseeing the
delivery of nimble and tailored services (Gassner and Gofen
2018; Gatenby et al. 2015). Empowered by the tools of new
managerialism, managers can employ the practices of their
private sector counterparts to devise strategies and distribute
incentives to shape the conditions through which street-level
workers act (Dias and Maynard-Moody 2006; Gatenby et al.
2015; Perna 2021; Spitzmueller 2018). After all, as the apho-
rism goes, management matters. Managerial activities such as
clearly defining goals, monitoring performance, and creating
a culture that values workers and their work can improve
organizational performance (Brewer 2005; May and Winter
2009; Meier and O’Toole 2007; Moynihan and Pandey 2005;
Riccucci 1995).
Yet, management matters only to the extent that managers
have control over the levers that influence performance (Meier
and O’Toole 2007; Moynihan and Pandey 2005). Because
street-level managers must satisfy the mandates imposed by
external bodies, goals set by the organization, duties arising
from their managerial charge, and commitments stemming
from their own professional and personal values, how do
managers cope, let alone exercise control over, these multiple
and often contradictory demands?
Coping
I have used the term “cope” to remain consonant with Lipsky
(2010) vocabulary in Street Level Bureaucracy. Whereas
coping in everyday parlance refers to how a person navigates
a difficult situation, Lipsky uses the term to describe the be-
havioral strategies that street-level bureaucrats employ to
deal with their on-the-job demands. Street-level bureaucrats
might, for instance, “move towards clients” by bending and
breaking rules for their clients’ benefit, “move away from
clients” by routinizing and rationing their services to avoid
building sympathetic relationships with clients, or “move
against clients” by rigidly adhering to rules to the detriment
of their clients’ needs (Tummers et al. 2015, p. 1100).
Street-level bureaucrats are different from street-level
managers, however. As idealized types, street-level bureaucrats
are torn between following the rules imposed by their man-
agerial superiors and providing services that are attuned to
their clients’ personal needs (see Lipsky 2010, pp. 18–25).
Street-level managers, in contrast, must enforce the rules
imposed by their organizational and regulatory superiors
while securing their staff’s professional growth and perfor-
mance. So, if street-level bureaucrats and managers face dif-
ferent sources and types of demands, they most likely employ
different strategies to cope.
How might street-level managers cope with their idio-
syncratic demands? Some scholars suggest that they cope
practically by bringing contradictory demands into greater
congruence. They engage in institutional or situational work by
wielding a laundry list of mechanisms. They pass, strengthen,
translate, adapt, and buffer the flow of government policies
down to street-level bureaucrats so that policies are tailored
to their local communities. They also counter and articulate
the flow of street-level experiences up to organizational and
policy elites in order to change policies (Breit, Andreassen, and
Fossestøl 2022; Gassner and Gofen 2018; Hupe and Keiser
2019; Klemsdal, Andreassen, and Breit 2022).
Other scholars suggest that street-level managers cope by
“moving against” their staff. They have sided with the hi-
erarchical dictates imposed by new managerial reforms,
leading to an “ideological war” between their “work-first”
approach and staff’s “social work” approach (Dias and

In this article, I layer the practical and the psychological
with the sociological to show a social way to cope: with role
distance.
Roles and role distance
Borrowing from a rich sociological tradition in role theory, a
role is created when the tasks, norms, and behaviors affixed to
a social position in a given role relationship are repeated such
that the individual inhabiting the role is expected to comport
to those tasks, norms, and behaviors (Hughes 1937; Merton
1940). Consider, for instance, the norms that govern how a
mother interacts with her child, a caseworker with her client,
or lovers on a date. And consider Robert Merton’s (1940, p.
562) classic portrayal of the bureaucratic role vis à vis the
client, where bureaucratic structures exert unremitting pres-
sure on the person in the bureaucratic role to be “methodical,
prudent, disciplined” in his dealings with clients so that im-
personal and impartial treatment is provided based on strict
adherence to rules.
Unlike Merton, who approaches roles as structures that cir-
cumscribe and constrain people’s behavior, scholars writing
from an interactionalist tradition view roles as loose structures
that people negotiate in face-to-face interactions. After all,
even if a person is given a role to play, she might perform
it effectively or defectively. Thus, to capture the truism that
expected and actual performances do not always nor fully co-
incide, interactionalist scholars have created an analytical dis-
tinction between role expectations and role enactment (Biddle
and Thomas 1966, p. 10; Goffman 1972/1961, pp. 75–84).
Role expectations are the tasks, norms, and behaviors that are
demanded by an audience and placed on the person playing
the role. Role enactment refer to the actual behaviors of the
person inhabiting a role while on duty. This distinction is so-
cially significant because if enough people in the same role
relationship depart from role expectations in the same way
for a long enough period of time, the role itself might change.
Role distance, a concept imported from Goffman’s
(1972/1961) essay of the same name, occurs when role
enactments and role expectations are askew. Role distance
is defined as “actions that effectively convey some disdainful
detachment of the performer from a role he is performing”
(p. 98). For Goffman, role distance occurs when a surgeon
sings inappropriate tunes while performing an operation to
assure his staff of his “more-human self” beneath his profes-
sional front (p. 112), a 60-year old man wears his hat with
a youthful flair to tell others “what he is but what he isn’t
quite” (p. 129), or a family member rejects food at an evening
meal to express her disaffiliation from the family (p. 131).
In this spirit, role distance can be read into studies on street-
level managers. Consider Gassner and Gofen’s (2018, p. 563)
interviews with street-level managers. One Police Station
Chief expressed strict adherence to his role, “Far and fore-
most I am loyal to the organization that selected me to the
mission,” yet distanced himself from that role by saying “it
doesn’t mean that I agree with all its policies.”
If role distance occurs when the person expresses distance
from role expectations, what exactly are the expectations that
the person is expressing distance from? In this article, I will
suggest that role distance might be part of the managers’ role
expectations. For, even if managers are expected to align their
decisions and actions to organizational policies, they are not
expected to agree with all its policies. And if managers are
institutionally permitted and even praised for having a good
sense of humor or intelligence to articulate their disagree-
ment with specific policies, enacting role distance in relation
to those policies might be part of their role expectations. In
these cases, the manager’s use of role distance might be part
of an orderly interaction ritual.
Role distance as an interaction ritual
Interaction rituals are patterned behaviors among humans in
physical proximity of each other. Interaction rituals, such as
shaking hands, taking communion, and exchanging wedding
vows, are important because they offer functions beyond their
manifest purpose. Goffman (1982, 1983), for instance, uses
interaction rituals to show how social order is maintained
amid the mundanities of everyday life, whereas Collins (2005)
uses interaction rituals to explain how micro-interactions can
make and modify macro-orders such as economic markets
and cultural movements. If we approach role distance as part
of an interaction ritual, we can draw on some sociological
insights on interaction rituals to posit some of its functions.
Preserving self
For Goffman, the person can express an organizational self by
performing a ceremonial “front” in line with role expectations.
The person can also express a “more-human self,” carved out
from little idiosyncrasies in appearance, comportment, tone
of voice, and quirks (Goffman 1983, p. 3). In this putatively
“more-human” vein, role distance allows the person to define
her “self” in relation to role expectations by showing “that
something of oneself lies outside the constraints of the moment
and outside the role within whose jurisdiction the moment
occurs” (Goffman 1972/1961, p. 101), allowing the person to
preserve what Goffman considers the most sacred object in the
contemporary Western world: the individual self.
In this way, role distance is different from emotional work,
or the management of one’s bodily and facial expressions
to get the job done (Guy, Newman, and Mastracci 2014).
Whereas emotional work entails using communicative
expressions to conform to role expectations, such as putting
on a professional “face” or “mask,” role distance involves
using communicative expressions to demonstrate a degree of
refusal to live up to role expectations.
Collectiveness
With intellectual roots from Durkheim’s (1913) The
Elementary Forms of the Religious Life to Collins’s (2005)
Interaction Ritual Chains, interaction rituals can create

energies and attentions are mutually aligned and gestures
and utterances move in synchronicity, feelings of group sol-

Likewise, when role distance is performed by a collective,
when people use similar gestures and verbal cries to distance
themselves from what their role requires, they are entrained
into feeling that they belong to the same social world filled
with shared beliefs, values, and experiences.
Coordination
By prescribing the role, script, and actions of people in given
social situations, interaction rituals enable coordination.
Consider, for instance, lining up at the checkout counter. The
norm of first-come-first-served produces a temporal–spatial
ordering that keeps the peace and prevents conflict unless
someone breaks the norm by jumping the queue (Goffman
1983, p. 14). Likewise, role distance can keep the peace and
prevent conflict. It does so by offering a discursive space for
the contradictions in street-level organizations—between
policy and practice, rhetoric and reality, and what people
want and what they must do—to exist without obliging
people to confront those contradictions, which runs the risk
of disagreement. Instead, role distance permits the managers
to circumvent those contradictions, thus enabling them to
carry out their tasks.
The preceding functions may be taken as conjectures about
the kinds of feelings and behaviors one might see and sense
when managers engage in an interaction ritual with role dis-
tance. Though I have analytically separated these functions, in
practice, they often operate simultaneously. Finally, by relating
those functions to the street-level literature on coping, I posit
a fourth function of role distance.
Coping
Role distance may help managers cope with the contradictions
of their work by insulating them from a type of cognitive dis-
sonance borne from trying to reconcile what they wish they
could do with what their organizational role demands. By
expressing that what they want to do is distinct from what
they are organizationally obliged to do, role distance allows
managers to offer up their organizational role, affixed with
duties and obligations, as a scapegoat to preserve the sanctity
of their “more-human self.” Doing so absolves this “more-
human self” from blame, which enables them to hold in abey-
ance individual and collective responsibility for the decisions
that the team makes.
Research approach
This research relies on ethnographic data from a network of
Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHC) located in the
Midwestern US (henceforth called “Network”). FQHCs are
federally funded community-based health centers. They are
also safety net organizations, providing primary health serv-
ices to people irrespective of their ability to pay and health
insurance status in a country where health insurance is not
universal. At the time of my fieldwork, the Network had over
700 employees and served over 80,000 patients.
The Network was chosen for this research for three
reasons: it receives most of its revenue from federal funds,
is mission oriented, and is beholden to a litany of rules and
regulations, many of which are imposed by outside bodies.
The Network therefore offers a useful setting in which to
probe how managers negotiate the bureaucratic complexities
of their work while upholding their own personal and profes-
sional values.
The data employed for this study focus on how
managers orient themselves to a performance measure, the
“Depression Screening and Follow-Up Measure” (hence-
forth, “Depression Measure”). The Depression Measure
was chosen not because I had a predisposed interest in de-
pression but because the Depression Measure consistently
appeared in the Network’s meetings. The core data that
make up the analysis draws from participant observations
(21 meetings) and interviews (17 interviews with 13 distinct
individuals: two senior leaders, six middle managers, two
medical assistants, three administrative staff). The data are
contextualized in a larger dataset where I participated in
at least 90 meetings, interviewed 30 individuals for a total
of 51 times, received consent to shadow 49 employees, and
spent 418 hours, two to four times a week, at the Network
between August 2019 and March 2020. To supplement par-
ticipant observations and interviews, written documents
such as instruction manuals, standard operating procedures,
meeting minutes, and PowerPoint slides were collected to
understand the materials that the managers used to support
and perform their work.
Data analysis
The article was originally written as a case study examining
what it takes for managers to improve performance against
the Depression Measure. Upon completing the case study, I
noted a peculiarity in the managers’ behaviors. In interviews,
they stressed the importance of depression screening to im-
prove patients’ health outcomes. Yet in meetings, they
laughed at the Depression Measure for failing to represent the
complexities of depression and the clinical encounter. And de-
spite their ambivalence, they determinedly sought to improve
organizational performance against the Depression Measure
as a team. How?
To answer this question, I engaged in an abductive process,
though not in the contemporary sense of inferring the best
explanation that justifies a hypothesis. Rather, I followed an
abductive process inspired by Charles Sanders Peirce’s for-
mulation of the term: as a search strategy that generates a
hypothesis where first, a surprising phenomenon is observed;
then second, a hypothesis is made to explain the phenom-
enon such that if the hypothesis was true, then the phenom-
enon would be observed (Douven 2021). I was surprised at
how the managers could carry out a task that they critiqued
in meetings. And, noting that laughter was constant in those
meetings, I hypothesized that there was something special
about laughter that could explain the managers’ behaviors,
which led me to organizational studies on humor and then to
role distance.
The ethnographic data were consequently analyzed using
the concept of role distance. Role distance was coded in two
ways. First, when the person used full-fledged sentences to
create a discursive distance between the tasks she is expected
to perform in her organizational role and what she report-
edly desires to do to. Second, when the person or collective
injected the meeting with cues suggestive of humor—a laugh,
grin, joke, or ironic statement—that (1) preceded or followed

At this point, let me remind you that role distance occurs
when the person expresses a degree of withdrawal from role
expectations. When I coded for role distance, I looked for
managers expressing detachment from the specific task of
creating, monitoring, and/or enforcing strategies to improve
organizational performance on the Depression Measure (for
greater conceptual clarity on the relationship between role dis-
tance and role expectations, refer to Supplementary Appendix).
Thereafter, I coded for role distance in the 21 relevant
meetings. I noted that role distance appeared in all but five
meetings: two were marked by inter-team conflict where
members from one team implied that members from the
other team were not doing their tasks; the other three were
informational, offering little room for the recipients of the
information to interject (refer to Supplementary Appendix
for more details). I then used the posited functions of role
distance—preserving self, fostering collectiveness, and
facilitating coordination—to offer an interpretation on how
an interaction ritual with role distance might be functioning
in some meetings.
Interpretation with narratives
I will present my empirical findings and analysis using
narratives, defined as sequences of events composed of an an-
tecedent, action, and outcome of that action, for three reasons
(Czarniawska 1997). First, it is consonant with a dramatur-
gical sensibility where we, the reader, are invited to observe
what goes on in the theater of everyday life as members of
the audience. Second, I am guided by the assumption that
constructs are illuminated by showing how they operate in a
thick social scene and context (Dyer and Wilkins 1991; Van
Maanen 2011). And third, as Pentland (1999, p. 711) writes,
“in the domain of process theory, stories are constructs,” for
stories show how antecedents are linked to outcomes. In this
spirit, I will tell a series of stories on how a group of managers
who profess critique at the Depression Measure (the ante-
cedent) nonetheless commit to improving organizational per-
formance on that measure (the outcome), and will suggest
that the antecedent and outcome are mediated by a highly
ritualized process involving role distance.
Since this ethnographic project relies on narratives, the
presented data are fragmentary and localized. One can
never collect enough data through systematic sampling to
make generalized claims. Furthermore, because I abductively
located role distance once I formally exited the field, I could
not retrieve my ethnographic informants’ reflections on role
distance to, for example, ascertain whether they intentionally
deployed role distance to subdue potential conflict, protect
their reputation, or cue alliance with others. These are limita-
tions of this research.
Even so, the aim of my research is not to establish the on-
tological realness of role distance and its functions. After all,
this ethnographic project is also an interpretive project. And
as an interpretive project, I am liberal with the use of specu-
lative qualifiers such as “perhaps” and “might” to acknowl-
edge my own interpretive presence and role as narrator in
the scenes. As such, I am not seeking the correct or even most
plausible explanation of what I have observed in the field.
Instead, I am offering a reasonable interpretation and imagi-
native redescription of those observations. I therefore ask the
reader to judge the quality of this research based on criteria
that John Van Maanen (2011), leading contributor to ethno-
graphic methodologies, formulated: whether the conjectures
are interesting, hang together as a coherent whole, and seem
to be true.
Inside the Network
To see role distance in action, we will enter three meetings
with members of management. Since the meetings occur in an
organizational context with a shared set of assumptions about
the Depression Measure, we must understand the Depression
Measure in relation to its organizational context. Let us there-
fore get acquainted with the Depression Measure, enumerate
some role expectations vis à vis the measure, then articulate
some tensions that the measure provoked at the Network be-
fore we enter the meetings.
The Depression Measure and role expectations
The Depression Measure is defined by an agency of the US
federal government as:
Percentage of patients aged 12 years and older screened for
depression on the date of the visit using an age-appropriate
standardized depression screening tool and if positive, a
follow-up plan is documented on the date of the positive
screen. (HRSA 2019, p. 89).
The terms of the Depression Measure are set: who is screened
(patients aged 12 years and older), the tool (a standardized
depression screening tool), the timeframe (on the date of the
visit), and the sequence of tasks (screening then follow-up if
the screen is positive). It is now up to the Network to exe-
cute the terms, which it has chosen to do as follows.
In a standard operating procedure called the Medical
Assistant Pre-Visit Workflow, the medical assistant, whose
role is to assist the physicians, must screen for depression. She
must ask the patient two questions from the Patient Health
Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) screening tool, “Over the last two
weeks, how often have you had little interest or pleasure in
doing things”; “Over the last two weeks, how often have you
been feeling down, depressed or hopeless?,” then document
the answers in the electronic medical record by clicking two,
rectangular boxes (fig. 1).
If the patient screens positive on the PHQ-2, the phy-
sician steps in. The physician must ask the patient nine
questions from the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)
screening tool, such as “over the last two weeks, have you
been feeling tired or having little energy”; “have poor appetite
or overeating.” The physician then engages in ­“follow-up,”
which often involves scheduling an appointment for the pa-
tient with a behavioral health specialist. She then clicks a
small, square box in the electronic medical record to docu-
ment that ­follow-up was done (fig. 2).

and physicians do the Depression Measure. Because the
managers do not sit on a single rung in the organizational
hierarchy but are stratified, different management roles come
with different expectations. The management roles that we
will encounter in the meetings are as follows (Table 1). At
the top of the hierarchy is the Chief Clinical Officer, who sets
the yearly performance target for the Depression Measure.
The Chief Clinical Officer leads the “Senior Clinical Leaders,”
the team that oversees the Network’s major clinical areas
such as pediatrics and reproductive health. Together, they
set the Physician Incentives Plan biannually, which contains
information on the bonuses that physicians receive for their
performance against performance measures, including the
Depression Measure. Below the Senior Clinical Leaders sits
“Middle Management,” who oversee lower-ranking clinicians
(e.g., physicians, residents, nurse practitioners, behavioral
specialists) and support staff (e.g., medical assistants, front
desk personnel, and case managers). They are responsible for
ensuring their staff reach the performance targets set by their
organizational superiors.
The contradictory tensions with the Depression
Measure
Based on internal reports, the Network’s performance on the
Depression Measure regressed; it failed to reach its perfor-
mance target. The managers were therefore called upon to
devise strategies to improve their staff’s performance on the
Depression Measure. After all, doing well on the Depression
Measure is important for at least two reasons. First, it can
lead to better behavioral health outcomes for patients. In
fact, in one-on-one interviews with leaders, managers, and
frontline workers, no one denied the importance of depres-
sion screening to “improve lives and outcomes,” as stated by
a manager of clinical operations.
Second, it is financially consequential. Each year, the Network
provides information on its performance against a suite of
performance measures, including the Depression Measure, to
an agency of the U.S. Health and Human Services. Not only
does performing in the top 10, 11–20, and 21–30 percent of
all peer health organizations against those measures grant the
Network the right to place a gold, silver, or bronze “Clinical
Quality Measure Badge” on its marketing material, designating
it a “Health Center Quality Leader,” it also determines the level
of financial rewards it receives. “And this is important because
the care we provide and the care patients deserve is expensive.
It costs more to give than what insurance and patients bring
in,” said the Chief Clinical Officer.
Even so, the Depression Measure was widely critiqued at
the Network. Due to the manner in which the Depression
Measure is executed (refer to figs. 1 and 2), people at the
Network snidely call it the “box-checking” or “box-clicking”
measure as if to phonetically trivialize the percussive sound of
a finger clicking a computer mouse to check off boxes in the
electronic medical record. The problem with the Depression
Measure, as one resident remarked, is that “there’s a huge dis-
connect between clicking that box and what actually should
be done.” For even if checking boxes shows evidence of clin-
ical quality on paper, it may not improve the clinical quality
of depression screening and follow-up in practice, let alone
guarantee that the screened patient will receive behavioral
health services thereafter.
What is more, the Network leaders were worried about
the effects of documentation on burnout. In an internal study
on employee well-being at the Network, most respondents
agreed with the statement that “the EMR [electronic med-
ical record] adds to the frustration of my day.” In the same
survey, 50 percent of respondents reported experiencing
some form of burnout, with qualitative findings indicating
that burnout was driven by the disproportionate time
workers spend on documenting in the electronic med-
ical record rather than with patients. To be sure, doing the
Depression Measure is not the primary cause of their doc-
umentary woes. However, it is part of the suite of federally
mandated performance measures that is commonly cited as
contributing to the problem.
How, then, did the managers carry out a task that they
considered consequential yet trivial, which they promoted de-
spite their critiques? By taking the reader into three meetings,
I will show how the interaction ritual with role distance may
have helped (Table 2).
The meetings were chosen because they cut across manage-
ment roles and teams in their pursuit of different strategies to
improve performance on the Depression Measure. Yet despite
those differences, the managers comported to the Depression
Measure in remarkably similar ways, using an interaction
ritual with role distance where:
1.	 A speaker makes an announcement: the Network
underperformed on the Depression Measure.
2.	 The announcement is accompanied by or provokes com-
municative expressions that convey role distance.
3.	 Once those communicative expressions are released, the
speaker and her listeners return to their task to devise
strategies to improve performance on the Depression
Measure.
I have segmented the meetings into “announcement,” “role
distance,” and “return to task” to help the reader sense the
contours of the interaction ritual. The subheading, “analysis,”
contains my own interpretation of how this ritual might be
functioning to preserve self, foster collectiveness, and facili-
tate coordination in the meetings, which together might help
the street-level managers cope with their task.

The meetings
Meeting 1: coordinating on the Physician
Incentives Plan
The first meeting is with the Senior Clinical Leaders of each
medical discipline, and is led by the Chief Clinical Officer. In
this meeting, the Chief Clinical Officer will suggest adding the
Depression Measure to the Physician Incentives Plan, which
specifies the financial rewards that physicians receive based
on their performance against a host of performance meas-
ures. The proposal, however, provokes disagreement from the
Director of Nursing, who implies that it is unfair.
Announcement
The Senior Clinical Leaders are seated, shoulder-to-
shoulder, around a small conference table. They have es-
tablished the rhythm of their interaction. In response to
each of the Chief Clinical Officer’s proposed revisions, at
least one participant says “yes” or agrees to take the con-
versation “offline” for further deliberation while the other
participants imply noncommittal assent through silence.
With this flow, most proposed revisions do not spark con-
troversy. Instead, the flow contributes to the feeling of in-
terdisciplinary teamwork.
The Chief Clinical Officer widens her mouth into a grin,
then announces “Right now, the [behavioral health] measures
are, I mean they’re kinda made up.”
Role distance
The Chief Clinical Officer does not clarify what she means by
“kinda made up,” but the mischief in her grin and ambiguity
of her words conveys her own mocking stance toward the
Depression Measure. In response, the Senior Clinical Leaders
fling their heads back and expel a laugh, affirming her state-
ment and validating her humor by turning the Depression
Measure into a collective laughing matter.
Return to task
Soon, however, their laughter subsides into silence so as to let
the Chief Clinical Officer speak. She continues: “I would vote
for at least making the depression screening measure one [of
the physician’s incentives] because it’s one where we’re not
doing well on.”
Interlude
The Chief Clinical Officer’s humor and rationale of “we’re
not doing well” does not garner the Senior Leaders’ assent
because the Director of Nursing—champion of medical
assistants and mastermind of “Medical Assistant Appreciation
Breakfasts”—speaks. She commences with a tone suggestive
of confidence, “I don’t think we should have the depression
for any of the. . .” which then dwindles to the uncertainty of
an unfinished sentence. She makes a second attempt. “That is
a medical assistant-driven workflow and paying [physicians]
for something they have nothing to do with. . .” Though this,
too, fades without an end.
Despite the ambiguity of two unfinished sentences, her
meaning is clear. If medical assistants are primarily respon-
sible for checking the boxes, the physicians should not get
the financial rewards for those checked boxes. With the hint
of unfairness in the air, the room is silent. How will the Chief
Clinical Officer recover from the awkwardness of silence and
move on with the task?
Role distance
After a short-lived debate among the Senior Leaders about
who, in practice, checks the depression screening boxes, the
Chief Clinical Officer turns to the Director of Nursing and
says: “I tell my medical assistant that you’re doing it. And I
agree hopefully we can remove it soon, but we are not in the
top quartile for this. [. . .]. And I think it’s part of what you
said. It can’t just be, oh my medical assistants aren’t doing it
so I’m going to ignore it.” In this statement, the Chief Clinical
Officer discursively creates a distance between her puta-
tive personal opinions (“I agree hopefully we can remove it
soon”) and what her organizational role obliges her to do to
(“but we are not in the top quartile for this”). By invoking the
power of quartiles and ranks which comes with money, one
might sense that the regulatory and funding system is what
forces her unfair hand.

Return to task
The Director of Nursing ceases to disagree. After all, the ex-
istence of an organizational hierarchy does not mean people
do not and cannot disagree across hierarchical lines. It does
mean, however, that subordinates are expected to acquiesce
to superiors’ decisions when conflicts cannot be resolved. The
Director of Nursing folds her arms across her chest, casts her
eyes to the ground, and shakes her head while muttering, “It’s
not OK. I’m not comfortable with it.”
Thus, the Depression Measure was added to the Physician
Incentives Plan.
Analysis
The Chief Clinical Offer made the first role distancing move
through the subtle use of irony, implying that the managers
must assess the reality of the physician’s performance based
on a measure that they think is “kinda made up.” Thereafter,
her role distancing act grew into a social role distancing
act as the Senior Clinical Leaders responded with collective
laughter. As several scholars have noted, laughter can func-
tion to create the appearance and even feeling of collective-
ness (Collins 2005; Coser 1960; Hatch 1997; Pouthier 2017).
It can also function to provide a discursive space for ironies,
including the suspicion that clicking the Depression Measure
boxes is loosely related to improving patients’ mental health
outcomes, to exist without obliging the managers to directly
confront those ironies, enabling them to coordinate on car-
rying out their task (e.g., see Hatch and Erhlich 1993; Hoyle
and Wallace 2008; Johansson and Woodilla 2005).
Yet, the Director of Nursing defected from the interac-
tion ritual. She stepped away from her role expectation as a
member of the Senior Clinical Leadership team to laugh at,
then agree with, the proposed revision to add the Depression
Measure to the Physician Incentives Plan. Instead, she
stepped into another role, champion of medical assistants, by
defending the medical assistants’ right to receive fair remu-
neration for the work they do. We are therefore reminded that
a person is made up of multiple roles which she can switch
between, but are left wondering how that switch is activated.
And so the Chief Clinical Officer responded with a second
role distancing move. She discursively separated what she pu-
tatively wants (“I agree hopefully we can remove it soon”)
from what her organizational role demands (“but we are not
in the top quartile for this”), thereby preserving her “more-
human self” while transforming the Director of Nursing’s
point of disagreement into a matter that is out of her hands
(cf. Jarzabkowski and Lê 2017; Pouthier 2017). After all, can
the Chief Clinical Officer really be held responsible when she
works in a bizarre system where marginal differences in box-
clicking practices can tilt the Network from the first to second
quartile, which translates to less money to cover the cost of
care that patients require but cannot afford, and the wages of
medical assistants?
Meeting 2: agreeing on the piece of paper
The second meeting is with Middle Management, who are
expected to ensure their staff meet the performance targets
set by their organizational superiors. In this meeting, they will
learn that the Network underperformed on the Depression
Measure. The news will provoke a stylized response from the
managers, commencing with cries of vexation then tempering
into the reasoned pursuit of a solution. And in this pursuit,
the managers will implicitly blame the medical assistants’
“culture” for underperformance.
Announcement
Twenty-nine managers are inside a conference room, humming
with chatter. Like kids whispering in school assembly, some
managers are offering side commentary in hushed voices as
they watch the meeting facilitator speak. Amid this backdrop,
the meeting facilitator announces, “With adult depression
screening, we knew this was a struggle for us.” She pauses,
allowing the news to sink in. “We’re not used to seeing the
Network’s aggregate data look this way. So far below our
goal.”
Collective role distance
The hums of chatter dwindle to silence, then unravel into noise.
At first, the managers ask questions about the manner, se-
quence, and location of the Depression Measure’s clicks. One
manager leans forward, squints her eyes at the PowerPoint
slide, and says, “So it sounds to me what people are saying is
that there’s a box. That you click.” Soon after, their questions
escalate to cries of vexation. “It’s clicking the box. That’s the
problem!” cries one. “Just like [the] smoking [measure], you
just have to click,” cries another, which receives the affirming
reply, “It’s like the box that says advised to quit. It’s kinda
messed up.” One manager loudly whispers, “Yeah, not that
you actually did it, just that you said you did,” while his col-
league stifles a laugh while shooshing him silent with flailing
arms.
Return to task
Yet almost immediately, they temper their passions in pursuit
of a solution. They first implicate the physicians as probable
culprits. One manager offers, “I am mystified about the dif-
ficulty we are having about this one [. . .] Putting it in the
[Medical Assistant’s] Pre-Visit Workflow has not been suc-
cessful,” which receives the reply, “maybe it’s checking the
[physician’s follow-up] problem box.” Another affirms, “I feel
like at our site people [physicians] sometimes forget to do the
follow-up because the [medical assistants] are really good.”
Despite their initial suspicions that the physicians are to be
blamed, they pivot their focus to the medical assistants. One
manager suggests,
Maybe some role-playing with the [medical assistants]
getting them more comfortable about asking depression
cos I think culturally for a lot of people it’s a lot of discom-
fort from the [medical assistant’s] standpoint more so than
from the patient’s, sometimes.
Despite the equivocation of “maybe,” “I think,” and “some-
times,” culture offers a safe explanation for the managers to
cohere. Culture is sufficiently familiar to summon stereotyp-
ical thinking yet adequately ambiguous for the managers to
agree for different reasons. Culture may refer to the med-
ical assistants as a professional group. Culture may refer to
the dominant identities who make up the Network’s med-
ical assistants, many of whom are young Hispanic women.
Irrespective of what culture refers to, it presumably is the
cause of a “lot of discomfort” that the medical assistants feel
when they ask the depression screening questions. A Senior
Operations Leader thus offers,

Analysis
The middle managers made the first role distancing move
with cries of vexation—“It’s clicking the box. That’s the
problem!”; “it’s kinda messed up”—expressing their seem-
ingly uncontainable, heartfelt, “more-human” feelings to-
ward the Depression Measure (preserving self). Yet soon
after, they snapped back to their role expectation, to agree on
a solution that is practically feasible and financially sound.
Their synchronized pivot from vexation to role expectation
can function to create feelings of collectiveness, reminding
them that they share feelings of frustration over the incon-
gruity between the appearance and reality of checking the
Depression Measures’ boxes in the putative name of perfor-
mance, yet must nonetheless find solutions to get their staff to
check those boxes (cf. Collins 2005; Hatch and Erhlich 1993;
Pouthier 2017).
In this way, this interaction ritual with role distance may
provide a powerful yet economical way for managers to co-
ordinate on tasks that rub against intelligence (cf. Hoyle
and Wallace 2008; Jarzabkowski and Lê 2017). After all,
they did suspect that the underperformance problem was
due to physicians not checking the follow-up box (refer to
fig. 2). And I suspect the managers did not really believe
the medical assistants’ culture was to blame. For when the
managers and this ethnographer ask the medical assistants
why they do not screen for depression, they often point to
the patients’ discomfort when answering the questions in
prying earshot of guardians, partners, or other patients in
the public setting of the waiting room. In fact one manager
noted, “It’s also very challenging for our adolescent popu-
lation because we are asking the question with the parent
in the room.” Another commented, “I love the idea of a
written form but in the waiting room with the partner and
parent, sometimes the parent fills out those questionnaires
for the patients, and the partner as well.” Perhaps, then,
the managers agreed on the piece of paper because no one
strongly disagreed with the proposal and their organiza-
tional role compels them to do something practical about
the Depression Measure, even if they think it is “kinda
messed up.”
Meeting 3: training the residents to click “that darn
box”
At the Network, it is widely known that the medical residents,
or physicians in training, struggle to do the performance
measures because they have yet to learn where, how, and why
they must do them. In this meeting, the manager of process
improvement will attempt to redress these educational gaps
despite her own ambivalence about some of the performance
measures. She even conceded in an interview that “It’s [the
Depression Measure] kinda like a silly measure cos it literally
means going on a different page [in the electronic medical
records] and clicking a box.”
Announcement and role distance
It is Friday afternoon. Nine residents are seated at tables, ar-
ranged as an amphitheater, giving way to a stage on which
the manager of process improvement is delivering her pres-
entation. The energetic movements of her body compensate
for the residents’ seeming tiredness. Some are resting deep in
their chairs with arms folded and eyes glazed over; others are
openly typing on their laptops; others still are swiping and
typing on their phones. At least two have put on the impres-
sion of interest, nodding and maintaining eye contact as she
speaks.
With the aid of a PowerPoint presentation, she moves on to
the Depression Measure. With a tone and rhythm consistent
with her preceding sentences, she announces, “It’s one that
we continue to struggle with [. . .] So pay attention when
you’re documenting. It’s a box-clicking one [. . .] So like liter-
ally clicking that darn box.”
The residents’ response
The residents stir from their daze. They begin to chatter
among themselves. The chatter then unfurls into a series of
questions about the manner and content of box-clicking,
which is then pierced by the cry, “I’m not doing that!” The
room catapults to silence bar one resident who rolls his eyes,
clasps his cheeks with two hands, then expels a sigh. The
resident-crier continues, “So what? What is the reason that
the [Network] cares about these measures?” She pauses. “Is it
payment?” One resident extends his hand to the air and rubs
his thumb and index finger around an imaginary paper bill.
The room is silent.
Role distance
In the silence, the manager is faced with a choice. As the
manager of process improvement, she is expected to improve
the efficiency and quality of all aspects of the Network. She
could discursively align with what her organizational role
prescribes by stressing the importance of the Depression
Measure to improve patients’ lives and outcomes. Or she
could distance herself from that expectation. “That’s a good
question,” she replies. She appends her statement with an
elongated “Ummm. . .” as if to imply she is formulating a
well-crafted response, then answers with the elegant blunt-
ness of “Yeah.”
Collective role distance
The residents erupt in laughter, then expel cries of vexation.
Some point out the disjuncture between what is captured in
the box and what happens in the clinic: “Obviously there’s
a huge disconnect between clicking that box and what ac-
tually should be done”; “It’s not a one-size-fits-all.” Others
release frustration. “This is a joke”; “This stupid compliance
checkmark”; “We’re talking about compliance for sure.”
Others, still, make sport out of it: “Check a box and save a
life?”
Return to task
Once the residents’ uproar about box-clicking subsides,
the manager returns to her didactic task. She projects the

Analysis
In this interaction, the manager of process improvement
made the first role distancing move by trivializing the
Depression Measure as a “box-clicking” measure that relies
on “literally clicking that darn box.” In so doing, she is
hinting at a “more-human,” less-professional opinion about
the Depression Measure (preserve self), which may have
precipitated a shift in social dynamics between herself, the
residents, and the Depression Measure. For, with the intro-
duction of the phrase, “box-clicking,” which almost becomes
a figure of speech as the residents begin repeating “click,”
“clicking,” “clicky,” she invites the residents to express their
own critical distance from the Depression Measure. And
with the manager’s concession that the residents must click
the boxes for the sake of money and willingness to play along
with the residents’ critical tone of questioning, the manager
has facilitated the residents’ transition from frustrated, even
hostile, students into shrewd co-conspirators. From this, we
can appreciate how an interaction ritual with role distance
can foster feelings of collectiveness and facilitate coordi-
nation. For with this ritual, they have come to terms with
the ironic constitution of their clinical world where a “little
clicky,” “squeeeeeny little” follow-up box is big enough to
squeeze compliance, money, and depression into its four tiny
edges, prodding the residents to fulfill the task to click that
“darn box.”
Discussion and conclusion
How do managers cope with the multiple and often con-
tradictory demands of their work? Drawing on sociological
insights, I posited that one way that street-level managers
cope is through performing an interaction ritual with role
distance, which can function to preserve self, foster collec-
tiveness, and facilitate coordination. By creating a separation
between one’s “more-human self” and organizational self, the
managers can acknowledge, then sidestep, the contradictions
between policy and practice, what they want and what they
must do, enabling them to carry out their task while holding
in abeyance individual and collective responsibility for the
decisions they make.
In this way, role distance might serve the individual’s in-
terest: it might relieve the person’s feelings of dissatisfaction
or even inauthenticity by allowing her to express her opinion,
show a degree of refusal to live up to role expectations, and
maintain some semblance of her putatively “more-human
self” on the job. It may also serve the organization’s interests:
it can help organizational members build camaraderie within
and across hierarchical lines, transform vexing organizational
objects, such as checkboxes and performance measures, into
symbols of group solidarity, and make policy implementation
work without having to resolve the contradictions inherent
in their work. Thus, unlike previous studies where managers
cope by bringing contradictory expectations into better align-
ment (Gassner and Gofen 2018; Hupe and Keiser 2019;
Klemsdal, Andreassen, and Breit 2022), satisfying one set of
expectations at the expense of others (Dias and Maynard-
Moody 2006; Spitzmueller 2018), or employing psycholog-
ical processes to bring contradictory cognitions into harmony
(Pandey and Welch 2005; Zacka 2017), role distance offers
a way for people to live with contradictions as part of their
experiential reality and get on with their tasks.
Despite these advantages, an interaction ritual with role
distance might also enable some professionals to reenact a set
of organizational roles and practices that rubs against their
preferences, well-intentions, and values. At the Network,
I suggested that role distancing was at play when some
managers sidestepped their discomfort of delegating a box-
clicking task that no one wants to do to the medical assistants
(meetings 1 and 2), ceased questioning the fairness of unevenly
distributing rewards and responsibilities despite residing in a
cultural world bound by concerns for fairness (meeting 1),
and diverted their attention from physicians not checking the
box to the medical assistants’ “culture” (meeting 2).
How might this ritual occasion such unintended
consequences? Sociologists have speculated that the re-
peated act of separating one’s putative private desires from
what one’s role demands might reproduce the feeling that
the “system”—bureaucratic, regulatory, societal, and so on—
has an agency of its own, leaving little agency for humans to
create change (cf. Gouldner 1971, pp. 378–90). Role distance
may consequently deplete some peoples’ willingness to stand
by internal feelings of right and wrong, so that they choose
instead to acquiesce to role expectations that are conditioned
on the “system.” It can enable well-intentioned people with
intelligent critiques of the system to reify the system in a way
that absolves their own complicity in reproducing the system.
Even if street-level managers reside in structurally deter-
mined systems, a central point made in the street-level and
public management literatures is that managers have a degree
of agency. They can implement initiatives to create meaningful
work environments amendable to organizational change
(Evans 2015, 2016; Hupe and Keiser 2019; Moynihan and
Pandey 2005). How, then, can these observations about role
distance be used toward more productive ends? At the level of
practice, organizations must continue to provide avenues for
feedback for when managers disagree with policies and prac-
tice, encouraging them to embrace and take charge of their
work rather than distancing themselves from it. At the level
of pedagogy, in addition to teaching street-level managers the
technical and theoretical tools of the trade, we must elucidate
some behavioral tendencies of the trade. We must identify and
explain how little interaction rituals, patterned and aggregated,

can cumulate to reproduce larger dynamics such as the quality
of teamwork and interpersonal relationships. From this, we
can invite managers to take seriously the small. We might, for
example, explain that when people distance themselves from
their work, even through signs as seemingly trivial as a repet-
itive joke about a vexing organizational task, those signs may
permit latent problems to rise to social awareness, becoming
the locus of organizational change (Hatch and Erhlich 1993;
Jarzabkowski and Lê 2017). For, just as small actions can be
complicit in reproducing the system, changing small actions
bit by bit can also cumulate to change the system.
This research was conducted in the healthcare domain, which
limits its resonance with other policy domains. Healthcare or-
ganizations in the US are imbricated with rules and regulations
imposed by government, regulatory, legal, professional, and
scientific bodies. They are also riddled with threats of legal lia-
bility because the services they provide can affect the life, death,
and good health of people, which places strong constraints on
the degree of discretion that managers can exercise. Future re-
search may therefore ask, what coping strategies, including that
of role distance, are employed in other organizational types
and policy domains where managers have different degrees of
discretion? How might the direction and strength of role ex-
pectations, such as top-down pressures from regulatory and or-
ganizational superiors, or bottom-up pressures from staff and
clients, mediate how street-level managers cope? And how do
asymmetries in power work in all of this?
Finally, this article is part of a broader agenda to high-
light the value of ethnographic, highly interpretive, research
in public administration scholarship. While the limitations of
this type of research are well established, what this research
does offer is the capacity to draw attention to previously
overlooked social dynamics, foster a sympathy for groups
that do not naturally elicit our sympathy, and provide an im-
aginative redescription of scenes that should be familiar to
the reader’s everyday experience while offering an element of
surprise. Yet as an interpretive project, you may not be con-
vinced by my analysis. Differences in interpretations, how-
ever, does not undermine this research but reveal how the
same observations are open to difference. And by inviting
you, dear reader, to have the final say, you have been brought
along this interpretive ride.

Data availability
The data underlying this article cannot be publicly shared to
protect the privacy of individuals who participated in the study,
and because the author promised her research participants
that their data would not be shared when they gave their in-
formed consent to participate in the study. Nonetheless, the
online appendix contains conceptual notes on the coding
process and offers data coding examples. Information about
the author’s positionality, the data collected for this study,
and a detailed case description is available for download
via: https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/4768?ln=en. This
research has been approved by the University of Chicago’s
Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 19-1131).

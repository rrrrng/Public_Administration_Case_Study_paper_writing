ABSTRACT
This study examines the role of public support, climate risks, and local government capacity in the adoption of innovative climate
adaptation measures, such as nature-­based solutions (NBS; e.g., green infrastructure, urban greening initiatives). I collected
climate policy documents from 20 US cities published after 2010 to investigate whether each city has adopted NBS in its climate
plans. Using qualitative comparative analysis, I found that: (1) high public support alone is neither necessary nor sufficient for
NBS adoption, (2) high public support and high climate risk can jointly promote NBS adoption, (3) when public support or climate
risk are low, high local government capacity alone can sufficiently enable NBS adoption. This paper ends with a discussion of
how local governments prioritize resources and efforts to strengthen their resilience to the impacts of climate change.
1   |   Introduction
The effects of extreme temperatures and precipitation pat-
terns are already being felt in all aspects of urban life (Melillo
et  al.  2014). In the United States, a significant proportion of
major disaster events (e.g., extreme heat, flooding, wildfires,
tornadoes, hurricanes) occurred in urban areas between 2006
and 2021 (Stephens et al. 2024). For example, the severity and
frequency of heat waves have exceeded the level that previ-
ous climate models considered virtually impossible (Bartusek
et  al.  2022; Philip et  al.  2021), and heat-­related injuries and
deaths increased significantly in the United States between 2016
and 2023 (Howard et al. 2024). Hurricanes and storms not only
cause substantial loss of life but also result in billion-­dollar prop-
erty damage (Stephens et al. 2024).
The growing emphasis on climate adaptation measures reflects
both the increasing frequency of climate-­related disasters and
the insufficient global progress in reducing greenhouse gas
(GHG) emissions to safe levels (Khan and Roberts 2013). While
climate mitigation efforts remain crucial, cities also face an
urgency to adapt to existing and future climate impacts. The
recent Conference of Parties (COPs) meetings have made prog-
ress in international agreements on reducing deforestation and
financing adaptation measures, but these efforts still fall short
of yielding aggressive, immediate policy actions needed at the
global level (Nyirenda 2023). In this context, local governments
become central in filling the gap for bold adaptation actions, as
the impacts of climate change and potential solutions are pri-
marily based at the local level (Vogel and Henstra 2015).
Nature-­based solutions (NBS) have emerged as a promising
approach for cities to improve climate resilience in innovative
ways (Escobedo et al. 2019; Frantzeskaki 2019; Frantzeskaki,
McPhearson, et al. 2019; Marcus 2022). NBS integrate protec-
tion, management, and enhancement of ecosystems to tackle
diverse societal challenges, enabling cities to simultaneously
mitigate and adapt to the impacts of climate change while
promoting human well-­being and biodiversity conservation
(Cohen-­Shacham et  al.  2016). The multitude of benefits that
NBS provide for people and nature in urban environments can-
not be easily substituted by artificial infrastructure and tech-
nology (IPBES  2019). NBS actions can be tailored to fit local
ecosystems, thereby addressing context-­specific climate risks in

This study investigates the conditions under which cities adopt
NBS to enhance their climate resilience. Studies have consistently
shown that strong public support is crucial for adopting local cli-
mate policies (Flynn et al. 2019; Krause 2012; Massey et al. 2014;
Yeganeh et al. 2020). This knowledge has been generated by exam-
ining whether various measures of public support (e.g., supportive
public opinion, higher education level, and democratic leaning)
are statistically significantly associated with the adoption of local
climate policy. However, statistical analysis assumes symmetrical
relationships—the presence of public support measures positively
correlates with local climate policy adoption, and its absence neg-
atively correlates with non-­adoption. This presumed symmetry
may not accurately reflect the complex dynamics of local policy-
making factors contributing to the adoption of climate adaptation
policies. The factors that enable policy adoption may operate dif-
ferently across varying local contexts, highlighting the need for a
more nuanced understanding of how social, environmental, and
institutional factors interact to shape policy adoption outcomes.
Local climate adaptation policy presents an ideal context for ex-
amining these interactions.
This study advances the local climate policy adoption literature
by introducing an innovative methodological approach, specifi-
cally addressing the understudied domain of climate adaptation
policy adoption. Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) com-
bines the strengths of case-­oriented qualitative analysis with
systematic cross-­case comparison to identify multiple pathways
leading to social outcomes (Ragin 2000). Using QCA, I examine
how the combinations of key climate policy adoption factors can
explain the initiation of NBS in US cities since 2010. Specifically,
the study asks two research questions: (1) whether local govern-
ments adopt an innovative climate adaptation policy (i.e., NBS)
in their policy documents (i.e., Climate Action Plans (CAP) and
Climate Adaptation Plans (CAAP) released after 2010) and (2)
how key policy adoption factors are combined for local govern-
ments to adopt such an innovative solution. For local govern-
ments seeking to adopt climate adaptation policies, the findings
offer context-­specific recommendations based on the experi-
ences of cities operating under similar institutional and social
conditions.
2   |   Nature-­Based Solutions as Climate Adaptation
Policy
Government efforts to make urban systems more adaptive to the
impacts of climate change vary in scope. Per different scopes, the
spectrum of planning documents spans from focusing on a sin-
gle natural hazard (e.g., single-­impact plan) to encompassing all
aspects of urban systems (e.g., comprehensive plan) (Figure 1).
Between the narrow-­scoped hazard mitigation plans and broad-­
scoped comprehensive plans, plans specifically focusing on
climate hazards are situated. While activities of climate adap-
tation and hazard mitigation efforts overlap (Blanco et al. 2009;
Dodman and Satterthwaite 2008; Thomalla et al. 2006), local
climate action plans can be distinguished based on the source of
natural hazards—non-­climatic hazards (e.g., earthquakes) ver-
sus climatic hazards (e.g., extreme heat waves)—and types of
interventions such as land use planning versus urban greening
(Lyles et al. 2018).
Because broad-­scoped plans (e.g., comprehensive plans) focus
on diverse urban systems beyond managing the risks of natu-
ral hazards, they may not include a substantial portion of cli-
mate adaptation efforts. The broad plan is also found to perform
less effectively when addressing risks of climate change than
narrower-­scoped plans with specific climate policies, such
as CAP and CAAP (Lyles et al. 2018). CAPs and CAAPs both
include responses to a wide range of climate change impacts

2.1   |   Nature-­Based Solutions (NBS)
NBS are actions to protect, manage, restore, or create ecosys-
tems that can effectively address societal challenges while en-
suring human and environmental well-­being (Babí Almenar
et al. 2021; Cohen-­Shacham et al. 2016). As an umbrella concept
that encompasses previous greening initiatives, NBS serve as a
“policy-­relevant pot” from which policymakers and research-
ers can devise sustainable practices (Nature Editorial  2017).
Bona et al. (2023) categorized NBS into six different types (see
Table 1).
In the United States, a local guide by the Federal Emergency
Management Agency (FEMA) provides examples of various
NBS activities (FEMA 2021). Green buildings, which include
vertical gardens and green roofs, can effectively alleviate
urban heat island effects in densely populated urban areas
(Dong et al. 2020). Blue infrastructure utilizes water bodies,
such as ponds, wetlands, rivers, estuaries, and riparian areas,
showing effectiveness in flood mitigation and water quality
improvement (Depietri and McPhearson  2017). Community
gardens contribute to urban agriculture by providing access
to food and enhancing soil quality (Bona et al. 2023). Green
areas designed for soil and water management (e.g., vegetated
swales, rain gardens, renaturing abandoned areas, previous
pavement, and opportunity plots) have demonstrated the abil-
ity to reduce local flooding (Kabisch et al. 2017). Infrastructure
incorporating green features is a strategically planned and de-
signed interconnected network of natural and semi-­natural
elements, including greenways, ecological corridors, and
tree-­canopied streets, which benefit stormwater management,
temperature regulation, and biodiversity increase (European
Commission  2013; Cohen-­Shacham et  al.  2016). In urban
contexts, parks and urban forests aim to provide equitable
environmental and health benefits (Kim et  al.  2023). These
benefits include carbon sequestration, cooling cities, and re-
ducing risks of floods, soil erosion, and landslides (Seddon
et al. 2021). Therefore, NBS refers to a broad array of actions
that local governments can consider as a strategy for climate
adaptation.

3.1   |   Public Support for Climate Policy
Research shows that community characteristics—including
environmental attitudes, educational attainment, and political
ideology—are significant predictors of local climate policy adop-
tion. Scholars have used different terminologies to refer to these
characteristics, such as public support, civic capacity, commu-
nity interest, citizen preferences, and organized interests, de-
pending upon research contexts (Krause 2012; Sharp et al. 2011;
Switzer 2019; Zahran et al. 2008). I use the term public support
in this paper in order to refer to the local citizens' collective at-
titudes and behaviors toward policies that impact the environ-
ment (Wan et al. 2017).
Studies have suggested that public opinion plays a significant
role in the adoption of climate mitigation policies (Anderson
et al. 2017; Tjernström and Tietenberg 2008). Those who support
climate mitigation strategies also tend to endorse climate adap-
tation strategies (Brügger et al. 2015). Specifically, a high level
of public awareness regarding the impacts of climate change has
been shown to be essential for supporting climate adaptation ac-
tions (Flynn et al. 2019; Massey et al. 2014). The public tends to
support climate policies if they believe in anthropogenic global
warming as long as the policy does not incur much cost to citi-
zens (Bakaki and Bernauer 2017; Fairbrother 2022).
Education levels contribute to the ability to evaluate and build
knowledge about climate change issues to form a supportive
attitude toward climate policy (Kollmuss and Agyeman 2002;
Torgler and García-­Valiñas 2007). Krosnick et al. (2006) found
that people with higher cognitive skills were more likely to accept
scientific information conveyed through media. Unfamiliarity
with climate change science or insufficient personal knowledge
tends to influence people to be less supportive of climate pol-
icy, which consequently affects national climate policy actions
(Tjernström and Tietenberg 2008). At the local level, education
also plays an important role in fostering public engagement and
participation in the democratic process of environmental policy-
making (Opp et al. 2014). Therefore, the level of education is used
as an indicator of citizens' support toward climate protection
and has been associated with the local adoption of mitigation
and adaptation climate policies (Kalafatis 2018; Krause 2012).
Not only education levels but also political leaning influence
supportive attitudes toward climate policy (Drews and van den
Bergh  2016; Tjernström and Tietenberg  2008). Public opinion
driven by liberal political ideology is found to be associated with
a stronger concern for environmental issues and the tendency
to adopt climate policy (Giordono et al. 2020; Rai 2020). While
these studies often use partisanship data on presidential elec-
tions, the political ideology of higher levels of government has
little influence over local policy decisions in the context of US
climate policies (Gerber 2013). Instead, a mayor's party affilia-
tion indicates public opinion on the expected local policy out-
comes (de Benedictis-­Kessner and Warshaw 2016; Tausanovitch
and Warshaw 2014). Localities with Democratic electorates and
elected Democrats show a strong association with the adoption
of local climate policies (Gerber 2013).
The studies reviewed above often show a statistical tendency be-
tween public support measures and climate policy adoption. As
a result, we can infer that the NBS policy tends to be adopted
when public support is high, whereas the policy may not be ad-
opted when public support is low. These findings do not nec-
essarily indicate whether local climate policy adoption requires
public support as a prerequisite (i.e., necessary) condition. If it is
a prerequisite, local governments of cities without high public
support may experience challenges in enhancing climate resil-
ience, given that “climate change” is a politically charged term.
If it is not a necessary condition, we can further inquire under
what circumstances cities with low public support can adopt cli-
mate adaptation policies.
3.2   |   Climate Risk
Experiencing extreme events is a critical factor that drives gov-
ernments to take policy actions (Birkland 2006). Both the fre-
quency of events (e.g., number, long-­term patterns) and their
severity (e.g., economic losses, fatalities) shape the adoption of
climate adaptation policy (Braunschweiger and Ingold  2023;
Giordono et  al.  2020; Kalafatis  2018; Rai  2020). While some
disaster strategies respond to historically experienced events
(such as past flooding), cities also develop strategies to prepare
for potential disasters not yet experienced locally, such as ex-
treme heat from rising temperatures (Nohrstedt et  al.  2022).
Therefore, climate adaptation actions reflect consideration of
both actual climate risks based on past disaster experiences and
projected risks from possible climate threats. Brody et al. (2008)
demonstrated that counties with higher climate risk—measured
through a combination of past experiences (i.e., casualties) and
future projections (i.e., coastal proximity, expected temperature
change from 2004 to 2099)—are more likely to adopt climate
policies.
The impact of disasters can act as a change agent to raise citi-
zens' awareness and perception of future risks that can lead to
disaster responses (Birkmann et al. 2010). Perceived risks are

on the risk perception of government officials or planners (e.g.,

to likely support climate adaptation policy (van Valkengoed

ing is happening and humans are responsible for the cause, they
are more likely to engage in collective climate change activism,
including voting for candidates who support adaptation policies
(Latkin et al. 2023; Roser-­Renouf et al. 2014).
Both actual and perceived climate risks were conceived as
important drivers of climate change policy adoption (Dilling
et al. 2017; Rai 2020; Shi et al. 2015). Yet, a few studies found
that actual climate risk (e.g., coastal area) is not necessarily a
significant driver for adopting climate policies compared to
public support measures, such as high income, education, and
environmental concern (Brody et al. 2008; Zahran et al. 2008).
Nohrstedt et al. (2022) concluded that it is not necessarily ac-
tual risk that drives the adoption of climate adaptation policy
unless cities have the institutional capacity to utilize the knowl-
edge required in adaptation policymaking. Therefore, the role
of climate risk in the successful adoption of climate adaptation
policies may depend on government capacity.
3.3   |   Government Capacity
The government's capacity to adapt to climate change is a
fuzzy concept to operationalize, considering the expansive
boundary of the governance system. Multiple variables were
tested to capture this condition, from transnational networks,
such as the International Council on Local Environmental
Initiatives (ICLEI) (Sharp et al. 2011; Krause et al. 2016), to
fiscal and staff capacity (Krause  2013). Having a member-
ship in the ICLEI increases the chance of adoption of cli-
mate policy because ICLEI can facilitate learning and enrich
government capacity to advance urban environmental plan-
ning (Sharp et  al.  2011; Frantzeskaki, Buchel, et  al.  2019).
Yeganeh's meta-­analysis (Yeganeh et al. 2020) shows that in-
tergovernmental dependence, which refers to the percentage
of city revenue from intergovernmental transfers, was found
to decrease the adoption of climate policy, but climate policy
adoption is driven more by the presence of dedicated sustain-
ability units (e.g., Chief Sustainability Officer), staff support
for climate initiatives, and environmental entrepreneurs than
fiscal independence.
Specifically, NBS require highly technical knowledge, expertise,
and experience in a comprehensive understanding of natural
ecosystems and their delicate impacts on society and biodi-
versity (Kabisch et al. 2016). Local governments often lack the
technical capacities and experience to seek and secure grants for
sustainability projects (Deslatte et al. 2023). Having a staff ded-
icated to sustainability is a crucial step to increase the uptake
and implementation of NBS (Sarabi et al. 2019), as it represents
the local government's determination to pursue substantive sus-
tainability actions (Hawkins et al. 2016). Managerial leadership
plays a crucial role in the diffusion of climate policy innovation
among municipal governments (Yi and Chen 2019). Local gov-
ernments can leverage the leadership and expertise of dedicated
sustainability staff and knowledge gained from ICLEI member-
ship to secure funding and navigate challenges in initiating NBS
actions for climate adaptation.

summarizes the theoretical expectations of the alignment of the
conditions and expected policy outcomes. (1) Strong evidence of
public support in the climate policy literature leads to testing if
high public support is a prerequisite for local climate change ad-
aptation. (2) The role of climate risk in explaining the adoption
of local climate adaptation policy may be conditional on govern-
ment capacity. (3) High government capacity may facilitate local
policy adoption when public support or climate risk is low, but it
may be insufficient to explain the policy adoption if both public
support and climate risk are low. To examine these conjunctural
hypotheses, I employ QCA.
4   |   Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA)
QCA is a research approach suitable for examining a spatial phe-
nomenon and related governing efforts that are sensitive to the
local context and its complexities (Kim and Verweij 2016; Verweij
and Trell 2019; Wang et al. 2023). This approach requires iden-
tifying the critical conditions to be considered in the analysis
and assigning the membership of cases to the conditions using
binary, fuzzy, or multi-­values. Identifying key conditions relies
on a theory or a framework, while assigning membership can
rely on analytical techniques such as clustering in the absence of
a solid theoretical guide for deciding membership.

single (or combinations of) explanatory condition is neces-
sary or sufficient to drive an outcome condition (e.g., climate

4.1   |   Cases
While NBS offers numerous benefits, including biodiversity en-
hancement, pollution control, and carbon sequestration, its most
relevant applications for climate adaptation are heat mitigation
and flood management (Quaranta et al. 2021; Zölch et al. 2016).
Given this, it is reasonable to consider cities experiencing rising
temperatures and/or severe storms as case studies. However,
since all regions of the United States experienced overall in-
creases in extreme precipitation events in the early 21st cen-
tury (Janssen et al. 2014; Stephens et al. 2024), flood risk alone
would not provide sufficient differentiation for case selection.
However, I incorporated flood-­related fatalities as a climate
risk indicator, recognizing NBS' effectiveness in mitigating this
threat. Therefore, this study prioritizes cities facing extreme heat
challenges as a case selection criterion, particularly those with
significant temperature disparities in historically disadvantaged
neighborhoods—areas that have experienced systematic disin-
vestment due to discriminatory practices like redlining. This ap-
proach is selected because widely adopted NBS strategies, such
as urban greening initiatives, are designed to address both heat
vulnerability and environmental justice concerns simultane-
ously. The research by Hoffman et al. (2020), which documented
temperature differences between redlined and non-­redlined
areas across 108 US cities, provided empirical evidence for these
disparities and informed this study's methodology by identify-
ing cities with the most pronounced heat inequities for further
investigation.
The selection criteria included cities that: (1) experienced signif-
icant temperature differences (exceeding 2°C) between redlined
and non-­redlined areas (i.e., A-­grade areas) in 2017 (Hoffman
et al. 2020); (2) had a population of more than 200,000 in 2017
(U.S. Census 2017); and (3) represented all four US regions (i.e.,
Midwest, Northeast, South, and West). Among the 32 cities that
met the temperature and population criteria, 17 cities were se-
lected based on the extreme temperature differences, ensuring
regional representation (3–4 cities per region). Additionally, I
included Birmingham, AL, and Chattanooga, TN, in the South
region, which did not meet the population threshold but ex-
hibited greater temperature disparities between redlined and
non-­redlined areas (Hoffman et al. 2020), and also Detroit, MI,
in the Midwest region that showed a temperature disparity of

4.2   |   Outcome Condition and Data
To identify a local government's climate adaptation policies
in the form of NBS after 2010 (outcome condition), I collected
the local government's climate action plans (CAP) and climate
adaptation plans (CAAP) from the city government's website.
While the term climate change adaptation emerged in the early
2000s, adaptation planning in the US and the term nature-­based
solutions were still at an inchoate stage until 2015 (Escobedo
et  al.  2019; Lyles et  al.  2018). CAP is thought to be designed
predominantly with tools for mitigation (Deetjen et  al.  2018);
however, planning for mitigation and adaptation does not nec-
essarily require mutually exclusive measures. Often, CAP in-
cludes visions and tools for both carbon emission reductions and
climate resilience and adaptation.
When conducting content analysis to identify NBS in the policy
documents, I used the smallest analytical coding unit—one word
(seme) whose combinations could represent a research object
(Greimas 1983). For example, “green” + “roof,” “green” + “space,”
or “community” + “garden” were combinations of semes used to
capture NBS identified in Table 1. I then carefully reviewed the
texts containing these combinations to determine whether the
city had initiated NBS. Because this study focuses on identifying
whether cities adopt NBS initiatives in their climate plans, the
finding was simplified as a binary outcome. For example, if a
city adopted a tree planting program to reduce the urban heat
island effect, the city was considered as having NBS as a climate
adaptation strategy.
For a city to be assigned as having a positive outcome for the
dependent variable, it must meet two conditions: having a cli-
mate plan and explicitly incorporating NBS in the plan. Figure 2
shows that 15 of the 20 cities incorporated NBS into at least one
climate plan adopted after 2010 (coded as (1) for NBS initiated). I
assigned a code of (0) to cities that either 1) lack a CAP or CAAP
or 2) have a CAP or CAAP but do not include NBS initiatives.
All cities in this study that have at least one CAP or CAAP in-
clude NBS. Therefore, cities assigned with (0) are those who do
not have a CAP or CAAP (Jacksonville, FL; Indianapolis, IN;
Birmingham, AL; Miami, FL; and Los Angeles, CA).

4.3.2   |   Climate Risk (CR)

Government capacity is captured using the ICLEI member-

5   |   Results
5.1   |   High Public Support Is Not Necessary
for Climate Adaptation Policy Adoption
None of the explanatory conditions, including high public sup-
port, were necessary conditions for initiating NBS (Appendix 2
in Supporting Information). Some cities initiated NBS in the
absence of high public support: Baltimore, Chattanooga, Dallas,
Detroit, Louisville, New Orleans, and Philadelphia. When three
indicators that make up the public support condition in Table 3
were tested separately for necessity analysis, the democratic
leaning variable was the only indicator close to necessary for the
initiation of NBS. However, Chattanooga adopted NBS, while
the city did not have a Democratic mayor around the time that
their CAAP was released in 2022. Therefore, high public sup-
port, in general, is not a prerequisite for the adoption of local
climate adaptation policy, but having a democratic mayor,
in particular, may be an important driver for such politically
charged policy issues like “climate change.” The first theoretical
expectation regarding public support as a necessary condition
was not supported.

5.2   |   Sufficient Pathways for NBS Adoption
Four models were examined for sufficiency analyses for NBS
adoption (Table 4). The first model (Model 1) is the base model,
which includes three critical conditions: Public Support (PS),
Climate Risk (CR), and Government Capacity (GC) with values
of 1 (high) or 0 (low). Model 2 expands the base model by in-
cluding public support and climate risk as they were in Model 1,
but it differs by types of government capacity (multi-­value) to ex-
amine the roles of external and internal government resources
separately. Models 3 and 4 test different types of climate risks
(i.e., Actual Risk (A_CR) and Perceived Risk (P_CR)), expand-
ing Model 1. See Appendix 1 in Supporting Information for raw
data and the assignment of membership of these conditions.
In Table 4, the first path type (PS*CR-­focused) suggests the adop-
tion of NBS based on the combination of high public support and
high climate risk. While high public support was not necessary
for NBS to be adopted, it was sufficient to adopt NBS when com-
bined with high climate risk in Model 1. When climate risk was
tested separately, Models 3 and 4 produced similar results that
each type of high climate risk combined with high public sup-
port is sufficient for the NBS adoption. The identified cities in
Models 1, 3, and 4 overlap (compare Appendices 3, 5, and 6 in
Supporting Information): Atlanta, Chicago, and New York City
in models 1 and 3 were also part of cities identified in Model 4,
along with Boston, Denver, Minneapolis, and Portland. This is
because Atlanta, Chicago, and New York City have both high
actual and perceived climate risks. Contrary to the second theo-
retical expectation based on previous research, climate risk was
combined with public support rather than government capacity
in explaining the adoption of local climate adaptation policy.
The results of sufficiency analyses show a lack of distinctive
paths for the climate risk condition. Climate risk alone was not
a sufficient condition for NBS adoption. When high climate risk
was presented, it was always part of the sufficient combinations
regardless of its type (actual, perceived, or both). While high cli-
mate risk was insufficient alone, it was necessary to combine
with high public support to lead to NBS adoption sufficiently.
The second path type (GC-­focused) suggests that government
capacity is a sufficiently effective condition for NBS adoption.
In Models 1, 3, and 4, high government capacity was a single
sufficient condition to adopt NBS. Identified paths in these mod-
els highlight the initiation of NBS led by strong government ca-
pacity, not necessarily by high public support nor climate risk
alone. Those cities that had low public support but adopted NBS
with at least some level of government capacity were Baltimore,
Chattanooga, Dallas, Louisville, New Orleans, and Philadelphia
(cities that adopted NBS despite low public support) (see
Appendix 1 in Supporting Information). Of the six cities, three
had internal government resources (Baltimore, Chattanooga,
and Louisville), one had external government resources (New
Orleans), and two had both internal and external government
resources (Dallas and Philadelphia).

6   |   Discussion and Conclusion
Research on local climate policy adoption has examined several
key explanatory factors. Among them, public support measures
(e.g., supportive public opinion, higher education level, and dem-
ocratic leaning) are often found to be the most significant factors
(Flynn et al. 2019; Massey et al. 2014; Opp et al. 2014; Portney
and Berry 2010; Rai 2020; Yeganeh et al. 2020). This raises the
question of whether high public support is a necessary condition
for the adoption of local climate adaptation measures such as
NBS. The statistical analysis does not provide an answer to this
question. Instead, the regression results imply that if cities have
low public support, there is little chance of adopting climate ad-
aptation measures. This becomes problematic if public support is
indeed a prerequisite when cities with low public support strug-
gle to increase their climate resilience to reduce the impacts of
future climate hazards by implementing adaptation policies.
A key finding of this study is that not only is high public support
unnecessary, but it alone is insufficient for NBS adoption. While
public support may be crucial for climate mitigation policy adop-
tion (which is studied more frequently), it alone may not guarantee
the adoption of climate adaptation measures. Similarly, climate
risk alone may be necessary or sufficient to explain hazard miti-
gation policy adoption but cannot fully account for climate adap-
tation policy adoption. Cities adopt NBS when both high public
support and high climate risk (PC*CR-­focused paths) are present,
regardless of risk type. This finding reveals that climate adaptation
policy adoption follows distinct explanatory patterns from those
driving climate mitigation or hazard mitigation policy adoption.
To our surprise, climate risk was combined with public sup-
port, rather than government capacity, for a city to adopt NBS
in its climate plans. The combined path of public support and
climate risk suggests a complex relationship between the two
conditions. The existing literature has hinted at the mechanisms
behind this finding: Directly experiencing climate disasters (i.e.,
actual risk) can raise citizens' awareness of climate change and
its impacts, which can then intensify climate risk perceptions
(Birkmann et al. 2010). When citizens' education level is rela-
tively high enough to be able to assess and accept climate change
science and build knowledge, risk perceptions are likely to be re-
inforced to support climate policies and require concrete actions
(Kollmuss and Agyeman 2002; Krosnick et al. 2006; Tjernström
and Tietenberg  2008; Torgler and García-­Valiñas  2007). This
theoretical link requires further research to better understand
local climate adaptation policy adoption.
The finding also shows that government capacity alone can suf-
ficiently lead to the adoption of NBS. Those cities experiencing
barriers to policy adoption due to low public support can focus
on improving government capacity. Out of the 20 cities in the
analysis, 5—Birmingham, Indianapolis, Jacksonville, Los
Angeles, and Miami—had low public support and had not re-
leased climate policy documents. These cities could benefit from
the experience of those cities that also had low public support
but adopted NBS with at least some level of government capac-
ity—Baltimore, Chattanooga, Louisville, and New Orleans. This
finding indicates that government capacity, especially expertise
for sustainability or NBS, is a crucial sufficiency condition for
adopting NBS as climate adaptation measures, especially when
public support for climate change is low.
When the policy environment for climate adaptation was con-
sidered unsuitable due to low public support or low climate
risk, government resources (e.g., CSO and ICLEI) played a cen-
tral and particularly relevant role for those cities that initiated
NBS—Chattanooga, Louisville, and New Orleans. Cities with-
out climate risk do not mean that they are “climate havens”
where people can migrate safely from climate threats. Since cit-
ies with low climate risk may not escape future climate risks and
social stressors due to climate migration, investments in infra-
structure for water and wastewater management and climate re-
silience efforts are critical (Marandi and Main 2021). Therefore,
low public support should not impede cities from enhancing
their climate resilience through NBS adoption, even when there
seems to be low climate risk in their cities because the lack of
adaptation jeopardizes public safety when unprecedented and
unexpected climate disaster occurs (e.g., Hurricane Helene in
Asheville, NC in 2024).
Planners and government officials of cities where public support
for climate change issues is low and imminent climate threats
may seem absent can consider channeling their attention to
increasing government capacity to initiate innovative climate
adaptation policies. Besides hiring CSO, other strategies to in-
crease local government capacity include “utilizing informal
and shadow networks” (Pahl-­Wostl 2009), such as working with
citizen groups or making alliances with other local governments
(Moloney and Fünfgeld 2015). Being a member of external net-
works like ICLEI may facilitate cities' efforts to enhance climate
resilience, even when either actual or perceived climate risk is
low, as in Boston, Denver, New Orleans, and Richmond.
The significance of government capacity (i.e., dedicated sus-
tainability staffing, ICLEI membership) for the adoption of
local climate adaptation policy is encouraging for cities. Even
in political circumstances that may appear unfavorable for the
use of terms related to climate change, local governments can
prioritize the enhancement of government capacity as a first
step to devising and adopting effective adaptation measures.
Specific expertise and understanding of the scientific and tech-
nical knowledge of climate hazards and impacts are essential for
local governments to be eligible for federal funding opportuni-
ties. Internal capacity, such as dedicated sustainability staffing
with adequate expertise, may also assist local governments in

A few limitations are worth mentioning. Relying solely on CAP
and CAAP to identify NBS adoption cannot conclusively deter-
mine the absence of NBS when cities do not have any of these
documents. For example, a potential reason for the city-­level ac-
tions missing in Los Angeles and Miami may be attributed to the
existence of countywide climate action plans (i.e., LA County
Climate Action Plan, Miami-­Dade County Action Plan). Second,
while this study defines government capacity based on the pres-
ence of dedicated sustainability personnel, I acknowledge that
this is one of several possible operationalizations that can in-
clude factors such as financial resources, interagency collabora-
tion, and leadership. Given methodological constraints and the
need for analytical clarity, empirically supported indicators that
are found to influence local climate policy adoption were prior-
itized. Third, this study's approach, based on previous research
(e.g., Hoffman et al. 2020), may overlook cities like Phoenix that
face acute heat risks. Future research could adopt alternative
criteria—such as overall heat vulnerability or rapid population
growth in arid regions—to better capture contexts like Phoenix.
Lastly, the choice of indicators used to measure and represent
explanatory conditions can be different, as any social research
is. For instance, the city's democratic leaning may be better
reflected through the percentage of voters in the presidential
election than mayoral party affiliation. Nonetheless, climate
policy documents are often proposed and endorsed by mayoral
leadership.
In conclusion, climate adaptation policies such as NBS are a
crucial first step for cities confronting climate change impacts,
arguably the most pressing issue of our era. This research ad-
vances both theoretical understanding and practical insights re-
garding local-­level climate adaptation policy. I argue that public
support, which is often identified as the most significant factor
of climate (mitigation) policy adoption, does not play alone as a
factor that sufficiently drives the adoption of local climate ad-
aptation policy. When there is high public backing and urgent
environmental pressures, cities can adopt NBS regardless of
government resources examined in this research (e.g., Atlanta).
Notably, this study finds that strong government capacity can
compensate for low public support or low climate risk for facili-
tating climate adaptation policy adoption. These findings point
to the need for future research to examine how such institu-
tional resources enable local climate adaptation policy.

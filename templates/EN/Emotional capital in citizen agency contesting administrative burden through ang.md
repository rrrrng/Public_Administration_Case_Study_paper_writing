Abstract
The literature on administrative burden has focused on cognitive, material, and social resources, leaving emotional strategies and processes
largely unexplored. This study begins to address this research gap by elaborating the concept of emotional capital in the context of citizen
agency. The article uses the concept of emotional capital to analyze claimant anger in response to administrative burdens examining the ques­
tion: how do citizens understand and maneuver the potential benefits and risks of expressing their anger when experiencing administrative
burdens? The article is based on ethnographic fieldwork in Danish job centers involving interviews with seventy-one claimants and observations
of ten conversations between caseworkers and claimants. The article contributes to theorize the role of emotions in citizen-state encounters by
showing that emotional capital works as a resource moderating the experience of and coping with administrative burden.
Key words: emotional capital; administrative burden; ethnography; citizen agency; emotions.

These two quotes from Danish benefit claimants reveal di­
rectly opposing views on the use of anger to gain influence in
employment services. They are evidence of a striking diversity
in disadvantaged claimants’ experiences of using emotions
to cope with administrative burden in the encounter with
the welfare state. Throughout the article, I examine anger
expressions in response to administrative burdens in Danish
employment services. I explore how claimants balance their
emotional displays in encounters with caseworkers to further
our understanding of the role of emotions in the agency of
people at the margins of the welfare state.
Disadvantaged claimants often have to cope with stig­
matization and shame, as well as feelings of stress, anxiety,
depression, and powerlessness as a consequence of an inse­
cure livelihood and multiple forms of disadvantage. This
emotional backdrop may hamper claimants’ voice, as they
feel disentitled to express their needs and wants. Emotions
are core to agency, since they drive or curtail action (Barbalet
1998), which makes it important to engage with emotions in
trying to understand citizen agency. Extant research suggests
that anger may be an outcome of administrative burdens
(Hattke, Hensel, and Kalucza 2019) and associated with
increased opposition in citizen encounters with the state (Bell,
Christensen, and Jessen Hansen 2022), making anger a rele­
vant starting point for understanding the role of emotions for
citizens contesting administrative burdens.
Employment services are an important arena for state
interventions in the lives of disadvantaged citizens. In this
context, anger can be a form of everyday resistance to the
individualizing, stigmatizing, and disciplining governance
of the unemployed (Peterie et al. 2019). While anger is as­
sociated with resistance, few studies have examined anger
in encounters between the state and citizens. Anger is a par­
ticularly active emotion that mobilizes energy to overcome
obstacles (Collins 1990: 43). Anger is a way of defending the
self against injustice and correcting the relationships with
others when others violate the person (Averill 1982; Lyman
1981). This suggests that anger may be a resource in contesting
administrative burden. However, anger may also disrupt
attempts to cope with or contest administrative burden (e.g.,
one may become too angry to think clearly). Research has
shown that the unemployed often feel shameful and hide their
anger (Peterie et al. 2019), however, little is known about how
claimants manage their anger and how claimants experience
the consequences of expressing anger in the encounter with
employment services. To begin to address these research gaps,

The article is based on ethnographic fieldwork in Danish

The article makes the following broad contributions: First,
it contributes to understanding the role of emotions in the
agency of disadvantaged citizens in citizen-state encounters
by expanding upon and detailing the concept of emotional
capital within the context of public administration research.
Emotional capital suggests that emotional energy, emotional
reflexivity, and emotion management may work as resources
citizens can use to contest core triggers of administrative
burden such as waiting time, communicative breakdowns, and
administrative errors (Holler and Tarshish 2022). Second, the
article contributes empirically by exploring how claimants
navigate the benefits and risks of expressing their anger when
experiencing administrative burdens. The article shows that
anger expressions can be fruitful from a claimant perspec­
tive, as claimants experience that their anger allows them to
set boundaries, account for resistance, convey neglected or
missing information, and mobilize energy. However, anger
expressions may also be risky from a claimant perspective, as
some claimants experience that their anger fosters mistrust,
redefines them as dangerous or mentally ill, or leads to an
increased emphasis on formal requirements and procedures,
thereby increasing the administrative burdens facing the
claimant. Finally, the article outlines a research agenda for
emotions in public administration.
The agency of citizens accessing public
services
The scholarship on administrative burdens represents a wel­
come focus on the bureaucracy from a citizen perspective
offering crucial insights into the costs imposed on citizens
and the inequities produced by “hidden politics” (Moynihan,
Herd, and Harvey 2015). While scholarship on administra­
tive burden emphasizes causes and consequences of admin­
istrative burden (e.g., Baekgaard et al. 2021; Brodkin and
Majmundar 2010; Döring and Madsen 2022; Herd and
Moynihan 2018; Moynihan, Herd, and Harvey 2015), studies
also attend to how citizens respond to and contest burdens
in the encounters with the state (e.g., Gilad and Assouline
2022; Holler and Tarshish 2022; Masood and Azfar Nisar
2021; Peeters and Campos 2021; Peeters and Laguna 2021).
Scholars have examined how citizens understand the causes
of administrative burdens (Barnes and Henly 2018; Nielsen,
Dall, and Madsen 2022), and developed typologies of cit­
izen strategies when navigating bureaucracies (Mik-Meyer
and Silverman 2019; Nielsen, Dall, and Madsen 2020; Nisar
2018; Peeters, and Laguna 2021). In this scholarship atten­
tion to cognitive processes (e.g., attributions, beliefs, expec­
tations) and material and cognitive resources (e.g., education,
income, knowledge) has been central (Baekgaard and Madsen
2024; Barnes and Henly 2018; Christensen et al. 2020; Danet
and Hartman 1972; Döring 2021; Gordon 1975; Hasenfeld
1985; Linos et al. 2022; Masood and Azfar Nisar 2021). This
cognitive focus leaves emotional strategies and processes
largely unexplored (for exceptions see Bell, Christensen, and
Jessen Hansen 2022; Peterie et al. 2019).
In understanding the agency of marginalized groups,
scholars emphasize that citizens have unequal access to re­
sources. Hasenfeld (1985) suggests that the power resources
of citizens (income and education) are crucial for their
possibilities of influencing frontline workers. Several studies
highlight the resources needed for citizens to exert influence
in encounters with the state, e.g., social capital (Döring and
Madsen 2022; Nisar 2018), administrative capital (Masood
and Azfar Nisar 2021), administrative literacy (Döring 2021),
bureaucratic competence (Gordon 1975), or claimant ca­
pacity (Brodkin and Majmundar 2010). Rather than focusing
on cognitive or material resources, this study focuses on the
role of emotions in the agency of citizens, a largely unex­
plored and undertheorized subject of research.
Typologies of citizen agency highlight citizen agency as
varying from everyday getting by to strategic and political
action (Lister 2004), or between roles as accommodators,
cooperators, activists, resisters, and fighters (Mik-Meyer
and Silverman 2019; Nielsen, Nielsen, and Bisgaard 2020).
Throughout the article, I suggest that emotions play a core
role as drivers of different forms of agency and that we need
to attend to emotions to understand why some citizens re­
spond to administrative burdens by accommodating and
others resist and fight.
Scott (1989) conceptualizes the quiet, disguised, almost
invisible resistance of marginalized groups as “everyday re­
sistance.” Several studies emphasize that this quiet resistance
is often the least risky form of resistance when encountering
the state, as citizens are expected to be submissive and coop­
erative (Lipsky 1980; Mik-Meyer and Silverman 2019; van
Oorschot 2000), convey enthusiasm regarding employment
prospects, and feel humble and shameful for receiving public
assistance (Peterie et al. 2019; Pultz 2018). Whereas studies
have begun to explore how the unemployed are governed
through emotions (Peterie et al. 2019; Pultz 2018), and
how emotions affect citizens’ coping with loss of access to
programs (Bell, Christensen, and Jessen Hansen 2022), there
is a lack of studies of what happens when citizens divert from
the normative constructive and compliant role and express
their anger. This study contributes to the broader research
agenda of understanding the role of emotions in citizen-state
encounters, how emotions drive agency and the consequences
of non-normative forms of participation for citizen voice in
encounters with the state.
Emotional capital and administrative burden
The scholarship on administrative burden conceptualizes
how the state imposes costs on citizens trying to access serv­
ices and resources. Herd and Moynihan (2018) distinguish
between different types of costs as learning costs (e.g., gaining
knowledge of public services and eligibility requirements),
compliance costs (e.g., efforts used to fulfill requirements),
and psychological costs (e.g., loss of autonomy, stress).
Administrative burdens are not static and uniform for all cit­
izens but may depend on the frontline delivery and micro-
level interactions between frontline workers and citizens,
as frontline workers’ informal practices may diminish or
increase the costs of interacting with the state (Brodkin 2011).
Whereas coping with these costs requires cognitive resources,

competence (Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021) or administra­

to address the emotional resources required to cope with
administrative burden. Emotional resources may be impor­
tant both in negotiating costs (e.g., appealing for sympathy)
and in coping with costs (e.g., enduring waiting). Even when
coping with administrative burdens that largely require cog­
nitive capacities (collecting and processing information about
public services, and writing complaints), claimants also need
emotional resources such as self-confidence to sustain their
efforts when their claims are rejected (emotional energy)
(Barbalet 1998; Collins 1990), the ability to articulate their
frustrations in a way that evokes sympathy and support
(emotional reflexivity) as well as the capacity to complain in
a way that is not perceived as threatening (emotion manage­
ment) (Hochschild 1979).
Theorizing how emotions can become resources in the
encounter between citizen and state the concept of emo­
tional capital is useful. Building on Bourdieu, Illouz (2007)
conceptualizes the ability to express an appropriate emo­
tional style and to communicate emotions as a form of emo­
tional capital. Emotional capital consists both of a particular
knowledge of emotions and a linguistic repertoire for talking
about emotions (often drawing on therapeutic discourse),
skills for managing emotions (aligning expressed emotions
with situated norms), and the capacity to express emotions
(Cottingham 2016). Emotional capital can be understood as
an embodied form of cultural capital (Bourdieu 1986; Jensen
and Prieur 2024). Approaching emotions as a form of capital
suggests that emotions can translate into social advantages
(Froyum 2010; Lavee and Strier 2019), and that emotional
capital is unequally distributed in society (Cottingham 2016).
The dimensions of emotional capital are not clearly
elaborated in extant research. Integrating insights across the
scholarship in the sociology of emotions, three dimensions of
emotional capital can be distinguished: (1) emotional energy,
(2) emotional reflexivity, and (3) emotion management.
Emotional energy.
Emotions provide the energy of action (Barbalet 1998).
Barbalet (1998) describes confidence as the affective basis of
agency, since confidence in one’s capacity is crucial to over­
come the uncertainty of attempting to achieve one’s aims.
Collins (1990) suggests that emotional energy derived from
successful social interactions underlies the experience of con­
fidence and that people who are marginalized or excluded
from group membership loose emotional energy to fight and
assert themselves. Barbalet (1998) proposes that confidence
relies both on social relations characterized by acceptance
and recognition, but also access to social support and re­
sources. Based on this, disadvantaged, marginalized, and iso­
lated people may lose the energy of emotion necessary to gain
voice.
Emotional reflexivity.
Emotions involve physiological and bodily sensations that can
be difficult to describe in words (Holmes 2010). Part of emo­
tional capital is the ability to translate embodied emotional
experience into discourse, to have knowledge of emotions
that enables one to recognize and reflect on emotions, as well
as a linguistic and cultural repertoire to describe, account for,
and justify emotions (Illouz 2007; Prieur, Jensen, and Nielsen
2019). Emotional reflexivity involves the ability to reflect on
bodily sensations, know how you feel and why, and transform
sensations into discourse. Research indicates that the long-
term outcomes of anger expressions for social relationships
may be more positive than negative, if the person is able to
express anger in a way that is perceived as justified and ap­
propriate (Averill 1982, 1993), indicating the importance of
being able to account for one’s emotions.
Emotion management.
In social interaction, people are expected to adjust their
expressions of emotions to the social situation. Hochschild
(1979, 1983) uses the term feeling rules to describe the norms
guiding emotions and conceptualizes emotion work as the
effort it requires of social actors to adjust their expression
of emotions to the social context. The scholarship on feeling
rules finds, that norms typically promote emotional control
(Gulløv 2016; Wouters 2004). While anger is sometimes
tolerated or even embraced (Rosenwein 2020; Sparks 2015),
anger risks being labeled uncivil, irrational, as a threat to dia­
logue, or associated with violence (Sparks 2015). Particularly
so for benefit recipients, who are expected to show grati­
tude and compliance (van Oorschot 2000) and are seen as
disentitled to be angry. Further, low-status groups are typi­
cally expected to be more cooperative and considerate than
high-status groups (Ridgeway 2007). This highlights, that the
ability to read the social norms and tailor expressions to those
norms may be an important prerequisite to gain influence.
Emotional capital then consists of three core components:
(1) emotional energy: the capacity to mobilize and convey
emotions, (2) emotional reflexivity: the capacity to translate
embodied emotional experience into discourse, accounting
for, and justifying emotions, and (3) emotion management:
skills for reading the social context, adjusting emotion
expressions to this context, including the capacity for control­
ling expressions of emotions. Together the three dimensions
of emotional capital allow citizens to express their emotions
in a controlled and contextually legitimate way.
While people’s actions may convey a higher or lower level of
emotional capital, it is important to note, that emotional cap­
ital is not a stable individual attribute, it is generated through
social interaction with others and depends on the person’s
position in the social structure (Barbalet 1998; Collins 1990;
Holmes 2010). Further, demands for emotion management
and emotional reflexivity are highly context-sensitive, as the
proper ways of presenting emotions and the legitimate lan­
guage of justifying emotions vary across social contexts.
In the analysis, I apply the concept of emotional capital to
claimant anger. Building on the notion of emotional capital,
the use of anger to gain social advantage both relies on the
capacity to mobilize emotions that energize action (emotional
energy), the ability to describe and justify anger (emotional
reflexivity), as well as the ability to interpret the emotional
requirements of the field and match anger expressions to
these requirements (emotion management).

into the labor market. Importantly, the participants in this
study are citizens with rights to welfare benefits (cash ben­
efit recipients). While the Nordic countries are renowned
for their active and generous welfare states, there has been
a shift towards welfare conditionality in this context. Access
to social benefits increasingly depends on citizens meeting
specific behavioral conditions, and economic sanctions are
employed for non-compliance with programs (Larsen 2013).
However, this approach is not uniformly applied across
target groups. For citizens facing challenges beyond unem­
ployment, such as issues related to drug use, homelessness,
mental health conditions, or disabilities, there is greater le­
niency in the utilization of sanctions and the requirements for
activation. Disadvantaged citizens are expected to participate
actively, but they are not obligated to accept any job offer.
Administrative burdens for disadvantaged claimants are often
caused by activation demands, waiting time, and lengthy doc­
umentation efforts.
Data
The data consists of interviews with seventy-one claimants
with problems besides unemployment and observations of
ten conversations between claimants and caseworkers (see
Table 1). I gained access to fifty one unemployed citizens
through caseworkers at the job centers where I conducted
my field work. Of these, twenty-two participated in indi­
vidual interviews, and the other thirty citizens participated
in six focus groups with 3–6 participants in each group.
Recruitment through caseworkers entails a risk of selection
bias, recruiting mainly citizens who have good collaboration
with their caseworker. This bias is countered by two samples
of unemployed citizens accessed through other strategies. The
first sample is small, recruited through a post on a municipal
website encouraging dissatisfied claimants to contact the re­
searcher. This resulted in four interviews. The second sample
was recruited through a gatekeeper in a resistance movement
against the job center resulting in fifteen interviews. These
claimants were highly critical of the job center and were often
involved in supporting other claimants or in activism against
the job center. Hence, each dataset is biased toward either
positive or negative narratives. While these biases outweigh
each other, the data cannot be seen as representative of citi­
zens’ experiences with the job center. However, it is useful in
teasing out the diversity of anger experiences in the context
of the job center.
The interviews focused on claimants’ experiences of ad­
ministrative burden, coping, and emotions. Anger has been
approached through a social and interactionist perspective
focused on how anger arises and is negotiated in social in­
teraction in the context of situated norms, rather than being
only an internal property of the individual. Anger can be
stigmatizing because it is often associated with violence or
incivility. To approach anger in a non-stigmatizing way, I used
the question, “Does it sometimes help to get angry?” This
made it possible for claimants to talk openly about anger
and reflect on how they navigate anger expressions. The in­
dividual interviews were valuable in gaining insight into
personal experiences, whereas the focus groups allowed in­
sight into the collective negotiation of norms for emotions.
Approaching anger primarily through interviews entails
that anger is analyzed through claimant accounts of their
experiences.
The research team observed and audio-recorded fifty
conversations between caseworkers and claimants. Ten
conversations contained anger expressed, often in mild forms,
such as disagreement, irritation, or frustration. Only in one
case was it seen as agitation, and in this case, the claimant
left the conversation. Because so few conversations contained
anger-related emotions, it was difficult to identify any sys­
tematic patterns across the conversations. However, the
conversations were used to deepen the understanding of the
patterns identified in the interview data.
All participants gave consent to participate in the study, and
the data collection and analysis followed national and univer­
sity guidelines on ethics and data protection. All participants
have been given pseudonyms, and identifying information has
been masked to ensure confidentiality.
Procedure
The data were coded in an iterative process, first performing
an open coding focused on disagreement, conflict, and
claimant expressions or mentioning anger-related emotions
in a broad sense, e.g., frustration, agitation, and hostility. This
first round of coding revealed that claimants manage anger
differently and claimants experience different outcomes of
expressing anger. These preliminary findings resulted in a
focus on how and when anger can work as a resource. The
three dimensions of emotional capital were therefore used to
guide the second coding round. To apply these dimensions to
the data, I operationalized them, as detailed in Table 2.
Emotional energy involves the strength, confidence,
courage, and assertiveness to speak up and struggle vis-à-vis
bureaucracy. Having a low level of emotional energy involves
feeling disempowered, shameful, disentitled to complain, de­
pressed, uncertain, or afraid to express yourself and contest
bureaucracy.
Emotional reflexivity involves the ability to interpret one’s
own emotions, describe, account for, and justify emotions in
a language that is understandable and acceptable to others.
The contrasting low level of emotional reflexivity involves in­
ability to account for emotions, or accounting for them in
ways that are seen as unintelligible or illegitimate by others.
Emotion management relates to the expression of emotions
and how well these expressions are tailored to the social con­
text. A high level of emotion management involves the ability
to read contextualized social norms and control emotions. A
low level of emotion management involves impulsive or un­
controlled expressions of emotion.

Analysis
The interviewed claimants experienced that anger expressions
had quite different consequences. Most often, claimants
described that their anger expressions had no consequences:
There came nothing out of it, neither positive nor negative.
I don’t know if he even took it in. But to me it was a kind
of a victory, that I could just call up and say, “I will not
take this, it is not okay.” (Interview claimant Paul)
Even though anger had no tangible effect, for claimants it
could be an important act of boundary setting. However, the
data also show that for disadvantaged claimants who reach
the limit of what burdens they can cope with and try to com­
municate it in anger, it could result in a sense of humiliation
and denial of status, undermining trust in public authorities
if their anger—and thereby boundary setting—was ignored.
In some of the claimant narratives, anger expressions had
negative consequences, for instance claimants described their
anger making the caseworker annoyed, “cold” and “turn
against you,” or “throw it back in your face until you stop.”
Often, the anger narratives were ambivalent, for instance,
describing a caseworker becoming annoyed but still speeding
the casework up. Finally, some of the anger narratives had
positive outcomes. These narratives contained descriptions of
being listened to, being recategorized to receive a more lenient
and holistic approach, being allowed to get a new caseworker,
and improving the collaboration with the caseworker.
In the following sections, I unpack these diverse experiences
of anger expressions by focusing on the three dimensions of
emotional capital: emotional energy, emotional reflexivity,
and emotion management.
Emotional energy
Erika’s quotation at the beginning of this article shows a
common theme in the data: anger is a way to make something
happen—to speed up employment services. For vulnerable
claimants with poor employment chances, important admin­
istrative burdens are experiences of standstill, uncertainty,
loss of autonomy, feeling caught in limbo, and not knowing
for how long (Lipsky 1980; Nielsen, Danneris, and Monrad
2021). Hence, being able to speed things up is important for
regaining (some) control of one’s life. However, claimants
often described feeling shameful, disentitled to complain,
or disempowered. These claimants described how wait time
and repetitions without progression lead to despondency,
draining their self-confidence, energy, and motivation to act.
These emotions made it difficult for them to muster the en­
ergy and confidence to speak up and contest assessments
and interventions. Hence, in the data claimants who felt se­
verely disempowered, disentitled to be angry or afraid of the
consequences of anger, often did not express their frustrations.
A claimant suffering from depression reflected on anger in
this way:
Eileen:
I think I am the kind of person who does
not get mad at other people. So, I have
probably, mmm, I have tried to say things
nice and quiet. Sometimes I’ve been sad, I’ve
cried, right.
Interviewer: 	 How did they respond when you were sad?
At meetings and things like that.
Eileen:
Well, they are compassionate. But they still
have to follow the rules of the system […]
You can probably push things in a direction
if you have ideas yourself and if they want to
go along with it […] In these winter months
where I am in slow motion, there are not so
many ideas coming out. That is strenuous,
that you are locked inside yourself, I think.
(Interview claimant Eileen)
Eileen highlighted both the cognitive (ideas) and emotional
(not being in slow motion) resources required to gain influ­
ence. Suffering from depression, she did not have the emo­
tional energy to push for anything. Another claimant, Ida,
described that she knows what she wants and needs and that
she can articulate it, but when the caseworkers do not listen,
she conforms to their suggestions. She believed that you need
to be able to “stand your ground,” if you are to get influence.
These cases illustrate the emotional energy needed to partic­
ipate effectively.
Claimants may also feel disentitled to get angry, because
of the individualization and stigmatization of unemployment.
They felt they could not blame others for their hardships,
as it was their own responsibility to get a job. Butler et al.
(2018) suggest that performing anger requires self-esteem and
that a person sees her- or himself as valuable in relation to
others. Hence, when claimants felt stigmatized, shameful and
without value, anger was sometimes turned inwards as deep
shame, rather than expressed, thus losing the opportunity to
voice concerns (Butler et al. 2018).
Other claimants described a fear of the consequences of
anger, that make them reluctant to articulate what they felt:
I am a bit afraid of what the consequences of it [anger]
would be. Do they throw you out of the system if you con­
tradict them or if you don’t want to do something? That is
my biggest fear that they say, “Well, then, we’ll just stop here
and then you are on your own. (Interview claimant Lisa)

When claimants experienced a poor collaboration with a
caseworker some described that they just said yes to any­
thing the caseworker suggested and waited while hoping
for things to change as staff turnover is high. Claimants
have the right to ask for a new caseworker, but they
described a fear of using that right, because they didn’t

claimants described a fear of doing something wrong or
damaging the relationship with the caseworker because the
caseworker has power over their livelihood. One claimant,

“Those job consultants, they are the puppet masters, right.
And we are the puppets.”
Disempowerment, disentitlement, and fear leave claimants
with a sense of hopelessness and insecurity depriving them
of the emotional energy to contest bureaucracy. Hence, not
all claimants can mobilize the resources required to fight for
influence or the energy to express dissatisfaction or anger.
Insisting on making your voice heard requires emotional en­
ergy: confidence, self-esteem, courage, and assertiveness. The
claimant Sophie is illustrative of this point. Sophie recounts
how she was able to overcome shame, gain voice, and partic­
ipate in collective mobilization:
At first, I was just a hungry and humble person who
wouldn’t talk to anybody but just hurried to get food
[from an NGO] and leave; no one should know who I
was or the other way around (laughs). It takes some time
before it [the injustice] occurs to you, but when it does,
you get angry […] and then I used a lot of energy getting
others to become angry: “Anne [name of friend] look what
they do, look what they say, you could be treated—in a
moment—like that.” I was angry, and I wanted others to
also get angry […]. Anger gives you a strength that you
don’t have. I didn’t have that strength; it came to me. So, I
thought, “Shut up, look what they do. If you get angry, too,
then we also have all your strength suddenly. Don’t be a
limp rag; they have stolen all your strength. Here it is, I am
giving it back to you.” (Interview claimant Sophie)
As Sophie described, she has undergone an emotional trajec­
tory from shame, self-blame, and feelings of disentitlement
to anger. She described an “awakening,” where she realized
that her hardship is not her own fault, but the result of injus­
tice, and that she is not alone, and this fueled her anger and
gave her the strength to fight back and “get organized” (Lister
2004). Whereas several contemporary scholars describe anger
as spurring from a sense of resentment, aggrieved entitle­
ment, or a sense of not being granted what one was prom­
ised in life (Hochschild 2016; Kimmel 2017; Mishra 2017),
the problem of anger may seem somewhat reversed for vul­
nerable and marginalized claimants, since they often suffer
from a sense of disentitlement. However, as Sophie’s narrative
reveals, anger is spurred the moment she realizes the injustice
of her hardship. Sophie’s case illustrates the social processes
involved in realizing that one is allowed to expect something
from the state. The data indicate that social movements may
be crucial to gain access to interpretive repertoires that allow
you to see your situation as an effect of injustice rather than
personal deficiencies, to gain emotional energy from social
interactions in the group and confidence from the sense of
community, recognition and support provided by the group.
For some claimants, then, anger played a key role in changes
in agency from a personal/everyday coping to political and
strategic coping. As such, anger is based on expecting some­
thing more (e.g., better welfare, more respect, and better in­
come), and it can help mobilize energy to struggle and speak
up and is therefore closely related to processes of coming to
voice (Lorde 1997).
Emotional reflexivity
When claimants express their anger, they sometimes account
for their emotions and actions and provide a justification.
This emotional reflexivity can be important in legitimizing
anger.
In the following excerpt the claimant Morten communicated
that he has been wronged when he has been financially
sanctioned for not living up to the eligibility requirements of
a regular job-seeker, but he also produced an account of his
lack of action (not applying for work):
It took me two years before I said, “Listen up, I feel terrible.
I have a drug problem, and you are not going to take any
more money from me.” It was actually, in the end, where I
said, “Don’t you understand, it is not because I don’t want
to apply for a job, it is because I can’t. It is simply because
I don’t have a computer. I don’t have anywhere to live. It
can’t be right that I am punished for that and that you are
taking my money, which I don’t have in the first place.” I
went up to the municipality and said that, and then I said,
“I refuse that you take any more money from me or any­
thing.” But, I have continued to become a more and more
difficult citizen because I have refused. And then I said,
“Damn no, this is what I want and what I do.” But I have
also had to bite the sour apple, participate in interventions,
and build LEGO bricks for them to assess me. (Interview
claimant Morten)
Morten’s anger fueled his efforts to confront bureaucracy
with the injustice of the sanctions imposed on him, but it
also allowed him to appeal for compassion and commu­
nicate the severe social problems he was suffering from.
Welfare conditionality in Denmark is administered more le­
niently to claimants with problems besides unemployment.
Therefore, Morten’s account of all the social problems af­
fecting him contained vital information, allowing Morten
to be recategorized as a claimant who should receive a more
holistic and lenient approach. Morten’s anger allowed him
to account for his inaction and claim a position as worthy of
help (willing but unable). Interestingly, Morten’s expression
of frustration might be acceptable because it fits with the
construction of the identity of a vulnerable claimant with
problems besides unemployment. A vulnerable claimant
cannot necessarily be expected to follow norms for emotional
control, making the anger fit with the identity ascription the
claimant seeks to achieve. Morten experienced that voicing
his frustration helped him avoid further sanctions, but not
demands of activation. Here, the experience of reaching the
limit of what you will accept and communicating your expe­
rience clearly appears fruitful.

to account for your emotions in a way that helps legitimize
them, hence appears to be an important resource.
The justification of anger may contain information that is
vital for the casework, making it possible for the anger to
translate into influence. Accounts of anger sometimes allowed
claimants to redefine the way they appear (as more vulner­
able than assumed). Having the discursive repertoire to talk
about emotions can be a resource that helps claimants have
their perspectives considered (Holmes 2010; Illouz 2007).
Still, the data do not suggest that claimants’ justification of
anger allows actual decision power; rather, accounts of anger
may allow claimants to push their concerns onto the agenda
and resist specific interventions. However, other claimants
described that you must be careful how you present yourself
because you may come across as too resourceful when voicing
your concerns forcefully:
I am the type to fly into a rage (laughs) […] I write to the

have my power, and I am also relatively intelligent, but that

are two different things. (Interview claimant Louise)
A strong-minded, well-articulated expression of anger may
lead to suspicion: If the claimant has these resources to pro­

The ability to account for their anger, then, is experienced as a
resource that may allow claimants to have their voices heard
and their situation reinterpreted, but not always to the benefit
of the claimant, since the ability to communicate clearly and
forcefully can be interpreted as a resource that casts doubt
over the motives of the unemployed (being seen as strategi­
cally angry and unwilling rather than desperate and unable).
Emotion management
The theoretical framework outlines two dimensions of emo­
tion management: (1) skills for reading the social context
and adjusting emotion expressions to this context, and (2)
the capacity for controlling emotions. This section begins by
examining emotion control and then continues to discuss
how claimants adjust their emotions to the social context.

The distribution of anger
The analysis has focused on the claimants’ experiences of anger.
However, some claimants did not report any anger. The data
suggest that the claimants who did not feel anger experienced
a lower level of administrative burden, and a sense of fair­
ness in the burdens they experienced (e.g., burdens are legiti­
mate because they are used to avoid fraud) as well as trust in
frontline workers. Further, claimants who did not report anger
often described agreement with frontline workers regarding
assessments and interventions. The claimants who experienced
lower levels of administrative burden were often those who
pursued education or work, as claimants who pursue supported
employment or disability pension are subjected to detailed docu­
mentation requirements to be eligible for these benefits.
As shown in the analysis, not all claimants will express
their anger. The claimants who did express their anger were
characterized by:
-	 having emotional energy to fight,
-	 perceiving administrative burdens as resulting from in­
justice (e.g., a deliberate delay),
-	 believing that anger is necessary to get the system to re­
spond,
-	 or being unable to control the anger they feel.
The interpretation of administrative burdens as either just or
unjust seems core to the experience and expression of anger,
highlighting the significance of attitudes to government and
government programs (Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021).
The claimants who did express anger were both claimants
with high levels of emotional energy (e.g., feeling confident)
as well as claimants with low levels of emotional management
(being unable to control their anger). The claimants who did
not express their anger were in contrast characterized by a
low level of emotional energy (being unable to mobilize the
confidence and power needed to fight) or a high level of emo­
tion management (being able to suppress their anger).
When anger was expressed, it was often in the encounter
with the individual frontline worker. Even so, anger was often
directed at the employment system rather than the individual
worker. This is in line with previous research that suggests
that citizens do distinguish between the bureaucrat and the
bureaucracy (Barnes and Henly 2018; Nielsen, Dall, and
Madsen 2022). Citizens in this study most often blamed the
bureaucracy and regarded frontline workers as “constrained
bureaucrats” (Barnes and Henly 2018) attributing adminis­
trative burden to system-level factors. This is also suggestive
of why some citizens believed that anger was counterpro­
ductive; they regarded the agency of frontline workers as
constrained and hence putting pressure on frontline workers
was unlikely to be effective.
Discussion: the role of emotional capital in
contesting administrative burden
The analysis based on claimant experiences suggests that admin­
istrative burden may give rise to anger that drive the agency of
claimants to contest bureaucracy. Further, the analysis suggests
that emotional capital may play a role in mitigating administra­
tive burden (see Table 3). Claimants with a high level of emo­
tional capital may be able to use their anger to call attention to
neglected information, push concerns on to the agenda, increase
the speed of casework, and push demands and responsibilities
on to caseworkers. Claimants with a low level of emotional cap­
ital may be unable to articulate their frustrations and challenge
bureaucracy, and when they do express their anger, they may
experience being seen as blaming others and failing to take re­
sponsibility for their own life, as threatening and dangerous or
as mentally ill. These claimants may experience that their anger
results in formal sanctions, the involvement of other professionals
(e.g., police), or an increased focus on formal requirements and
the claimants’ own responsibilities.
Emotional energy is crucial for claimants to summon
the strength, courage, self-confidence, and perseverance to
challenge bureaucracy. However, mobilizing a high-level of
intensity can have detrimental consequences, as claimants ex­
perience being seen as dangerous or mentally ill. Hence, emo­
tional energy needs to be balanced by emotion management.
From claimant experiences it seems anger is particularly
fruitful when it provides caseworkers with novel insights.
This highlight the importance of emotional reflexivity, which
is the ability to account for and justify emotions in a way that
is understandable and acceptable to others.

However, two findings indicate the higher levels of emo­
tional capital are not always fruitful for claimants, and that
low levels of emotional capital are not always problematic.
Firstly, claimants who are seen as too resourceful may be met
with suspicion, because being unemployed and resourceful
may indicate that you are playing the system. Secondly, some­
times bursts of anger that are not threatening nor aggressive
are described by claimants as being met with understanding
and reappraisal of the case. However, this may require some
level of emotional reflexivity. This indicates that loss of some
level of emotional control may convince caseworkers that
claimants are more vulnerable than assumed. This reveals
a paradox of emotions for disadvantaged claimants where
emotional capital on the one hand is crucial to contest ad­
ministrative burden, but claimants on the other hand must
be careful not to come across as too resourceful (energetic,
well-articulated, and in control of their emotions) to be de­
serving help.
Holler and Tarshish (2022) suggest that particularly
waiting, communicative breakdowns, and administrative
errors generate administrative burdens. The analysis shows
that anger expressions may influence these elements involved
in generating burdens. Anger expressions may contribute to
renegotiating compliance costs, as the emotional energy of
anger may create intensity and speed up the casework thus
diminishing waiting. Further, claimants’ emotional reflex­
ivity in accounting for anger may address communicative
breakdowns where claimants lack crucial information or
struggle to communicate information regarding their spe­
cific circumstances and needs to the state. When claimants
experience administrative errors, it requires careful skills in
emotion management to call attention to these errors without
appearing to be threatening or blaming caseworkers.
However, anger expressions are not always successful in
diminishing administrative burden. Often claimants expe­
rience that their anger has no tangible effect. This perhaps
reflects that some level of anger is expectable in a context
where frontline workers control access to resources needed
by disadvantaged citizens and often have to make decisions
to which claimants may not agree. However, it may also re­
flect that anger is seen as irrelevant noise easily disregarded as
reflecting the disadvantaged position of the claimant. Hence,
it may require emotional capital to have your anger heard
as relevant to assessments and decisions. If claimants express
anger to assert their boundaries and experience that their
anger and hence boundaries are ignored, it may increase the
psychological cost of interacting with the state (e.g., in terms
of humiliation and sense of powerlessness).
As shown in Table 3, high-intensity anger, well-articulated
anger expressions, and blame placed through anger may
hinder claimants from successfully renegotiating adminis­
trative burden. Sometimes, these forms of anger expression
may result in counterproductive outcomes with increased
administrative burden, both in terms of compliance costs
(increased waiting, sanctions for not complying) and psycho­
logical costs (mistrust). For anger expressions to be a resource
for claimants trying to contest administrative burdens seems
to require either a controlled, low-intensity form of expres­
sion, that sets proper boundaries and accounts for actions
and experiences (involving high levels of emotional capital)
or anger expressions that signal vulnerability and thereby call
for empathy (see also Davidovitz and Cohen 2022).
This suggests that anger exists within a field of tension, caught
between the forces of agency (energy to fight) and the constraints
of social control (appropriate expressions). The informal policy
design (the frontline delivery) is likely to affect the feeling rules
in important ways (e.g., the education of frontline workers, the
time for interactions between frontline workers and citizens, and
the available interventions). The case of Morten is illustrative
of this point, as the formal and informal policy context allows
frontline workers to interpret his anger as a sign of vulnerability
warranting a more lenient and holistic approach.
Further research
This study highlights the need for a research agenda on
emotions in public administration. Particularly there is a need
for research on emotions in citizen experiences and responses
to administrative burden as well as research on frontline
responses to citizen emotions. In the following, I will suggest
some important areas of research.
Scholarship on emotions and agency suggest that emotions
are core drivers of agency (Barbalet 1998; Collins 1990;
Wettergren 2019). Emotions provide the energy of action,
making it crucial to attend to emotions in understanding
citizen agency. Anger drives persistence, courage and directs
blame outwards and thereby drives us to confront challenges
and overcome shame and fear. In contrast, shame and fear
may drive us to avoid or withdraw from engagement. Further
research should seek to integrate emotions in the scholarship
on citizen coping, because different emotions may underpin
different forms of agency.

In addition, this study suggests that emotional capital is
core to citizen coping. Further research should examine the
association between emotional capital, citizen coping, and the

An important, but easily overlooked dimension of emo­
tional capital is the capacity to express emotions. Claimants
may feel burdened by shame and feelings of disentitlement,
sometimes making it difficult to voice frustrations and contest
administrative burden. Anger mobilizes energy to approach
challenges, and as such, it is part of an affective basis of
agency and resistance (Barbalet 1998; Collins 1990; Holmes
2004; Lyman 1981; Peterie et al. 2019). The capacity to ex­
press anger may be a scarce resource for the most isolated and
powerless claimants; the analysis indicates that not all citizens
have anger available as an action repertoire in encounters
with public bureaucracies (see also Gilad and Assouline
2022). Here, trajectories of disadvantage seem to matter.
Being habituated to feel shame over a long life of marginaliza­
tion and poverty is likely to make it more difficult to express
anger. Based on this study, I would hypothesize that sudden
loss of status, e.g., due to accidents, is more likely to make
anger available, than long-term deprivation. Further research
should engage with the emotional trajectories of claimants
and how they affect claimant coping. A life course perspective
could be relevant in this regard (e.g., Mayer 2004).
The study of citizen anger highlights moral aspects of ad­
ministrative burden. Administrative burdens are not just about
costs of interacting with the state, they are sometimes experi­
enced as violations, injustice, humiliation, and indignity. The
apprehension of the cost of interacting with the state as delib­
erate and unjust is core to the response of anger and the drive
to resist, fight and engage in activism rather than cooperate
and accommodate (to use the typology of Nielsen, Nielsen,
and Bisgaard 2020). These moral dimensions of administrative
burden should be examined and theorized in future research.
The data indicates that the experience and expression of
anger may depend on collective processes of coming to see the
situation as shared, unjust, and inflicted by others. While ad­
ministrative burden has been conceptualized as the individual’s
experience, shared constructs may impact an individual’s con­
ception of administrative burden. This highlights a need for
further research to engage with the role of social movements
in claimant coping, particularly in terms of how claimants
interpret, affectively appreciate, and navigate administra­
tive burden. Social movements make interpretive repertoires
and counter-emotions available for their members (see Flam
2005) which may be crucial in terms of whether administra­
tive burdens are understood as unavoidable costs or struc­
tural inequalities and whether citizens feel shame or anger.
While scholars have engaged with the significance of social
capital in coping with administrative burden (e.g., Döring
and Madsen 2022; Nisar 2018), as well as attitudes toward
government (Chudnovsky and Peeters 2021), research needs
to further engage with the role of social communities in cit­
izen responses to administrative burden.
In addition, there is a need for research examining frontline
responses to citizen emotions (including frontline coping
strategies, e.g., building on Davidovitz and Cohen 2022).
Citizen emotional responses may constitute a feedback loop
affecting informal frontline practice.
Administrative burdens affect citizens unequally (Halling
and Bækgaard 2024). Moreover, Hochschild’s (1979, 1983)
theoretical framework suggests that feeling rules are so­
cially stratified so that the anger of the less powerful is less
accepted. Hence, some citizens (e.g., with minority back­
ground) may find their anger expressions more closely
governed and sanctioned than others and therefore be less
successful if trying to use anger to contest administrative
burdens. Accordingly, we might expect the feeling rules gov­
erning encounters between state and citizen to exacerbate the
inequities of administrative burdens. Future research should
examine these social stratifications of feeling rules in the en­
counter between state and citizen and their consequences for
the use of emotional capital in the coping of marginalized cit­
izens. This could help explain social differences found in the
coping of citizens experiencing anger (Bell, Christensen, and
Jessen Hansen 2022).
Limitations
The analysis is primarily based on interviews with claimants.
This leaves two obvious limitations: Firstly, caseworker
experiences of responding to claimant anger might add im­
portant nuances to the understanding of the role of emo­
tional capital, how citizens express anger to renegotiate
administrative burdens and what the outcomes of anger are.
Secondly, basing the analysis on claimant accounts (rather
than observations) makes it difficult to ascertain precisely
how anger was expressed, and hence to analyze the signifi­
cance of specific forms of anger expressions (e.g., facial ex­
pression, tone of voice). Even though observational data was
collected, unfortunately, it was not possible to gain access to
observations of actual, high-intensity anger-expressions.
Conclusion
This article has analyzed how citizens understand and ma­
neuver the potential benefits and risks of expressing their
anger when experiencing administrative burdens. The study
shows that using anger to contest administrative burdens
relies on emotional capital. Based on claimant narratives, le­
gitimate anger involves communicating the intensity of their
needs without engendering fear or expanding the problem
definition to include mental illness, accounting for actions and
wishes without seeming too resourceful, and communicating
failed expectations without blaming. To deal with bureauc­
racy, citizens not only need cognitive resources, education,
social network, and material resources, they need emotional
resources to drive their efforts, justify their experiences, read
the normative requirements for expression of emotions, and
control their emotions.
At the same time, the study suggests that not all citizens have
anger at their disposal since citizens may be too disempowered
to mobilize the emotional energy to fight back. Further, anger
expressions may lead to detrimental outcomes increasing the
experiences of administrative burdens. For anger expressions
to have potentials for diminishing administrative burdens,
they must walk a fine line avoiding the dual risks of being
seen as a deliberate strategy to avoid responsibility for one’s

These findings point to the relevance of emotional capital as
a resource for citizens coping with administrative burden. The
article suggests conceptualizing emotional capital with three
dimensions: emotional energy, emotional reflexivity, and emo­

may help citizens informally renegotiate the conditions of
the encounter with the state to reduce administrative burden.
Further research should examine this proposition.

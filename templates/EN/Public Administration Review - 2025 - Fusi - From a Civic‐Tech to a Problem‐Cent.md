ABSTRACT
This research investigates the configurations of openness and policy conditions influencing the implementation of environmen-
tal justice (EJ) open government data (OGD) dashboards across U.S. states. EJ dashboards represent a shift from a civic-­tech ap-
proach to OGD, which emphasizes the public release of vast amounts of data, to a problem-­centered approach where government
agencies release and curate selected data to address a policy-­specific issue. Because of their differences, problem-­centered OGD
initiatives require distinct organizational structures and processes to facilitate both data release (openness conditions) and the
representation of the problem of interest (policy conditions). Using fuzzy-­set qualitative comparative analysis, this research shows
that the implementation of EJ-­OGD dashboards responds to either a supply–demand model or a government-­driven model. Lack
of implementation results from low institutionalization of problem solving or complacency toward EJ issues. Overall, EJ-­OGD
implementation is supported by an operationalizable problem definition and/or a dedicated administrative structure leading and
informing policy-­specific data provision.
Through the release of open government data (OGD) to the
public, government agencies aim to further transparency, par-
ticipation, and collaboration goals (Ingrams et al. 2020). Yet, as
OGD initiatives become increasingly popular worldwide (Lee-­
Geiller and Ali  2024), scholars find that public agencies take
different approaches to OGD—that is, they make different deci-
sions about which data to release, how, for whom, and for what
purposes as well as their role in supporting the use and anal-
ysis of data (Heimstädt and Dobusch 2018; Ruijer et al. 2020;
Sieber and Johnson 2015). The term OGD has, therefore, come
to describe a variety of initiatives with different goals and de-
signs (Yu and Robinson 2012). This ambiguity opens avenues
for experimentation and innovation, but it can hinder theoret-
ical progress if left unaccounted for. It is critical to recognize
that OGD initiatives have continuously evolved in response to
the changing expectations of both the public and policymakers
as well as technological advancements (Ruijer et al. 2023; Wang
and Shepherd 2020) to understand what these changes mean for
public organizations.
This research distinguishes between a civic-­tech and problem-­
centered approach to OGD to advance scholarship on the imple-
mentation of OGD initiatives. The civic-­tech approach finds its root
in the civic-­tech movement, which involves a collective mobili-
zation of civic-­minded, data-­savvy individuals and organizations
advocating for the release of vast amounts of government data
for free use by the public (Boehner and DiSalvo 2016; Reggi and
Dawes 2022; Sieber and Johnson 2015). By contrast, the problem-­
centered OGD approach (Ruijer et al. 2017; Susha et al. 2015) ad-
dresses the complexity of many present-­day policy problems, such

The differences between a civic-­tech and a problem-­centered ap-
proach are theoretically relevant when investigating the condi-
tions shaping the behaviors and activities required to implement
OGD initiatives (Young  2020). So far, scholarship highlights
openness conditions that incentivize data release according to
a civic-­tech approach, such as technological capacity, public
influence, and institutional pressures (Grimmelikhuijsen and
Feeney 2017; Young 2020). Problem-­centered OGD initiatives,
however, entail additional efforts for public organizations to
carefully select, format, visualize, and contextualize data in the
policy domain (Obe 2013; Bae et al. 2010). Therefore, we posit
that their implementation also necessitates policy conditions
defined by distinct organizational arrangements, institutions,
and resources directly aligned with problem-­solving efforts
(Obe 2013; Den Exter et al. 2015). While existing scholarship
hints at how openness and policy conditions might coalesce,
it lacks a coherent theoretical framework to explain their joint
effect on OGD implementation and, so far, it offers limited em-
pirical evidence to support policymakers and public managers.
We address these gaps by investigating the implementation of
environmental justice (EJ) OGD dashboards—which exemplify
a problem-­centered approach to OGD—across U.S. states.
Environmental justice (EJ) refers to “the fair treatment and mean-
ingful involvement of all people regardless of race, color, national
origin, or income, with respect to the development, implementa-
tion, and enforcement of environmental laws, regulations, and
policies” (U.S. EPA 2023). Data play a critical role in EJ policymak-
ing to identify the complex relationships between environmental
harms and socioeconomic status of communities (LoMonte and
Delgado 2023). Several studies, however, challenge the civic-­tech
assumption that making data publicly available is sufficient to fur-
ther transparency and participation (Atlas 2007; Bae et al. 2010).
Among others, the sophisticated formats of the raw data and users'
low technical knowledge are critical barriers to the use and con-
sumption of environmental data (Bae et al. 2010). U.S. state gov-
ernment agencies are addressing these challenges by shifting from
repositories of raw EJ data—in line with a civic-­tech approach—to
EJ-­OGD dashboards—in line with a problem-­centered approach
(Fusi et al. 2023; Lee 2020). Variation across US states offers a
unique opportunity for investigating the conditions shaping the
implementation of problem-­centered OGD initiatives. Considering
the complex and nonlinear relationships between openness and
policy conditions, we use fuzzy-­set qualitative comparative analy-
sis (fsQCA) to answer our research question: What configurations
of openness and policy conditions shape the implementation (or
lack thereof) of problem-­centered EJ-­OGD dashboards?
Our study makes three primary contributions. First, it theorizes
the richness and variety of OGD initiatives by identifying two
approaches, each reflecting a different logic of action—the civic-­
tech approach aligns with a technology-­driven logic, while the
problem-­centered one aligns with a user-­focused logic. This dis-
tinction provides a conceptual lens through which to move for-
ward a theory-­driven scholarship on OGD implementation, the
unique organizational characteristics of these initiatives, and how
they might affect government-­citizen relations and outcomes.
Second, we draw from our theoretical distinction to expand prior
scholarship on OGD implementation—traditionally focused on
civic-­tech portals—and investigate the configurations of open-
ness and policy conditions shaping problem-­centered OGD ini-
tiatives. We identify three paths. A supply–demand path shows
that states implement EJ-­OGD dashboards when public pressures
are matched with a dedicated administrative structure around a
salient problem. The two other paths show that implementation
follows a government-­driven model characterized by either high
institutionalization of problem solving or bureaucratic respon-
siveness to severe problems. The significance of policy conditions
across all pathways reinforces our argument that research on the
implementation of problem-­centered OGD initiatives should not
view them solely as a technology-­driven tool but rather needs to
account for the policy-­specific, contextual, and institutional fac-
tors that critically influence their implementation.
Finally, our findings have implications for the literature on pol-
icy making and implementation by showing that governments
can systematically apply OGD as an informational instrument
to engage the public or respond to public pressures. The use of
OGD for policymaking, though, can only occur if governments
adopt an operationalizable definition of the problem and develop
policy-­specific administrative apparatus.

The civic-­tech approach regards all data as a potentially valu-
able resource, assuming if enough data are made publicly avail-
able, someone will eventually use them to create value, such as
improving government services and policies or creating new
economic opportunities (Boehner and DiSalvo 2016; Nikiforova
and Lnenicka 2021). Accordingly, it urges governments to facil-
itate free, unburdened access to as many datasets as possible.
Government agencies worldwide have embraced the civic-­tech
approach (Lee-­Geiller and Ali 2024) as it limits their role to data
release and curation—for example, metadata and searchabil-
ity—and shifts the effort and cost of analyzing and interpreting
data to the public (Ingrams et al. 2020). Public agencies have
released OGD on web-­based portals, designed as one-­stop ac-
cess points to vast repositories of otherwise scattered datasets
across a spectrum of administrative and policy domains (Ansari
et al. 2022; Lee-­Geiller and Ali 2024; de Souza et al. 2022; Tang
and Jiang  2021). Other civic-­tech tools include Application
Programming Interfaces (APIs), which allow users to extract
data from an existing application, and crowdsourcing initia-
tives, such as data hackathons through which governments have
encouraged data re-­use and partnerships with data mediators
(Reggi and Dawes 2022; Sieber and Johnson 2015).
The civic-­tech approach has led to various successful examples
of citizen-­led applications (Reggi and Dawes 2022) and positive
outcomes such as increased perceived transparency and trust in
government (Schmidthuber et al. 2023). Yet critics point out that
“data management and publication practices” (Ruijer et al. 2020,
4) have limited the effective use of civic-­tech initiatives (Yu and
Robinson 2012). First, government agencies have released data-
sets of low quality or relevance for the public (Huang et al. 2020;
Lourenço 2015; Schwoerer 2022). Second, most individuals do
not have adequate skills for data manipulation, analysis, and
visualization (Nikiforova and Lnenicka 2021; Park 2022; Ruijer
et al. 2023; de Souza et al. 2022), resulting in civic-­tech OGD
portals being predominantly used by data experts (Begany
et al. 2021). Civic-­tech organizations often translate OGD into
formats and applications usable by a broader audience; however,
they lack financial and organizational capacity to consistently
serve as brokers (Reggi and Dawes 2022).
The problem-­centered approach shifts the focus from data ac-
cess to data engagement (Ruijer et al. 2017; Susha et al. 2015).
It relies on two assumptions. First, an average user is more in-
terested in consuming data products (e.g., analyses or visualiza-
tions) than in creating them (Ansari et al. 2022; Bae et al. 2010;
Chokki et al. 2022; de Souza et al. 2022). Second, OGD initiatives
have value if they help the public understand a specific problem
and participate in policymaking (Heimstädt and Dobusch 2018;
Ruijer et al. 2017; Susha et al. 2015). Public agencies, therefore,
take upon themselves the task of providing data that are “fit-­
to-­use” by identifying and meeting policy-­specific data require-
ments (Fusi et al. 2023). They also process data into digestible
formats that highlight relevant patterns and trends to enhance
interpretability (Gil-­Garcia et al. 2023; Lourenço 2015).
Data are released on portals and dashboards dedicated to sin-
gle policy problems using visual representations to reduce
complexity for diverse audiences (Barcellos et al. 2017; Chokki
et al. 2022). Public agencies also promote collaborative initia-
tives to collect the public's preferences and data needs around
the issue of interest (Ruijer et al. 2020; Boda et al. 2023). The
problem-­centered approach increases costs and efforts for pub-
lic agencies (Ansari et al. 2022; Nikiforova and Lnenicka 2021;
Obe 2013), but it relieves users of the burden of data selection,
cleaning, organization, and analysis, allowing them to focus
more on interpretation, decision-­making, and deriving insights
from the data (Chokki et al. 2022).
The problem-­centered approach has its unique trade-­offs. A
more structured representation of data increases readability
and accessibility, potentially fostering user engagement (Ansari
et al. 2022; Chokki et al. 2022; Fusi et al. 2023) and offering
insights to initiate and catalyze action (Ganapati 2011). Yet it
grants government significant control over how to select, pres-
ent, organize, and visualize data. Government agencies might
choose to beautify their activities (Matheus et al. 2020; Ruijer
et al. 2023) or prioritize their preferences over community needs
(Boda et al. 2023). As such, the legitimacy of problem-­centered
initiatives requires frequent interactions between the govern-
ment and relevant stakeholders to define the problem and users'
data requirements. Meanwhile, government agencies also have
their concerns. By facilitating data analysis and use, they may
expose ineffective policies and programs, service gaps, and blind
spots, leaving themselves vulnerable to criticism from both the
public and legislative bodies (Huang et al. 2020).
At present, several government agencies are harnessing both
approaches simultaneously to achieve breadth and depth of
data provision. Civic-­tech OGD initiatives provide breath and
can spur community-­led innovation while requiring low com-
mitment and resources from government agencies (Kornberger
et al. 2017; Nikiforova and Lnenicka 2021). Meanwhile, problem-­
centered initiatives offer depth and can streamline the use and
interpretation of data by the public, leaving government agen-
cies to sustain a great part of the cost of analyzing and visualiz-
ing data (Ansari et al. 2022; Chokki et al. 2022; Fusi et al. 2023).
2   |   Implementation of OGD Initiatives: A
Configurational Approach
The implementation of any OGD initiatives requires substan-
tial changes in organizational routines and behaviors (Matheus
et al. 2020; Young 2020). As public organizations—particularly
IT departments (Zhao and Fan  2018)—need to assemble and
release multiple data files (Yang and Wu 2021), the main chal-
lenges include locating and collecting data across working units
and departments (Yang and Wu 2021), persuading department
heads to release them (Park 2022; Yang and Wu 2021) and build-
ing capacity and tools to organize and share data files and ar-
chives (Zhao and Fan 2018). Accordingly, scholars have drawn
from innovation, organizational capacity, and public value the-
ories (e.g., Young 2020; Zhao and Fan 2018; Grimmelikhuijsen
and Welch 2012) to understand organizational and individual
resistance to and opportunities for “opening” government data.

Technological capacity encompasses available digital infrastruc-
tures, skills of public employees, digital technologies, open data
training, and cybersecurity preparedness (Grimmelikhuijsen
and Feeney  2017; Wang and Lo  2016; Young  2020; Zhao and
Fan  2018). Public influence refers to pressures from civic
society organizations, residents, and private companies on
government actors to provide (or restrict) access to data
(Fusi and Feeney  2020; Grimmelikhuijsen and Feeney  2017;
Grimmelikhuijsen and Welch  2012; Ruijer et  al.  2020; Yang
and Wu 2021; Zhao and Fan 2018). Finally, institutional pres-
sures include agency internal regulations, informal norms and
values that influence departments' willingness to release their
data (Kornberger et al. 2017) as well as coercive pressures from
statutory and regulatory laws that can affect governments' pro-
pensity to provide data access (Ingrams 2017; Park 2022; Zhao
and Fan 2018).
The implementation of problem-­centered OGD initiatives goes
beyond these openness-­related activities, as public agencies also
need to select, format, visualize, and contextualize data to depict
the problem, or/and demonstrate its causes and consequences
(Obe 2013; Chokki et al. 2022; Matheus et al. 2020). Effective
data visualization involves determining how to overlay the
data, harmonize disparate datasets, and fine-­tune visual design
choices (Chokki et  al.  2022). The antecedents of these activ-
ities are captured by a different scholarship focused on policy
conditions, such as problem characteristics and the institution-
alization of problem solving, which trigger sensemaking and
collective problem solving.
Problem characteristics encompass the severity, frequency,
tractability, or magnitude of the problem of interest, which can
influence governmental attention and likelihood of interven-
tion (Kim and Verweij  2016; Ringquist and Clark  2002). The
institutionalization of problem-­solving entails the process by
which systematic approaches, methods, and practices for ad-
dressing a policy issue are formally embedded into governmen-
tal systems, becoming a standardized part of decision-­making
and policy implementation. It can involve a formal definition of
the problem in question, which acts as a catalyst for sensemak-
ing (Weiss 1989). Government must also structurally place (i.e.,
anchoring) problem-­solving activities within their formal pol-
icy framework and administrative structure to ensure activities
are integrated into existing routines and processes (Den Exter
et al. 2015; Kim and Verweij 2016). While problem character-
istics might prompt the institutionalization process (Ringquist
and Clark 2002), this is not always the case. Public agencies can
institutionalize problem solving in response to political prefer-
ences (Kim and Verweij 2016; Konisky and Woods 2012) or pub-
lic pressures (Kraft et al. 2011; Wang and Lo 2016).
We summarize our identified openness and policy conditions
in Figure  1. The framework focuses on high-­level condi-

ple, problem characteristics can include severity, frequency,
magnitude, and tractability. To facilitate insights for public
managers, we separate internal conditions over which the or-
ganization has high control from external conditions which
can hardly be controlled. Most importantly, in line with our
argument that problem-­centered OGD initiatives require both
openness and policy conditions, our framework focuses on
their configurations rather than their net effects over imple-
mentation. This configurational approach relies on prior evi-
dence. For instance, research finds that government agencies
are more likely to release data when legislative mandates are
coupled with clear leadership to coordinate and manage data
collection (Park 2022) or with administrative capacity to seek
solutions (Ruijer et  al.  2017, 2020) and respond to concerns
raised by legislative bodies (Huang et al. 2020). Other research
shows that problem severity only motivates data release when
paired with public pressures urging government interventions

3   |   Implementation of Problem-­Centered
Initiatives: EJ-­OGD Dashboards
EJ policymaking starts with the identification of areas expe-
riencing uneven environmental burdens and socioeconomic
disadvantages by combining and geographically locating data
on socioeconomic vulnerability (e.g., percentage of minority
residents, residents with low income and/or education, and im-
migration status) and environmental harms (e.g., air and water
quality, location of landfills and brownfields) (i.e., “EJ data”)
(Boda et al. 2023; LoMonte and Delgado 2023). These data are
vastly and primarily collected by government agencies (e.g.,
environmental protection and Census agencies), making their
release critical—if not essential—for stakeholders and com-
munities to assess the equity implications of environmental
policy decisions and meaningfully participate in policymaking
(Lee 2020; LoMonte and Delgado 2023). Accordingly, several US
agencies in the last decade have launched EJ-­OGD civic-­tech
initiatives to further their commitment toward community en-
gagement in EJ policymaking.
Yet, the use of EJ-­OGD through civic-­tech initiatives requires
interested stakeholders and communities to possess specialized
knowledge of environmental issues (e.g., the effect of exposure
to different air pollutants), the ability to combine data from mul-
tiple sources, and Geographical Information Systems (GIS) ex-
pertise to pinpoint environmental hazards to draw information
from the data (Fusi et al. 2023). For instance, a user who wants
to identify EJ priority areas through the EPA Open Government
web portal1 needs to download and link several datasets on
environmental issues (e.g., water and air pollution) with addi-
tional data from the US Census Bureau, define a geographical
unit of analysis, and map the new dataset on a GIS platform. All
these activities require advanced data analysis and visualization
skills.
Government agencies are shifting toward a problem-­centered
approach to EJ-­OGD data to reduce some of these burdens
and facilitate data use. The U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency (EPA) promoted this approach with the launch of
the EJScreen2 (Figure  A3.2a in Supporting Information), a
map-­based dashboard linking demographic, environmental,
and health data at the Census block group level. EJScreen
shows the environmental burdens faced by a community
and whether those burdens are proportionate to the commu-
nity's socioeconomic standing. Approximately 20% of U.S.
states also adopted a problem-­centered approach (Fusi et al.
2023) and launched EJ-­OGD dashboards, such as California
(CalEnviroScreen), Maryland (MD EJSCREEN), Washington
(Environmental Health Disparities Map), and North Carolina
(NCDEQ Community Mapping System, Figure  A3.2b in
Supporting Information).
These dashboards have facilitated access to EJ data for pol-
icy, education, and research purposes (e.g., Driver et al. 2019;
Lee  2020; Liang  2016; Miles and Ya'Ukuu  2024; Zrzavy
et  al.  2022), informed government's policymaking (e.g.,
environmental permit review, cumulative impact assess-
ment) (Becerra et  al.  2024; LoMonte and Delgado  2023),
and strengthened community engagement (Besse and Rojas-­
Rueda 2022; Boda et al. 2023). The value of those dashboards
is also exemplified by the outcry from advocacy and science
organizations at President Trump's decision to discontinue
EJScreen (Sierra Club 2025; Harvard Law School 2025) and
the efforts of the scientific community to reconstruct and re-
lease the EJScreen data.
To date, several studies and reports analyze, compare, and eval-
uate these dashboards (Boda et al. 2023; Driver et al. 2019; Fusi
et al. 2023; Lee 2020; Zrzavy et al. 2022), but no study has probed
the conditions facilitating their implementation. We take advan-
tage of this variation to investigate how openness and policy
conditions, specifically relevant to the EJ policy domain, shape
the implementation (or absence) of problem-­centered EJ-­OGD
dashboards.
3.1   |   Openness Conditions
3.1.1   |   Technological Capacity
Substantial evidence shows that low technological capacity can
prevent government agencies from engaging in expensive un-
dertakings such as OGD initiatives (Young 2020), particularly
when they require intense data and technological skills, such
as problem-­centered ones (Obe  2013). Grimmelikhuijsen and
Welch (2012) find that technological capacity not only improves
the provision of general information but also increases access to
environmental policy data in both raw and processed formats.
In a subsequent study, Grimmelikhuijsen and Feeney  (2017)
show that high technological capacity advances the implemen-
tation of both transparency and participation tools, including
OGD portals. Similarly, Zhao and Fan (2018) find that techno-
logical capacity is positively correlated with OGD implementa-
tion, whereas the availability of financial and human resources
has no effect. Wang and Lo (2016) also show that technological
infrastructures underscore organizational readiness to adopt
and implement OGD.

OGD implementation often results from government response to
and negotiations with various stakeholders (Grimmelikhuijsen
and Welch  2012; Wang and Lo  2016), which are crucial for
maintaining the relevancy of OGD within the policy context
(Heimstädt and Dobusch 2018). In the environmental policy do-
main, where conflicting interests among governments, private

3.2   |   Policy Conditions
3.2.1   |   Problem Severity
The EJ problem lies at the intersection of a community's social
and environmental vulnerabilities. Social vulnerability stems
from socioeconomic characteristics that make certain com-
munities more susceptible to harm and less able to respond to
it (Boda et al. 2023). Environmental vulnerability reflects “the
extent to which the natural environment is prone to damage
and degradation” (Kaly et al. 2004). Higher levels of pollutants
or soil contamination, for instance, increase the environmen-
tal vulnerability of an area. Policy research suggests a posi-
tive correlation between problem severity and government's
adoption of EJ-­oriented policies (Kim and Verweij  2016),
with problem severity prompting government intervention
(Ringquist and Clark 2002). OGD research echoes this argu-
ment. Young (2020) finds that governments confronted with
severe policy problems (e.g., high unemployment) tend to re-
lease data to the public, likely to stimulate collective problem
solving. Fusi and Feeney (2020) also find that high inequality
leads to greater data sharing.
3.2.2   |   Problem Definition
To address environmental inequity, government agencies
need to define and identify areas of EJ concern (LoMonte and
Delgado 2023). A clear and actionable definition of EJ areas es-
tablishes a shared foundation for pinpointing EJ concerns across
communities, thereby fostering transparent, consistent, and in-
formed decision making in pursuing EJ goals (Boda et al. 2023).
It also promotes clarity and criteria in designing, implementing,
and monitoring EJ policies (Liang 2016), while providing guid-
ance for EJ data collection. Conversely, the absence of a problem
definition can cause confusion, perpetuate complacency, and
complicate the process of data collection and presentation since
bureaucrats lack clarity on what should be measured and repre-
sented (Sieber and Johnson 2015).
3.2.3   |   Legislative Anchoring
At the apex of the policy hierarchy, legislative responses el-
evate EJ on the policy agenda, making it an issue that must
be addressed statewide (Bullock et al. 2018). The legislation
establishes governments' responsibilities and accountability
for addressing EJ concerns, while also conferring the neces-
sary legitimacy and authority to EJ-­focused policy initiatives
(Ringquist and Clark  1999). Indeed, legislative backing re-
duces potential resistance and uncertainty while affirming
the relevance, credibility, and importance of EJ efforts in the
eyes of both the public and government stakeholders (Strelau
and Köckler 2016). Therefore, EJ legislation can motivate gov-
ernment agencies to release EJ data while enhancing their
legitimacy and accountability (Huang et  al.  2020). Indeed,
prior research shows that public organizations are less likely
to release data on issues for which they do not feel directly
responsible or do not have the authority to address (Ruijer
et al. 2020).
3.2.4   |   Administrative Structure
The creation of dedicated administrative structures is in-
strumental to innovative inter-­departmental programs, such
as EJ (Den Exter et al. 2015; Ringquist and Clark 1999). An
EJ-­focused working unit or program can deliver clear task de-
lineation, promote intra-­ and inter-­organizational coordina-
tion, and sustain internal support for EJ data practices (Den
Exter et al. 2015; Park 2022). Those structural arrangements
are crucial for aligning policy goals and enhancing vertical
and horizontal integration of EJ data across government units
(National Conference of State Legislatures  2023). Without
them, public officials might lack awareness of the importance
of releasing EJ data (Ruijer et al. 2020) and lack a pivotal lead-
ership role in motivating participation and facilitating crucial
decisions related to data, including determining which data to
present and how to effectively visualize them (Obe 2013; Zhao
and Fan 2018).
4   |   Method and Data
We use fuzzy-­set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) to
explore the configurations of openness and policy conditions
leading to the implementation of EJ-­OGD dashboards in all
50 U.S. states. fsQCA conceptualizes conditions as sets and as-
signs each case a membership score to each set (Ragin 2009).
Additionally, it treats each empirical case as a unique config-
uration of conditions and systematically compares cases to
identify generalized patterns leading to the outcome (or lack
thereof). Results identify two types of conditions. A necessary
condition implies that the outcome cannot occur without its
presence. A sufficient condition is always associated with the
occurrence of the outcome.
Importantly, fsQCA enables us to capture three aspects of causal
complexity: conjunction, equifinality, and causal asymmetry.
First, it explores whether the outcome results from the conjunc-
tion of a distinct set of causal conditions rather than from any of
those causal conditions in isolation (Ragin 2009). This aptly fits
our purpose of exploring the configuration of openness and pol-
icy conditions driving the implementation of EJ-­OGD. Second,
fsQCA embraces equifinality by capturing whether an outcome
can result from different causal paths (Fiss 2011). Practically,
it explores whether state governments can implement problem-­
centered EJ-­OGD through more than one causal path. Finally,
fsQCA accommodates asymmetric causality, allowing us to dis-
cern the distinct sets of causal conditions that contribute to the

4.1   |   Outcome Condition: Implementation Status
of EJ-­OGD Dashboards
Our outcome condition measures the extent to which U.S. states'
environmental protection agencies (full list in Appendix  B in
Supporting Information) have adopted a problem-­centered ap-
proach in the EJ policy domain by implementing an EJ-­OGD
dashboard. We classified the implementation status of EJ-­OGD
dashboards based on the extent to which they meet: (1) policy
requirements by integrating environmental and socioeconomic
data (Ruijer et al. 2017) and (2) user requirements by using map-
ping tools to present EJ data (Lourenço 2015). Data were last
updated in March 2023.
No implementation of EJ-­OGD dashboard (=0) indicates that a
state has yet to adopt a problem-­centered approach to EJ OGD.
EJ-­related data might be scattered across multiple webpages or
provided on a civic-­tech portal, such that the burdens of find-
ing, analyzing, and visualizing the data are entirely placed on
users. Low implementation of EJ-­OGD dashboard (=0.33) indi-
cates early steps toward a problem-­centered approach. States
in this category display at least three sources of environmental
harms on the same map-­based dashboard, helping users iden-
tify sources of environmental hazards within a community
but not their socioeconomic status. Moderate implementation
of EJ-­OGD dashboard (=0.67) underscores greater efforts to
use OGD for EJ policy making. EJ-­OGD dashboards in this
category explicitly identify areas of EJ concern using socio-
economic data, including either single variables (e.g., income
and/or race/ethnicity) or aggregate indicators (e.g., a socio-­
vulnerability index). Finally, high implementation of EJ-­OGD
dashboard (=1) indicates that a state has fully adopted an
EJ-­focused map-­dashboard integrating both socioeconomic
and environmental data. Users can easily intersect data to
identify areas of EJ concern.
Figure 2 maps the states and their membership score in the out-
come condition. Eleven states are fully in, with high EJ-­OGD
implementation (=1, dark blue). Five states have moderate lev-
els of implementation (=0.67), yet they are “more in than out”
of the outcome condition due to their integration of socioeco-
nomic indicators (light blue). Ten states are “more out than in”
as they only integrate environmental data (=0.33, gray). About
half of the states are fully out as they only provide scattered data,
mostly on environmental burdens (=0, white).

5   |   Analysis and Results
The analysis uses the QCA package in R (Dusa and
Thiem 2006). We started by identifying necessary conditions
for the implementation of EJ-­OGD dashboards. We then used
a truth table to identify sufficient conditions for the outcome.
We further harness the unique ability of QCA in handling
causal asymmetry and perform tests for the absence of the
outcome.
5.1   |   Necessary Conditions
Table 3 reports the necessity test for either the implementation
of EJ-­OGD dashboards or its absence. We start with the analysis
of atomic conditions and then, following Thiem (2014), expand
the necessity test by including complex conditions (i.e., combi-
nation of conditions that represent higher-­order concepts). For
the implementation of EJ-­OGD dashboards, none of the atomic
nor complex conditions alone meet the typical consistency
threshold of 0.9 and coverage threshold of 0.6 (Schneider and
Wagemann 2012), indicating that no single or complex condi-
tions are necessary for the outcome.
For the absence of EJ-­OGD dashboards, we identified two sets
of complex conditions: (1) low problem severity or low legisla-
tive anchoring, and (2) low problem severity or low technologi-
cal capacity or low administrative structure. For both sets, low
problem severity is a necessary condition. This means that most
states that have not implemented an EJ-­OGD dashboard experi-
ence low problem severity.
5.2   |   Sufficient Conditions
Table  4 presents the truth table for analyzing the sufficient
conditions, sorted in descending order by consistency score.
The empirical cases represent 27 out of 32 logically possi-
ble configurations. Given our small sample size, we adopt a
minimum threshold of one case per configuration. The truth
table also includes proportional reduction in consistency
(PRI) scores indicating the degree to which a configuration
leads to the outcome's presence and absence (Schneider and
Wagemann 2012). Configurations with low PRI are excluded
to improve the reliability and robustness of the subsequent
analysis. We then combine the consistency and PRI scores to
select rows for minimization (coded as 1 and 0 otherwise in
the Output column). Using a consistency threshold of 0.8 for
sufficient conditions (Ragin 2009), only the first seven rows
are included. No deviant case is detected, suggesting that all
cases that meet the configuration of causal conditions score
high in the outcome condition.

structure is high, EJ-­OGD implementation is likely driven by a
responsive bureaucracy, dedicated to EJ awareness and actions.
This path might indicate a shift toward participatory governance
wherein EJ-­OGD dashboards are used by public officials to in-
crease public participation in solving complex policy problems
(Matheus et al. 2020; Ganapati 2011). New Jersey Department
of Environmental Protection, for instance, has made substantial
investments in administrative capacity and problem definition.
Among others, the main goals of the EJ office are to “increase
public participation and educational opportunities for com-
munity members” and “increase access to state government
environmental information” (Department of Environmental
Protection of New Jersey 2023).
Regarding the absence of implementation, Path 3 and Path 4
cover a significant number of states while differing substan-
tially in core conditions. Path 3 has one core condition, low ad-
ministrative structure, coupled with no quantifiable problem
definition (peripheral condition). This configuration is clearly
symmetrical to Path 2a and shows that low institutionalization
is detrimental to the advancement of EJ-­OGD dashboards. Path
4 has a very low unique coverage and represents states with no
problem definition, low problem severity, and low technological
capacity as core conditions. These states might fall into compla-
cency—they lack both the capacity and problem pressure to im-
plement OGD from a problem-­centered approach. As EJ-­OGD
dashboards become more common, future research should ex-
plore what factors, such as isomorphic pressures or leadership,
can drive some of these states out of a complacency status and
toward implementing an EJ-­OGD dashboard.

administrative structure, problem severity, and problem defini-
tion—whereas openness conditions play a more marginal role.

OGD initiatives. Additionally, two pathways (2a and 3) only

The interpretation of our results should acknowledge a few lim-
itations. First, QCA allows only a moderate number of conditions
to minimize logical reminders and produce interpretable re-
sults. We focus on causal conditions most relevant to the EJ pol-
icy context and relatively actionable for practitioners (National
Conference of State Legislatures 2023). As a robustness check,
we reanalyzed the solutions by incorporating alternative mea-
sures of technological capacity (namely, the presence of Chief
Data Officer) and measures of institutional pressures, such as
open data legislation, but both of which led to higher complexity
and lower coverage in the solution. Yet, we might have excluded
conducive conditions, such as political ideology. Political ide-
ology is—to a certain extent—captured by problem definition,
administrative structure, and legislative anchoring (Kim and
Verweij 2016; Ringquist and Clark 1999, 2002). Because policy-
makers and public managers may not have immediate control
over a state's political ideology, we chose more actional condi-
tions that mediate political preferences. For instance, our data
shows that deep red states such as Utah and Mississippi have
an EJ program implemented by a non-­EJ related unit or office
(calibrated as emerging administrative structure in our analy-
sis), while Texas and Florida have pending EJ legislation. Still,
our results cannot fully account for the possible constraints that
political ideology directly exerts on the discretion and capabil-
ities of policymakers and public managers. For instance, since
the first Trump administrations, several researchers pointed to
the EPA's struggles in preserving environmental data (Walker
et al. 2018).
Second, our analysis does not provide adequate insights into po-
tential strategic uses of EJ-­OGD dashboards (Ruijer et al. 2020).
For instance, in Path 2a, government agencies demonstrate high
levels of institutionalization in EJ policymaking. Our analysis,
however, does not allow us to discern if the integration of EJ-­
OGD dashboards into this institutionalization framework gen-
uinely supports decision making or only symbolically signals
government commitment to garner political support. Further
research is needed to gather qualitative insights into the inte-
gration of EJ-­OGD dashboards into policymaking and their
strategic use. Finally, we recognize generalizability limits. In
the U.S. and at the time of writing, EJ was a salient policy area
receiving substantial attention from the federal government and
the public. Some conditions, such as problem severity or public
influence, might be more critical in policy areas or national con-
texts characterized by weak centralized policy leadership or less
favorable public opinion.
7   |   Conclusions
With an empirical focus on EJ-­OGD dashboards across U.S.
states, this research contributes to two fundamental questions
in OGD scholarship (Sieber and Johnson  2015; Gil-­Garcia
et  al.  2023): Are government agencies experimenting with
new approaches to OGD? Do different OGD approaches re-
quire different facilitative conditions for their implementation?
Our research defines two OGD approaches and teases out the
fundamental differences that their implementation entails.
The distinct role played by policy conditions in the identified
pathways validates the importance of such theoretical distinc-
tion. We therefore echo Gil-­Garcia et al. (2023) in supporting a
more in-­depth investigation of the unique contextual, organiza-
tional, and institutional elements that shape different OGD ini-
tiatives as public agencies continue to experiment. This line of
research departs from a rigid representation of OGD as neutral,
technology-­driven tools and recognizes the intricacies of their
implementation.
Additionally, scholars have concerned that OGD are “not yet
seen as an instrument [for policy], like other instruments such
as laws or subsidies” (Ruijer et al. 2020). Our results point in
a different direction, showing that the implementation of EJ-­
OGD dashboards goes hand in hand with government respon-
siveness to policy demands and/or the institutionalization of
problem solving. This trend signals a shift from data provision
as an end (Sieber and Johnson 2015) to the use of OGD as an
information-­based tool for policymaking (Ruijer et  al.  2017).
Problem-­centered initiatives can catalyze public attention to-
ward a problem and support the co-­creation of policy solutions
(Ganapati 2011; Chokki et al. 2022).
Our findings provide important insights for practitioners to
build data capacity at a time when problem-­centered initiatives
are emerging in various countries and across policy domains,
such as public health, climate change, and social justice. Our
results highlight the need for government agencies to (1) adopt
an operationalizable definition of the problem and (2) develop
a policy-­specific administrative apparatus. Both these starting
points are within the authority of government agencies. For in-
stance, convergence toward a common operationalizable defi-
nition offers the opportunity to convene multiple stakeholders
at the table to initiate active collaboration. Organizationally,
it also means that OGD initiatives are being progressively de-
centralized (Young 2020). A problem-­centered approach shifts
OGD initiatives beyond their traditional confines within IT de-
partments, requiring more widespread support—for example,
financial, technical, and human resources—throughout the
organization. Government agencies could offer organization-­
wide training and workshops to facilitate the implementation of
problem-­centered initiatives in areas where they are lacking or
at an early stage.
Results also send a positive signal for public managers and civic
society organizations. Conditions which are often perceived as
barriers to OGD, such as a lack of technological capacity or a low
problem severity, can be overcome by the joint presence of other
more actionable factors, such as active collaborative efforts be-
tween government and civil society organizations for the former
and the institutionalization of problem solving for the latter.
Both activities could be critical to moving forward implemen-
tation in contexts where problem-­centered OGD initiatives are
lagging or only partially implemented.
In terms of future research, we encourage scholarships at the
intersection of policymaking, government outcomes, and
problem-­centered OGD initiatives. The contextual use of
problem-­centered OGD initiatives in policymaking is yet to be

investigated, particularly its effects on governance outcomes.
For instance, a lack of inclusion of relevant stakeholders at crit-
ical phases, such as the problem definition, might undermine
the legitimacy of and trust toward problem-­centered OGD ini-
tiatives, with data perceived as biased or not representing the
underlying issue (Boda et al. 2023; Becerra et al. 2024). We also
know little about the use of problem-­centered initiatives in other
phases of policymaking—for example, policy design, implemen-
tation, enforcement, and evaluation. Early studies show that
environmental OGD initiatives increase the accountability of
government officials during policy enforcement but do not fos-
ter responsiveness to residents (Li 2023). Other studies highlight
the challenges faced by communities when using OGD to keep
government and non-­government actors accountable during
policy evaluation and design (Boda et  al.  2023; LoMonte and
Delgado 2023). Mismatches between data provision and policy
action might translate into perceptions of government openness
as a smokescreen, with negative repercussions on trust.
We also encourage the testing of our framework across different
policy domains, geographical contexts, and government levels
to establish a rich body of empirical evidence. Policy domains
might differ in key problem characteristics (e.g., magnitude,
frequency, and severity) and in public influence and support.
Moreover, diversity in government structures can affect the
ability of subnational governments to adopt specific policy ap-
proaches and frameworks. In these contexts, technological
capacity is likely to play a more central role. Similarly, public
pressure can exert varying levels of influence on government,
typically affecting local governments more than state or federal
governments. Future studies might also find other conducive
conditions that expand our framework, especially as problem-­
centered OGD initiatives become more diffused.
To conclude, a more solid scholarship on problem-­centered
OGD initiatives will enable government agencies to harness the
synergies of the civic-­tech and problem-­centered approaches to
advance open government goals. Government agencies struggle
to find significant resources and interest to support civic-­tech
open data. As a Chief Data Officer stated, “shared services, and
especially new shared services [such as data], are structurally
underfunded. They have no natural constituency groups, in-
cluding interest and advocacy groups, to push for their funding
and support” (Bonaguro 2023). Problem-­centered OGD initia-
tives have the potential to coalesce broader constituents, such
as advocacy and economic interest groups. Furthermore, policy
specialization can facilitate the creation of shared open data in-
frastructures and practices across government agencies. While
civic-­tech OGD initiatives have been and should continue to be
critical for promoting overall transparency, problem-­centered
OGD initiatives can promote government's goals of participa-
tion and accountability through a different approach to data
openness.

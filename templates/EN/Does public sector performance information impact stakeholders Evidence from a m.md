Abstract
Performance information (PI) has received significant attention in public adminis-
tration research. However, evaluating the impact of public sector PI on stake-
holders is challenging due to varying empirical results. Drawing on information
propagation theory, as well as social and cognitive psychology, we conduct a
meta-analysis to examine the effect of public sector PI. Using 461 effect sizes from
75 studies, the meta-analysis reveals PI’s positive effects on stakeholder attitudes,
behaviors, and perceptions of performance. Moreover, the effects tend to be stron-
ger when PI is sent by third parties, received by citizens, delivered with positive
valence, presented in absolute forms, and disseminated in law enforcement
administrative subfields and in societies characterized by low power distance. The
findings reinforce the significance of public sector PI and illuminate the complex
interplay between it and stakeholder responses.
Evidence for practice
• Public sector performance information significantly impacts stakeholder atti-
tudes, behaviors, and perceived performance.
• Third-party delivery of public sector performance information is more influential
on stakeholders than self-reporting.
• Citizens are the most responsive to public sector performance information, while
politicians are the least.
• Using positively valenced and absolute performance information is more effec-
tive in engaging stakeholders.
• Enhance transparency in performance information disclosure in law enforce-
ment subfields and in low power distance cultures to meet stakeholder
demands.
INTRODUCTION
Performance information (PI) is key to performance manage-
ment and has become a critical focus in public administra-
tion (PA) research (Moynihan & Pandey, 2010). Over the past
two decades, the utility of information on the performance
of public services has dramatically expanded, spanning
countries, sectors, and levels of government (Behn, 2003;
Hood, 2012; James & Van Ryzin, 2017; Moynihan, 2008b;
Pollitt, 2018; Walker et al., 2024). A central aim of the PI sys-
tem is to improve governance (James, 2011). Consequently,
scholars have shown increasing interest in exploring the
impact of PI in public sectors. Recently, the number of avail-
able PI studies has grown considerably, establishing a thriv-
ing
field
in
PA
scholarship
(e.g.,
Baekgaard
&
Serritzlew,
2016;
Cantarelli
et
al.,
2023;
Hansen
&
Nielsen, 2022; Kroll, 2015; Porumbescu et al., 2022).
Despite the growing availability and diversity of stud-
ies, a pressing question persists: “Does public sector PI sig-
nificantly impact stakeholders1?” Opinions among scholars
are divided. Some argue that PI has failed to contribute as
intended. For instance, Petersen et al. (2019) conclude that
PI exhibits the least credibility as a management tool
among frontline employees in poorly performing public
organizations. Others contend that PI has a substantial
impact on public sector employees, whether it is negative,
positive, or average (e.g., Nielsen & Jacobsen, 2018). This
debate points to a broader issue: the real-world utility of PI,
which often leaves practitioners puzzled by the mixed
results from empirical studies.

Another significant issue is, “How can PI be utilized
more effectively?” In PA research, this question is among
the three major inquiries aimed at finding ways to measure
performance
to
enhance

(Behn,
2003).
According to information propagation theory, the effect of
information is determined by several key factors: senders,
recipients,
information
characteristics,
and
contexts
(Lasswell, 1948). While current PI research involves diverse
senders, recipients, and information characteristics across
various administrative subfields and cultures, it often fails
to comprehensively explore how these elements influence
stakeholder responses. Without a thorough understanding
of these dynamics, establishing the necessary conditions
for successful performance management remains challeng-
ing (Kroll, 2015; O’Toole & Meier, 2015).
To address these questions, we conducted a meta-
analysis of the effects of public sector PI on its stake-
holders based on 461 effect sizes from 75 studies
(N = 132,647). Meta-analysis is based on independent
original studies but goes beyond them, allowing us to
obtain a population estimate of the relationships between
variables (Schmidt & Hunter, 2015). By leveraging theories
from information propagation and integrating insights
from social and cognitive psychology, we identify key
moderators—such as PI senders, recipients, characteris-
tics, and contexts—that influence PI’s effect.
The goals of the present meta-analysis are twofold:
(1) comprehensively search, review, and utilize meta-
analytic techniques to examine the effect of PI in public
sectors and (2) to identify the moderating effects of PI
senders, recipients, characteristics, and contexts. The find-
ings will contribute to theory and inform administrative
practices in public performance management. Scholars
will gain deeper insights into PI-stakeholder dynamics
and key contingencies, facilitating the development of
more nuanced theoretical frameworks that guide future
research and advance the field. Practitioners will be able
to determine who is expected to send PI, how PI affects
different recipients, which types of PI are most engaging,
and where to focus attention when transferring these
conclusions from one context to another.
In the following sections, we first present the “5W”2
information propagation model, along with insights from
social and cognitive psychology, and integrate these theo-
ries to derive expectations. Then, we introduce the meta-
analytic method and present the results. Finally, we con-
clude with a discussion of the results, implications for the lit-
erature, practice, and recommendations for future research.
LITERATURE REVIEW
PI in the public sector
Public sector PI is defined as any regular, systematic, and
quantitative information that captures the inputs, actions,
outputs,
or
outcomes
of
public
services
and
the
organizations
that
deliver
them
(James,
2011;
Olsen, 2015b). The surge in publicly available PI on public
services and service providers is linked to the “New Public
Management” reform wave across many countries. As
part of the trend, PA practitioners have swiftly realized
the significance of PI. As Williams (2003) has noted, PI
connects contemporary performance measures to the
development of the modern state. Similarly, PA scholars
have also paid keen attention to PI research, initially
focusing on the antecedents of PI use (Kroll, 2013, 2015).
More recently, both theoretical and empirical studies
have begun to focus on its consequences (e.g., Cantarelli
et al., 2023; Porumbescu et al., 2022; Walker et al., 2024).
The “5W” information propagation model, initially
introduced by Lasswell in 1948, provides a comprehen-
sive framework for summarizing the effects of PI. This
model identifies five core elements in the information
propagation
process:
senders,
recipients,
information characteristics, contexts or channels, and the
effects it produces. While the “5W” information propaga-
tion model delineates the communication process, it does
not fully account for the psychological intricacies involved
in how stakeholders interpret information. This is particu-
larly evident in the context of PI within the public sector,
where cognitive processing is not merely a rational
endeavor but is deeply intertwined with heuristics and
personal experiences (Battaglio et al., 2019; Olsen, 2015a).
To address this limitation, we have integrated theories
from cognitive and social psychology into our framework.
These theories provide insights into fundamental psycho-
logical processes such as attention, memory, and cogni-
tive biases, which influence how individuals perceive,
process, and react to information. Building on these theo-
ries, we have developed a conceptual framework to guide
our meta-analysis (see Figure 1).
The effects of PI
Extensive public sector PI research has examined its
effects across diverse variables (Cantarelli et al., 2023).
Our study focuses on three critical stakeholder responses:
attitudes, behaviors, and perceptions of performance,
owing to their theoretical, practical, and empirical signifi-
cance. Theoretically, cognitive and social psychology
posits that human information processing influences per-
ceptions, attitudes, and behaviors, thereby providing
insights into individual experiences (Battaglio et al., 2019;
Olsen, 2015a). Practically, issues of democracy and effi-
ciency remain central to modern governance. Stakeholder
attitudes and their use of PI for accountability are key
indicators of democracy, while perceptions of perfor-
mance are essential for evaluating public sector efficiency
(Brunner
et
al.,
2022).
Empirically,
this
categorical
approach is well-recognized for enhancing our compre-
hensive understanding of public sector PI’s impact on its
stakeholders (e.g., James, 2011; Porumbescu et al., 2019).

When reviewing research on PI in the public sector,
the findings regarding its effects have been mixed. Most
studies indicate that PI significantly influences stake-
holder attitudes, behaviors, and perceptions of perfor-
mance (e.g., James, 2011; Nielsen & Baekgaard, 2015),
while a few studies argue that PI does not achieve its
intended effects (e.g., Mizrahi & Minchuk, 2019; Petersen
et al., 2019). Building on information propagation theory,
insights from social and cognitive psychology, and find-
ings from most empirical studies, we expect that public
sector PI significantly affects stakeholders’ attitudes,
behaviors, and perceptions of performance.
Potential moderators
PI sender
While PI is disseminated across countries, sectors, and
domains, it is essential to avoid automatically assuming
that the public sector is the exclusive source of PI
(Petersen, 2020). Therefore, the primary question revolves
around, “How does self-reported PI regarding public sec-
tors impact stakeholders compared to PI reported by
third parties?” The latter includes informal, non-routine
sources of PI, such as personal experiences with public
services or media coverage of incidents and cases
(Kroll, 2013).
Empirical studies suggest that PI from third parties
might have a stronger effect. This is because public sec-
tors, often subject to anti-public sector bias and perceived
as inefficient (Marvel, 2015), may not be viewed as objec-
tive in their performance reporting (Meier et al., 2022).
Reinforcing this skepticism, numerous documented cases
reveal that public sectors have manipulated or misrepre-
sented
data
to
present
themselves
more
favorably
(Christensen & Lægreid, 2021), often engaging in strategic
manipulation
or
“gaming”
(Bevan
&
Hood,
2006;
Pollitt, 2013).
In contrast, PI provided by third parties, which lack
direct governmental and financial connections, is per-
ceived as more trustworthy and less biased, thereby
enhancing its credibility (James & Van Ryzin, 2017). Addi-
tionally, PI from third parties, such as news reports and
personal narratives, tends to be more engaging and relat-
able than the abstract data typically presented by public
sectors (Moynihan, 2008a; Olsen, 2015b; Petersen, 2020).
Supporting this argument, Valant and Newark (2020)
found that parents’ comments significantly affect percep-
tions of school quality, even more than official ratings.
Based on these insights, we incorporate PI sender as a
moderator in our meta-analysis, specifically to examine
whether PI from third parties exerts a greater influence
on stakeholders than PI from the public sectors.
PI recipient
The “to whom” element of the “5W” information propa-
gation model focuses on the recipients of information.
Regarding public sector PI, recipients generally fall into
categories such as citizens, public sector employees, pub-
lic sector managers, and politicians (Olsen, 2015b; Walker
et al., 2018). Insights from social and cognitive psychology
reveal that the individual characteristics and cognitive
structures of these groups influence their interpretation
and response to PI (Olsen, 2015a; Webeck & Nicholson-
Crotty, 2020). The public, often possessing less specialized
knowledge and having limited access to PI (Fuenzalida
et al., 2021), tends to exhibit a more instinctual “stimulus–
response” pattern. In contrast, public sector officials and
politicians,
leveraging
their
extensive
knowledge
and skills, engage with PI in a more analytical and rea-
soned manner (Geys & Sorensen, 2018; Olsen, 2015b).
Empirical evidence indicates that these more informed
groups
tend
to
hold
low
valuations
of
PI
(e.g., Demaj, 2017). Therefore, we include PI recipient as a
moderator in our meta-analysis.

PI characteristics
In the “5W” information propagation model, “says what”
concerns the nature of the information being communi-
cated, encompassing two primary aspects of PI research:
form and valence. The form of PI pertains to its presenta-
tion style, distinguishing between absolute and relative
forms. Absolute PI describes specific service attributes,
focusing on intrinsic qualities without comparisons. Rela-
tive PI compares a local service to similar entities or its
past performance, providing a comparative framework
(Barrows et al., 2016; James & Moseley, 2014). Social and
cognitive psychology suggests that individuals have
innate
tendencies
to
seek
comparative
benchmarks
(Festinger, 1954). Building on this theory, empirical stud-
ies have demonstrated that PI with reference points will
affect citizens’ perceptions of performance even in the
presence of absolute PI (e.g., Olsen, 2017). We therefore
include PI form as a moderator, particularly to investigate
whether relative PI has a greater influence on stake-
holders than absolute PI.
The second aspect of the research pertains to
stakeholders’ different responses to negative-valence
or positive-valence PI (James et al., 2016; Nielsen &
Moynihan, 2017). Social and cognitive psychology pro-
vides a critical lens for understanding these differen-
tial responses, particularly through negativity bias and
equivalence framing effects (Battaglio et al., 2019).
Negativity
bias
posits
that
individuals
react
more
intensely to poor public sector performance compared
with good performance (Hood, 2007). Further, the
equivalence framing effect clarifies the distinct impact
of descriptive valence by demonstrating that negative-
valence PI has a more substantial effect than positive-
valence PI, even when both convey logically equiva-
lent information.
Empirical evidence generally supports these argu-
ments. For instance, Boyne et al. (2009) demonstrate that
only the distinction between low and minimally accept-
able performance matters, with no rewards for high per-
formance. Similarly, James and Moseley (2014) observe a
significant drop in citizen satisfaction in response to low
performance, without a corresponding increase in satis-
faction when performance was high. Additionally, Olsen
(2015a) reveals the equivalence framing effect, showing
that presenting a 10% dissatisfaction rate leads to notably
lower evaluations of public service compared with pre-
senting a 90% satisfaction rate, despite these figures
being logically equivalent.
However, it is essential to recognize that some studies
question the generalizability of the above effects. They
suggest that, while stakeholders may exhibit bias, they
are not blind (Christensen, 2018), and that negativity bias
in
response
to
PI
is
reduced
(e.g.,
Alon-Barkat
&
Gilad,
2017;
Mizrahi
&
Minchuk,
2019;
Nielsen
&
Baekgaard, 2015). Given the mixed evidence, we include
PI valence as a moderator in our meta-analysis.
Contextual factors
In the “5W” information propagation model, the “chan-
nel” component primarily concerns the contexts in which
information is disseminated. In the context of PI research,
this encompasses varying administrative settings across
public service sectors and levels of government, as well
as diverse social and political cultures (James & Van
Ryzin, 2017; Walker et al., 2024). While numerous contex-
tual variables can influence the effects of PI, only a limited
number are widely applicable and reported in PI studies.
Administrative subfield and national culture are two nota-
ble factors.
First, PI contexts vary significantly across administra-
tive
subfields
(Filtenborg
et
al.,
2017;
Geys
&
Sorensen, 2018). Social and cognitive psychology sug-
gests that information has a more significant impact
when the environmental context is closer to an individ-
ual’s psychological proximity. Consequently, PI in law
enforcement, healthcare, and social services subfields
may elicit stronger responses due to their direct links to
public safety, health, and quality of life—areas of signifi-
cant public interest (Baekgaard, 2015; Christensen, 2018;
van den Bekerom et al., 2021). In contrast, PI in education
and core administration, while essential, often impacts
more indirectly and over the long term, leading to less
immediate public reactions. Supporting this argument,
Mizrahi and Minchuk (2019) note that the public views PI
on police services as more influential than educational
PI. Thus, we expect that the implications of PI are likely to
vary across administrative subfields.
Second, as PI studies expand across the globe, it
becomes imperative to evaluate the applicability of their
findings across diverse cultural contexts (Marvel, 2015). A
key factor in this evaluation is power distance (PD), recog-
nized for influencing stakeholders’ implicit attitudes
toward the public sector (Marvel, 2015). Specifically, PD
describes the extent to which individuals accept and
expect power to be distributed unequally within organi-
zations and societies (Hofstede, 2001). In high PD con-
texts,
where
the
public
sector
is
perceived
as
authoritative, there is typically a conservative approach to
the accessibility of public sector PI. Conversely, in cultures
characterized by low PD, there is an expectation of more
equitable
power
distribution.
They
place
a
greater
emphasis on democratic values and participation (Zhang
et al., 2022). Thus, individuals in such societies may be
more proactive in using public sector PI and respond
more actively to it. Accordingly, we include power dis-
tance as a moderator to assess whether the effects of PI
are influenced by national cultural values.

to
acknowledge
that

utilizes
correlational methods and is not designed to confirm cau-

correlations as indicators of PI consequences is based on
theoretical support (Lasswell, 1948; Olsen, 2015b) and is
consistent
with
accepted
practices
in
PA
research
(Backhaus & Vogel, 2022; Ringquist, 2013).
Literature search
We conducted an extensive literature search to include all
relevant studies. First, we conducted an online literature
search in the Web of Science, Wiley Online Library, and
JSTOR
databases
using
the
term
“performance
information,”
with
the
research
area
restricted
to
PA. Second, we manually searched relevant journals
(e.g., Public Administration Review, Journal of Public Admin-
istration Research and Theory, Public Administration, Public
Management Review, Public Performance & Management
Review, Government Information Quarterly, and Interna-
tional Review of Administrative Sciences). Third, we exam-
ined the reference lists of relevant meta-analytic reviews
and
theoretical
reviews
on
PI
(e.g.,
Kroll,
2015;
Olsen, 2015b). The cutoff date for our search was October
31, 2024. Employing these search strategies, we initially
found 921 records. After removing duplicates, we had
576 records for further screening.
Inclusion criteria
We included studies that met the following criteria. First,
they had to be empirical. Second, they should test the
effect of PI on stakeholder consequences (i.e., attitudes,
behaviors, and perceptions of performance). Third, they
should report sample sizes and provide either correlations
for
relevant
relationships
or
other
statistical
data
(e.g., regression coefficients and standard errors) to calcu-
late correlation-based effect sizes. Last, when the same
sample was used in multiple studies, we retained each
unique correlation only once. Ultimately, these criteria
yielded
a
total
of
179
independent
samples
from
75 studies (Online Appendix 1 presents a flow chart of
our screening processes, and Online Appendix 2 presents
details of the studies included in the meta-analysis).
Coding procedure
Coding was conducted in three steps. First, the authors
developed a coding scheme together. Two authors then
independently coded several studies based on this initial
scheme. Any coding discrepancies were resolved through
discussion, resulting in a refined coding scheme. In the
second
step,
two
authors
independently
coded
all
75 studies using the refined coding scheme. The inter-
coder agreement was 97%, and any remaining differences
were resolved through discussion. In the final step, the
first author double-checked all samples to ensure coding
accuracy. For each study in our meta-analysis, we
recorded statistical information necessary for calculating
effect sizes, sample sizes, key variables (i.e., PI and its con-
sequences), measurements of key variables, their reliabil-
ities,
and
publication
information.
Additionally,
for
moderation analyses, we documented PI senders, recipi-
ents, characteristics, and contexts. Detailed coding is
described as follows.
Performance information
All types of PI were grouped into a broader category for
the main effects of meta-analysis. For moderating ana-
lyses, we coded the PI senders, PI forms, and PI valence:
First, we assigned primary studies to either self-reported
PI (PI from the public sector) or PI from third parties
(e.g., PI from citizens, PI from media). Second, the PI form
was divided into two categories: relative and absolute.
Third, PI is identified as either positive or negative. This
coding was based on an assessment of the PI valence,
which was mostly evident from the label or description of
the variables. It’s important to note that some studies
were excluded from moderating analyses due to the
absence of a clear sign in terms of source, form, or
valence.
PI consequences
First, the consequences of PI were coded as stakeholder
attitudes, behaviors, and perceptions of performance to
inform the main effects. To better explore the effects of PI
on different recipients, we further coded the PI conse-
quences into four categories: Citizen-related (e.g., trust in
police), public sector employee-related (e.g., acceptance
of management authority), public sector manager-related
(e.g., managerial innovation adoption), and politician-
related (e.g., preference for reforms). Grouping multiple
original variables into width-band categories is a common

Meta-analytic procedures
We adopted Schmidt and Hunter’s (2015) meta-analytic
method to analyze the data. For studies including more
than one effect size estimate for the same relationship,
we
used
the

(Dahlke

effect sizes. When studies did not report reliability esti-

To test the main effects of PI, we reported the

RESULTS
Heterogeneity test
The heterogeneity test analyzes the variability of effect
sizes to determine whether a fixed-effects model or a
random-effects model should be used in the meta-
analysis. If the effect sizes are homogeneous, a fixed-
effects model should be used. However, if heterogeneous,
a random-effects model should be used. As shown in
Table 1, the results indicate high heterogeneity in the
effect sizes of each meta-analytic relationship, with all
Q statistics being significant (p < .001) and I2 values
exceeding 75%. Therefore, we conduct the meta-analysis
using a random-effects model.
Main effects and publication basis tests
Table 1 shows that PI positively relates to overall stake-
holder consequences (ρ = .181, 95% CI [0.141, 0.220]).
Specifically, PI is positively associated with stakeholder
attitudes,
behaviors,
and
perceived
performance
(ρ = .345, 95% CI [0.271, 0.419]; ρ = .065, 95% CI [0.021,
0.110]; ρ = .131, 95% CI [0.088, 0.175], respectively). More-
over, all Nfs are well above 5 k + 10, suggesting that the
examined meta-analytic relationships in our study are
robust to publication bias. In addition, the significant Q-
statistics, high I2 values, and wide 80% CVs further indi-
cate the existence of potential moderators.

Specifically, we note that the original studies included in
the meta-analysis involve various research designs, such as
field experiments, survey experiments, and surveys. Accord-
ing to James et al. (2017), these designs vary in their causal
inference strength. Thus, we explore how research design
affects PI’s effect (for details, see Online Appendix 5). The
results show that there are differences in effect sizes across
designs (Qb = 261.686, p < .001). Specifically, PI demon-
strates stronger effects in the survey experiments (ρ = .196),
while exhibiting weaker effects in the field experiments and
surveys (ρs = .064 and .068, respectively).
Moreover, we adopt a bandwidth approach to code
the dependent variables, which provides clearer cues for
management decisions but inevitably encompasses multi-
ple original variables under each category (for details, see
Online Appendix 3). To retain as much detail as possible,
we analyze the relationships between PI and more
nuanced categorized dependent variables (for details, see
Online Appendix 6).
Finally, we conduct robustness checks in five ways (for
details, see Online Appendix 7). First, we examine the
classification of dependent variables, using expert ratings
to identify any ambiguities. We reanalyze controversial
variables under alternative categories, and the findings
generally align with the initial results (for details, see
Table A1 and Table A2). Second, we perform one-sample
removed sensitivity analyses to determine the impact of
outliers
on our
meta-analytic results. As shown in
Figure A1, the estimates range from 0.175 to 0.192, which
do not differ much from PI’s overall effect size in our
meta-analysis (ρ = .181). Third, we use the bootstrap
method to test the robustness of the main effects of PI
(for details, see Table A3). The results show that our meta-
analytic effect sizes for the impacts of PI on attitudes,
behaviors, and perceptions of performance fall within the
95% bootstrap CIs, indicating that all these meta-analytic
relationships
are
statistically
significant
and
robust.
Fourth, we change the analytical approach for the moder-
ating variables. Specifically, we conduct meta-regressions
to analyze the moderating role of categorical variables
(i.e., PI source, recipient, form, valence, and subfield), all
yielding robust results (for details, see Table A4). We also
convert the continuous PD scores to a categorical variable
and conduct a subgroup analysis to examine its moderat-
ing effect (for details, see Table A5). Fifth, we exclude
three independent studies with the largest sample sizes
(N > Nmean + 3 �SD), recalculate the moderating effects,
and find that the results are robust. Details are provided
in Table A6 and Table A7 in Online Appendix 7.
DISCUSSION
Our findings corroborate existing literature while also
offering new insights. Using a meta-analytic approach, we
identify significant relationships between PI and stake-
holder attitudes, behaviors, and perceptions of perfor-
mance. This finding contributes to the ongoing debate on
the effects of PI in the public sector, reinforcing the con-
clusion that PI has a meaningful impact on stakeholders
and providing a foundation for future research. Moreover,
the observed impact of PI on stakeholders supports our
view of PI as part of a broader, interconnected communi-
cation network, thereby advancing the integration of
information propagation theory with the PI literature.
Regarding the moderating role of PI source, we find
that PI from third parties exerts a greater influence on
stakeholders than PI from the public sectors. This finding
is consistent with prior literature and can be attributed to
the greater perceived credibility of third-party sources
(James & Van Ryzin, 2017). It prompts a critical reassess-
ment of how public entities manage and disseminate
PI.
The
reassessment
is
amplified
by
documented
instances of data manipulation by public sectors (Bevan &
Hood, 2006; Pollitt, 2013), which have bolstered the skep-
ticism and bias (Marvel, 2015; Meier et al., 2022).
Another focus of the study is the moderating role of
PI recipients. The results indicate that citizens are highly
responsive to PI, suggesting that it can effectively engage
the public by raising awareness and shaping expectations
of the public sector. This engagement helps create a
more informed citizenry that can better articulate its
needs and hold the public sector accountable. In contrast,
politicians are less influenced by PI, which aligns with the
view that cognitive engagement and professionalism dif-
fer between elites and the public (e.g., Webeck &
Nicholson-Crotty, 2020). These findings suggest that while
PI effectively engages citizens, further research is needed
to explore how it can be better tailored to engage politi-
cians and strengthen political accountability.

We also examine the moderating role of PI form and
valence. Surprisingly, relative PI exerts less influence on
stakeholders than absolute PI. This result may arise from
how they are presented. Absolute PI is typically clear and
direct, evoking a stronger immediate reaction. In contrast,
relative PI involves comparison and requires more cogni-
tive effort, leading to a more indirect response. Moreover,
contrary to our expectation, the result indicates that
positive-valence PI has a stronger effect than negative-
valence PI. One possible explanation is that PI may help
clarify performance differences, potentially shifting the
traditionally
negativity-based
game
(Nielsen
&
Baekgaard, 2015). Another factor could be the growing
use of promotional symbols alongside PI in public sectors,
which may subtly shift negativity biases and trigger more
positive responses from stakeholders (Alon-Barkat &
Gilad, 2017). Methodological factors may also help explain
this finding. In moderating analyses, we combined atti-
tude, behavior, and perception of performance as an
overall stakeholder outcome. However, PI shows a rela-
tively weak connection to behavior. A large portion of the
outcome variables examined for negative-valence PI are
behavioral, which may contribute to the smaller effect
observed. Given this, the counterintuitive results should
be
interpreted
with
caution
and
warrant
further
investigation.
In response to persistent calls within PI research
(e.g., Marvel, 2015; O’Toole & Meier, 2015; van den
Bekerom et al., 2021), we finally explore the moderating
roles of administrative subfields and power distance. The
results demonstrate that PI significantly influences stake-
holders in the law enforcement and health subfields,
aligning with previous findings that highlight the direct
impact of these areas on personal security and health
(Hansen & Nielsen, 2022; Meier et al., 2022). Additionally,
we observe that PI in public sectors has significantly
stronger impacts in low power distance societies. This
may be because the disclosure of public sector PI aligns
better with the implicit preferences of individuals in these
cultures, leading to a more pronounced influence. Overall,
these findings not only advance our understanding of the
effects of PI in different administrative subfields but also
suggest directions for future research, such as investigat-
ing how PI strategies can be adapted to different cultural
contexts.
CONCLUSION
As the effects of PI in PA continue to receive increasing
attention without a clear consensus, we conducted a
meta-analysis based on 75 studies. Our results underscore
the significance of PI, consistently demonstrating positive
relationships with stakeholder attitudes, behaviors, and
perceived performance. Furthermore, we identify factors
contributing to heterogeneity in these relationships,
including
PI
senders,
recipients,
characteristics,
and
contextual factors. Specifically, the effects of PI are stron-
ger when sent by third parties, received by citizens, deliv-
ered with positive valence, presented in absolute forms,
and disseminated in law enforcement administrative sub-
fields and in societies characterized by low power
distance.
The present research offers significant theoretical con-
tributions. First, we introduce the “5W” model of informa-
tion propagation to PA, integrating cognitive and social
psychology to explore PI effects. The “5W” model pro-
vides a robust framework for understanding key elements
in PI’s dissemination process (Lasswell, 1948), while psy-
chological insights reveal micro-mechanisms shaping
stakeholders’ responses toward PI (Battaglio et al., 2019).
Building on these theories, we conduct a meta-analysis to
synthesize empirical findings, provide staged conclusions
on PI effects, and advance understanding of its broader
implications for stakeholders. Second, this study advances
PI literature by clarifying the boundary conditions of its
effects. We find that PI is more impactful when sent by
third parties, highlighting the importance of independent
sources in shaping public opinion, especially when stake-
holders are skeptical of direct public sector reports. Our
research also underscores the moderating role of PI recip-
ients, offering a more nuanced view of stakeholder
engagement and suggesting the need for tailored com-
munication strategies to address the needs of multiple
stakeholders. By examining the effects of PI form and
valence, our research responds to calls for strategies to
enhance PI effectiveness (Moynihan, 2008a; Olsen, 2015b)
and contributes to the ongoing debate on negativity bias.
Furthermore, we advocate for culturally sensitive and
administratively nuanced approaches to PI, setting the
stage for future research on context-specific strategies to
communicate PI.
This study also provides practical implications. Public
sectors should recognize the importance of PI and focus
on the key elements of its dissemination process to use it
more effectively. Specifically, while PI from third-parties
plays an important role, distributing formal performance
reports is equally vital, especially given the limited access
to high-quality PI through informal channels. By coordi-
nating different sources of PI, public sectors can more
effectively inform and engage a broader range of stake-
holders. For PI recipients, public sectors should adopt an
iterative approach, continuously refining PI by incorporat-
ing citizen feedback, which makes the information more
communicative and responsive. The limited impact of PI
on politicians highlights the need for them to give more
attention to these metrics when shaping public service
agendas. Regarding PI characteristics, public sectors are
encouraged to present PI in a clear and straightforward
manner. They are also advised to transparently disclose
PI, regardless of whether it is positive or negative. This
approach better informs stakeholders and helps mitigate
concerns about potential manipulation. For different con-
texts, public sectors could tailor their PI communication

strategies. For example, in the subfield of education,
which targets complex and long-term objectives, provid-
ing detailed explanations of how PI relates to educational
goals is essential for improving stakeholder understand-
ing. In cultures with high power distances, inclusive PI
strategies are essential to facilitate equal dialogue and
mutual understanding.
Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First,
meta-analysis is a correlational method, which restricts
causal conclusions. Referring to correlates as PI conse-
quences is a conceptual choice consistent with PA litera-
ture (Backhaus & Vogel, 2022; Ringquist, 2013). Future
research should adopt more rigorous designs to draw
causal inferences. Second, studies included in this meta-
analysis primarily examine the effects of PI on proximal
outcomes—attitudes,
behaviors,
and
perceptions
of
performance—mainly within the field of behavioral public
administration, based on survey experiments conducted
in developed countries. As PI research advances, future
research could benefit from employing more nuanced
coding schemes to capture a wider range of PI conse-
quences, including objective performance measures and
long-term impacts. They could also explore the weak-
nesses of PI in relation to the broader concept of perfor-
mance management within PA and broaden PI studies
across diverse contexts, particularly those with varying
levels of trust and corruption. Third, while several moder-
ators of PI effects have been considered, the mechanisms
underlying these effects remain underexplored. Future
research could examine additional moderators, such as
individual characteristics, and investigate the pathways
through which PI influences stakeholders. Last, the public
and private sectors have distinct responsibilities and
social roles, which may lead to different implications of
PI. However, recent research suggests that performance
measurement and management systems might be more
transferable
across
sectors
than
previously
believed
(Webeck & Nicholson-Crotty, 2020). Further research is
recommended to clarify the effects of PI across different
sectors and assess the generalizability of our findings.

How Public Officials Perceive Algorithmic Discretion:
A Study of Status Quo Bias in Policing

ABSTRACT
Algorithms are disrupting established decision-­making practices in public administration. A key area of interest lies in algo-
rithmic discretion or how public officials use algorithms to exercise discretion. The article develops a framework to explain
algorithmic discretion by drawing on status quo bias theory and bureaucratic discretion. A study with police officers in the UK
shows that—while officers still value their discretion—it is resistance via the aspects of status quo bias that accounts for a more
substantial explanation. Transition costs, loss aversion, and performance uncertainty determine resistance and, in turn, reluc-
tance to delegate discretion to algorithms. The study contributes to public administration research that demonstrates the influ-
ence of cognitive biases in the increasing use of algorithms in areas like policing. The article concludes with recommendations
for embedding algorithmic discretion into the professional development of public officials to mitigate sources of status quo bias.
1   |   Introduction
Algorithms and data-­intensive technologies are being increas-
ingly used in public services like welfare, healthcare, emergency
management, law enforcement and education (Busuioc  2021;
Meijer et al. 2021; Vogl et al. 2020). An emerging area of interest
lies in how algorithmic technologies interact with the decision-­
making processes of public officials (Roehl and Hansen 2024;
Schwarz et al. 2022). Discretion remains a fundamental aspect
of decision-­making in the public sector and is essential for street-­
level bureaucrats to navigate uncertainties in their daily roles
(Lipsky  2010). Building on Bovens and Zouridis  (2002) and
Buffat (2015), scholars have explored the relationship between
discretion and digital technologies. Their findings focus on how
technologies enable or curtail discretion and the extent to which
technologies affect the enforcement styles of street-­level bureau-
crats (de Boer and Raaphorst 2023; Deng and Sun 2024; Headley
et  al.  2025; Wang et  al.  2024). Another significant line of re-
search has shown how algorithmization affects discretion in dif-
ferent administrative contexts (Bullock 2019; Meijer et al. 2021;
Nagtegaal 2021).
Critical to these perspectives is the need to understand how
public officials interpret changes to their decision-­making
authority (Afzal and Panagiotopoulos  2024; Roehl and
Hansen  2024). Scholars have theorized the automation, re-
placement, augmentation, and exercise of discretion under-
pinned by digital technologies with concepts like digital
discretion (Busch and Henriksen  2018), automated discre-
tion (Zouridis et  al.  2020), and artificial discretion (Young
et al. 2019). Our approach focuses on algorithmic discretion
as the perceptions of public officials when using algorithms in
their routines, practices, and decisions to exercise discretion.
The starting point is that the inherent properties and opaque-
ness of algorithms present a complex setting for public offi-
cials when interpreting changes to their discretion beyond
automating tasks. These manifest in issues like low explain-
ability, interpretability, and scrutiny of algorithmic outputs
(Busuioc 2021).

how public officials evaluate algorithmic decisions. Notably, a
large-­scale experiment by Alon-­Barkat and Busuioc (2023) found

The need to examine public officials' underlying behavioral re-
sponses guides our research question: to what extent does status
quo bias affect public officials' perceptions of algorithmic discre-
tion? Our attention to status quo bias comes from the limited
understanding of the sources of skepticism to change that public
officials are assumed to exhibit when algorithmic technologies
challenge their discretion. By explaining algorithmic discretion
from a status quo bias perspective, we aim to extend our under-
standing beyond merely distinguishing its negative or positive
impacts and to consider how professional development prac-
tices can more critically integrate algorithmic discretion. This
is timely when cognitive biases like loss aversion are frequently
observed in automated decision-­making but remain largely un-
explained (Battaglio et al. 2019).
To pursue this aim, we develop a theoretical framework that in-
tegrates two concurrent explanations for algorithmic discretion.
We first hypothesize that public officials attach great impor-
tance to the discretion they perceive to possess (discretion-­as-­
perceived) in routine decision-­making (Anteby et  al.  2016;
Freidson 2001; Thomann et al. 2018). As a result, they would
tend to oppose delegating part of their decision-­making author-
ity to algorithms to avoid consequences like fears of reduced au-
tonomy and deterioration of professional standards (Buffat 2015;
Vogl et al. 2020). Second, we draw on the status quo bias theory
to account for the limitations and assumptions of public offi-
cials and explain their sources of reluctance to change (Kim and
Kankanhalli 2009; Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988). The the-
ory allows us to incorporate the properties of algorithms in the
form of different sources of status quo bias that prevent changes
to public officials' decision-­making routines.
To empirically validate the framework, we present results
from a survey study with police officers in the UK (n = 260).
Algorithmic practices like predictive policing have triggered
significant debates over how they affect the discretion of police
officers when transitioning from established ways of working
(Bullock 2019; Meijer et al. 2021). Policing—generally regarded
as a high-­discretion profession—presents a stimulating setting
to contribute to our understanding of algorithmic discretion
and its implications in other areas of public administration
(Bullock 2019; Young et al. 2019).
The findings show that, while perceived discretion exhibits an
expected negative relationship with algorithmic discretion, it
is resistance via the different aspects of status quo bias that ac-
counts for a more significant outcome. In particular, attitudes
formed around transition costs, loss aversion, and performance
uncertainty explain resistance and, in turn, hesitancy to dele-
gate discretion to algorithms. The findings indicate that, al-
though police officers value their discretion, they would be
willing to engage more positively with algorithms depending on
their assessment of decision-­making complexities, implementa-
tion costs, and confidence about the outputs of algorithms. We
conclude with suggestions on how algorithmic discretion should
be embedded in the professional development of public officials
to mitigate sources of status quo bias.
2   |   Theoretical Development and Hypotheses
Discretion is a key aspect of decision-­making in public admin-
istration and its relationship with digital technologies has been
ambiguous. Bovens and Zouridis  (2002) framed the profound
implications of digital technologies for decision-­making as a
move from street-­level to screen-­level and system-­level bureau-
cracy. The transition from face-­to-­face interactions to screens,
computers, systems, data, and algorithms marks different as-
pects of standardization, formalization, and centralization of
public professionals' working practices. The adoption of algo-
rithmic tools in policing, such as the UK's Harm Assessment
Risk Tool (HART), exemplifies this transformation (Oswald
et al. 2018). HART uses a random forest algorithm to catego-
rize offenders into risk groups, providing police officers with de-
tailed metrics on their screens, like the risk of reoffending and
harm scores. Many officers, however, resist using algorithmic
tools like HART, often questioning their accuracy and appro-
priateness, and instead tend to prioritize their own professional
judgment (Ratcliffe et al. 2020; Sandhu and Fussey 2021).
Buffat  (2015) illustrates the enablement and curtailment hy-
potheses as balanced propositions of how digital technologies
reshape the decision-­making fundamentals of public officials.
Studies have shown that public officials may experience a
higher loss of discretion in rigid hierarchical cultures (Meijer
et  al.  2021), when dealing with large amounts of formalized
transactions (Bovens and Zouridis 2002; Wang et al. 2024) or
when performing tasks of low complexity and low uncertainty
(Bullock 2019). They tend to retain more discretion when per-
forming highly complex tasks with some degree of uncertainty
(Bullock 2019; Nagtegaal 2021; Wang et al. 2024). Building on
this work, our approach focuses on understanding public offi-
cials' perceptions when exercising discretion via algorithms. We

2.1   |   Perceived Discretion
Every profession demands personal judgment due to its spe-
cialized nature, requiring training and experience (Anteby
et al. 2016; Freidson 2001). Street-­level bureaucrats and man-
agers like teachers, police officers, caseworkers, doctors, and
nurses may be able to exercise substantial degrees of autonomy
to determine the nature, amount, and quality of benefits and
sanctions provided to the public (Lipsky 2010). Discretion and
autonomy play a key role in addressing uncertainties and ambi-
guities around organizational goals, individual circumstances,
and work pressures (Hupe 2019).
Discretion has traditionally been a core component of the police
profession, where its exercise can have severe social and politi-
cal implications (Goldstein 1963). Contemporary policing is an
increasingly specialized field that involves proactive interven-
tions, monitoring potential offenders, and mediating conflicts
to preserve order (Afzal and Panagiotopoulos 2024; Brayne and
Christin 2021; Maile et al. 2023). Police officers are required to
critically exercise discretion to effectively deal with challenging
situations. For example, police officers may avoid making arrests
in trivial cases or discard traffic violations to redirect limited re-
sources. Gray areas and conflicting demands surrounding such
activities necessitate the exercise of experience-­guided discre-
tion (Brayne and Christin 2021; Goldstein 1963). Accordingly,
the public anticipates police officers to act as autonomous agents
of the state, applying discretion in line with ethical practice
and citizen welfare considerations (Eterno et  al.  2017; Maile
et al. 2023).
Discretion in bureaucratic practice serves as an umbrella con-
cept that includes three distinct dimensions: (1) discretion-­
as-­granted or the freedom formally granted to street-­level
bureaucrats, (2) discretion-­as-­used that refers to how they ac-
tually use the freedom, and (3) discretion-­as-­perceived that cap-
tures the degree to which street-­level bureaucrats perceive they
possess the ability to exercise discretion (Hupe 2019; Thomann
et  al.  2018). Recent research emphasizes the importance of
discretion-­as-­perceived because street-­level bureaucrats must
feel that they have discretion before they can effectively apply
it (e.g., de Boer and Raaphorst 2023; Deng and Sun 2024). Even
when significant formal discretion is granted, they may still per-
ceive that their flexibility is limited due to constraints in their
immediate work environment, including digital technologies.
Discretion-­as-­perceived, in essence, serves as a precondition
for discretion-­as-­used and is key to translating discretion-­as-­
granted into practice. We, therefore, focus on discretion-­as-­
perceived or the perceived discretion of public officials in the
context of algorithmic technologies.
Algorithmic decision-­making leads to higher standardization of
work processes and outputs; therefore, it enables central authori-
ties to control the discretion of operational managers (Bovens and
Zouridis 2002; Roehl and Hansen 2024). In policing, algorithms
can potentially replace some of this latitude and force police of-
ficers to deal with matters differently than their usual working
practices (Young et al. 2019). However, police professionals at-
tach high valence to exercising personal judgment based on their
expert knowledge, evaluation of laws, and contextual sensitivi-
ties (Maile et al. 2023). The intricate nature of police judgments
conflicts with universal rules when officers exercise discretion
(Brayne and Christin 2021; Maile et al. 2023). Imposing gener-
alized rules via algorithmic technologies can hinder intelligent
reasoning, risk deskilling, and enable excessive centralized mon-
itoring of street-­level work (Brayne and Christin 2021; Roehl and
Hansen 2024).
Therefore, in line with Buffat (2015)'s curtailment view, algo-
rithmic technologies are likely to limit police professionals'
perceived discretion by shifting decision-­making to actors
like software developers, policymakers, or IT experts and—
as a result—foster skepticism in work practices (e.g., de Boer
and Raaphorst  2023; Deng and Sun  2024). Similarly, Adams
et al. (2021) found a negative association between law enforce-
ment officials' use of body-­worn cameras and their activation
practices. A possible explanation was directly attributed to
the suppressive effects of body-­worn cameras on the exercise
of discretion. Studies like Selten et al. (2023) and Alon-­Barkat
and Busuioc (2023) have further indicated that public officials

2.2   |   Resistance and Status Quo Bias
Our second theoretical explanation points to the cognitive
biases that public officials experience when evaluating sit-
uations that deviate from established behaviors (Battaglio
et  al.  2019; Bellé et  al.  2018; Moynihan and Lavertu  2012).
Scholars have argued that individuals are endowed with
bounded rationality and frequently resort to heuristics when
making decisions, particularly in  situations characterized
by ambiguity and uncertainty (Simon 2013). These cognitive
biases introduce systematic errors, increasing individuals'
probability of making suboptimal decisions (Bellé et al. 2018).
Consequently, individuals being generally risk averse may
tend to avoid making decisions and prefer not to deviate from
their default option (Bellé et al. 2018; Simon 2013). Samuelson
and Zeckhauser (1988) have described this tendency as a sta-
tus quo bias. The status quo bias perspective posits that every
decision an individual makes has a status quo option that acts
as an anchor for other alternatives. Typically, individuals are
predisposed to maintaining the status quo; that is, they tend to
do nothing or maintain their current or previous situation “out
of convenience, habit or inertia, policy or custom, fear, innate
conservatism or through simple rationalization” (Samuelson
and Zeckhauser 1988, 10).
Technology adoption studies often presume a comprehensive
understanding of a new technology's proposition; however,
this is often not the case when complex technologies like
algorithms do not facilitate a balanced assessment (Meijer
et al. 2021; Young et al. 2019). To this end, the status quo bias
perspective has been deployed to account for the differences
between rational and actual user behavior (e.g., Bhattacherjee
and Hikmet  2007; Kim and Kankanhalli  2009; Polites and
Karahanna  2012). Central to its behavioral explanation is
the concept of resistance to change that accounts for the neg-
ative expectations associated with new systems like loss of
power and deterioration in working practices (Lapointe and
Rivard  2005). As Bhattacherjee and Hikmet  (2007) noted,
resistance is a cognitive force that focuses on the changes
from the status quo introduced by new technologies in a pro-
fessional context. Therefore, explaining resistance is key to
understanding the barriers that inhibit more successful tran-
sitions to new IT-­enabled working practices.
Considering changes to discretion associated with algorithms,
we argue that public officials will first actively engage with the
proposition of algorithmic applications. The outcome of this cog-
nitive assessment determines a range of behavioral responses to
how algorithms are manifested as a threat, opportunity, stress-
ful feeling, or other reaction against changes to professional
practices that represent the status quo (Knox and Arshed 2024;
Lapointe and Rivard 2005; Marienfeldt 2024). As the impacts of
algorithms become more embedded in working practices, pub-
lic officials will have to realistically decide whether to embrace
their outputs or rely on more familiar processes. In the social
and technical environment of police organizations, changes
shape officers' willingness to delegate decisions and rely on al-
gorithms for critical tasks like tracing and arresting criminals
amidst competing work pressures (Marienfeldt  2024). These
changes are assessed against maintaining the prevalent prac-
tices of continuing to rely on professional judgment. Police offi-
cers exhibiting higher reluctance to embrace changes associated
with algorithms are more likely to perceive the delegation of
discretion to algorithms as counterproductive. Accordingly, we
hypothesize:
Hypothesis 2.  (H2): Police officers' resistance to algorithms
is negatively associated with their perceptions of algorithmic
discretion.
Identifying the potentially significant influence of resistance to
change in relation to the status quo does not by itself explain its
origins. In other words, establishing the presence of resistance
alone does not demonstrate why individuals prefer to continue
with an incumbent course of action. The status quo bias the-
ory brings together a rich set of cognitive beliefs that act as an-
tecedents of resistance under different circumstances (Kim and
Kankanhalli  2009; Oschinsky et  al.  2021). While current re-
search has identified the importance of confirmation and auto-
mation biases in similar settings (Alon-­Barkat and Busuioc 2023;
Selten et al. 2023), the manifestation of a set of cognitive biases
that influence the status quo offers the opportunity to assess the
relative balance of interpretations to public officials' resistance
to change. These cognitive biases fall into the three categories of
rational decision-­making, cognitive misperceptions, and psycho-
logical commitment (Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988).
2.2.1   |   Rational Decision-­Making
Human decision-­making involves evaluating the costs and
benefits of switching from one situation to another (Samuelson
and Zeckhauser  1988). An individual might recognize that
another system or working mode may increase the efficiency
of performing a given task. However, the perceived costs of
learning the new system or working mode may not be greater
than the potential gain. Such costs are called transition costs
(Oschinsky et  al.  2021; Polites and Karahanna  2012). Users
are often uncertain about the performance of new systems
because of limited knowledge and lack of practical experi-
ence in using that system (Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988).
They avoid risks and tend to stick to the existing systems or
make similar decisions based on past practices (Kim and
Kankanhalli 2009). This source of bias is called performance
uncertainty.
Police officers develop their practices over years of accumu-
lated experience when, for example, they use their knowledge
to allocate resources for crime prevention (Ratcliffe et al. 2020).
When asked to use algorithms in critical tasks like identifying
crime hotspots, they face the dilemma of reevaluating how re-
sources are traditionally allocated to such tasks. This often
incurs unforeseen expenditures to allow hiring civilian ana-
lysts, posting sworn officers as analysts, training police officers
in data analytics, and displacement of other resources (Afzal

Hypothesis 3.  (H3): Police officers' perceived transition costs

Reconciling the outputs of algorithms with the professional
judgment of police officers entails high level of uncertainty.
Algorithmic practices do not consider the peculiarities of indi-
vidual circumstances and social complexities. They may lead
to inaccurate results and poor decisions because of inherent
data biases or incompleteness, as evident from rising arrest
rates in petty offenses (Brayne and Christin  2021; Roehl and
Hansen  2024). Even when the outputs of algorithms are suf-
ficiently legitimate in the eyes of their users, they may suffer
from ambiguities in interpretability (Busuioc  2021; Oswald
et al. 2018). Police officers may, therefore, feel uncertain about
the performance of algorithms and tend to challenge their use
(Sandhu and Fussey 2021). Accordingly, we hypothesize:
Hypothesis 4.  (H4): Police officers' perceived performance
uncertainty is positively associated with resistance to algorithms.
2.2.2   |   Cognitive Misperception
Cognitive misperception defines perceived loss or loss aversion
as an individual's tendency to weigh losses heavier than gains
in decision-­making (Kim and Kankanhalli  2009; Oschinsky
et al. 2021). Loss aversion among public officials is generally as-
sociated with outcome uncertainty, threats to autonomy, and a
higher penchant for avoiding losses than acquiring equivalent
gains (Nicholson-­Crotty et  al.  2017). The novelty of authority
delegation to algorithmic technologies is inherently risky as it
introduces uncertainty of outcomes. Police  officers may tend
to weigh losses heavier than gains when these transitions take
place. Because of loss aversion, they may feel safer and more
confident in making decisions based on their past experiences.
We, therefore, hypothesize:
Hypothesis 5.  (H5): Police officers' perceived loss aversion is
positively associated with resistance to algorithms.
2.2.3   |   Psychological Commitment
Psychological commitment can be a consequence of misper-
ceived sunk costs (Polites and Karahanna 2012; Samuelson and
Zeckhauser 1988). Sunk costs refer to an individual's reluctance
to cut losses and justify commitment to a course of action. The
higher the effort and time commitment in learning an existing
system or developing processes and skills, the higher the like-
lihood of sustained use and continuity (Oschinsky et al. 2021;
Polites and Karahanna 2012). Policing is a highly professional
occupation with established norms (Goldstein  1963). Police
officers are trained regularly to enhance their performance,
inculcate compliance, and harness good practice (Ratcliffe
et al. 2020). Considerable effort is invested into learning how
to be an officer, as well as the skills, practices, and routines of
policing. The decisions police officers make require unique ex-
pertise and cannot be made by untrained or less experienced
public servants. Thus, police officers are likely to highly bal-
ance the sunk costs of their existing routines and practices and
resist switching to algorithmic technologies. We subsequently
hypothesize:
Hypothesis 6.  (H6): Police officers' perceived sunk costs are
positively associated with resistance to algorithms.
3   |   Methods
Survey methodology incorporating multiple constructs was
adopted to operationalize the variables and test the six hy-
potheses. This choice follows studies like Moynihan and
Lavertu  (2012), Liu et  al.  (2017) and other scholarly works
cited in Battaglio et al. (2019) where surveys were deployed to
examine perceptions that can reasonably be associated with
cognitive biases. While surveys, compared to experimental
methods, lack the capacity for observation of actual behav-
ior, they allow the examination of a wider range of perspec-
tives based on real-­world experiences. This, in turn, enables
the comparison of findings across diverse research contexts,
facilitating an assessment of the relevance of status quo bias
aspects (Godefroid et al. 2023).
We developed a survey instrument comprising closed-­ended
questions measured on a seven-­point Likert scale from strongly
disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). The full measurement indica-
tors are detailed in Supporting Information: Appendix A. The
items for resistance to algorithms, loss aversion, sunk costs,
transition costs, and performance uncertainty were adapted
from studies like Kim and Kankanhalli  (2009), Polites and
Karahanna  (2012) and Bhattacherjee and Hikmet  (2007) and
redesigned in the context of algorithms. The items for perceived
discretion were adapted from Wortley (2003). The items measur-
ing algorithmic discretion were operationalized from the most
relevant conceptualization of Young et al. (2019) that attempts to
capture both the perceived benefits, such as enhanced decision
quality, error reduction, and consistency, as well as potential
drawbacks, such as constraints on professional discretion and
decision-­making freedom (reverse-­coded items). This balanced
design ensures that the construct of algorithmic discretion is as-
sessed from both the empowering and limiting dimensions of
algorithms. The instrument was pretested with forty (40) police
officers over two rounds of iteration, and the questions were re-
vised according to their feedback.
The population for this study includes police officers of all
ranks working in different police organizations across the
United Kingdom. To ensure the relevance and consistency of
their responses to the research aims, the survey's first ques-
tion required participants to disclose their experiences with
data-­driven technologies and algorithms as part of their
decision-­making routines. The options included all different
tools known to have been deployed by police organizations
in the UK for analyzing crime patterns, predictive policing
(prediction or forecasting of violent crimes, individual harm
or risk assessment, offender assessment, etc.), facial recogni-
tion and surveillance, sensor alerting (ANPRs, shot spotters,

on the platform in two stages. The first stage involved seek-
ing informed consent from the relevant police officers while
explaining voluntary participation and response anonymiza-
tion. In the second stage, the survey links were emailed to 547
willing participants to record responses. The survey remained
open for 4 months, between June and September 2021. Survey
respondents were not offered a direct incentive, but we do-
nated £2 to the police welfare organization Police Care UK for
every completed survey.
Of the 333 respondents that opened the survey, 260 fully com-
pleted it (response rate 47.5%), while 73 provided responses for
less than 10% of the survey and were excluded. The participant
demographics are summarized in Table  2. Given the difficulty
of administering surveys to police officers, the dataset provides
a valuable source of information despite its relatively moderate
size. The sample included 66.6% male and 30.8% female respon-
dents representing police officers of all ranks, from constables
to chief officers, with an average work experience of 19.3 years.
These figures are representative of gender composition (70% male
and 30% female) and the most common length of service (be-
tween 15 and 20 years) in the UK police force (Home Office 2020).
Around 57% of respondents indicated that they had completed a
university degree. Other control variables included rank and size
of the organization. A priori power analysis using the Kock and
Hadaya  (2018)'s conservative inverse-­square root method—as
recommended by Hair et al. (2022)—shows that the sample size
is sufficient to detect correlations of sizes ≥ 0.16, (where small
≥ 0.1, medium ≥ 0.3, and large ≥ 0.5), with 80% power at the 0.05
significance level. The more commonly used G*power shows that
our sample size is sufficient to detect a correlation with an effect
size ≥ 0.06, (where small ≥ 0.02, medium ≥ 0.15, and large ≥ 0.35),
with a power of 80% at 0.05 significance level.
4   |   Results
Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-­SEM)
was performed using SmartPLS 4.0 to evaluate both the mea-
surement and structural models. This method was chosen for its
ability to handle complex models, its robustness to distributional
assumptions, and its suitability for smaller sample sizes (Hair
et al. 2022).
4.1   |   Measurement Model Evaluation
Indicator reliability, convergent validity, composite reliability,
and discriminant validity were evaluated for the measure-
ment model following Hair et  al.  (2022). The indicator reli-
ability is assessed using factor loadings and Cronbach's alpha.
Items with loadings greater than 0.6 on their respective con-
structs were retained (Supporting Information: Appendix B).
The Cronbach's alpha for all constructs ranged from 0.71 to
0.92, above the minimum threshold of 0.7. Convergent validity
is examined using average variance extracted (AVE), which is
greater than the minimum threshold of 0.5 for all constructs.

5   |   Discussion and Implications
The study contributes to public administration research that
demonstrates the influence of cognitive biases in the increas-
ing use of algorithms in public officials' decision-­making pro-
cesses (e.g., Alon-­Barkat and Busuioc 2023; Selten et al. 2023).
We operationalized and examined algorithmic discretion as
the perceptions of public officials when using algorithms in
their routines, practices, and decisions to exercise discretion.
The framework that empirically guided the research inte-
grates two perspectives—bureaucratic discretion and status
quo bias theory—to examine algorithmic discretion in the
context of policing.
Building on scholars like Alon-­Barkat and Busuioc (2023) and
Selten et al. (2023), the study offers new insights into how dig-
ital technologies affect the interpretation of public officials'
decision-­making authority. The results show that police officers
in the UK appear more moderately concerned about the direct
negative impacts of algorithms on their perceived discretion.
More than fear of losing their discretion in the transition to algo-
rithmic decision-­making, officers exhibit specific beliefs about
the adverse consequences associated with algorithms in relation
to their properties and outcomes. In turn, resistance to changes
introduced by algorithms is primarily explained by the aspects
of status quo bias associated with performance uncertainty, loss
aversion, and transition costs. Police officers are cautious about
the unknown proposition of algorithmic technologies, their
effectiveness compared to existing practices, and implementa-
tion costs. The extent of performance uncertainty particularly
underlines officers' concerns about the risks of relying on algo-
rithms (Busuioc 2021; Sandhu and Fussey 2021). This may be at-
tributed to limited exposure to consistent algorithmic outcomes,
overexposure to negative consequences of algorithms in other
contexts, or the necessity to mitigate adverse outcomes (Alon-­
Barkat and Busuioc 2023; Selten et al. 2023). Potentially, these
concerns are amplified by the sensitive nature of decisions that
police officers make compared to other public professionals.
Sunk costs were not identified as a source of resistance, pointing
to the fact that time and effort invested in learning and developing
policing skills, routines, heuristics, and processes do not preclude
incorporating algorithms per se. Arguably, many police officers
would consider this transition a natural evolution within their pro-
fessional practice. The costs of this transition, however, indicate
another source of tension when the use of limited resources, hiring
of expertise, and level of organizational change associated with al-
gorithms go beyond police officers' tolerance.
Public administration research on compliance with perfor-
mance management systems shows that street-­level bureaucrats
often resist such systems to safeguard their professional judg-
ment (Gassner et al. 2022; Knox and Arshed 2024). Our findings
expand this notion by revealing that cognitive biases—such as
loss aversion, transition costs, and performance uncertainty—
have a substantial influence on resistance to system compliance
in addition to concerns over professional judgment. Similar to
what the introduction of statistical tools in sports has taught
public administration, the availability of useful performance in-
formation alone does not guarantee its use but can be powerful
when combined with leadership, communication, and cogni-
tive beliefs (Moynihan 2006). We recommend that research on
performance management systems focuses more closely on the
influence of cognitive biases to better understand compliance
dynamics within their context of use.
The behavioral explanation of algorithmic discretion adds to
previous research that identified the impact of automation
on discretion (e.g., de Boer and Raaphorst  2023; Deng and
Sun 2024) and its influence on organizational processes (e.g.,
Bullock 2019; Meijer et al. 2021; Nagtegaal 2021). Respondents
in our study came from diverse points of view regarding per-
formance and transition costs. Their concerns potentially
reflect uncertain expectations on the accelerating level of tran-
sition to data-­driven, algorithmic, and artificial intelligence

This is where we believe that translating these findings to prac-

managers to resourcefully embed algorithmic discretion in pro-

that data and algorithms will allow public officials to retain dis-
cretion is not critical on its own. It is more important to embrace
algorithmic discretion via training for de-­biasing and informed
understanding, confidence, and competencies in responsible
data practice. Mitigating cognitive biases is key to transition-
ing from the conventional view of discretion that police officers
are trained to exercise based on heuristics developed through
peer knowledge and practice (Goldstein  1963; Lipsky  2010).
Training officers to augment and blend experiential knowledge
with data and algorithms can help create awareness about the
implicit biases and assumptions behind the heuristics triggering
snap judgments (Sellier et al. 2019). Evidence suggests that this
is possible when, for example, Meijer et al. (2021) identify the
manifestation of an ‘algorithmic colleague’ in implementations
of predictive policing where officers could visibly combine their
knowledge with system outputs.
More advanced competencies can ensure that officers develop
skills in practice, like algorithmic impact assessment, that embed
explainability, accuracy, auditability, and informed interpreta-
tion of algorithmic outputs (Busuioc 2021; Oswald et al. 2018).
Once these baseline skills have been established, novel design
configurations can empower public officials to enact profes-
sional responsibility with algorithmic practices. In particular, the
human-­in-­the-­loop configuration assigns active roles to human
oversight when monitoring algorithmic system performance
(Vogl et al. 2020). Public officials can use their domain expertise
to create positive feedback loops that detect errors in algorithmic
performance and improve the interpretation of outputs. These di-
rections have, for example, informed how data professionals in
the police interact with algorithmic systems (Fest et al. 2023).
6   |   Conclusion
Considering possible limitations of the study, the impact of algo-
rithms on discretion may indeed vary across other professions
in the public sector, with differences in task complexity and un-
certainty characteristics (Bullock 2019; Wang et al. 2024). The
study did not consider the nature of specific tasks due to aiming
to capture a range of respondents with diverse experiences. The
priority was to establish validity within the population of police
officers across the UK, which is a difficult one to survey (Adams
et al. 2021; Nix et al. 2019). Future research may examine the
moderating effects of different task types depending on their
complexity, uncertainty, and degree of automation. It might be
possible that various tasks stimulate distinctive types of status
quo bias that can be mitigated in a more personalized way. It is
further possible that the direct measurement of cognitive biases
presents limitations that alternative designs using experimental
methods could have captured better.
In the UK, there is more emphasis on the autonomy of street-­level
police officers due to its localism and community-­led traditions
(Maile et al. 2023). Despite this orientation, we have to consider
that the weak relationship between perceived discretion and al-
gorithmic discretion may be the outcome of sample limitations.
Disproportionate regional representation on the Knowledge
Hub platform may restrict generalization to the entire UK police
force. Future studies with larger, more robust national samples
or in other administrative contexts may produce different re-
sults because of differences in mechanisms like control, auton-
omy, and decision-­making (Hupe 2019; Meijer et al. 2021). For
example, findings may differ in institutional contexts like the
US, where intelligence-­led policing tends to dominate within a
more complex landscape of diverse traditions.
Future research should explore the boundary conditions influ-
encing resistance to algorithms, particularly the impact of orga-
nizational incentives in reinforcing existing biases. For example,
between 2003 and 2012, the New York Police Department's re-
liance on CompStat-­driven stop-­and-­frisk operations linked po-
licing efficacy to stop volume, limiting officer discretion (Eterno
et  al.  2017). The resulting surge in stop volume searches and
following racial profiling complaints suggested minimal offi-
cer resistance. It remains unclear whether perceived discre-
tion or algorithmic reinforcement of pre-­existing biases drove
these outcomes. Different cases could be investigated to clarify
when status-­quo bias fosters resistance and when it is bypassed
through alternative mechanisms.
In a final point of consideration, algorithmic discretion as a
concept and construct requires additional validation. Public of-
ficials exercising discretion in welfare, education, nursing, and
social work would approach algorithmic discretion differently
from law enforcement because of variations in degrees of au-
tonomy, observability of individual outputs, and public expecta-
tions (Hupe 2019; Meijer et al. 2021). When research investigates
discretion in these contexts, other aspects of status quo bias like
the anchoring effect (influence of reference points and early in-
formation on subsequent decisions) might meaningfully inform
the exercise of discretion. For example, public officials' direct ex-
posure to AI applications is currently more limited at the street
level and analysts remain the dominant users (Meijer et al. 2021;
Selten et al. 2023). Continuously evolving personal experiences
and societal narratives might produce significant anchoring ef-
fects as AI becomes more relevant in all aspects of public profes-
sionals' practice.

ABSTRACT
Organizational legitimacy is essential for effective crisis governance. This study analyzes the rapid erosion of legitimacy faced
by the German State Office for Health and Social Affairs (LAGeSo) during the 2015 refugee crisis, triggering cascading failures
in public service delivery. Analyzing news articles and press statements, the study traces stakeholders' interactions with the
LAGeSo, reconstructing key events and reactions to identify critical public governance failures. The findings show that a lack of
network governance and anticipatory leadership contributed to a self-­reinforcing process of legitimacy erosion, which culminated
in organizational collapse. The case demonstrates that public sector crisis management requires more than technical responses;
it demands strategic awareness of legitimacy dynamics and strong leadership in network governance. Adopting a legitimacy-­as-­
process perspective, the study provides novel conceptual and practical insights for improving public crisis governance.
1   |   Introduction
Public administrations worldwide face an increasing number of
transboundary crises and the task of solving complex “wicked
problems” (Reinecke and Ansari 2016; Boin 2009), as illustrated
by unprecedented events such as the COVID-­19 pandemic
(Schomaker and Bauer 2020; Christensen and Laegreid 2020),
challenges regarding refugee migration and their societal inte-
gration (Radtke and Fleischer 2019; Phillips and Hardy 1997;
Kreichauf 2018), and the ongoing civil service transformation
(Seidemann and Weißmüller  2024; Oberfield  2016; Ritz and
Weißmüller  2025). Growing complexity, ambiguity, and un-
certainty in institutional environments present significant
challenges for public service delivery (Lenz 2024; Smith 2014;
Carpenter and Krause 2011).
Failures to adequately address these challenges undermine the
legitimacy of public agencies and frequently lead to media and
political backlash (Lotta et al. 2024; Bauer and Becker 2020;
Hendriks et al. 2024). Consequently, effective crisis manage-
ment and governance capacities are increasingly critical for
public organizations. Research on crisis management shows
that besides effective modes of (cross-­sectoral) collaboration
and network governance (Parker et al. 2020; Moynihan 2009),
managing crises involves far more than technical contain-
ment and logistical responses: Crises expose organizational
conflicts, detrimental power dynamics in networked public
service delivery, issues of trust, and challenges to institu-
tional legitimacy (Christensen et  al.  2016; Geisemann and
Geiger  2024). The recurring states of (poly)crisis strain the
legitimacy of public organizations (Svenbro and Wester 2022;
Samkin et  al.  2010; Boin  2009) and challenge the delivery
of crucial emergency service responses (Bundy et  al.  2017).
This study analyzes how public governance failure during
crises is linked with the erosion of institutional legitimacy,
using the case of a public agency that became emblematic of

2   |   Crisis Management and Legitimacy Erosion
2.1   |   Crisis Management Through Network
Governance
The importance of effective crisis management for public sec-
tor organizations has become obvious as major crises, such
as natural disasters, public health emergencies, or domes-
tic terrorism, test the capabilities of public administration
(Boin and Lodge 2016; Thornton et al. 2023; Christensen and
Laegreid  2020; Hunter et  al.  2025). Governments and public
agencies must adapt quickly to dynamically unfolding situations,
whilst facing complex governance dilemmas and challenges in
coordinating diverse stakeholders in crisis response (Lenz 2024).
These developments highlight the growing significance of crisis
management and the growing research interest (Varela Castro
et al. 2023; Eckhard et al. 2021; Di Mascio et al. 2020; Boin 2016;
Rosenthal and Kouzmin 1997; Lenz 2024).
Crisis management refers to the practices that organizations
adopt to address a crisis at every stage—before, during, and
after—by identifying, assessing, understanding, and responding
to it to mitigate its impact and support recovery (Christensen
et al. 2016). A fundamental principle of good crisis management
is to prepare for both foreseeable risks and unexpected contin-
gencies while allocating necessary resources to reduce the neg-
ative impact of catastrophic events on individuals and society
(Parker et al. 2020). While crisis management is a key responsi-
bility of public sector organizations, the uncertainties, risks, and
errors of complex crises require collaboration beyond individual
entities and hence call for coordinated activity across sectoral
boundaries; it demands a new public governance approach, that
is, concerted coordination across organizations, jurisdictions,
and regulatory boundaries to solve wicked problems (Fischer
and Weißmüller  2024b, 2024a; Nohrstedt et  al.  2018; Parker
et al. 2020; Ansell et al. 2010).
While research broadly agrees that “crisis response inevitably
depends on collaborative processes to succeed” (Moynihan 2009,
897), collaboration can introduce challenges, such as tensions
and competitive dynamics between organizations, referred
to as the “battle of the Samaritans” (Rosenthal et al. 1991), or
principal-­agent conflicts and sector biases in public-­private part-
nerships (Weißmüller and Vogel 2021; Weißmüller et al. 2023).
Strategies to address these risks range from hierarchical struc-
tures to more collaborative approaches (Moynihan  2009).
Strategic network governance is one specific model in this spec-
trum (Koliba et al. 2019; Wang and Ran 2021).
While sometimes used interchangeably, network gover-
nance and collaborative governance are distinct theoretical
constructs. Collaborative governance refers to processes in
which public agencies engage with non-­state actors in struc-
tured, consensus-­oriented communication and collaborative
resource exchange to make collective decisions together and
reach shared goals (Ansell and Gash 2008). In contrast, net-
work governance emphasizes the structural and relational
dimensions of inter-­organizational networks in which co-
ordination emerges not necessarily through consensus but
rather through strategic interactions, resource dependencies,
and negotiated accountability (Provan and Kenis 2008; Klijn

crises requires understanding partners' motives, goal conflicts,
public agencies' competencies, social dynamics, and the general
public's perception of crisis governance outcomes (Christensen
et  al.  2016). Furthermore, crisis management research high-

lic agencies' crisis response. Legitimacy is often viewed as an
outcome rather than a central factor influencing the develop-
ment of a crisis (Bundy et al. 2017). Public management scholars
thus call for further exploration of how the legitimacy of public
agencies impacts their capacity for effective crisis management
(Christensen et al. 2016; Demiroz and Unlu 2018; Lenz 2024).
2.2   |   The Role of Legitimacy for Public
Organizations
Following institutional theory, the ability to create and pre-
serve legitimacy constitutes a necessary condition for organi-
zational survival (Meyer and Rowan  1977). Legitimacy is “a
generalized perception or assumption that the actions of an
entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially
constructed system of norms, values, beliefs, and definitions”
(Suchman  1995, 574). This concept is especially relevant for
public organizations that provide administrative services and
coordination as their outcomes and performance—although
hard to quantify—are often under intense sociopolitical and
media scrutiny (Wæraas 2020). According to Suchman (1995),
legitimacy can be understood through three dimensions: prag-
matic, moral, and cognitive. Pragmatic legitimacy is considered
the most tangible dimension (Alexiou and Wiggins 2018), based
on the judgment that an organization is legitimate if stake-
holders receive something of value from it (Dart 2004). Since
public organizations are meant to create public value through
diverse administrative functions and service delivery for citi-
zens, their pragmatic legitimacy is based on the perceived ef-
ficiency, frugal resource use, and the stability and reliability of
services (Frumkin and Galaskiewicz  2004). Moral legitimacy
“reflects a positive normative evaluation of the organization
and its activities” (Suchman  1995, 579). Moral legitimacy is
vital for public organizations, as it relies on the public's belief
that they are motivated by benevolence and committed to so-
cietal goals. Since their survival depends on being perceived
as morally legitimate, lacking moral legitimacy would severely
compromise their ability to function and maintain public sup-
port (Wæraas 2020; Rimkutė and Mazepus 2025). Lastly, as a
passive form of evaluation, cognitive legitimacy is described as
an elusive and intangible dimension of legitimacy often oper-
ationalized by an “absence of questions about or challenges to
an entity” (Tost 2011, 692), that is, the degree to which an or-
ganization's stakeholders perceive it as “necessary or taken-­for-­
granted” (Alexiou and Wiggins 2018, 473).
While earlier legitimacy research argued that legitimacy was a
stable concept—“a property, resource or a capacity” resulting
from an entity's actions (Suddaby et al. 2017, 451)—recent ap-
proaches highlight its inherently dynamic nature (Haack and
Rasche  2021; Ralph  2021). This perspective of legitimacy-­as-­
process (Suddaby et al. 2017) suggests that legitimacy is continu-
ously challenged, maintained, and (re-­)constructed (Long 2016).
Research on organizations in both the public and private sectors
identifies numerous practices for (re)constructing legitimacy,
such as providing verbal accounts (Elsbach  1994; Patriotta
et al. 2011; Verhoeven et al. 2012; Svenbro and Wester 2022),
employing strategic silence (Le et al. 2018), issuing disclosures
(Bachmann and Ingenhoff  2016; Blanc et  al.  2017; Linsley
and Kajuter  2008), implementing restructuring measures
(Wang 2010; Radtke and Fleischer 2019), engaging in symbolic
distancing (Pfeffer 1981), and fostering cooperation (Svensson
et al. 2019; Cristofoli et al. 2021; Galwa and Vogel 2021). The
legitimacy literature highlights various occasions in which le-
gitimacy (re)construction becomes critical, for example, cases
concerning the loss of public trust (Samkin et  al.  2010), cor-
ruption scandals (Blanc et al. 2017; Villoria et al. 2012; Eberl
et al. 2015), stigmatization (Tracey and Phillips 2016), or critical
revelations (Elsbach  1994). These studies typically start from
a state of legitimacy loss rather than examining the process of
legitimacy erosion. While legitimacy repair processes are often
complex and problematic (Suddaby et al. 2017; de Vaujany 2019;
Haack and Rasche 2021), the dynamics of legitimacy loss itself
remain underexplored. Building on the public management dis-
course that calls for further research on legitimacy and its loss
during crises (Christensen et al. 2016; Demiroz and Unlu 2018),
this study aims to explore the erosion of legitimacy in a public
sector organization during a crisis. Svenbro and Wester (2022,
55) stress that agencies struggle to secure public compliance and
acceptance without legitimacy, which is crucial for effective cri-
sis management, underscoring the timely need for this research.
3   |   Methods
3.1   |   Case Selection: LAGeSo Berlin During
the German “Refugee Crisis”
This study's empirical evidence is based on an in-­depth case
analysis (Yin  2018) of the State Office for Health and Social
Affairs (LAGeSo), a key public agency in Berlin, Germany. In
2015, LAGeSo, with its approximately 1000 employees, pro-
vided social and health services to over 600,000 Berlin residents

affairs, the latter overseeing refugee-­related services such as
registration, medical care, accommodation, and administra-
tive tasks. In early 2016, the State Office for Refugee Affairs
(LAF) was established to take over these responsibilities after
LAGeSo's operational collapse in the winter of 2015/2016.
During this “refugee crisis,”1 the acronym “LAGeSo” became
a nationwide metaphor for chaos, mismanagement, and over-
burdened agencies (Reimann  2017), representing one of the
worst crises a German public agency has ever suffered (Eddy
and Johannsen  2015). Headlines such as “Failed City Berlin”
or “Worst Agency in Germany” show the intensity of the public
outcry (Reimann 2015; Prantl 2015).
3.2   |   Context
In the summer and autumn of 2015, Germany faced a historic
surge in refugee arrivals, with about 1.1 million seeking asylum.
Berlin, its capital and largest city, became a key arrival hub that
welcomed more than 80,000 refugees (BLZ 2015). The sharp in-
flux was driven by the Syrian civil war and ongoing conflicts in
Afghanistan and Iraq that forced many people to seek refuge in
Europe. This unprecedented migration wave posed significant
challenges for local governments and social systems through-
out Germany (Kroet 2016; Spindler 2015). While many agencies
across Germany struggled with limited administrative capacity
and resources, LAGeSo's failure was unprecedented. Media re-
ports distributed worldwide showed endless waiting lines, freez-
ing children, and shelter tents on the agency's grounds, which
were compared with “civil war-­like” conditions. By the end of
2015, protests and public outcry against LAGeSo's catastrophic
performance intensified, with warnings that “we cannot rule
out that people will die here” (Dobberke 2015). LAGeSo is an ex-
treme case (Flyvbjerg 2016); however, it reflects the characteris-
tic struggles of an “average” German agency facing resource and
personnel shortages due to years of austerity, underfunding, and
HR policies that failed to attract staff with crisis management
competencies. Despite LAGeSo's dramatic failure, the prevailing
sentiment in Germany's public discourse was clear: any agency
could have become a “LAGeSo” case (Schammann 2020).
3.3   |   Data Collection
To understand LAGeSo's collapse during the “refugee crisis”
through a legitimacy lens, 339 newspaper articles and 72 press
statements issued about the agency's actions were collected
and analyzed qualitatively. Using secondary data was crucial
as legitimacy “is not something that can be claimed by orga-
nizations, but is instead something that is given” by specific
sources (Massey  2001, 156), emphasizing the importance of
capturing public and media perceptions of LAGeSo's actions
(Deephouse and Suchman 2013). The media are a key indica-
tor of legitimacy, and media data are often used to measure it
(e.g., Deephouse  1996; Lamertz and Baum  2009; Pollock and
Rindova 2003; Etter et al. 2016). Additionally, the media “are
routinely targeted by organizations […] seeking to build or repair
legitimacy” (Deephouse and Suchman 2013, 56), making media
data a crucial source for legitimacy research.
Focusing on newspaper articles and press statements provides
a processual perspective, thereby capturing the evolution and
dynamics of the context. While the months before and after the
crisis were analyzed to develop a holistic understanding of the
context, the final set of articles focuses on the peak crisis pe-
riod—from July 2015 to January 2016—when LAGeSo's oper-
ative capacity to provide refugee services effectively collapsed.
This period ended with the establishment of the LAF, an agency
that was founded specifically to replace and take over refugee-­
related tasks from the dysfunctional LAGeSo. To capture a
broad political spectrum and minimize partisan reporting bias,
five significant newspapers with varying political orientations
were selected: Der Tagesspiegel (TSP; n = 111; liberal), Berliner
Zeitung (BLZ; n = 97; center-­left), Berliner Kurier (BK; n = 59;
conservative tabloid), Süddeutsche Zeitung (SZ; n = 34; center-­
left), and Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung (FAZ; n = 38; center-­
conservative). TSP, BLZ, and BK are local newspapers2 that had
a close focus on reporting on LAGeSo, while SZ and FAZ are
respected national outlets with a daily reach exceeding one mil-
lion readers. Additionally, 72 press statements (PS) by LAGeSo
and various municipal and state-­level agencies in Berlin were
analyzed.
The analytical set of articles was retrieved using the follow-
ing criteria: (1) Systematic database searches with keywords
[“LAGeSo” OR “Landesamt für Gesundheit und Soziales”
OR “LAF” OR “Landesamt für Flüchtlingsangelegenheiten”]
(n = 1978). (2) All hits were screened by title/abstract for rele-
vance; duplicates and false positives were excluded. (3) Articles
and press statements that discussed the “refugee crisis” without
referencing LAGeSo's actions were excluded. The final set in-
cludes 339 newspaper articles and 72 press statements. While in-
terview data could have served as an alternative data source, the
complexity of the case and the multitude of (partially strongly
politicized) stakeholders involved made secondary data a more
reliable choice. Moreover, potential interviewees, such as for-
mer LAGeSo employees, faced legal restrictions on giving in-
terviews, and the historical scope of the crisis carried risks of
retrospective sense-­making and biased accounts (Eisenhardt
and Graebner 2007).
3.4   |   Data Analysis
Data were analyzed using an abductive approach (Timmermans
and Tavory 2012; Sætre and Van de Ven 2021), unfolding through
six distinct steps in an iterative process that combined data anal-
ysis with the engagement of relevant theoretical concepts.
First, the authors familiarized themselves with the development
of the crisis and identified the central crisis period as ranging
from July 2015 to January 2016. During this familiarization
process, it also became evident that—although the newspaper
articles naturally varied in the details they provided—the over-
all progression of the crisis was portrayed in a similar manner
across the different outlets.
Second, relevant actor and stakeholder groups were identified:
(1) low-­level (municipal) public agencies, (2) high-­level (state-­
level) public agencies, (3) political agents, that is, state-­level po-
litical leaders and policymakers of Berlin, (4) the general public

(i.e., citizens of Berlin), (5) private sector for-­profit partners of
LAGeSo, (6) non-­profit and charitable or civil society partners of
LAGeSo, and (7) the beneficiaries, that is, refugees.
Third, using temporal bracketing (Langley 1999), critical events
and key conflicts during the crisis were identified to isolate four
distinct phases of the crisis trajectory.
Fourth, LAGeSo's responses to the escalating crisis and the
(re)actions of relevant stakeholders were identified within each
of the four phases. In this step of data analysis, LAGeSo's fail-
ure to govern the emergent network of non-­public partnerships
that provided emergency refugee services proved to be a key fac-
tor in understanding the crisis and the erosion of the agency's
legitimacy.
Building on these initial findings, subsequent data analysis fo-
cused particularly on the progressing legitimacy erosion through
the lens of network governance (Wang and Ran 2021; Koliba
et  al.  2019). A governance network is defined as a “relatively
stable pattern of coordinated action and resource exchanges,
involving policy actors from various sectors—public, private,
and non-­profit—across different geographic levels. These actors
interact through a range of competitive, command-­and-­control,
cooperative, and negotiated arrangements to achieve their objec-
tives, which they cannot accomplish on their own due to limited
resources and interdependencies” (Koliba et al. 2019, 71–72).
Fifth, considering the nodes, ties, and resources within the
network (Wasserman and Faust 1994), the eroding legitimacy
of the LAGeSo was analyzed. Building on the understanding
that legitimacy is an attribution (Massey 2001; Suchman 1995),
passages reflecting externally imposed perceptions of the orga-
nization were systematically identified. Both explicit criticism
and negative attributions were identified, along with descrip-
tions that conveyed (unmet) expectations toward the LAGeSo
as a public agency—for instance, references to characteristics
and values typically associated with legitimate public institu-
tions, such as accountability and procedural fairness (Jørgensen
and Rutgers  2014). These passages were then analyzed using
Suchman's (1995) dimensions of pragmatic and moral legitimacy.
Given the elusive nature of cognitive legitimacy—which is often
assessed retrospectively through its absence (Tyler  2006)—
perceived trustworthiness was included as a proxy indicator
of cognitive legitimacy, following best practices (Tyler  2006;
Bachmann et al. 2015; Schoon 2022). Trustworthiness—opera-
tionalized through the dimensions of benevolence, competence,
and integrity (Mayer et al. 1995)—is widely used as a legitimacy
indicator and enriches the analysis by complementing moral
and pragmatic legitimacy.
For example, reports on failing administrative processes (e.g.,
welfare organizations advising refugees not to attend their ap-
pointments, TSP, 12/14/2015) were interpreted as indicating a
perception of LAGeSo's inability to deliver reliable public ser-
vices. The missing attribution of functioning service provision
was then categorized as a loss of pragmatic legitimacy. Another
example concerns news reports about security guards beating
refugees and the resulting public outcry that was triggered by
accounts of witnesses to this brutality (BLZ, 12/15/2015). These
accounts were interpreted as indicating a serious violation of
moral expectations associated with public service delivery. They
reflect a perceived loss of benevolence and respect for human
dignity—both essential elements of moral legitimacy and in-
stitutional trustworthiness. The passage was therefore catego-
rized as reflecting a decline in moral legitimacy and an erosion
of trustworthiness. The deductive categorization of attributions
(Fife and Gossner 2024) along the dimensions of pragmatic legit-
imacy, moral legitimacy, and trustworthiness enabled a system-
atic tracing of legitimacy erosion. To mitigate potential biases in
the coding and categorization process, the main coding of legit-
imacy erosion was conducted by the first author, while the co-­
authors critically reviewed and challenged the interpretations.
This collaborative approach supported analytical triangulation
and strengthened the credibility of the findings (Flick 2022).
Finally, as a sixth step, the emerging network dynamics and
the effects on LAGeSo's legitimacy and trustworthiness were
synthesized into a process summary of legitimacy erosion
(Figure 1) that presents the progression from regular conditions
to emerging crisis, escalating crisis, and ultimately refugee ser-
vice collapse.

4.1   |   Phase 1: “We Can do This”–Business as Usual
in a Small Network [July 2015–August 2015]3
4.1.1   |   Situational Context
In the summer of 2015, an increasing number of refugees ar-
rived in Germany, drawn by its relatively welcoming asylum
policies and initial stance, making it a more appealing destina-
tion than other European countries. Berlin, the German capi-
tal, became a focal point for many refugees due to its perceived
larger administrative and logistical capacities as well as its mul-
ticultural population and international appeal. In August 2015,
German Chancellor Angela Merkel famously declared, “Wir
schaffen das!” (“We can do this!”) in response to the growing in-
flux of refugees. Her statement reflected widespread optimism,
underscoring Germany's economic strength as a foundation for
providing humanitarian support. All refugees arriving in Berlin
were required to register at LAGeSo to apply for asylum and ac-
cess support services. As refugee numbers surged, the agency's
premises descended into chaos within days, with many refugees
camping on-­site to secure their spot at the front of the waiting
lines. BLZ vividly described the scene of refugees attempting to
access the agency:

4.1.2   |   LAGeSo's Actions and Stakeholders' (Re)actions
Responding to the situation, LAGeSo attempted to bolster its
crisis management capacities with various measures. For in-
stance, the agency “extended its working hours and introduced
a two-­shift system to register more refugees” (TSP, 08/11/2015).
LAGeSo hired new staff, who were often reluctantly sourced

agency's formal governance structures. Initially relieved by the
“invaluable contribution to the care and support of refugees”
(PS, 09/24/2015) of these organizations, LAGeSo accepted and
thereby formalized these relationships, legitimizing them. This
marked a departure from previous practices, as neither legal
procedures regarding public procurement nor formal selection
processes were followed, violating core public values and the
foundational principles of German bureaucracy (Pollitt 2015;
Painter and Peters 2010).
At the same time, the expansion of private for-­profit partner-
ships impacted the emerging refugee aid network, adding
to its complexity. While the emerging accountability gaps
would only become evident in the later stages of the crisis,
there were first warning signs of the agency's failure to ex-
ercise leadership and conduct quality audits. For instance, as
early as September, TSP reported “issues with the transfer of
cost allowances” to hostels sheltering refugees, noting that
“LAGeSo is unable to keep up with processing the invoices”
(TSP, 09/09/2015).
The increasing involvement of non-­profit and private sector
partners not only diminished LAGeSo's ability to govern the ex-
panding and increasingly complex network effectively but also
highlighted its growing dependency on external support to ful-
fill its core mission and responsibilities. For example, a reporter
from BLZ highlighted the agency's dependence on welfare or-
ganizations, stating that “without their support, the agency
will never succeed in managing the refugee numbers” (BLZ,
09/03/2015). Hence, the growing criticism—both from network
partners and the media—regarding the lack of oversight over its
partners and LAGeSo's apparent inability to manage the situ-
ation without voluntary support began to undermine its prag-
matic legitimacy. Public doubts arose about whether LAGeSo
could effectively handle the task and adequately address the
needs of refugees.

evident not only with for-­profit partners but also affected non-­

By the end of the year, the situation at LAGeSo had escalated,

nal perspectives from employees were leaked to the press. The
growing loss of moral legitimacy prompted employees to pub-
licly distance themselves from LAGeSo. Employee revelations
exposed the agency's internal chaos and operational collapse, as
well as the glaring lack of leadership. The SZ reported insights
from interviews with these whistleblowers:
A staff member reports that there is no leadership

Summarizing the four phases of legitimacy erosion through the
evolving network dynamics highlights the interconnectedness
of legitimacy processes and network governance. The initial
erosion of LAGeSo's pragmatic legitimacy—its capacity to effec-
tively fulfill its mission—led to the formation of the non-­profit
sub-­network and influenced the structure of for-­profit network
ties. However, as the network grew in complexity, adverse dy-
namics such as node drifting and the creation of informal
sub-­networks that excluded LAGeSo emerged. These develop-
ments revealed and aggravated the agency's lack of network
leadership, particularly its inability to effectively govern its
partnership network and its inability to monitor due diligence
and ethical conduct. Accountability gaps and node drift further
weakened LAGeSo's pragmatic legitimacy. Integrity violations
and rule-­breaking ultimately undermined its moral legitimacy,
culminating in a public outcry that prompted political leaders to
intervene, leading to LAGeSo's collapse.
5   |   Discussion
The insights from analyzing LAGeSo's loss of legitimacy con-
tribute to at least two scholarly debates. First, the rapid erosion
of the agency's legitimacy highlights the interplay between
trust, moral legitimacy, and pragmatic legitimacy, revealing
the tensions that emerge from these interactions, especially
during crises. Second, the case emphasizes the critical role of
network dynamics and the consequences of ineffective network
governance during times of crisis, providing valuable concep-
tual and practical implications for public leadership and crisis
governance.
5.1   |   The Interplay of Legitimacy Dimensions
and Crisis Management
In the legitimacy discourse, the loss of legitimacy often serves
as a starting point for examining how organizations attempt to
restore their legitimacy (Samkin et al. 2010; Wang 2010; Linsley
and Kajuter 2008). However, the rapid erosion of LAGeSo's le-
gitimacy over just 6 months provides unique insights into the
process of legitimacy loss itself, highlighting the perspective of
legitimacy-­as-­process (Suddaby et al. 2017; Johnson et al. 2006).
Although crisis management literature acknowledges that cri-
ses can impact organizational legitimacy (Long 2016; Hartley
and Jarvis 2020; Massey 2001) and argues that effective crisis
management goes beyond technical readiness and responses
(Christensen et  al.  2016), the nuanced relationship between
legitimacy dynamics and crisis management capacity has not
been sufficiently explored. The study presents unique insights
into these dynamics. The LAGeSo case illustrates how the
unfolding “refugee crisis” impacted the agency's legitimacy
and how the erosion of its organizational legitimacy further
undermined its ability to manage the crisis on a fundamental
level. This is a critical insight for both scholarship and practice.
Early in the crisis, LAGeSo faced stretched resources and an
overwhelming task, which immediately strained the agency's
pragmatic legitimacy (Schoon 2022) and affected citizens' per-
ception of its organizational trustworthiness, particularly re-
garding its task-­related competencies (Mayer et al. 1995). Since
business-­as-­usual public-­private network governance did not
require risk-­savvy and strategic leadership capabilities in the
past, LAGeSo had failed to establish these crucial competen-
cies necessary to navigate crisis response networks (Fischer and
Weißmüller 2024a; Provan and Kenis 2008). As the crisis pro-
gressed, adverse dynamics emerged that accelerated the erosion
of the agency's legitimacy on multiple levels. To maintain its op-
erational service provision pragmatically, the agency abandoned
core bureaucratic principles and resorted to bending and break-
ing the rules. While these measures could have theoretically
contributed to maintaining pragmatic legitimacy if they had
led to success (Suchman  1995)—by, for instance, eliminating
the chaotic circumstances and achieving effective task fulfill-
ment—they ultimately undermined LAGeSo's moral legitimacy.
This is because pragmatic rule-­breaking (Kaufmann  2018)

408

conflicts with fundamental public values such as equity, the rule
of law, and accountability (Jørgensen and Rutgers 2014; Rajala
and Jalonen 2025). The case illustrates that public opinion may
be lenient and forgiving if public agencies deviate from rigid bu-
reaucratic procedures as a last resort emergency crisis response,
but only if they succeed. If they fail, violations of public values
during times of crisis can have detrimental effects on institu-
tional legitimacy, public trust, and crisis management outcomes.
The LAGeSo case also highlights the self-­reinforcing interplay
between the erosion of organizational legitimacy (both moral
and pragmatic) and operational effectiveness. Once service re-
cipients lost trust in LAGeSo's benevolence, competence, and in-
tegrity, their engagement in “workarounds” further diminished
LAGeSo's ability to provide services, as both beneficiaries and
partners became unwilling to cooperate. These observations
support Svenbro and Wester (2022, 55), who argue that “agen-
cies will struggle to gain compliance and acceptance of their
interventions in people's lives” when legitimacy and trust are
eroding—a dynamic particularly detrimental in times of crisis.
These findings underscore the need for anticipatory and strate-
gic crisis management to account for potential legitimacy loss
and anticipate the impact that eroding trust and legitimacy may
have throughout all phases of a crisis (Christensen et al. 2016;
Lenz 2024).
While the LAGeSo case demonstrates a rapid erosion of legit-
imacy, crisis management literature emphasizes the grow-
ing prevalence of polycrisis settings (Boin  2009; Lawrence
et al. 2024) and slow-­onset crises (Geisemann and Geiger 2024;
Boin et  al.  2021). The increasing complexity of crisis settings
presents new challenges for maintaining the legitimacy of pub-
lic organizations, highlighting the tensions between prioritizing
specific dimensions of legitimacy over others. These develop-
ments call for more nuanced crisis management strategies that
extend beyond managing the technical aspects of crises and
address the growing “messiness” of crises as they unfold. This
study's findings emphasize the importance of adopting a strong
process perspective on legitimacy (Cloutier and Langley 2020),
which entails conceptualizing legitimacy not simply as an out-
come but as an ongoing enactment and co-­creative achievement.
This approach is crucial for public agencies to remain opera-
tional during times of crisis.
5.2   |   The Role of Network Dynamics and Network
Governance in Legitimacy Crises
Applying a network governance perspective (Wang and
Ran 2021; Koliba et al. 2019) helps explain LAGeSo's rapid ero-
sion of legitimacy by revealing the underlying dynamics that
fueled this process. A key finding concerns the significant im-
pact of the agency's lack of leadership capacity in network gover-
nance. LAGeSo's failure to develop these capacities throughout
the unfolding crisis allowed for a rapidly growing network that
became increasingly independent of its central actor, resulting
in para-­social ties that enabled non-­public partners to gain le-
gitimacy by proxy while evading accountability and exploiting
the lack of oversight. Eventually, this network drifted apart
(Milward and Provan 2000; Raab and Milward 2003), exploiting
accountability gaps that further exacerbated the crisis. The case
highlights the importance of prioritizing the development of
leadership capabilities in anticipation of a crisis and strategically
directing resources to deploy these capabilities once the crisis
actually unfolds (Berthod et al. 2017; Provan and Kenis 2008).
Without these capabilities and anticipatory crisis management,
LAGeSo's leadership became distracted by increasing public
and political pressure, prioritizing operational process man-
agement over strategically governing its partnership network.
This shift likely hindered potential effective crisis responses
(Moynihan 2009). The “ripple effects” of failed network gover-
nance exacerbated the erosion of both pragmatic and moral le-
gitimacy, leading to a downward spiral that ultimately rendered
LAGeSo's crisis management attempts futile.
In the age of (poly)crisis, this case demonstrates that effective cri-
sis management depends on strong network governance mech-
anisms. Practitioners can learn that managing crises requires
anticipatory crisis governance, strong leadership, and strategic
management skills to effectively govern complex and dynamic
multi-­stakeholder crisis response networks. This includes dedi-
cating resources to due diligence in public procurement—such
as contracting out, establishing enforcing mechanisms for rule
compliance, and following essential bureaucratic procedures
(Kim and Brown 2012)—but also reflection, critical introspec-
tion, and foresight to avoid getting trapped in operational crisis
management, such as restructuring or filling short-­term re-
source gaps. Developing creative and pragmatic crisis response
skills is crucial to preparing public agencies for future crises
(Boin and Lodge 2016; Barbera et al. 2024). However, upholding
public values in conduct is equally essential to avoid jeopardiz-
ing legitimacy and operational effectiveness, speaking to the ne-
cessity of a public value-­based governance approach (De Graaf
and Paanakker 2014).
The case also cautions against viewing governance networks in
civil service provision as a panacea, particularly regarding cri-
sis management (Hermansson 2015; Moynihan 2009; Berthod
et  al.  2017). The literature on crisis management and grand
challenges (Ferraro et al. 2015) generally agrees that effective
crisis management necessitates coordinated efforts across sec-
tors to overcome territorial and authority boundaries (Nohrstedt
et al. 2018; Parker et al. 2020) and that complex societal problems
cannot be addressed through the linear logic of contracting out.
Instead, “wicked” problems (Reinecke and Ansari 2016) neces-
sitate network governance approaches (Rittel and Webber 1973).
The LAGeSo case highlights the often-­neglected challenges of
practical, ethical, and public value-­oriented multi-­sectoral net-
work governance, particularly in high-­stakes, high-­pressure sit-
uations. While many of LAGeSo's network issues stemmed from
weak governance capacities, a lack of resources, and inadequate
strategic leadership, some challenges that exacerbated LAGeSo's
crisis are inherent to multi-­stakeholder, cross-­sectoral networks.
The goals and values of LAGeSo's non-­profit and civil society
partners differed significantly from those of private for-­profit
partners, creating tensions and conflicts that LAGeSo failed to
recognize or address. While such conflicts are well-­documented
in the public management literature (Reynaers  2013; Forrer
et al. 2010; Klijn and Teisman 2003; Weißmüller and Vogel 2021),
the LAGeSo case offers valuable insights into infighting within
the non-­profit network. Albeit generally aligned regarding their
prosocial mission, the case illustrates how the heterogeneity of

LAGeSo's limited capacity to govern the emerging sub-­networks

had become untrustworthy in the eyes of the non-­profit orga-

opinion through its para-­social association with the agency (i.e.,
legitimation-­by-­proxy). Effective multi-­sectoral network gov-

into crisis management strategies to maintain legitimacy and
ensure control and due diligence. Given the development of
polycrises, slow-­onset crises, and increasing conflicts in eval-
uating urgencies and priorities among stakeholders (Lawrence
et  al.  2024; Geisemann and Geiger  2024), these management
skills are essential for effective public sector crisis management
in the 21st century (Ritz and Weißmüller 2025).
5.3   |   Practical Implications and Limitations
The LAGeSo case is one of the most severe instances of public
agency failure in post-­war Germany. It had significant adverse
effects on the reputation of public administration and caused
suffering among refugees, volunteers, and LAGeSo employees.
In addition to highlighting the fragility of institutional legit-
imacy during crises, it offers practical lessons, particularly re-
garding the need for anticipatory crisis management skills and
network governance capabilities.
First, the case demonstrates that crisis management should not
be viewed solely as a technical process (Christensen et al. 2016),
but also in terms of its long-­term impact on organizational legiti-
macy and public trust. The interplay between legitimacy loss and
crisis progression highlights the importance of strategic reflec-
tion and recalibration, even during the peak of the crisis (Geiger
et al. 2021). Allocating space for strategic planning and interven-
tions not covered by the original crisis strategy could have broken
the cycle of legitimacy erosion and helped LAGeSo regain con-
trol over its network by leveraging political support, which was
instead lost. The LAGeSo case illustrates how an agency became
fixated on operational crisis management—such as conducting
internal restructuring processes, increasing staff, and decentral-
ization—and neglected strategic management and leadership
within its network. In this narrow operational focus, the agency
disregarded the deeper issues of network partners' drift from in-
stitutional oversight and worsened public sub-­network infight-
ing, which eroded LAGeSo's legitimacy and amplified the crisis
(Berthod et al. 2017; Milward and Provan 2000). Maintaining le-
gitimacy is crucial for public agencies' capacity to manage a cri-
sis effectively, and a lack of strategic capacity building can render
all operational crisis management activities ineffective.
Second, these observations highlight the importance of develop-
ing public leadership capabilities for network governance. Crises
often involve a wide range of (ad hoc) collaborators, such as non-­
profit relief organizations, volunteer groups, for-­profit partners,
and other public agencies. Effective crisis management requires
public agencies to provide strong coordinating leadership within
these emergent networks to prevent legitimacy loss, exploitation,
and the misappropriation of public resources for private gain.
Network governance requires leaders with the ability to manage
competing demands and tensions during crises (Backhaus et al.
2021; Boin and Hart 2003), both within their own agency and
externally, to ensure the effective coordination of crisis response
and emergency relief service co-­creation.
Third, LAGeSo's case underscores the importance of an organi-
zation's ability to develop and enhance leadership skills during
a crisis. Given the increasing frequency of (poly)crises, which
makes crisis management the new normal rather than an ex-
ception (Bechky and Okhuysen 2011), public agencies involved
in crisis and emergency responses should adopt an agile and
processual approach to crisis management while building lead-
ership capacities to govern multi-­stakeholder crisis response
networks effectively. These insights also open new avenues for
research on crisis leadership and the governance of public ser-
vice networks, highlighting the need to carefully identify and
assess potentially dysfunctional network dynamics (Raab and
Milward 2003).
Like any empirical research, this study has some limita-
tions. First, it presents an extreme case of legitimacy ero-
sion. However, such cases often “reveal more information
because they activate more actors and basic mechanisms”
(Flyvbjerg  2016, 229). The findings do not aim to general-
ize beyond the historical context but instead highlight the
interplay of legitimacy dimensions in a specific crisis man-
agement context. Although LAGeSo's failure during the “ref-
ugee crisis” had severe consequences, the agency is a typical
German agency facing resource scarcity, leadership deficien-
cies, and anti-­public sector sentiment (Marvel 2015; Ritz and
Weißmüller 2025), providing valuable insights for other pub-
lic agencies facing similar challenges. Second, the analysis
is based on secondary data—newspaper articles and press
statements. While these sources enabled a detailed processual
analysis (Langley 1999) of the unfolding LAGeSo crisis, they
cannot capture individual actors' motivations and intentions
in detail. Nonetheless, since legitimacy is attributed by actor
groups—such as the public, political leaders, and beneficiaries
(Massey  2001; Johnson et  al.  2006)—media data is valuable
for analyzing the multi-­level crisis and the interplay between
LAGeSo's actions and actors' responses. Furthermore, media
outlets have their own motivations for reporting crises, such as
profit-­driven incentives and their role in scrutinizing political
decisions (Jacobs and Schillemans 2019). The crisis was por-
trayed as balanced as possible by including newspapers with
diverse political orientations and local and national coverage.
Additionally, press statements and interview material from
newspaper articles helped counter potential media biases and
provide a broader range of perspectives. For similar reasons,
social media data were not included in the data collection
since the LAGeSo's “refugee crisis” was often discussed in the
broader context of German migration and integration policies,
mixing very different issues and contributing to polarization
streams. Newspaper articles provided a “hygiene factor” in the

a deeper understanding of the conditions under which legiti-

Despite these limitations, the LAGeSo case marks a historically
significant moment in the German administrative landscape. Its
insights into the agency's failure and rapid legitimacy erosion reso-
nated beyond Germany, still influencing political debates, admin-

later. The effects of the German “refugee crisis” and LAGeSo's

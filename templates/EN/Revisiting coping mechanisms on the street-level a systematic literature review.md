Abstract
Street-level bureaucrats’ (SLBs) coping mechanisms are an integral part of their interaction with clients, and as such have received much
research attention. Since the last review of the behavioral coping mechanisms adopted by SLBs, conducted more than 10 years ago, many
researchers have focused on understanding these mechanisms and what influences them. Using the PRISMA model, we conducted a system-
atic review of 165 studies to update our understanding and explore the manifestations of both old and new coping mechanisms identified in the
literature. Moreover, we seek to determine the antecedents of these coping mechanisms. From our findings emerged a new family of coping
mechanisms that we call “moving with clients.” These behaviors include intentional efforts to involve clients in the process of service delivery,
for example, by deliberating and cooperating with them to achieve the desired policy goals. We find that most SLBs’ behaviors are associated
with organizational and environmental factors. Personal factors, although highlighted frequently in the literature, are not associated with most
coping behaviors.

Introduction
Street-level bureaucrats (SLBs) are pivotal actors in
implementing policy (Lipsky 2010). Their discretion and be-
havior during their interactions with clients influence policy
outcomes, as their decision-making often becomes policy itself
(Hupe and Hill 2007; Maynard-Moody and Musheno 2012).
SLBs often adopt coping mechanisms to deal with the multiple,
increasing, and contradictory demands they face (Brodkin
2011). These mechanisms have attracted researchers’ atten-
tion due to their influence on policy outcomes (Davidovitz
and Cohen 2022; Lavee 2021; Oberfield 2009; Pedersen and
Pors 2023).
Almost 10 years ago, the Journal of Public Administration
Research and Theory published an important literature re-
view of the topic. This review, conducted by Tummers and
colleagues in 2013 and published in 2015, suggested a
new typology for Michael Lipsky’s concept of street-level
coping. Scholars have adopted this typology and often use
it to describe existing and new coping mechanisms. For
example, a study about civil servants in the Netherlands
(Blijleven and Hulst 2021) demonstrated SLBs’ use of rule
bending and instrumental actions, mechanisms previously
identified in Tummers et al.’s review (2015). However, this
same study noted a new coping mechanism that SLBs use
in their work—rapport-building (Blijleven and Hulst 2021).
In another example, counselors who used routinizing and
resource rationing in their work also began outsourcing the
work to the clients who came to ask for assistance (Breit et
al. 2021).
These new mechanisms highlight the need to explore the
changes and advances in the literature in the past decade.
Moreover, as Tummers et al. (2015) mentioned, we must
identify the antecedents of street-level coping mechanisms
and the personal, organizational, or environmental factors
(Cohen 2018) that are associated with the emergence of spe-
cific coping mechanisms. Thus, the aim of this study is to ex-
amine recent developments in street-level coping mechanisms
and identify the factors that shape their emergence.
To accomplish these goals, we conducted a literature re-
view of the behavioral coping mechanisms that SLBs adopt
and the factors associated with them. Using the PRISMA
framework, we reviewed 165 studies that explore SLBs’
coping mechanisms, intending to answer the following re-
search questions:

This study makes two key contributions to the literature
on SLBs and their coping mechanisms. First, it provides an
updated, comprehensive review of SLBs’ coping behaviors,
illustrating the scale, variation, and significance of these
responses in everyday practice. By identifying new forms of
coping that have emerged since the foundational work of
Tummers et al. (2015), the study not only reflects changes
in street-level practice but also refines and expands the ex-
isting typologies of coping mechanisms. In particular, we pro-
pose a new family of coping behaviors, “moving with clients,”
which highlights how SLBs actively collaborate with clients
to achieve policy goals. This theoretical addition enriches the
coping typology and offers a novel lens through which to un-
derstand SLBs’ adaptive strategies. This deeper understanding
contributes to a more accurate and up-to-date portrayal of
SLBs’ behavior across contexts.
Second, the study fills an important gap in the literature
by systematically exploring the antecedents of SLBs’ coping
mechanisms. While much of the existing research describes
what SLBs do to manage the often contradictory demands
of their work, less is known about why they adopt spe-
cific coping strategies. By examining how personal, organi-
zational, and environmental factors influence the choice of
coping mechanisms, this study enhances our understanding of
the conditions under which certain behaviors emerge.
Theoretical background
Street-level coping
SLBs play a crucial role in the implementation of public
policy, as their decisions and interactions with clients sig-
nificantly shape policy outcomes (Lipsky 2010). Positioned
on the frontlines of the public sector, SLBs engage daily with
clients, and through these interactions, in essence participate
in the making of policy (Durose 2007; Gofen 2014). Given
that the demand for public services consistently exceeds avail-
able resources (Lipsky 2010), their work is characterized by
heavy workloads, limited time, and ongoing tensions between
the written policies and the needs of their clients (Loyens
2019). Within this context, SLBs exercise considerable dis-
cretion in implementing policy, determining how resources
and sanctions are distributed (Maynard-Moody and Portillo
2010; Tummers and Bekkers 2014  ). Faced with constraints
such as insufficient time, knowledge, or support, they must
adapt the policies as written to the realities on the ground
to fulfill both the goals of the organization and the needs of
individual clients (Hupe and Hill 2007; Sager et al. 2014 ;
Thomann, Maxia, and Ege 2023).
The unique role of SLBs on the frontlines often leads to
their use of coping mechanisms (Lipsky 2010). Given that
SLBs have limited resources and face challenges and pressures
both from their clients and their organizations (Jacobsson and
Johansson 2025), they cope with the demands of their job in
various ways (Brodkin 2011; Lipsky 2010; Maynard-Moody
and Musheno 2003). To deal with the mismatch between
policy and practice (Maynard-Moody and Musheno 2012),
they adopt coping mechanisms, what Folkman and Lazarus
(1980) referred to as cognitive and behavioral efforts, to re-
duce external and internal conflicts.
In their extensive review, Tummers et al. (2015) concen-
trated on the “behavioral efforts frontline workers employ
when interacting with clients, in order to master, tolerate,
or reduce external and internal demands and conflicts they
face on an everyday basis” (p. 1101). We follow their clas-
sification of these coping mechanisms into three groups: (1)
moving towards clients, (2) moving away from clients, and
(3) moving against clients.
Moving towards clients refers to behaviors that involve the
adjustment of the policy that is being implemented to meet
the clients’ needs (Tummers et al. 2015). Previous literature
provided several examples of such behavior. The first is rule
bending, meaning the adjustment of the rules to meet clients’
needs (McDonald and Marston 2008). An example is social
workers in Denmark and Sweden who look for loopholes to
help their clients (Møller and Stensöta 2019). The second is
rule-breaking, meaning the deliberate obstruction of rules to
meet clients’ needs (Kelly 1994). An example is Israeli Arab
teachers who teach material to their students that is outside
of or contradicts the approved curriculum (Diab and Cohen
2022). The third type of moving towards clients is instru-
mental actions, meaning the execution of a long-term solution
to benefit the client (Wagenaar 2004). An example is frontline
municipal workers in Denmark who establish collaborative
forums with stakeholders to allow the latter’s participation
in implementing policy (Agger and Tortzen 2023). The fourth
is the prioritization of clients, meaning giving certain clients
more time, resources, and energy (Maynard-Moody and
Musheno 2012). An example is teachers in Denmark who
focus on students who are more likely to succeed (Baviskar
2019). The fifth type of moving towards clients is the use of
personal resources, meaning the use of one’s personal time
and money to benefit the client (Mutereko and Chitakunye
2014). Examples include healthcare professionals in Mexico
who use their personal time and cellphones to contact clients
(Campos and Peeters 2022), as well as teachers, nurses, and
social workers in Israel who invest their personal time, money,
and emotional efforts to assist clients (Lavee 2022).
Moving away from clients refers to SLBs avoiding mean-
ingful interactions with clients (Tummers et al. 2015). It
involves routinizing service delivery, meaning treating citi-
zens in a standardized way (Summers and Semrud-Clikeman
2000). An example is counselors in Norway who answer all
inquiries the same way (Breit et al. 2021). Moving away from
clients also involves rationing services by reducing their avail-
ability or their attractiveness to clients (Tummers and Rocco
2015). Examples include Israeli police officers who tell crime
victims to come back at another time, when the station is less
busy (Edri-Peer and Cohen 2023) and American employment
counselors who dismiss clients prematurely, regardless of
their needs being met (Kelly 1994).
Lastly, moving against clients refers to confrontations with
clients, meaning implementing the policy in a way that might
harm clients (Tummers et al. 2015). These behaviors include
rigidly following the rules in an inflexible way that may go
against clients’ needs and demands (Henderson 2013). An ex-
ample is police officers in China who follow the rules strictly

highlights the need for further exploration of the literature.
The antecedents of street-level coping
Coping is a byproduct of the characteristics of street-level
work. As Tummers et al.’s (2015) definition of coping suggests,
there are internal and external demands and conflicts that
SLBs must handle as part of their daily work. Thus, different
demands may lead to different behaviors, as no single theory
can fully explain street-level decision-making and behavior
(Loyens and Maesschalck 2010).
Nevertheless, previous attempts have suggested dividing
attempts to deal with these demands into three groups of
factors: personal, organizational, and environmental (Cohen
2018; Hupe and Buffat 2014 ). Personal factors refer to the
SLBs’ personal ideology, preferences, opinions, and values.
Examples include the SLBs’ sense of justice and fairness (Kelly
1994), their values that conflict with the policy (de Graaf,
Huberts, and Smulders 2016), stereotypes and assessments
about the clients (Gershgoren, Edri‐Peer, and Cohen 2024;
Jilke and Tummers 2018; Maynard-Moody and Musheno
2003; McGann, O’Sullivan, and Considine 2022; Ratzmann
2021), and the SLBs’ own personal background (Raaphorst
and Groeneveld 2019  ).
Organizational factors refer to the values, norms, and
mechanisms of street-level organizations. Examples include
performance management (Brodkin 2012; Møller and Hill
2021  ), professional values and norms (Hupe and van der
Krogt 2013), professional training (Davidovitz et al. 2023;
Frisch-Aviram, Beeri, and Cohen 2021), professional account-
ability (Gershgoren and Cohen 2023; Hupe and Hill 2007),
role perceptions and conflicts (Thomann 2015; Tummers et
al. 2012), and group and team learning (Foldy and Buckley
2010).
Environmental factors refer to the general climate and
context in which the public services are being delivered. For
example, Cohen (2018) noted the important role of culture
in shaping SLBs’ behavior, and Brodkin (2021) wrote about
coping in times of crises. Other studies have identified the
importance of community in terms of its variance (Weissert
1994), political orientation (Stensöta 2012), public opinion
(Cohen and Golan-Nadir 2020; Hupe and van der Krogt
2013), the influence of New Public Management (Considine
and Lewis 2012), and the type of welfare state regime (Møller
and Stensöta 2019).
Nevertheless, despite the extensive research into the factors
that influence street-level decision-making and behavior, the
big picture remains unclear. The relationship between these
factors and SLBs’ decision-making and behavior is still
missing, as the antecedents of the SLBs’ coping have not been
systematically studied (Tummers et al. 2015).
Methodology
To conduct our systematic review, we used the Preferred
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis
(PRISMA) framework (Liberati et al. 2009), a well-accepted
procedure to ensure replicability and transparency in system-
atic reviews. The methodology is divided into two stages: the
search strategy and the coding process.
Search strategy
Building on previous systematic reviews of the public admin-
istration literature (e.g., Edri-Peer et al. 2023; Frisch Aviram
et al. 2020; Tummers et al. 2015), we searched for studies in
two academic databases: The ISI Web of Science and Scopus.
These databases enable researchers to identify peer-reviewed
journal articles in numerous fields (Tummers et al. 2015).
The search was conducted in February 2024 with the terms
that Tummers et al. (2015) used: “street-level bureauc*” OR
“frontline” OR “field-level” OR “policy implementation”
AND “coping” OR “cope.” This search yielded 1,757 papers.
Additionally, we searched for articles that cited the review
conducted by Tummers et al. (2015) and the articles that
Tummers et al. (2015) cited. These searches yielded 706 and
99 articles, respectively. In July 2024, we conducted a manual
search in the five top-ranked public administration journals
listed in the Journal Citation Report in the past five years:
Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, Public
Administration Review, Public Management Review, Climate
Policy, and Policy and Society. This search yielded 74 articles,
resulting in a total of 2,636 studies.
In the next step, we screened these articles’ titles and
abstracts, and kept only those that (1) were not duplicates;
(2) were published between 1980 and 2024; (3) were in the
English language; (4) were published in an international peer-
reviewed journal; (5) concerned the population of street-
level bureaucrats; and (6) referred to coping during public
encounters. After applying these criteria to our dataset, we
were left with 370 articles for a full-text review. Then, using
the same preestablished criteria, we excluded irrelevant ar-
ticles based on their full-text reading, thus resulting in 165
articles for full-text analysis (see Appendix A for the full list
and Supplementary Appendix B for the PRISMA flow chart).
Next, we coded the textual data and identified the themes that
emerged using a preprepared code book (see Supplementary
Appendix C) (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005).
While our search strategy used Tummers et al.’s (2015)
search terms, we utilized a different search strategy. Thus, we
expected our review to include different studies than those
included in the previous review.
Coding process
The coding process consisted of two stages. First, we
conducted a descriptive overview of the full texts, including
the following descriptions: policy domain, geographic area,
occupation of the SLB, sector of the SLB, gender of the SLB,
main theory, and research methods employed. These variables
were close coded. These descriptions are commonly used in
systematic reviews of public administration literature to as-
sess the distribution of a phenomenon across different sectors
and occupations (Jones et al. 2015) .
Second, we conducted a thematic analysis of the data to
identify, organize, and categorize patterns of meaning (Braun
and Clarke 2012). Relying on previous research on coping
mechanisms, we first coded ways of coping identified in

Results
Background characteristics
Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the studies in-
cluded. Similar to the findings of Tummers and colleagues
(2015), most studies were conducted in developed countries
(85 percent), with most studies being conducted in the US (18
percent) and Denmark (13 percent). Most studies explored
just one country (90 percent), while others compared two
or more countries (10 percent). Most studies used qualita-
tive approaches (76 percent). Most studies were published
after 2015, the year in which Tummers et al.’s (2015) review
was published (80.7 percent). In addition, there was an ex-
ponential growth in such studies after 2020. Only 18 per-
cent employed quantitative approaches, and only 6.1 percent
used mixed methods. Surprisingly, only 5.5 percent of the
studies concerned the COVID-19 pandemic. Like Tummers
et al. (2015), we found that most studies included in the anal-
ysis were published in the Journal of Public Administration
Research and Theory (9.6 percent).

ferent professions of SLBs. Most studies included in our re-

teachers, nurses, doctors, other healthcare professionals such

such as tax inspectors and environmental inspectors. Other
professions were coded together.1 Unsurprisingly, most studies

their analysis, and 26 percent mentioned men.

way towards clients. In the category of aggressive behavior,
we also included the decision to enforce the law or sanction
clients in an aggressive way. For example, in this category we
included inspectors who used the terms “enforcement with an
iron fist” or “severely cracking down on violations” (Liu et
al. 2022: 420).
A new family of coping mechanisms: moving with
clients
In the past decade, more and more studies have identified new
coping mechanisms that Tummers and colleagues (2015) did
not discuss. We coded 197 portions of text referring to new
coping mechanisms. The names of these mechanisms were
taken from the literature. Table 3 summarizes these findings.
Some of these mechanisms fit Tummers et al.’s (2015) three
categories. However, we identified a new family of coping
behaviors that we called “moving with clients.”
Our findings suggest that under certain conditions, SLBs
develop new coping mechanisms in order to achieve policy
goals. While the previous families of coping mechanisms in-
volved asymmetrical, one-sided behaviors between SLBs
and their clients, we found evidence in the literature of a
shift in these dynamics (Breit et al. 2021; Cohen, Benish,
and Shamriz-Ilouz 2016). Our new category of moving with
clients marks a change in the relationship between SLBs and
their clients that provides more balance to their relationship.
This change refers to the increasing involvement of SLBs’
clients in achieving the desired policy outcomes.

Another important and recurring new coping mechanism
was rapport building. Instead of having short interactions with
clients, SLBs purposefully worked to create and maintain a
strong, lasting relationship that led to the clients’ cooperation
in achieving policy goals. SLBs referred to building long-lasting
relationships as “the key to effective regulation” (Fineman
1998: 957) and the engagement in meaningful conversations
with clients as a way to gain their trust (Blijleven and Hulst

to clients’ cooperation with policies (Davidovitz 2023; Lillvis
2019). For example, doctors in rural districts who had few
resources relied on their personal relationships with clients
to make diagnoses, because the doctors lacked the equip-
ment to do so otherwise (Pare Toe and Samuelsen 2021). We
considered such coping mechanisms as examples of “moving
with clients” because they were intentional efforts on the
part of SLBs to make clients feel comfortable with them, as
a fundamental part of their work (Mortensen and Needham

clients’ behavior (Lavee 2024) and underscored the impor-

reported in 18.7 percent of the studies.
These behaviors all represent a shift in SLBs’ perceptions
of their work. In some cases, they have moved away from the
idea of handling clients by making one-sided decisions. They
now engage with their clients, allowing them to participate in
the policy process and influencing policy outcomes as a way
to achieve desired policy goals.
Nevertheless, our review also found three new coping
mechanisms that fit Tummers et al.’s (2015) previous typology.
Thus, to their original category of new moving away from
clients, we added referrals and outsourcing, and distancing
from clients. In addition to their original category of new
moving against clients, we added deviating from the rules.
Moving away from clients
We found that SLBs use referrals and outsourcing as a way
of moving away from their clients. Instead of supplying the
clients with the services requested, they gave the clients the
responsibility of accomplishing the policy goals or passed
them on to other agencies and policy actors. One example
is counselors who outsourced their services by teaching
the clients how to perform the task themselves (Breit et al.
2021). In another example, teachers referred students who
needed special attention to other employers or organizations
(Mutereko and Chitakunye 2014). This coping mechanism
differs from the rationing of services because the services were
not just temporarily unavailable to the clients. The clients
were actually sent away in other directions and to other
facilities as a way of reducing the SLBs’ stress. Thus, the SLBs
limited their engagement with the clients by reducing their job
responsibilities. While rationing often refers to supplying min-
imum standards of service, referrals and outsourcing mean
supplying no services at all. Indeed, this coping mechanism
put the responsibility for the service on the users or other
SLBs. This new coping mechanism occurred in 14.5 percent
of the studies.
A less frequent coping mechanism involved creating dis-
tance from clients. Establishing boundaries put both meta-
phorical and physical distance between the SLBs and the
clients (Eriksson and Johansson 2022) to maintain control
(Borrelli 2021) and reduce workloads (Civinskas, Dvorak,
and Šumskas 2021). While it minimized the SLBs’ stress, it
could also limit the clients’ access to services. In Civinskas,
Dvorak, and Sumskas (2021), healthcare professionals
distanced themselves during the COVID-19 pandemic and
were surprised to find out how clients managed themselves.
In other examples, SLBs set up physical boundaries between
themselves and their clients by refusing to supply services
to certain clients (Davidovitz and Cohen 2022) or societal
boundaries by not sharing their personal information with
clients (Davidovitz and Cohen 2023). Some practices were in-
tended to do both. For example, Karadaghi and Willot (2015)
described how doctors worked to create boundaries by
dressing and speaking differently than their clients, and often
placing themselves behind large desks to maintain physical
distance as well. In other cases, where boundaries are fuzzy
and blurred, SLBs must work harder to create such distance
to protect themselves (Lotta, Nieto-Morales, and Peeters
2023). This coping mechanism occurred in 18.7 percent of
the studies.
Moving against clients
Tummers et al. (2015) placed rule-bending and rule-breaking
in the category of moving towards clients. They reported
that SLBs sometimes adjusted the rules to help clients when
the policy and the reality did not correspond. However, we
found that SLBs sometimes deviated from the rules for their
own benefit, even if it meant harming clients. One example is
migration officers who withhold information from migrants
facing deportation, despite rules and procedures that call for
transparency. They do so to avoid having the clients escape
and to meet policy goals (Borrelli 2021). In another example,
SLBs denied educational opportunities to unvaccinated
children by ignoring the grace periods that the policy allowed
(Harvey and Attwell 2023). In another study, police officers
used workarounds to fine noncompliant clients during the
Covid-19 pandemic by taking their pictures and sending their
fines to their homes, instead of fining them on the spot, as
the policy directed (Charbonneau, Boisvert, and Bégin 2023).
This mechanism occurred in 9.6 percent of the studies.

Our findings suggest that Tummers et al.’s (2015) typology
is still a good fit with the current literature, as many of the
ways of coping identified in the previous review appeared
in our updated review. We also discovered a new family of
coping mechanisms, “moving with clients,” which includes
behaviors that involve clients in the process of public service
delivery. This family has several new mechanisms: delibera-
tion, empowering clients, negotiation with clients, and rapport
building. In addition, we identified other mechanisms such as
outsourcing services, creating distance, and rule deviation in
a way that can hurt clients that belong in Tummers et al.’s
categories. Lastly, we found that while all of the factors are
important in shaping SLBs’ behavior, organizational factors
are the most prevalent group of factors and are associated
with moving away from clients. Environmental factors pro-
mote both moving toward and away from clients. Personal
factors, while still prevalent, are insignificant.
What contributions does this review make to the litera-
ture on street-level coping mechanisms? First, adding a new
family of coping mechanisms, “moving with clients,” sheds
further light on how the ever-changing environment in which
SLBs operate leads to the development of new, more inclu-
sive mechanisms. As our findings suggest, SLBs adopt these
mechanisms due to their lack of power and resources, com-
petition in providing services, and high levels of public pres-
sure. While Tummers et al.’s (2015) typology refers mainly
to decisions made solely by the SLBs, we see in these new
coping mechanisms a shift in power to the clients (Cohen,
Benish, and Shamriz-Ilouz 2016). In order to achieve policy
goals, SLBs now must consider their clients’ opinions and
nudge their clients into the desired direction using tools such
as persuasion and trust. These changes go hand-in-hand with
newer trends of the coproduction of services (e.g., Mortensen
and Needham 2023) that see clients as an integral part of
the policy process. While Tummers et al.’s (2015) typology
still holds true, there is room for a new family of coping
mechanisms that takes into account the client in the process
of allocating public goods (Bjerregaard and Klitmøller 2010).
Second, our findings show the importance of organizational
pressures on SLBs’ decision-making and behavior (Cohen
et al. 2023; Meza et al. 2021). These pressures are particu-
larly important in their choice to move away from clients.
Surprisingly, we did not find conclusive evidence that their
personal opinions, ideology, and values are relevant when it
comes to their work, despite the attention that personal factors
have received in recent research (Gofen 2014; Thunman
2016; Xiao et al. 2024 ). Note that we do not claim that
these factors are unimportant in the SLBs’ decision-making.
Rather, organizational pressures exert a stronger influence on
their choices than their personal preferences when it comes
to making discretionary decisions about delivering services.
Another contribution is the unexpected role of environ-
mental factors, including policies imposed on SLBs from above
(Gofen et al. 2019; Lotta, Lima-Silva, and Favareto 2022),
reforms in service delivery such as New Public Management
(van Loon and Jakobsen 2018), and public pressure (Cohen,
Benish, and Shamriz-Ilouz 2016). These environmental factors
have an immense influence on SLBs’ behavior during public
encounters. As our findings suggest, despite the importance
of these factors, they have received somewhat less research
attention. Thus, there is a need for further examination of the
effect of these factors on street-level coping mechanisms.
The fourth contribution is identifying new types of coping
behaviors, particularly mechanisms for moving towards
clients. In times of scarce resources, competing demands, and
procedures that tie their hands, SLBs who lack the power to
assist their clients using the “old” ways, such as rule bending
or prioritizing clients, try to create and sustain relationships
with their clients to accomplish the goals of the policy and help
their clients. For example, deliberation is not just about making

dissertations, and unpublished work, which might lead to pub-
lication bias in our dataset (Cooper 2017). Nevertheless, to
maintain an appropriate standard of the studies included in this
review, we chose to limit our dataset to peer-reviewed papers,
particularly because peer-reviewed journals in public adminis-
tration are now more sensitive to null findings in preregistered
studies (Torgerson 2006). Future research can explore unpub-
lished materials as well. Additionally, our review focuses on
identifying coping mechanisms and their antecedents. As such,
we did not examine the consequences of coping behaviors. Thus,
a future systematic review can also concentrate on this topic.
A second limitation concerns the potential for research bias
within the reviewed literature itself. Our findings are based on
how coping mechanisms and their antecedents have been framed,
measured, and interpreted in prior studies. As such, they are
shaped by the research questions, theoretical lenses, and method-
ological constraints of those studies. This may result in an over-
emphasis on certain forms of coping or influencing factors while
overlooking others, particularly in areas where scholarly attention
has only recently emerged, such as citizen-centered perspectives.
Furthermore, this review was conducted after the COVID-
19 pandemic. However, given that SLBs still function in a
world of major challenges, such as polarization and popu-
lism, climate change, artificial intelligence, and other growing
pressures, we maintain that SLBs are likely to develop coping
mechanisms for dealing with these issues. Thus, it is impor-
tant to reexamine coping mechanisms on the street level.
Our findings also call for the need to conduct more compara-
tive research, whether between countries, sectors, or professions.
There is a need for further understanding of the different
contexts in which SLBs operate and how these contexts influ-
ence coping behavior. Lastly, as our data suggests, most studies
about coping mechanisms among SLBs use qualitative methods.
While these methods provide deep and thorough insights into
coping behaviors, future research should use quantitative and
mixed methods to examine more aspects of this important topic.

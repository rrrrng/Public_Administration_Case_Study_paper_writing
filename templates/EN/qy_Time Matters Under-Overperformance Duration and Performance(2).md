ABSTRACT
Prior research on public organizations' strategic responses to performance feedback has focused solely on the intensity of per-
formance feedback while neglecting its temporal dimensions. This study aims to fill this gap by incorporating the lens of time
and investigating how performance feedback duration affects performance improvements in the public sector. Drawing on var-
ious theoretical perspectives, we theorize an inverted U-­shaped relationship between underperformance (negative performance
feedback) duration and public organizations' subsequent performance improvements, and a U-­shaped relationship for overper-
formance (positive performance feedback) duration. Empirical analyses using the case of China's official city air quality ranking
provide evidence supporting our theory. Our findings reveal that “time” (duration) can shape public organizations' responses to
performance feedback in nonlinear ways and help reconcile inconsistencies in the existing literature, highlighting the impor-
tance of incorporating temporal dimensions of performance to advance performance feedback theory in the public sector.
1   |   Introduction
During the past decades, governments worldwide, in tandem
with the global wave of “New Public Management” reforms,
have adopted performance management in various forms to
govern public organizations (Holm  2018). Echoing this trend,
there has been a surge of interest among both scholars and
practitioners in understanding public organizations' strategic
behaviors in response to performance management, which, in
turn, affects organizational performance (Chen  2025a; Ji and
Hong 2025). Particularly, with its roots in the classical behav-
ioral theory of the firm (BTOF; Cyert and March 1963), a bur-
geoning body of research has recently emerged to investigate
how performance feedback, a crucial element of the perfor-
mance management cycle, shapes subsequent management in
the public sector (Chen 2025a).
Research in this field has gathered extensive insights into
how performance feedback elicits a wide range of strategic re-
sponses as well as various aspects of organizational outcomes.
For the former, studies show that performance feedback sig-
nificantly affects not only managerial decisions, such as the
gender composition in top management teams (Huang  2024),
the inclination to involve frontline professionals (Østergaard-­
Nielsen 2025), the (re)prioritization of goals (Chen and Jia 2023;
Zhu and Rutherford 2019; Holm 2018), and the selection of man-
agerial strategies (Cheon 2021), but also gaming responses, such
as manipulation of performance data (Chen 2024, 2025a) and
effort substitution (Ji and Hong 2025). As for organizational out-
comes, research demonstrates that performance feedback exerts
considerable impacts on public organizations' subsequent per-
formance improvements (Hong et al. 2024; Kim and Ko 2023;
Wei et al. 2023), managerial quality (Hong et al. 2020), manage-
rial turnover (Yu and Chen 2025), and the reduction of red tape
(Hong 2020).
Despite the considerable progress achieved, a notable and per-
sistent gap, however, is that previous research has focused
solely on the intensity of performance feedback, i.e., public or-
ganizations' actual performance relative to (social, historical,

it is essentially static and overlooks the temporal dimensions of

fully the complexities in public organizations' responses to per-
formance feedback. As has been widely recognized, organiza-
tions are attuned not only to the performance intensity relative
to aspirations but also to the historical trajectory of their perfor-
mance (Bowers and Prato 2019). Particularly, we can expect that
organizations that have experienced a long period of continuous
success or failure or high levels of performance volatility over
time can behave substantially differently from those without
such experiences (Jung et al. 2024; Goyal and Goyal 2024; Yu
et al. 2019). Thus, incorporating the temporal dimensions of per-
formance is vital, providing a dynamic perspective for the fur-
ther development of performance feedback theory in the public
sector. Notably, the importance of the temporal dimensions of
performance was explicitly noted in the seminal work by Meier
et al. (2015), which pioneered performance feedback research in
the public sector. Considering this fact, it is particularly puzzling
that no subsequent research has addressed this topic to date.
This research offers one of the first systematic efforts to fill this
gap. We concentrate on performance feedback duration, a par-
ticular temporal dimension of performance that has recently at-
tracted growing scholarly attention in the private management
literature (Liu et al. 2024; Derayati 2024; Goyal and Goyal 2024;
Yu et  al.  2019), and investigate how underperformance and
overperformance duration can exert complex influences on
public organizations' subsequent performance improvements,
one of the organizational outcomes of most concern in perfor-
mance feedback research (Chen 2025b; Wei et al. 2023; Kim and
Ko 2023; Ji 2022; Min and Oh 2020; Hong 2019; Holm 2018).1
Drawing on various theoretical perspectives, we develop a sys-
tematic theoretical account of how under/overperformance
duration affects public organizations' subsequent performance
improvements. Specifically, we predict that underperformance
duration exhibits an inverted U-­shaped relationship with sub-
sequent performance improvements, whereas the duration of
overperformance shows a U-­shaped effect.
We obtain empirical evidence from China's official city air qual-
ity ranking, a crucial performance management scheme wherein
the center evaluates and ranks monthly a set of cities based on
their performance in air pollution control. Using two-­way fixed
effect linear panel data models, we find curvilinear relationships
between under/overperformance duration and cities' subsequent
performance improvements that corroborate our theory.
This study contributes to the performance feedback research by
bringing attention to the temporal dimensions of performance
feedback, paving a new path for advancing performance feedback
theory in the public sector. Specifically, we go beyond the exist-
ing literature, which has primarily centered on the intensity of
performance feedback, by incorporating the lens of performance
feedback duration, a dynamic perspective that helps further our
understanding of how performance feedback shapes public orga-
nizations' strategic behaviors. Moreover, previous studies mostly
suggest linear impacts of performance feedback on organizational
behaviors. We depart from them by theorizing and evidencing
nonlinearity in the relationships between under/overperformance
duration and subsequent performance improvements, adding nu-
ances about public organizations' complex behaviors in response
to performance feedback. Notably, by doing so, this research also
provides a perspective to reconcile inconsistent theoretical views
in the prior studies regarding public organizations' responses to
underperformance and overperformance.
2   |   Theory and Hypotheses
This section develops the theory and hypotheses. Following the
tradition of BTOF, we assume that public managers are boundedly
rational and tend to satisfice rather than maximize (Hong 2019).
Moreover, they are concerned about performance and rely on as-
pirations, such as past performance (historical aspiration), peer
organizations' performance (social aspiration), and targets set by
superiors (pre-­set aspiration), as reference points for evaluating
their performance status (Kotiloglu et al. 2021; Meier et al. 2015).
In accordance with previous performance feedback research, we
distinguish two performance feedback conditions, i.e., underper-
formance (negative performance feedback, NPF), where organiza-
tional performance falls short of aspirations, and overperformance
(positive performance feedback, PPF), where organizational per-
formance surpasses aspirations.
In the following, we develop theoretical predictions on the im-
pacts of underperformance and overperformance duration.
Across both conditions, our central argument is that the tem-
poral persistence of performance feedback triggers a dynamic
interplay between two opposing sets of mechanisms, with their
relative dominance gradually shifting as duration increases,
yielding nonlinear relationships between feedback duration and
subsequent performance improvements.

In the initial phase of underperformance, public organizations'
subsequent performance improvement is positively associated
with the duration of underperformance, driven by two mech-
anisms that intensify as the duration increases. First, rooted in

the classical BTOF (Cyert and March 1963), the internal man-
agerial mechanism posits that public managers perceive NPF
as problematic, triggering “problemistic search.” This process
involves strategic actions (e.g., innovative search, risk-­taking,
and goal reprioritization) to achieve satisfactory performance
(Holm 2018; Meier et al. 2015). Crucially, as the duration of un-
derperformance increases, ambiguities and random noise de-
crease (Zhao and Xiao 2024; Piening et al. 2021; Iyer et al. 2019),
making the problem more salient and compelling public or-
ganizations to initiate this search process. Second, embedded
in political systems characterized by a negativity bias (Hong
et al. 2020; Holm 2018; Nielsen 2014), public organizations are
more motivated by blame avoidance than credit claiming. The
external political accountability mechanism thus plays a pivotal
role: public organizations are particularly attentive to NPF and
inclined to improve performance to address blame for failures
(Chen  2024). However, short-­term underperformance may be
attributed to uncontrollable external factors through “presen-
tational strategies” (e.g., providing excuses and justifications),
allowing organizations to avoid blame without costly changes
(Hood  2010). As underperformance repeats, these strategies
lose effectiveness due to intensifying accountability pressures,
forcing organizations to strive for performance improvements to
mitigate blame.
However, as underperformance duration lengthens, the oppos-
ing set of mechanisms gradually gains dominance, leading to a
negative relationship between underperformance duration and
public organizations' subsequent performance improvements.
Three mechanisms drive this reversal. First, a crucial precon-
dition for organizations to take actions to address performance
shortfalls is that they perceive the problems as largely “repair-
able” (Derayati 2024). Prolonged underperformance signals the
ineffectiveness of prior actions, inducing “learned helplessness”
(Hiroto and Seligman 1975). This leads public organizations to
adopt pessimistic expectations about their capabilities to turn
the problem around (Derayati 2024), resulting in decreased re-
sponsiveness or even abandonment of corrective efforts (Zhao
and Xiao 2024). Second, persistent underperformance activates
the threat-­rigidity effect, where public managers perceive the
situation as threatening and respond rigidly (Staw et al. 1981).
Specifically, decision-­makers tend to restrict information search
and processing, reducing the richness and novelty of information
needed for generating solutions, while being risk-­averse and re-
lying more on formalization and well-­established routines (Van
der Voet and Lems 2022). As a result, this rigidity impedes effec-
tive adaptation, hinders innovation, and even exacerbates initial
problems, resulting in declines in performance improvements
(Liu et al. 2024; Piening et al. 2021). Third, public organizations
face multiple goals but have only limited resources and attention
(Chen and Jia 2023). They tend to strategically shift priorities by
moving away from a goal where they have been underperform-
ing for a long duration and redirecting attention and resources
toward other goals with better chances of achieving satisfactory
performance (Chen and Jia 2023).2 Such strategic reprioritiza-
tion further undermines performance improvements.
Synthesizing the above arguments, we propose that the rela-
tionship between underperformance duration and public orga-
nizations' subsequent performance improvements is nonlinear.
That is, performance improvements initially increase and then
decrease with underperformance duration. Particularly, the
nonlinearity arises from gradual shifts in the relative dominance
of two opposing sets of mechanisms as underperformance du-
ration lengthens, leading to continuous and smooth changes in
the marginal effect. Thus, the proposed nonlinear relationship is
most appropriately conceptualized as inverted U-­shaped, rather
than other alternatives such as the threshold effect (character-
ized by a discontinuous and abrupt change) and the S-­shaped
relationship (in which the rate of change varies, while the di-
rection of marginal effect remains consistent throughout).3 We
therefore hypothesize:
H1.  There is an inverted U-­shaped relationship between under-
performance duration and public organizations' subsequent per-
formance improvements.
2.2   |   The Impacts of Overperformance Duration
We predict a U-­shaped relationship between overperformance
duration and public organizations' subsequent performance
improvements, which emerges from the shift in the relative
dominance of two opposing forces as the duration of overperfor-
mance lengthens.
At early stages of overperformance, feedback duration is ex-
pected to be negatively associated with public organizations'
subsequent performance improvements, arising from two
mechanisms that intensify as the duration extends. First, when
public managers perceive organizational performance as satis-
factory, they are more interested in maintaining the status quo
and gradually decrease the intensity of actions (e.g., innovative
search), resulting in performance stagnation (Wei et al. 2023;
Kim and Ko 2023). Second, and relatedly, public organizations
facing multiple goals often adopt the sequential attention rule,
under which they shift attention to the next goal when the per-
formance of the first goal is deemed satisfactory (Chen and
Jia 2023; Greve 2008). This process causes a decrease in priority,
which in turn engenders deterioration of future performance.
As overperformance repeats over time, public organizations are
better able to rule out random noise and confirm that their per-
formance has been satisfactory, thereby strengthening the influ-
ence of the above mechanisms.
Nevertheless, as overperformance continues over time, an op-
posing set of mechanisms gradually dominates, turning the
relationship between overperformance duration and public
organizations' subsequent performance improvements into a
positive one. Three mechanisms underpin this reversal. First,
continuous overperformance activates the positive momentum
effect, fostering optimistic beliefs about future performance
(Lehman and Hahn 2013). Public organizations increasingly
prioritize goals where they have confidence in further im-
provements, seeking relative advantages in interorganiza-
tional competition (Chen and Jia 2023) or enhanced reputation
(Zhu and Rutherford 2019; Holm 2018). Second, public organi-
zations tend to raise aspirations as overperformance prolongs,
which motivates them to further improve performance to
meet the elevated aspirations. According to the aspiration ad-
aptation theory, rather than remaining fixed, aspiration levels
are adapted dynamically in response to performance feedback

(Piening et al. 2021; Labianca et al. 2009). Particularly, when
public organizations experience sustained overperformance,
they are likely to engage in “striving comparisons,” bench-
marking the performance of organizations they aspire to
emulate to set striving aspirations (Labianca et al. 2009). In
addition, rising expectations from external stakeholders—
such as political superiors and citizens—also amplify the
upward adjustment of aspirations as public organizations
continuously achieve high performance (Mishina et al. 2010).
Third, prolonged overperformance accumulates organiza-
tional slack and autonomy (Meier et al. 2015), enhancing pub-
lic organizations' capability to further improve performance
by enabling them to engage in more intensified and distant
search (Yu et al. 2019), experiment with innovative but risky
solutions (Goyal and Goyal 2024; Nicholson-­Crotty et al. 2017;
Salge 2011), and absorb potential losses due to the experimen-
tations and explorations (Derayati 2024).
Consistent with our prior discussion, as our theory posits a di-
rectional change in the marginal effect of feedback duration,
which occurs continuously and smoothly rather than discon-
tinuously and abruptly, the proposed nonlinear relationship
aligns more closely with a U-­shaped pattern than other alter-
natives. Based on the above discussions, we have the follow-
ing hypothesis:
H2.  There is a U-­shaped relationship between overperformance
duration and public organizations' subsequent performance
improvements.

Notably, although not the primary focus of this study, our the-
ory can be extended to generate insights into the interactive
effects of performance feedback intensity and duration. As
an illustration, it is reasonable to infer that feedback intensity
will moderate the effect of feedback duration by strengthen-
ing the mechanisms through which feedback duration exerts
influences. Consequently, the (inverted) U-­shaped relationship
between performance feedback duration and subsequent perfor-
mance improvements is expected to be steeper as the intensity
of feedback increases. Further, we can conjecture that public
organizations' responses to a long duration of mild feedback
and a short duration of severe feedback are likely to differ. For
instance, sustained mild underperformance is more prone to
trigger mechanisms (learned helplessness, threat-­rigidity, and
strategic reprioritization) that dampen organizations' inclina-
tion to take action. In contrast, brief episodes of severe under-
performance are more likely to intensify the mechanisms of
“problemistic search” and blame avoidance, heightening moti-
vation to address performance shortfalls.
3   |   Empirical Context
This study draws empirical evidence from China, where, over
the past several decades, the central authority has come to rely
heavily on performance management systems to monitor and
manage local governments' implementation of crucial party-­
state policies (Chen  2025a). Since the 11th Five-­Year Plan,
China has made environmental protection a top priority in its
central agenda (Chen and Jia 2023). To support this shift, the
government has implemented various performance manage-
ment schemes to ensure local compliance with environmental
policies. We focus on one such scheme, China's official city air
quality ranking, which has unique features that render it ideal
for testing our theoretical claims.
As a vital measure to reinforce the central authority's commit-
ment to combat air pollution, China's official city air quality
ranking was first implemented in January 2013. The Ministry
of Environmental Protection (MEP) of China designed the
comprehensive air quality index (CAQI), a composite per-
formance index measuring the concentrations of six major
air pollutants (i.e., PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, CO, and O3). The
CAQI ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating
poorer air quality. Based on the CAQI, the MEP began to eval-
uate and rank the air quality of 74 cities on a monthly basis.

4   |   Research Design
4.1   |   Sample
This research uses city-­month as the unit of analysis. We focus
on the second batch of ranking, which was initiated in June
2018, as it is timelier and covers a wider range of cities than the
first batch. The final dataset contains monthly data from June
2018 to December 2023 for all 168 cities included in the air qual-
ity ranking. Of note, as of January 2024, the MEE reports no
longer include information on cities' CAQI, presenting only their
rankings. Therefore, the data from December 2023 is the latest
we can use.

4.2.2   |   Explanatory Variables
As noted earlier, a desirable feature of the ranking system is
that whether a city is underperforming or outperforming is
clearly defined by the “shame roll” and “honor list” provided by
the MEE. In other words, with this particular empirical case,
we fend off the potential problem of subjectivity (ambiguity) in
measuring appropriate performance aspirations (Nielsen 2014).
Based on this, we first developed two count variables, under-
performance duration and overperformance duration, and then
used the quadratic terms of these two variables as the key ex-
planatory variables.
Specifically, aligning with operations of previous studies
(Derayati 2024; Goyal and Goyal 2024; Yu et al. 2019), underper-
formance (overperformance) duration is measured by the num-
ber of consecutive months a city has been listed in the “shame
roll” of bottom 20 performers (“honor list” of top 20 performers)
until time t −1.6 The count of underperformance (overperfor-
mance) duration will be reset to zero each time a city is removed
from the “shame roll” (“honor list”).7
4.2.3   |   Control Variables
In addition to incorporating city and time (year–month) fixed
effects to eliminate potential biases caused by confounders that
vary only across cities and those only across time, we included
two sets of control variables in the analyses. The first set of vari-
ables accounts for meteorological conditions that are widely
found to influence air pollutant concentrations, including baro-
metric pressure, wind velocity, temperature, and specific hu-
midity (Chen 2024). These variables were measured by city and
month, with data obtained from the National Meteorological
Information Center of China. The second set includes five
variables that reflect cities' socioeconomic conditions. These
variables are gross regional product (GRP) growth rate, GRP
(logged), secondary industry's proportion in GRP, population
(logged), and budgetary fiscal revenue (logged). We obtained
the data for this set of variables from the China City Statistical
Yearbook.

6   |   Conclusion and Discussion
This research investigates the impacts of performance feedback
duration, a crucial temporal dimension of performance, on pub-
lic organizations' subsequent performance improvements. Our
theory predicts an inverted U-­shaped relationship between un-
derperformance duration and public organizations' subsequent
performance improvements, and a U-­shaped relationship for
overperformance duration. Empirical analyses using the case of
China's official city air quality ranking yield evidence support-
ing these predictions.
This study provides several significant contributions to the
field of performance feedback research in the public sector.
First and foremost, we depart from the prior literature that
focuses solely on the intensity of performance feedback and
bring attention to the temporal dimensions of performance,
which helps advance performance feedback theory in the pub-
lic sector. On the one hand, with its primary focus on perfor-
mance intensity, prior research has essentially adopted a static
perspective, which suffers from notable inherent limitations.
Incorporating temporal dimensions of performance feedback
enables us to move beyond the static perspective to a dynamic
one, which is indispensable to obtaining a deeper and more
complete understanding of public organizations' responses
to performance feedback. On the other hand, performance
encompasses multiple dimensions, and its effects cannot
be fully understood through performance intensity alone
(Derayati  2024). By systematically examining the relation-
ships between under/overperformance duration and public

organizations' subsequent performance improvements, this
research offers one of the first systematic efforts to address
this scholarly omission. More broadly, this research paves a
new path for the further development of performance feed-
back theory in the public sector by bringing in the lens of time.
Second, this research theorizes and empirically confirms the
curvilinear relationship between performance feedback du-
ration and public organizations' subsequent performance im-
provements, revealing the complexities in public organizations'
strategic behaviors in response to performance feedback. With
only a few exceptions (e.g., Nicholson-­Crotty et al. 2017), most
research in the field of public performance management fo-
cuses on linear impacts of performance feedback. This is par-
ticularly the case when it comes to the thread of literature that
concentrates on how performance feedback affects public orga-
nizations' subsequent performance improvements, which holds
the general view that negative (positive) performance feedback
exerts positive (negative) impacts (Wei et  al.  2023; Kim and
Ko 2023). Our research extends this literature by demonstrating
that the impacts of performance feedback could be complex, and
that “time” (duration) can shape public organizations' responses
to performance feedback in nonlinear ways.
Third, and relatedly, by introducing the lens of performance
feedback duration, we provide a perspective to reconcile in-
consistencies in the prior performance feedback literature. The
extant research exhibits divergent theoretical views regard-
ing the impacts of performance feedback in the public sector.
Regarding the impacts of NPF, one stream of research asserts
that public organizations tend to increase search activities to
solve the problem (Hong 2020; Salge 2011). In contrast, another
stream proposes a competing view that underperformance can
trigger rigid responses from public organizations, leading to a
decrease in search behavior (Van der Voet and Lems 2022). The
inconsistency remains, if not becomes more pronounced, when
examining the impacts of PPF. While some argue that public or-
ganizations that adhere to the “satisficing rule” possess a lower
motivation to take further strategic actions when facing PPF
(Kim and Ko 2023; Wei et al. 2023), others contend that overper-
formance motivates public organizations to increase strategic
changes to enhance reputation or maintain/achieve advanta-
geous positions (Chen and Jia 2023; Zhu and Rutherford 2019).
This research is among the first to acknowledge and address
these inconsistencies. Specifically, we propose that the relative
dominance of these seemingly conflicting mechanisms shifts
with performance feedback duration, which helps reconcile the
divergent theoretical views.
Notably, while our empirical setting is rooted in China's hier-
archical accountability system—where performance feedback
is transmitted top-­down through a centrally designed and en-
forced performance management scheme—the core theoretical
arguments (i.e., temporal dimensions of performance matter in
shaping public organizations' strategic behaviors, and feedback
duration can shape public organizations' responses in nonlin-
ear ways) are likely to extend beyond this context. This potential
for generalizability stems from the fact that the behavioral and
organizational mechanisms underpinning our theoretical argu-
ments are not institution-­specific; they have been documented
across diverse organizational settings, including private firms,
nonprofits, and public agencies in democratic systems. That
said, nuanced variations may emerge across different political
systems. For instance, in decentralized or pluralistic systems—
such as electoral democracies—public organizations face mul-
tiple, often competing accountability pressures (e.g., elections,
media scrutiny, interest group advocacy), which may attenuate
their sensitivity to performance feedback duration, leading to
less steep (inverted) U-­shaped relationships between (under-
performance) overperformance duration and subsequent per-
formance improvements. Moreover, political factors acting as
boundary conditions shaping the relationships differ across dif-
ferent political systems. In electoral democracies, factors such
as the intensity of electoral competition, distance to an election,
incumbent ideology, and changes in incumbency are expected
to serve as crucial boundary conditions. In hierarchical systems
(like China's), by contrast, top-­down political factors, such as re-
wards and sanctions from political superiors, policy priorities of
these superiors, and patron–client networks, are more likely to
play the key moderating role.9
The findings of this study carry significant practical implica-
tions. On the one hand, targeted interventions based on the
duration of under/overperformance are required. For organi-
zations in the early stage of underperformance—when mo-
tivation to address performance gaps is strong—supportive,
capacity-­building interventions (e.g., targeted funding and tech-
nical assistance) can reinforce improvement efforts, preventing
them from sliding into sustained underperformance. For those
in prolonged underperformance, more assertive, structural in-
terventions (e.g., mandatory improvement plans, leadership
accountability reviews, and diagnostic audits) are warranted,
paired with targeted support to counter demoralization and re-
build organizational capacity. As for organizations experiencing
overperformance: for those in the early stage, proactively com-
municating stakeholders' high expectations and providing nec-
essary support and incentives can prevent complacency, helping
them sustain overperformance and enter a state of positive mo-
mentum; for those in sustained overperformance, designing
more ambitious goals, granting greater autonomy, and encour-
aging the use of slack for innovation are recommended to lever-
age the positive momentum they have generated.
On the other hand, instead of delivering feedback at uniform and
fixed intervals regardless of performance trajectory—a common
yet suboptimal practice in public performance management—
an adaptive design of feedback frequency calibrated to perfor-
mance duration offers a more effective approach. For instance,
high-­frequency feedback is appropriate for organizations in the
early stage of underperformance, as it facilitates problem iden-
tification. However, for those in persistent underperformance,
problems are already well identified, and substantial perfor-
mance improvement requires structural changes that unfold
over extended time horizons. In such cases, reducing the fre-
quency of performance feedback is advisable—not only to align
with the longer timeframes for the structural changes but also to
avoid exacerbating learned helplessness and threat-­rigidity.
This research is an initial step toward a broader research agenda
that incorporates temporal dimensions of performance into perfor-
mance feedback research. Several limitations and corresponding
avenues for future research are worth noting. First, our empirical

analysis is grounded in a single case of environmental governance
in China, precluding us from empirically validating the generaliz-
ability. It should be intriguing for future research to further probe
the boundary conditions shaping the impacts of under/overper-
formance duration and conduct empirical investigations across di-
verse policy domains and political systems. Second, this research
focuses on public organizations' performance improvements, a
crucial aspect of organizational output, as the dependent vari-
ables. Future studies can further explore how under/overperfor-
mance duration affects the “intermediate inputs,” including both
ethical managerial decisions (e.g., innovative search) and uneth-
ical gaming responses (e.g., data manipulation). Third, we only
study one particular temporal dimension of performance feed-
back, i.e., the duration. Incorporating other temporal dimensions,
such as volatility/stability of past performance (Jung et al. 2024),
constitutes a promising avenue to further develop performance
feedback research. Fourth, this research only investigates the
effects of performance feedback duration. Future research can
further probe how the duration and intensity of performance
feedback can interactively affect public organizations' strategic
responses.10 Fifth, our empirical analysis relies on quantitative
methods. Future studies employing qualitative approaches would
be valuable, especially for exploring the mechanisms in greater
depth. Last but not least, as with most, if not all, prior research on
performance feedback, what we empirically establish in the pres-
ent study are essentially the short-­term impacts of performance
feedback. Whether, and more importantly, under what conditions,
performance feedback can drive lasting behavioral changes is a
promising research avenue worthy of future exploration.

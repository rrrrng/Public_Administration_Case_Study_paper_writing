Abstract
This article examines the relationship between social vulnerabilities and the attention paid to them by local governments. Drawing from collective
action and cognitive theories, we conceptualize planning as an organization-level inference process of selecting strategies to minimize gaps
between expected and desired futures. Responding to public administration scholars who have called for advancing theories of managing risks,
we examine whether local planning processes are responsive to community disadvantages and how the administrative arrangements and coor­
dination efforts of local governments influence this sensitivity. In doing so, we highlight a phenomenon labeled organizational divergence, in which
a collective’s perceived community disadvantages differ from reality. Examining climate action, economic development and sustainability plans,
combined with survey and spatial data on environmental burdens for 462 municipal governments, we find evidence, using a multiphase Bayesian
analytic strategy, that institutional designs which empower professional city managers increase the probability cities will engage in broader types
of planning but decrease the odds they will prioritize social vulnerabilities. However, these cities are more likely to minimize divergence relative
to mobility and water-related vulnerabilities. Conversely, greater functional coordination across governmental units increases the probability of
prioritizing vulnerabilities in planning but also increases organizational divergence.
Key words: collective action theory; local government; active inference; social vulnerability
Introduction
Local governments face significant community impacts from
increasing environmental variability and lingering urban disin­
vestment (Chu et al. 2023). These challenges present public
administrators with both probabilistic risks and lesser-understood
ambiguities, such as health, infrastructure, and economic threats
exacerbated by environmental changes (Anderies 2015). While
new tools and technologies for addressing social vulnerabilities
are proliferating through intergovernmental and inter-sectoral
collaborations, public managers are creatures of their own expe­
rience and normative domains of interest (March 1991; Simon
1947). A growing body of research suggests that even as
sustainability-related challenges become more salient to com­
munities, a majority of local governments remain tethered to
legacies of isolation or urban disinvestment—uncommitted,
inert or muddling through their responses to interconnected
economic, health and environmental dilemmas (Hawkins,
Krause, and Deslatte 2021; Homsy and Warner 2015; Park and
Krause 2020). Fundamentally, this reflects a challenge in under­
standing how the risk-perceptions of public administrators
aggregate to planning for complex challenges at collective scales
(Bullock, Greer, and O’Toole 2019; Howell-Moroney 2008;
Hughes 2020; Norman-Major 2011).
This article examines the relationship between such social
vulnerabilities and the attention paid to them within local
governments. To do so, we conceptualize organization-level
processes like planning as inferential risk-management efforts
aimed at minimizing gaps between expected and desired futures
(Ostrom 2009; Veissière et al. 2019). Public administration
scholars have long called for advancing theories of managing
organizational risks, which can be probabilistically understood
under bounded rationality assumptions (Bullock, Greer, and
O’Toole 2019; O’Toole and Meier 2015). Within organiza­
tional processes like planning, risk must be distinguished from
ambiguity, which presents greater uncertainty to decision-
makers and can be attributed to two sources (Bottemanne and
Friston 2021; Sykes 2022). The first source is the inherent com­
plexity of environmental dynamics. Managing risks in public
organizations depends on being able to quantify or estimate
the likelihood that a given policy or programmatic intervention
will have an intended effect (Bullock, Greer, and O’Toole
2019). However, the climate is a chaotic system with nonlinear,
slow-moving dynamics which can thwart action-to-outcome
relationships (Ostrom 2010; Swain et al. 2020). Moreover,
urban disinvestment and environmental injustices are multidi­
mensional problems which have stymied governments for
decades (Hawkins 2014, 2018). This presents challenges for
local government managers who are oriented through profes­
sional training and career incentives to focus on narrowly
framed or predictable problems (Nelson and Stenberg 2017;

Nelson and Svara 2015). The second source originates from
the uncertain actions of others. Collective action dilemmas stem
from the difficulties enacting binding commitments and the
incentives to free ride on the efforts of others (Friedman et al.
2024; Lee and Koski 2014; York et al. 2021). Fragmented
governance arrangements, characterized by a large number of
local governments in most metropolitan regions, extend such
dilemmas to the organization level, as individual local govern­
ments are seen to act alone or free-ride on other governments’
efforts (Ostrom 2010).
We contend that planning for needs like infrastructure or
social welfare reflect collective inferential efforts which neces­
sitate balancing analogous types of information-processing
tradeoffs between complexity and accuracy, ambiguity and
risk, or epistemic and pragmatic evidence-seeking (Pezzulo,
Rigoli, and Friston 2018). However ubiquitous these tradeoffs
may seem; extant research has not adequately assessed the suit­
ability of administrative arrangements or management pro­
cesses for navigating these tradeoffs, and thus, adaptively
identifying and planning to meet ambiguous threats. An anal­
ogous view is emerging in cognitive science and referred to as
active inference (Friston et al. 2017; Parr, Pezzulo, and Friston
2022). This framework essentially holds that all self-organizing
systems—including individuals and collectives—seek to sustain
themselves by developing and improving a Bayesian-like “gen­
erative model” of both current and future external conditions
(Bottemanne and Friston 2021; Parr, Pezzulo, and Friston
2022). By drawing from collective action theory, we adapt this
framework to study organizational responses to social vulner­
abilities exacerbated by environmental change and urban dis­
investment (Ostrom 2010).
Our study asks a central research question: how do local
government administrative arrangements and coordination
efforts influence the identification and prioritization of com­
munity disadvantages? The analysis follows an abductive line
of inquiry better suited for the increasing use of “big data” and
computational methods in social science (Brandt and Timmer­
mans 2021). An abductive inquiry starts from a surprising
empirical finding which lacks adequate theoretical explanation
and then involves using more flexible analytic strategies to
develop a “hypothesis on probation” which can explain the
quixotic results (Brandt and Timmermans 2021: 204). Given
the theoretical puzzle from prior research that cities in frag­
mented metropolitan regions make only halting progress
addressing vulnerabilities, our research question focuses on a
need for stronger theory linking planning and strategic man­
agement in local governments (Bryson, Edwards, and Van Slyke
2022; Vandersmissen and George 2023) and for how public
organizations assess and manage risks (Bullock, Greer, and
O’Toole 2019; O’Toole and Meier 2015).
To address our question, we construct a novel dataset of
climate action, economic development and sustainability plans
combined with survey and quantitative data on transit, work­
force, housing and water-related burdens. We then use a mul­
tiphase Bayesian analytic strategy to assess whether features of
administrative authority and coordination minimize a phenom­
ena we label organizational divergence, in which perceived
community disadvantages (the generative model) differ from
reality (the data generating process). We find evidence consis­
tent with prior research that governmental fragmentation in
metropolitan regions increases information costs and is asso­
ciated with lower prioritization of social vulnerability in plans
(Deslatte et al. 2024). Surprisingly, cities with institutional
designs empowering professional managers are less likely to
prioritize social vulnerability in plans, but they are more likely
to minimize organizational divergence relative to specific
infrastructure-related vulnerabilities. Conversely, greater func­
tional coordination across governmental units appears to
increase the probability of prioritizing vulnerabilities in plan­
ning processes while over-estimating housing-related burdens.
Integrating these insights, we theorize that the institutional
arrangements shape how local governments iteratively recon­
cile divergent generative models of the environment within their
administrative domains of authority (Deslatte et al. 2025).
The inferential nature of planning and
administrative domains
Many planning and administrative processes resemble collec­
tive and active inference in which organizations seek to reduce
surprise by either changing beliefs to minimize discrepancies
or changing observations to maximize evidence (Parr, Pezzulo,
and Friston 2022). In planning, this can appear as an effort to
minimize prediction error (updating beliefs about economic,
environmental, or social threats) or maximize evidence (col­
lecting data from surveys, cases, peer networks, or focus
groups) (Bryson 2016). Cognitive approaches like active infer­
ence hold that surprise is optimally minimized when an actor
can find the simplest explanation for data (minimizing com­
plexity) that reasonably accounts for observations (maximizing
accuracy) (Bottemanne and Friston 2021; Friston et al. 2017).
Tending to only half this equation can be problematic for sur­
vival. The reason this developing picture of the brain is poten­
tially valuable for public administration research is that
individuals and organizations can be motivated by an analo­
gous goal—to minimize future uncertainties (Constant et al.
2022; Hodson, Mehta, and Smith 2023; Smith, Friston, and
Whyte 2022).
At an organization level, planning can function to adjust pro­
grams, policies and effort through iterative, perception-action
feedback loops aimed at minimizing risks (Anderies 2015).
Extant research from local planning, public management and
environmental policy suggests that planning is capable of align­
ing goals and group norms (Hawkins 2018), and producing
positive outcomes (Brody and Highfield 2005; Burby and Dal­
ton 1994; Martín and McTarnaghan 2018; Vandersmissen and
George 2023). A key limitation of this research is its failure to
assess the suitability of planning processes for reducing uncer­
tainty (Jones 2002). For example, collective action theory has
established how structural and contextual constraints impede
the ability of groups to make optimal choices—which is analo­
gous to making accurate inferences about risks (Ostrom 2009).
In particular, administrative authority or discretion, organiza­
tional coordination mechanisms, and jurisdictional fragmenta­
tion are characteristics that can influence a phenomenon we
label organizational divergence, operationalized here as the
difference between assessments of environmental risks and their
reality. Essentially, organizational divergence is equivalent to
the potential for prediction error (or Bayesian surprise) in a
shared generative model, while planning reflects an iterative
effort to minimize it. We focus on two structural characteristics
that influence this process: the role of administrative authority
in maintaining discretionary domains of accepted activity; and
the capacities for coordinating within and across jurisdictions.

Administrative authority and domain maintenance
Administrative arrangements which place more authority or
discretion in the hands of a manager or chief administrative
officer (CAO) increase the likelihood that their own profes­
sional norms will guide inferential effort (Simon 1947).
Local governments in the United States and several other
federal systems organize their administrative structures differ­
ently, depending on the form of government they adopt. In the
United States, the council-manager form of government origi­
nated from the Progressive Reform movement (1900–1917) as
an outgrowth of calls for neutral, nonpartisan and profession­
alized administration of government. Today, the form is used
by roughly half of US cities (Svara and Watson 2010). A lasting
criticism of this form of government is that it functionally iso­
lated lower-income, racial and ethnic minorities from
decision-making (Bridges 1999; Simmons and Simmons 2004).
While the form has been significantly adapted over the years,
it still generally places hiring, budgetary authority and day-to-
day operational oversight of departments under the control of
a professional manager (Carr 2015; Frederickson, Johnson,
and Wood 2004; Svara and Watson 2010). Evidence suggests
local governments led by professional managers tend to expe­
rience less corruption (Afonso and Nelson 2023), greater bud­
getary solvency (Jimenez 2020), accountability and efficiency
(Wei 2022). They are also more likely to have populations with
higher incomes and education levels (Wei, Butler, and Jennings
2019). While the discipline emphasizes fairness as a profes­
sional ethic (McCandless and Ronquillo 2020), studies have
found inconclusive evidence for their responsiveness to equity
concerns (Carr 2015).
One way managers assess community needs is through plan­
ning processes which function as framing mechanisms for col­
lective inference (Kwon, Berry, and Jang 2013; Nelson and
Svara 2012). From this perspective, local governments develop
more sophisticated and interconnected “ecologies of plans” to
forge consensus or a shared generative model about develop­
mental goals, the sustainability of services or the resilience of
community groups (Brinkley and Stahmer 2021; Campbell
1996; Connerly, Stewart Chapin, and Higgins 2007; Vander­
smissen, George, and Voets 2022; Woodruff et al. 2018). Sus­
tainability, resilience, and climate plans, in particular, emerged
in the 2000s in the United States to pay greater attention to
environmental or social threats, but were not required to
address social vulnerabilities until after 2022 when federal pro­
grams authorized by the Inflation Reduction Act began empha­
sizing such foci (Deslatte, Siciliano, and Krause 2025; Woodruff
et al. 2018).
Expanding the scope of planning requires managers to
engage more diverse groups of stakeholders and departments,
such as planning, public works, transportation, or community
development (Krause and Hawkins 2021). It also entails bal­
ancing the potential gains from new information with the abil­
ity to “satisfice” by relying on prior knowledge, competencies
or experience within the organization (Simon 1947). For
instance, climate adaptation planning typically requires far
more data-collection and analysis of physical infrastructure
and social vulnerabilities than the traditional (and more famil­
iar) processes of capital improvement planning and business
recruitment (Woodruff et al. 2018).
City managers must also navigate policymaking-accountability
tensions and possess strong prior beliefs about the importance
of outcomes within their control: they prioritize programs or
policies which build or protect their professional reputations
(Teske and Schneider 1994), improve the fiscal positions of
their organizations (Jimenez 2020, 2013), and leverage their
career experience and training in public administration to
emphasize cost-efficiencies (Nalbandian 1999; Svara 1998). In
their generative model, planning can support risk-minimization
through fiscal health, credit ratings, citizen satisfaction, and
prudent, low-risk ventures into new or improved areas of ser­
vice delivery (Connolly 2018; Teodoro 2011).
While professionalized city management should support
developing strategies for addressing vulnerabilities when accu­
rately assessing the risks, managers also are trained to accept
the advisory bounds of their policy influence (Carr 2015;
Nelson and Svara 2015; Zhang and Feiock 2009). Evidence
shows city managers remain deferential to elected officials on
politically contested matters (Zhang and Feiock 2009). Herbert
Simon called this an effort to protect the organizational “equi­
librium …within areas of [discretionary] acceptance” between
constituent groups of employees, managers, policymakers and
citizens (Simon 1947: 123–153). Administration, in the words
of James D. Thompson, requires the management of this
“domain consensus” between interdependent constituencies in
the face of environmental uncertainty (Thompson 1967:
34–38). An example of this balancing act comes from land-use,
in which manager-led cities are more likely to support inclusive
or “sustainable” land-use planning which also provides clear
development benefits (Deslatte, Swann, and Feiock 2017; Haw­
kins 2014). Despite the potential for contested welfare goals
within organizations, we expect that local managers can gen­
erally maintain domain consensus through inference processes
which narrowly frame achievable and co-beneficial develop­
mental, social and environmental goals (Bullock, Greer, and
O’Toole 2019).
Functional and intergovernmental coordination
In recent years, collaborative forums and cohorts have emerged
for cities to exchange knowledge and experience across a vari­
ety of energy-efficiency, land-use, economic development, and
greenhouse-gas reduction initiatives (Brinkley and Stahmer
2021; Campbell 2016; Connerly, Stewart Chapin, and Higgins
2007; Deslatte et al. 2025; Deslatte Siciliano, and Krause 2025;
Hawkins 2018; Woodruff et al. 2018). This phenomenon illus­
trates how coordination across departments and governments
can play an important role in forming and updating shared
generative models. Active inference contends that agents in
collectives underwrite social learning and risk-minimization by
“thinking through other minds” or leveraging shared identities,
networks or “epistemic communities” they form (Albarracin
et al. 2022; Mercier and Sperber 2018; Veissière et al. 2019).
At the organization level, coordination between functional
units and governments work under the same efficiency-motivated
principle: doing so can save the time and effort of trying to
learn everything or develop risky innovations from scratch
(Deslatte, Siciliano, and Krause 2025; Veissière et al. 2019).
An important contextual consideration is that local govern­
ments in the United States and other federal systems exist in
fragmented or polycentric governance systems which increase
information and coordination costs (Hendrick 2011). This
jurisdictional fragmentation—the proliferation of governmen­
tal jurisdictions within metropolitan regions—can alter the
incentives to collaborate on complex problems, and thus, the

All collaboration involves some risk that hoped-for benefits
will not be realized (Hansen, Mullin, and Riggs 2019; Kim et
al. 2022). Collective action theory holds that greater jurisdic­
tional fragmentation can add uncertainty by increasing the
number of potential collaborators or competitors, and thus,
the costs of coordinating, negotiating and monitoring others
(Ostrom 2009). In more heavily fragmented regions, collective
action dilemmas emerge when the costs of collaboration moti­
vate governments to ignore problems, take half-measures, or
wait for higher authorities to address them (Homsy and Warner
2015; Jimenez 2014). Given the barriers to mobility across
jurisdictions (wealth, racial exclusion), fragmentation creates
incentives for governments to protect their exclusivity, pursue
short-term growth, or resist redistributive policies addressing
social needs (Howell-Moroney 2008; Kübler and Rochat 2019;
Sadler and Highsmith 2016).
Even when underwriting the costs of planning through col­
lectives, local governments must confront well-recognized
capacity and bureaucratic constraints (Hawkins, Krause, and
Deslatte 2021). Cities can face functional collective action chal­
lenges internally, when managing risks requires governments
to coordinate across departments or units with distinct service
responsibilities and different planning horizons (Krause and
Hawkins 2021). This can drive governments to try finding syn­
ergies across departments through formal coordination mech­
anisms, like “green teams” (Krause, Feiock, and Hawkins
2014), which may also lead to more divergent planning goals
by involving units tasked with more varied risk-management
responsibilities. Planning and public works staff, for instance,
might focus on physical infrastructure and land-use while com­
munity and economic developers may gravitate toward social
and economic challenges (Deslatte, Siciliano, and Krause 2025;
Krause and Hawkins 2021).
Given these information and coordination challenges, col­
laboration as a risk-management mechanism often functions
through networking with similar-looking “peer” communities,
oversampling from best practice or “success stories” and
under-sampling from failures (Bardach 2004). Much like moti­
vated reasoning in individuals, public agencies confronting high
information costs can oversimplify the nature of a problem
(stereotyping to minimize discrepancies with existing beliefs),
or more selectively sample new information (exploiting evi­
dence). This potential led Bullock, Greer, and O’Toole (2019)
to hypothesize that governments will manage risks less success­
fully when they are interorganizational or intergovernmental
in nature (Bullock, Greer, and O’Toole 2019).
This presents a theoretical puzzle: divergent individual beliefs
or experiences can create conflicts but also introduce diverse
viewpoints and experiences to organizational problem solving.
Refinement of these models through interaction and feedback
can lead to a more robust and adaptive collective generative
model for the organization. While structural features of admin­
istrative
authority,
functional
coordination
and
inter-organizational collaboration are all likely related to orga­
nizational divergence, there is a dearth of evidence to support
deductive hypothetical claims in this regard.
We expect that manager-led cities will be more likely to
engage in sustainability-related planning as a means for
framing and mitigating risks to existing services, economic
or fiscal conditions. However, given their need to maintain
consensus between organizational constituencies and the non­
random assignment of manager-led cities in regions, we
expect they will be less likely to prioritize more ambiguous
or longer-term social vulnerabilities, disadvantages or bur­
dens (Rivera and Knox 2023). In other words, manager-led
cities may be more focused on managing risks—easily quan­
tifiable and less politically contested planning challenges.
Moreover, structural features like the coordination mecha­
nisms between and within organizations should render an
organization more likely to engage in identifying a broader
range of uncertainties surrounding social vulnerabilities
(Rivera and Knox 2023). However, it may also introduce
divergent goals that can slow the ability of an organization
to develop strategies, implement them and evaluate
progress—to effectively learn and update the generative model
(Friston et al. 2017; Krause et al. 2019).
Given this theoretical puzzle over how structural features of
public organizations might work across purposes for establish­
ing, evaluating or updating organizational risk and uncertainty,
our research design detailed below follows an abductive
approach. We first examine features associated with prioritizing
vulnerabilities in planning processes, and then explore whether
administrative authority and coordination increase or decrease
organizational divergence surrounding specific types of social
vulnerabilities.
Analytic approach
The analysis proceeds in two phases which are integrated to
examine attention to vulnerabilities in planning and the struc­
tural drivers of organizational divergence. In the first phase,
we model the effects of organizational and environmental
characteristics on the prioritization of social vulnerabilities
in plans that US cities developed over a roughly 20-year
period (prior to the wholesale implementation of IRA pro­
grams). In the second phase, we explore the effects of admin­
istrative form, functional and intergovernmental coordination
on a measure of organizational divergence, calculated as the
difference between these perceived vulnerabilities derived
from our survey data and realized social vulnerabilities as
measured through federal data. We detail the data collection,
processing and modeling separately for both phases below
and elaborate on them in the supplementary material.

Phase 1: social vulnerability within plans
For the first phase, we combine administrative data from the
US Census Bureau, the International City/County Management
Association (ICMA) 2015 “Local Government Sustainability
Practices” survey and its 2014 “Economic Development” sur­
vey, and data from the White House Council on Environmental
Quality’s Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool
(CEJST), with a text corpus constructed from climate action
plans (CAPs), sustainability plans, and economic development
plans collected from respondent cities.

We collected all publicly available plans in 2022 (via
reviewing websites and contacting city employees) for munic­
ipalities that responded to both 2014 and 2015 ICMA surveys
(N = 462). Due to the previously noted tendency of cities to
adopt an ecology of plans and focus on overlapping themes
or topics within them (e.g., community development, infra­
structure, resilience, green jobs), we opted to use the plan
from each city with the highest proportion of text devoted to
social vulnerability and to control for the age of the plan in
the analysis. Because cities do not uniformly synchronize the
development or updating of plans, and because they operate
under planning procedures that may be dated, we pool
adopted plans over time as is standard practice in the urban
planning literature (Campbell 2016; Woodruff et al. 2018).
Given the shift in planning focus following the IRA passage,
we determined the appropriate study period should encom­
pass plans adopted between 2000 and 2022, which produced
a corpus including 276 cities and towns. In a handful of
instances where cities had updated existing plans during this
period, we included the newer plan (the percentage of cities
with each type of plan is reported in Table 2 below). The first
phase analysis pools these plans with the administrative and
survey data generated within this timespan. Due to missing­
ness in the survey, the number of observations drops to
N = 264.
Outcome measure: vulnerability topic proportion
Our phase 1 outcome is a proportion of text within each plan
devoted to the topic of social vulnerability. To derive a measure
of social vulnerability topic proportion, we used a seeded
sequential topic model followed by a dictionary-based
approach (see Supplementary material Appendix A for more
detail).
We used the “seedLDA” package in R to iteratively develop
seven known topics of interest (economic development, envi­
ronmental sustainability, resilience, climate action, community
development, and social vulnerability). Additionally, we
extracted 13 “residual topics” which are differentiated from
those that we pre-specified. We then extracted the top 100
bigrams (two-word terms) pertaining to social vulnerability
and estimated the vulnerability topic proportion by dividing

Burdens
We construct eight measures of burdens experienced at the
neighborhood level using the CEJST tool. This dataset com­
piles information reported by the US Census Bureau spanning
several decades (2000–2020), along with the National Insti­
tutes of Health, the US Environmental Protection Agency, and
other agencies, on numerous slow-moving social and economic
burdens faced at the neighborhood-level (US Census tract).
From 2023 to 2024, these burden themes were used to deter­
mine whether cities were eligible for federal climate-related
funding under the White House’s Justice40 Initiative
(Executive Order 14096 of April 21, 2023, “Revitalizing Our
Nation’s Commitment to Environmental Justice for All,”
repealed in January 2025 by President Donald Trump). This
classification scheme was two-pronged: whether a neighbor­
hood was at the 90th percentile or higher (with respect to the
state or nation) for an observed burden; and at the 65th per­
centile or higher for low-income population (below 200% of
federal poverty level). If it met both criteria, it was designated
as disadvantaged.
The specific burdens we include mirror the CEJST themes:
climate change burden, which captures features of the neigh­
borhood like flood risk and expected economic and popula­
tion losses from natural hazards; energy burden, which
captures household energy costs relative to income; transpor­
tation burden, which captures diesel exhaust in the air, trans­
portation barriers and congestion; housing burden, which
captures neighborhoods impacted by race-based “redlining,”
lack of green space, lead paint use, and housing costs; legacy
pollution burden, which includes proximity to contaminated
or hazardous waste sites; water burden, featuring modeled
exposures to wastewater discharge or releases from under­
ground storage tanks; health burden, which features neigh­
borhood rates of asthma, diabetes, heart disease and life
expectancy; and workforce burden, which captures education
levels, unemployment, and linguistic barriers. We aggregate
Census tracts to the city level for each theme and generate a
share of city population within disadvantaged tracts based on
this designation (see Supplementary material Appendix A for
details). For reasons we elaborate upon below, we then scale
the values for each burden from “0” to “3” with “0” indicat­
ing that the city is in the bottom quartile for burdens under
the federal classification and “3” indicating that the city is in
the top quartile.
Administrative form of government
There have been multiple critiques and alternative proposals
for measuring characteristics of the US council-manager versus
mayor-council form of government (Frederickson, Johnson,
and Wood 2004; Nelson and Svara 2012; Wei 2022). These
arguments typically focus on professional adaptations of
mayor-led cities (Frederickson, Johnson, and Wood 2004) or
political characteristics of city council structure (partisan vs.
nonpartisan elections; at-large or district-based elections) (Wei,
Butler, and Jennings 2019). We argue the essential feature of
our theoretical interest—the maintenance of organizational
domains by a professional manager—is captured by the nom­
inal council-manager form, which vests executive authority and
day-to-day departmental oversight with the city manager (Nel­
son and Svara 2015). Thus, we include a dichotomous measure
from the 2015 survey taking the value of “1” if the city relies
on a city manager and “0” otherwise.
Coordination and capacity
From the 2015 survey, we created a measure for functional
collective action (FCA) capturing the extent to which depart­
ments within cities coordinate on 11 sustainability and
resilience-related programs or policies. Because additive indices
can over- or under-weight items, we use the mirt package in R
to estimate a two-parameter Item Response Theory (IRT)
Model to create a weighted FCA scale. Similarly, we create an
IRT-scaled measure of institutional collective action (ICA) from
the 2015 survey capturing the extent to which cities coordi­
nated with other jurisdictions on 13 sustainability-related pro­
grams or policies. Because economic development often plays
a central role in city affairs, we use the 2014 survey to create
an IRT-scaled measure from eight items capturing the variety
of ED revenue sources available to cities. The IRT modeling
details and items for all three of these measures are included
in Supplementary material Appendix A.
Also from the 2015 survey, we create a measure for the
centrality of the administrative assignment of sustainability
staffing. We use a four-category ordinal scaled measure for
sustainability staffing coded “0” if the city reported no dedi­
cated staffing, “1” for dedicated staffing within a single depart­
ment, “2” for dedicated staffing across multiple departments,
and “3” for dedicated staffing in the chief elected or appointed
official’s office.
Fragmentation measures
We calculated both horizontal and vertical fragmentation mea­
sures using the 2012 US Census of Governments data (Hen­
drick 2011). Horizontal fragmentation is measured by totaling
the number of general-purpose governments per capita within
a metropolitan area. Vertical fragmentation is measured by
totaling the number of special purpose governments divided
by the total number of governments (special purpose and gen­
eral purpose).
Socio-economic conditions
Because the socio-economic and demographic conditions
of the location shape responses to social vulnerabilities
(Trounstine 2020), we include a series of socio-economic and
demographic control variables in the first phase collected from
the US Census Bureau through the CEJST data. These include
the share of the population that is Black, the percentage of the
population below the federal poverty level, and the size of the
city’s population logged.
Phase 2: organizational divergence
The second phase involves developing a measure that approx­
imates the divergence between community burdens and the
organization-level awareness of them. To provide more intu­
ition about this construct, active inference scholars sometimes
refer to the notion of Bayesian surprise (Parr, Pezzulo, and
Friston 2022), which is the difference between a prior and
posterior probability of observing environmental conditions or
states. Bayesian surprise can be quantified using a formulation
from information theory called the Kullback–Leibler (KL)

divergence, which is the statistical difference between two log
probability distributions (detailed in supplementary material
Appendix B). This KL divergence scores the potential magni­
tude of Bayesian belief updating that exists; in other words, it
approximates how well the generative model of the organiza­
tion fits the data it is trying to explain.
Organizational divergence outcome measures
Detailed in Table 1, we created four measures of divergence at
the organizational level by comparing two sources of data:
perceptual data on barriers collected through the 2014 eco­
nomic development survey; and quantitative measures of social
burdens drawn from the CEJST tool. In the first source, local
government survey respondents were asked to “indicate if your
local government faces the following barriers to economic
development and their importance” across a list of 21 potential
barriers. Reported in Table 1, we selected four of these barrier
items: “poor public transit,” “lack of skilled labor,” “high cost
of housing,” and “inadequate infrastructure (e.g., no fiber optic
cable, water, wastewater).” Each of these measures are scaled
from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating no barrier in this area and 3
representing a “high” barrier.
For the second source, we use four of the previously con­
structed CEJST burden scores for transportation, workforce,
housing and water. These four were selected because they have
a close corollary to the perceptual measures. For example, the
CEJST “workforce development” theme uses metrics for lin­
guistic isolation, poverty, unemployment and education, while
ICMA includes an item asking whether “lack of skilled labor”
is considered a barrier to development. We normalize the scale
of this CEJST measure (share of neighborhoods that are vul­
nerable), and then, discretize the value to match the perceptual
measures (0–3).
Because we are examining Bayesian surprise, we take the
difference between the perception of vulnerabilities and the
actual burdens. So, a respondent who indicated that a skilled
workforce was not a barrier (coded 0) would receive a work­
force divergence score of “−3” if the CEJST burden score fell
between the third quartile and maximum value (coded 3).
Thus, divergence is an approximation of the difference between
perceptual distributions of vulnerabilities and the reality. This
matching enables us to examine the organizational and con­
textual features associated with transit divergence, workforce
divergence, housing divergence, and water divergence, scaled
from −3 to 3 for each measure. The median for all four of
these measures was 1, indicating a majority of local govern­
ments only slightly over-estimated the levels of burdens. To
aid in interpreting findings, we use a multinomial logit esti­
mation strategy which requires collapsing these values into
three categories: underestimation (for scores of −3 and −2);
accurate estimation (−1, 0, and 1); and overestimation
(2 and 3). Descriptive statistics for all measures are reported
in Table 2.
Bayesian inference
We employ a Bayesian inferential strategy for both phases for
three reasons. First, public administration scholars have noted
Bayesian approaches are more appropriate for population-level
and administrative data (Gill and Meier 2000; Gill and Witko
2013). Second, Bayesian mechanics and probability theory are
the mathematical underpinnings of active inference in
neuroscience, in which the strength (or precision) of prior
beliefs always interact with new information (sensory inputs)
to estimate posterior probabilities (the generative model)
(Smith, Friston, and Whyte 2022). Third, Bayesian inference
allows for a more intuitive interpretation of findings through
probabilistic summarizations of the posterior distributions (Gill
and Witko 2013). This lets us surmise the probabilities or odds
of observing specific positive or negative relationships between
model parameters of interest. Beliefs in Bayesian inference are
expressed as probability distributions, and updated based upon
new observations as expressed in Bayes’ theorem:

Administrative arrangements and domain
maintenance
Our core focus is on how specific institutional arrangements
influence the accurate identification of disadvantage, or from
a collective inference lens, whether they minimize divergence
between organizational beliefs about disadvantages and the
reality in their communities. Abductively integrating the phases
of the study is central to answering our research question,
because the first phase helps us identify whether local govern­
ments are prioritizing vulnerabilities in planning and the factors
that drive this process; however, they give us no insight into
whether these governments are appropriately identifying or
responding to the actual levels of vulnerability in their com­
munities. The phase 1 outcome modeling (vulnerability pro­
portion in plans) are reported in Figure 2 and Table 3. Table
4 reports the results from the phase 2 burden overestimation
category from the four Bayesian multinomial logit models.
Because the distributions of our divergence measures skew
toward overestimation of burdens (and very little under-
estimation), we use accurate estimation of burdens as the
reference category. Thus, the reported posterior means and
probabilities summarize support for whether the model fea­
tures have positive (>0) or negative (<0) effects on overestima­
tion. Negative posterior means indicate model features are
lowering the probability of divergence while positive posterior
means are increasing it.
Relative to administrative authority, we find strong evidence
that council-manager governments were less likely to focus on
vulnerabilities in adopted plans (.97 probability). This may
reflect the nonrandom assignment of council-manager govern­
ments within metro regions, meaning that cities with more
educated, affluent populations were more likely to have that
form of government and less likely to prioritize or experience
social vulnerabilities. However, we find evidence in the second
phase that council-manager governments reduce divergence
relative to water-infrastructure related burdens (.96 probabil­
ity). We find weaker evidence they reduce divergence for transit
(.86) and housing burdens (.81). Thus, municipalities led by
city managers were relatively more accurate at identifying bur­
dens related to their infrastructure-related water and transpor­
tation vulnerabilities. This could reflect the community-wide
and even regional nature of these types of urban problems.
Among other structural variables likely affecting administra­
tive decision-making, we find only a .83 probability that having
dedicated sustainability staffing positively influenced a vulner­
ability focus and only a .76 probability that economic devel­
opment revenues negatively influenced vulnerability focus
(Table 3). While dedicated staffing also had no effect on diver­
gence (Table 4), we find that the availability of economic devel­
opment revenues increased organizational divergence relative
to water-related infrastructure (.95 probability) and workforce
burdens (.94 probability). Thus, more revenue sources for
development is associated with heightened attention or over­
estimation of these challenges.
Functional and intergovernmental coordination
Relative to coordination within and between local govern­
ments, functional coordination (FCA) on sustainability appears
to be the strongest driver of prioritizing vulnerability within
planning processes. Cities where more departments were coor­
dinating on sustainability efforts were more likely to prioritize
vulnerabilities (>.99 probability). However, we find a .99 prob­
ability that functional coordination actually increases
housing-related divergence and a .84 probability it increases
water-infrastructure related divergence. Integrated with the
phase 1 results, we conclude that functional collective action
increases organizational attention to specific elements of vul­
nerability which may have more localized or neighborhood-scale
salience even when the relative share of these burdens is lesser.
This would seem to line up with our findings that planning
processes were generally responsive to housing (.99 probabil­
ity) and energy burdens (.96) but not other measures of disad­
vantage. Energy- and housing-related burdens, which tend to
be more spatially clustered, were deemed a higher priority
during planning processes.
We find weaker evidence that intergovernmental coordina­
tion (ICA) reduced the focus on vulnerabilities in planning (.85
probability), reduced housing-related divergence (.86 proba­
bility), and increased water-infrastructure related divergence
(.84). Thus, these functional and intergovernmental coordina­
tion efforts appear to have some counteractive influences on a
vulnerability focus in planning and identifying housing-related
burdens, although the posterior means for FCA are larger for
both (meaning they don’t completely offset each other). This
result could reflect the transaction costs and tradeoffs govern­
ments make between coordinating internally and collaborating
externally, which has been hypothesized but not sufficiently
examined in the literature (Deslatte, Siciliano, and Krause
2025). Relative to these information and coordination costs,
we find vertical fragmentation, which is the proportion of
special-district governments to all local governments in a
region, appears to increase housing (0.92), workforce (1.00),
and water divergence (0.98), while horizontal fragmentation
increases workforce divergence (1.00). Generally, the literature
suggests information costs and asymmetries are more prevalent
in more fragmented regions, and our findings are consistent
with this view.
Finally, it is noteworthy that both functional and intergov­
ernmental coordination appear to increase organizational
divergence relative to water-infrastructure related burdens,
which puts the institutional arrangements guiding these
processes seemingly at odds with the influence of the
council-manager form of government—a recurring point we
revisit in our concluding section.
Discussion and conclusion
Our study presents a theoretical puzzle: the apparent inertia of
governments in addressing social vulnerabilities may originate,
in part, from divergent individual-level assessments of risks
that must be reconciled across different levels of an organiza­
tional hierarchy. Through an abductive analytic approach, we
find evidence that US cities were more responsive to some types
of risk-based vulnerability than others, and cross-cutting
administrative and functional coordination effects can lead to
more- or less-accurately identifying specific types of social bur­
dens. Specifically, city manager-run governments and greater

terms of identifying and prioritizing some specific social needs.
Our emerging “hypothesis on probation” is that managers
prioritize more easily quantifiable risks. However, institutional
mechanisms may allow local governments to iteratively and
adaptively reconcile divergent “generative models” of the envi­
ronment (based on normative beliefs, experiences, and organi­
zational roles) within their own administrative domains of
authority.
The makings of this puzzle are not novel. In her 1997
presidential address to the American Political Science Asso­
ciation, Elinor Ostrom labeled the failure to develop a
“behavioral theory of collective action” a central challenge
for political scientists (Ostrom 1998). “Thin” models based
on rational choice theory, she argued, were insufficient to
explain why collectives behave altruistically and with norms
of reciprocity in some social dilemmas and uncooperatively
in others. Moreover, structural factors such as group size and
heterogeneity, face-to-face communication, the ability to
defect, and information on past actions all seemed to interact
in patterned ways with behavioral features such as learning
heuristics, trust and reputations (Ostrom 1998, 2009).
Ostrom was also framing a contemporary challenge for public
administration: the need to cling to methodological individ­
ualism while also better accounting for organizational or
network-level outputs and outcomes (Jones 2002, 2017;
Moynihan 2018). This calls for linking microlevel and
mesolevel approaches to study both behavioral and structural
features that may co-determine organizational responses to
uncertain situations.

focus on the organizational level. Specifically, we argue that the
principle of surprise minimization underwriting active
inference—a formulation of predictive coding that extends the
goal of minimizing surprise to action, planning and adaptive
behavior—represents a potential advancement to rational choice
theory which can be extended to an organization or network.
This presents one way for public administration scholars to
bridge the micro-meso-macro divide which has hindered devel­
oping a more unified “behavioral theory” of organizational
choice (Jones 2017; Moynihan 2018; Ostrom 1998).
Organizational endeavors like planning must increasingly
confront environmental uncertainties which require increas­
ing predictive accuracy (or minimizing potential surprise).
Management and policy processes that are not adequately
adaptive to environmental conditions can fail to motivate
collective action when the proposed strategies or goals appear
unrealistic, uninformed about ground truths or overly aspi­
rational. Planning can fail to motivate collective action
among stakeholders when it does not adequately identify
necessary resources and provide feasible sequences of actions
for implementation and evaluation. In both scenarios, plan­
ners have failed to adequately receive and process informa­
tion and make realistic predictions about the future. In other
words, they have made faulty inferences about the efficacy
and desirability of future potential “states” or conditions,
and/or the policies or courses of actions required to realize
them. Plans are artifacts of inference. The content or focus
of planning is a manifestation of collective efforts to navigate
hierarchies of organizational goals, contextual constraints,
and epistemic-pragmatic evidence-gathering tradeoffs to
maintain an organizational consensus that approximates real­
ity. However, the value of extending an active inference
approach into public administration studies depends on
whether it can provide a more holistic understanding of how
public organizations effectively adapt to ambiguous threats.
Along these lines, our study has several limitations requiring
further inquiry. The first deals with the aforementioned chal­
lenge linking microlevel and mesolevel theory. While this study
is an organizational-level foray into collective inference, iden­
tifying the individual-level mechanisms which underlie organi­
zational divergence and the institutional arrangements which
shape it will require research at the microlevels, mesolevels and
macrolevels. Active inference makes a simplifying assumption
that decision-making is inference-based and motivated by the
imperative to minimize surprise; we argue this assumption can
be a useful augment to bounded rationality, meaning organi­
zational behavior previously assumed to be boundedly rational
may indeed be “adaptively,” or “more than” rational over time
and under specific conditions (i.e., institutional arrangements
which help to minimize prediction error). However, testing
such a claim in public organizational contexts requires model­
ing and measuring the information and priors’ individuals and
groups within organizations use, the information they overlook
or ignore, and the active efforts they make to develop, curate,
or mobilize new information. For example, our contradictory
findings for the influence of inter-governmental and functional
coordination on vulnerability planning could signal cities
making different epistemic-pragmatic tradeoffs in their

Because we pool planning documents over the study period,
the analysis is cross-sectional and causal inference is limited.
Nevertheless, we argue the analysis establishes a useful baseline

promotion of social vulnerability tools and assessment meth­

a potential turning point or “exposure” that merits ongoing

systems analysis will be needed to test why specific types of
organizational divergence are minimized over time moving for­
ward. Because we wanted to assess planning responsiveness and
divergence during what is effectively a pre-treatment period
(prior to the IRA implementation), we are also limited to survey
data which were already collected with instruments not designed
specifically to measure divergence. Thus, our measure is an
inexact proxy for capturing this phenomenon. Future studies
can improve on this design to better measure and monitor orga­
nizational divergence over time.

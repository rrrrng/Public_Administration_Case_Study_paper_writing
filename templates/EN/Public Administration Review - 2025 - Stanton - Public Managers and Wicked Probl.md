ABSTRACT
Over 50 years ago, Rittel and Webber published their seminal article on “wicked” problems that generate second-­order effects that
often limit or defeat ambitious initiatives. The Rittel-­Webber model, in all of the 10 distinguishing characteristics they specified,
closely fits dilemmas of public managers seeking to advance social equity. Analyzing the experiences of U.S. public sector social
equity initiatives through the Rittel-­Webber lens provides lessons about the wickedness of problems of advancing social equity,
and also about approaches to increase the chances of success. Taking multiple systematic smaller steps can be more effective and
sustainable than a single large initiative. A systematic all-­of-­government approach also might achieve significant progress in ad-
vancing social equity. To advance social equity, public managers must consider multiple stakeholder perspectives, and in today's
environment of polarization and heightened partisanship, they ignore lessons from the Rittel-­Webber framework at their peril.
1   |   Executive Summary
Over 50 years ago, Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber published
their seminal article, “Dilemmas in a General Theory of
Planning.” In it, they distinguished what we now call “tame”
problems from those that they considered “wicked.” In con-
trast to tame problems that are backed by consensus about the
nature of the problem and its solution, wicked problems lack
stakeholder agreement about both. The value of understanding
a problem as being wicked in the Rittel and Webber sense is that
the analysis directs attention not only to policy issues, but also
to the politics of how to gain stakeholder support and anticipate
second-­order effects. The Rittel-­Webber analysis provides im-
portant practical lessons for public managers about advancing
social equity, a multifaceted and traditionally difficult area of
American policy.
Stakeholder disagreement is especially likely on matters of pub-
lic policy. Different stakeholders may have fundamental differ-
ences about the causes and appropriate responses to problems
relating to race and poverty, for example. Moreover, the general
public may not see the linkage between a problem and solution
the way that policy analysts might. Thus, more whites might
be open to greater integration of schools than accept busing
as a solution. Similarly, although many white individuals may
support increased workplace integration, they might oppose
affirmative action as a means to achieve it. One major issue to
anticipate is the extent that some stakeholders may perceive a
particular social equity initiative to present a zero-­sum situa-
tion, whereby gains for one group are seen as coming at the ex-
pense of another, rather than a win-­win that benefits all groups
together.
Wicked problems are wicked because of how they defy easy
resolution. First, the public manager may face constraints in
pursuing the most effective solutions. Thus, the 1974 Supreme
Court decision Milliken v. Bradley, 418 U.S. 717 (1974), prohibits
integration of school districts across jurisdictional lines for de-
segregation purposes unless it can be shown that each district
deliberately engaged in a policy of segregation. This makes it dif-
ficult or impossible to integrate inner-­city schools with those of
the suburbs even if that would be an equitable solution. Second,

One sophisticated approach for addressing wicked problems
comes from Jeff Conklin, who recommends convening gather-
ings of multiple stakeholders, including those who initially may
not favor a particular initiative, not to achieve an ideal solution,
but rather to make progress in helping to achieve what he calls
“shared understanding” so that, “the stakeholders understand
each other's positions well enough to have intelligent dialogue
about the different interpretations of the problem, and to exer-
cise collective intelligence about how to solve it…Having a few
brilliant people or the latest project management technology is
no longer sufficient.”
Another approach comes from Theda Skocpol, who concluded
from her years of study of economic inequality that, “The his-
tory of social policy has a clear lesson. Programs that benefit all
citizens do more to reduce poverty than programs targeted to
the poor. So a new strategy for family security makes more sense
than another War on Poverty.” The same is true of race: link-
ing public policies to race diminishes support for those policies.
This logic led to the 2021 temporary expansion of the Child Tax
Credit, which enjoyed both policy and political success. Some
40% of children, and over half of Black children, were lifted out
of poverty as a result. While the temporary expanded Child Tax
Credit had been authorized for only one year, its demonstrated
success means that the program remains high on the priority
of congressional advocates of both parties. Another antipov-
erty measure, the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), enjoys bi-
partisan support. Again, this is a benefit for all eligible people
that provides considerable benefits to Black households as well
as white.
An important approach to wicked problems of achieving social
equity is to define and attack a piece of each problem that has
more of the attributes of a tame problem, that is, greater stake-
holder consensus on the problem and ways to ameliorate it. This
has many advantages, including fitting a reasonable solution
to available resources and stakeholder support. King County,
WA, has evolved from a department-­by-­department approach
into a coordinated and systematic all-­of-­government approach
to implementing improvements in social equity. The county set
implementation goals in key areas: (1) child and youth develop-
ment, (2) economic development and jobs, (3) environment and
climate, (4) health and human services, (5) housing, (6) informa-
tion and technology, (7) the justice system, and (8) transporta-
tion and mobility. At the federal level, the Biden Administration
also adopted an all-­of-­government approach. One benefit of such
a wide-­ranging approach is that, in contrast to taking a great
leap, each agency is proposing and implementing initiatives that
build on substantive knowledge—about the politics of an issue
as well as the policies—that they already possess. The final re-
sults of the Biden Administration initiatives will take time to
evaluate, especially given the likelihood of retrenchment after
President Biden leaves office.
The Rittel-­Webber analysis of wicked problems offers important
insights about difficulties that public managers can face in trying
to advance social equity as a part of their responsibilities. These
difficulties occur on multiple levels. In the policy realm, affected
individuals and groups may respond in ways that undercut the
effectiveness of social equity initiatives. On the political level,
difficulties include constraints imposed by actors such as courts
or legislatures as well as the real chance of backlash that not only
can overturn current initiatives but also threaten the careers of
public managers who are perceived as supporting them. The
Rittel-­Webber analysis is especially important in the way that its
exposition of the attributes of wicked problems can provide guid-
ance about how public managers can reduce the likelihood of
disruptive second-­order effects to advance social equity for their
constituents. To advance social equity, public managers must
address multiple wicked problems, and in today's environment
of polarization and heightened partisanship, they ignore lessons
from the Rittel-­Webber framework at their peril.

3   |   Wicked Problems as Public Managers Face
Them
Wicked problems are best understood by contrasting them with
tame and complex problems. Tame problems are those that can
be clearly defined and solved using structured, step-­by-­step ap-
proaches. Consider the steps in solving a tame problem such
as building a public building: determine specifications, obtain
funding, obtain a qualified vendor to do the building, and over-
see the construction process until completion. While each of
these steps may be difficult or complicated (e.g., Kassel 2010),
progress and success are measurable. The problem has bound-
aries and can be addressed with good management. In contrast
to such tame problems, complex problems involve issues of stake-
holder disagreement: while there may be consensus about the
nature of the problem, the proposed solution may be a point of
contention: if a community is expanding and a new suburb is
being developed, how should new infrastructure be funded? To
what extent should existing residents bear the cost of building
the new infrastructure? What kind of new infrastructure is ap-
propriate? These represent political as well as policy choices. A
wicked problem is yet more difficult to address than a complex
problem because of stakeholder disagreement not only about a
proposed solution but also about the nature of the problem it-
self. Rittel and Webber state that the definition of the problem
becomes clear only when there is a solution in mind: if a locality
contains neighborhoods largely segregated by race and income
of residents, who shall define the problem? Housing advocates
may see the problem as one of poor housing and may favor build-
ing multifamily housing in segregated neighborhoods while
others may see the problem as one of improving individual op-
portunity and may favor supporting residents who wish to move
to middle-­class neighborhoods (Chetty et al. 2016).
Stakeholder disagreement is especially likely on matters of
public policy. Different stakeholders may have fundamental
differences about the causes and appropriate responses to prob-
lems relating to race and poverty, for example. Moreover, the
general public may not see the linkage between a problem and
solution the way that policy analysts might. More whites might
be open to greater integration of schools, for example, than ac-
cepting busing as a solution. Similarly, although many white
individuals may support increased workplace integration, they
might oppose affirmative action as a means to achieve it.
Wicked problems may pose even greater problems for public
managers than the Rittel and Webber model suggests: while a
consensus may seem to emerge to implement a particular solu-
tion, that consensus may be unstable. Patashnik  (2008, 155)
explains: “It takes time for reforms to embed themselves in
governing routines, but time is a luxury reform advocates may
not have. Reform coalitions may hold power only momentarily,
giving opponents an opportunity to wait for the political winds
to shift.” He concludes that many of the reform examples that
he provides in his book “have been tales of disappointment and
dashed expectations…” It may be of only some comfort to public
managers trying to advance social equity that, “… even reforms
that have been seriously unraveled…often leave behind a signif-
icant residue of accomplishment” (161).
In short, even when consensus among stakeholders temporarily
emerges to define a solution, this consensus may mask significant
stakeholder disagreements that can result in a different consensus
about the nature of the problem and solution when circumstances
change. The current backlash against DEI, discussed below, may
be an example of what had been a temporary burst of support for
DEI, especially after the death of George Floyd, that now illus-
trates the transitory nature of agreement about at least some DEI
practices. The following schematic helps to clarify the typology of
tame, complex, and wicked problems (Table 1).

Organizations may struggle with the definition of the social
equity goals that they are trying to achieve. Is race the pri-
mary factor? Health or economic disparities? Gender issues?
LGBTQ+? As one social equity expert warned a group of pub-

compounded, as H. George Frederickson observed long ago,

impact of public services tend to mirror social, economic, and
political status, that is higher quality services go to those with
higher status.”
In other words, to return to the Rittel and Webber formulation,
it can be even more difficult to achieve agreement about a solu-
tion than it is to agree about the nature of a problem. This is
not merely a matter of definition. There are practical implica-
tions as well: consequences for failing to recognize the wicked
aspects of policies to achieve social equity, such as anticipating
the possibility of backlash, can be major, both for the cause of
social equity and potentially for the public manager as well. The
article turns next to applying the Rittel-­Webber Model to prob-
lems of social equity, and then to practical lessons about how
to make progress despite the constraints posed by the problems'
wickedness.
5   |   Applying the Rittel-­Webber Model to Problems
of Social Equity
Wicked problems are wicked because of how they defy easy
resolution. First, the public manager may face constraints in
pursuing the most effective solutions. Thus, the 1974 Supreme
Court decision Milliken v. Bradley, 418 U.S. 717 (1974), prohib-
its the integration of school districts across jurisdictional lines
for desegregation purposes unless it can be shown that each
district deliberately engaged in a policy of segregation. This
makes it difficult or impossible to integrate inner-­city schools
with those of the suburbs even if that would be an equitable
solution. To take another example, litigation following the re-
cent Supreme Court decision, Students for Fair Admission v.
Harvard, 600 U.S. 181 (2023), effectively ending affirmative
action in college admissions, threatens to expand to constrain
the ability of a public agency to make special efforts to im-
prove the diversity of its business partners or its higher-­level
career staff.
A second constraint that public managers face is the chance
that their actions may trigger a backlash (see generally, Eric
M. Patashnik 2024). Examples of backlash against equity ini-
tiatives abound, such as massive Southern resistance to the
landmark Supreme Court case of Brown v. Board of Education
(Brown v. Board of Education 1954) or, more recently, a 2022
change in administration in the Commonwealth of Virginia
that led to a reduced emphasis on race-­based equity and re-
placement of its Office of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion by a
Chief Officer of Diversity, Opportunity, and Inclusion, “with a
stronger and more focused role on promoting ideas, policies,
and economic opportunities for disadvantaged Virginians,
including Virginians living with disabilities and bringing
Virginians of different faiths together” (Brown 2024), or the
2024 Washington Post article titled, “The movement to diver-
sify Silicon Valley is crumbling amid attacks on DEI” (Nix
et al. 2024).
Third, the response of affected people may thwart reform ef-
forts. The classic example is “white flight” when white families
move out of neighborhoods undergoing integration, or when
white families remove their children from a public school sys-
tem that is undergoing integration and place them into private
schools. Fourth, organizational dynamics may thwart efforts
to improve diversity and inclusion. This occurred at many or-
ganizations that adopted DEI training programs that helped
to insulate them against legal challenges of discrimination
but often did little to promote inclusion and engagement of
a diverse workforce (e.g., Dobbin and Kalev 2016). In short,
“Wicked problems defy full understanding of their nature
and implications” (Danken et  al.  2016, 18). Especially given
the distinct dynamics of race relations and other equitable
disparities (see e.g., Schelling 1971), public managers need to
consider second-­order effects and unintended consequences of
any social equity measures that they propose. Is the proposal a
gesture or even counter-­productive, or will it actually advance
social equity?
Rittel and Webber's  1973 formulation of wicked problems in
social policy planning specified 10 distinctive characteristics.
The pattern of continuing great inequity in the United States,
as shown by a range of social indicators, makes social equity
a prime candidate to be considered through the Rittel/Webber
lens: to what extent does social inequity meet the 10 criteria that
Rittel and Webber posit to be distinguishing characteristics of a
wicked problem? Consider each of them in turn.
5.1   |   There Is No Definitive Formulation of a
Wicked Problem
This is a core attribute of a wicked problem: “The information
needed to understand the problem depends on one's idea for
solving it” (Rittel and Webber, 161; italics in original). The prob-
lem of segregated and inadequate housing is a classic example
of how a perceived solution leads policymakers and managers
to define the problem. Is the problem one of housing segrega-
tion or of inadequate housing quality? Given that the HUD-­
administered Section  8 housing voucher program has such
limited funding that it serves only about one in four eligible
recipients, how should that funding be allocated? To the very

Other social equity issues raise the same dilemma: policymak-
ers and public managers and their stakeholders need to pos-
sess agreement on a solution before they can conceptualize the
problem that they are trying to solve. Social welfare programs
seem to possess a long-­term tension in stakeholder perceptions
regarding whether beneficiaries should be trusted to seek their
own best outcomes or the extent to which outcomes must be
mandated. Some stakeholders, for example, support having the
government “supervise poor citizens” (Mead 1998, 97). For those
stakeholders, but not for others who may vehemently disagree,
social safety net programs would be improved by including work
requirements (Fording et al. 2011).
5.2   |   Wicked Problems Have No Stopping Rule
A wicked problem lacks “criteria that tell when the or a solu-
tion has been found” (Rittel and Webber, 162, italics in origi-
nal). For many social equity programs that are not entitlements,
the binding constraint may be availability of appropriations or
other sources of funds. Rather than the perceived problem being
resolved, a program runs out of “time, or money, or patience.”
For the public manager, a decline in stakeholder support, rather
than a general perception that a social equity problem has been
solved, may spell the end of the effort.
An illustrative example of the lack of a stopping rule in a
major social equity effort was the unsuccessful revitalization
of the low-­income segregated Sandtown-­Winchester section of
Baltimore. A 10-­year effort spending $ 130 million to build
or renovate about 1000 houses, and millions more to improve
schools, health outcomes, and other services, generated few
indicators of success. Policy analysts who studied Sandtown-­
Winchester concluded that, “Overall, the picture that emerges
from the data underscores the durability of social inequality
and the persistence of overlapping social problems in high
poverty and racially segregated neighborhoods” (DeLuca and
Rosenblatt 2013, 10). Former Mayor Kurt Schmoke, who had
led the revitalization effort, similarly noted that, “It was a real
challenge, because you still had high levels of poverty in an
area that had a significant infiltration of street-­level drug deal-
ers…In dealing with issues like that, you have no final victo-
ries, and you have to have people coming behind you to build
on your work” (Wenger 2015). Shortcomings and disparities in
the quality of public services such as primary and secondary
education, healthcare, and public safety, for example, show
similar intractability. Many of those problems interrelate and
would need to be addressed at the same time if resources were
available.
Another way of looking at advancing social equity as a problem
with no stopping rule is to recognize that there may be feedback
loops that add stability to a problem despite efforts to change
it. Racial residential segregation remains high because of its
dependence on the dynamics of social mobility patterns. There
are important feedback loops so that segregation operates “not
only as an outcome of economic inequality but as a structural
driver of this inequality” (Krysan and Crowder 2017, 254, italics
in original).
5.3   |   Solutions to Wicked Problems Are Not
True-­or-­False, But Better or Worse
Again, Rittel and Webber (163): there are no “conventionalized
criteria for objectively deciding whether the offered solution…
is correct or false…[Stakeholder] judgments are likely to differ
widely to accord with their group or personal interests, their
special value-­sets, and their ideological predilections.” While
success depends on a range of stakeholder measures, and thus
cannot be measured to stakeholders' satisfaction, failure is eas-
ier to recognize.
A major example of large-­scale failure was the effort by Housing
and Urban Development Secretary George Romney in the late
1960s to provide heavily subsidized housing for low-­income
and minority inner-­city residents. Real estate brokers used the
opportunity to frighten white residents into selling their homes
cheaply and moving to the largely white suburbs. They then in-
creased the prices and enticed poor black households to use gov-
ernment subsidies to purchase the homes, often only to default
soon thereafter. As the Washington Post's Leonard Downie, Jr.
noted soon after the termination of the programs that had led
to the debacle, “All the public money spent on housing [Romney
said] added up to ‘a $100 billion mistake.’ ‘The truth is,’ Romney
admitted just before resigning from HUD, ‘none of us are now
sure what are the right things to do’” (Downie Jr. 1974, 43; see
also Bradford 1979, and Taylor 2019). That failure, compounded
by harm from larger economic forces such as the loss of manu-
facturing jobs, blighted many cities for years to come (American
Assembly 2011).
Sometimes stakeholders may disagree even about program fail-
ure. While many analysts (Orfield and Stancil  2017) consider
the building of low-­income housing in segregated low-­income
neighborhoods to reinforce a harmful environment for resi-
dents, others support such housing as a way to promote commu-
nity development (Goetz 2018).
5.4   |   There Is No Immediate and No Ultimate Test
of a Solution to a Wicked Problem
Here, Rittel and Webber point to the importance of second-­order
effects and unforeseen consequences and the time that these
may take to show themselves. Indeed, “the next day's conse-
quences of the solution may yield utterly undesirable repercus-
sions which outweigh the intended advantages…In such cases,
one would have been better off if the plan had never been car-
ried out” (Rittel and Webber, 163). The failure of HUD Secretary
George Romney's efforts to provide housing for impoverished
center-­city residents might be one example of the destructive
impact of a well-­intentioned effort.
The considerable progress that social scientists have made
in assessing long-­term effects of social equity interventions
(Chetty et  al.  2016; Garcia et  al.  2020) would seem to con-
tradict this Rittel-­Webber precept. However, the problem

responded with a more equitable allocation of its audit resources
(Werfel 2023b).
5.5   |   Every Solution to a Wicked Problem Is
a “One-­Shot Operation”; Because There Is No
Opportunity to Learn by Trial and Error, Every
Attempt Counts Significantly
As Rittel and Webber note (163): “[E]very implemented solution
is consequential. It leaves ‘traces’ that cannot be undone. One
cannot build a freeway to see how it works, and then easily cor-
rect it after unsatisfactory performance…” The U.S. Department
of Transportation is implementing a multi-­year grant program,
the Reconnecting Communities Pilot Program, that recognizes
this issue. The Federal Highway Administration (n.d.) explains
the purpose of the program as trying to address the way that,
“Low-­income and minority Americans suffered disproportion-
ately from the destruction and division in their neighborhoods
[from the building of interstate highways in the 1950s and 1960s],
resulting in economic, social, and mobility challenges that per-
sist today” (Federal Highway Administration, “Reconnecting
Communities and Neighborhoods,” n.d.). Even this effort comes
far too late to try to reconstruct communities and the residents
whose lives were disrupted by the original flawed program.
5.6   |   Wicked Problems Do Not Have an
Enumerable (Or an Exhaustively Describable) Set
of Potential Solutions
Reviewing again the Sandtown-­Winchester housing effort, one
is hard pressed to determine what the appropriate solutions
might be. There is a myriad of potential improvements to the
multimillion-­dollar effort, especially with the benefit of hind-
sight about the solutions that were unsuccessfully tried. The
same is true for public managers who seek solutions to over-
coming major disparities between Black and white households,
such as with respect to household wealth: In 2022, the median
Black household possessed wealth of $44,900; the median
white household held wealth of $285,000, or over six times that
amount (Aladangady et al. 2023). If poverty is a major conse-
quence of America's racial history, what solutions can help to
overcome that disparity? Or life expectancy: as of 2021, Blacks
had a significantly shorter life expectancy at birth (70.8 years)
than whites (76.4 years) (Hill et al. 2023). Potential solutions are
indeed innumerable given multiple likely causes of this dispar-
ity, such as findings from a Century Foundation report that doc-
uments disproportionate mortality rates of Blacks compared to
whites, including high Black maternal pregnancy deaths, deaths
from illness such as cancer and heart disease, and childhood
deaths from gun violence (Taylor 2019).
In other words, the wicked problems of advancing racial equity
are so great that multiple approaches would be needed at the
same time to try to offset the disadvantages that have accrued
over time: “Racial inequities in the United States are…cumu-
lative and reinforcing, [including] outcomes in health, educa-
tion, employment, environmental risk, occupational status, and
crime…[With some exceptions] these differences compound ex-
ponentially to generate a cycle…that continues generation after
generation” (Gooden 2015, 39).
5.7   |   Every Wicked Problem Is Essentially Unique
As one careful analysis of major community initiatives concludes,
“Evaluators caution that it is not advisable to make cross-­site
comparisons [of community-­level initiatives] because all com-
munities are different” (Congdon et al. 2021, 41). Communities
tend to adjust initiatives to meet perceived local needs, and these
adjustments make it difficult or impossible to compare either
initiatives themselves or their outcomes (Congdon et al. 2021,
4). Given the multiplicity of disparities in circumstances across
communities and individuals and the multiplicity of potential
problems that underlie those circumstances, “it is hard to know
if the outcomes of the initiative were a result of factors unique to
its implementation (in a particular place and time and with par-
ticular groups of people) or if the initiative would achieve those
outcomes in other places.” The Rittel-­Webber conclusion seems
sound that “one can never be certain that the particulars of a
problem do not override its commonalities with other problems
already dealt with” (165, italics omitted).
5.8   |   Every Wicked Problem Can Be Considered to
Be a Symptom of Another Problem
Here, an illustrative example is public education and the dispa-
rate outcomes of Black and white students. The problem of lack
of readiness for college can be considered to reflect each of many
distinct problems that face many more Black than white chil-
dren and that build on one another. Gary Orfield lists almost two
dozen problems in a Black child's life, “a sequence of obstacles,”
(Orfield 2022, 48) as he calls it that lead to a major disparity in
access to college and successful college completion, compared to
whites. These obstacles occur at multiple stages (54):
•	 Early in life (e.g., greater incidence of developmental issues
and untreated medical conditions; low quality preschools),

•	 In elementary school (e.g., low quality teachers and
instruction)
•	 At the high school level (e.g., schools unable to teach essen-
tial study, research and writing skills; frequent interruptions
from unstable living conditions; frequent discrimination
from teachers and staff), and
•	 In general college preparation (e.g., schools often lack ad-
vanced classes, adequate counseling services and suitable
connections to colleges).
As Orfield concludes, “These are not problems that can be cured
by a single national policy…Unequal preparation for college
starts in homes, neighborhoods, the experiences of students,
and the highly unequal offerings from educators and others who
support their learning.” (192).
5.9   |   The Existence of a Discrepancy
Representing a Wicked Problem Can Be Explained
in Numerous Ways. The Choice of Explanation
Determines the Nature of the Problem's
Resolution
Looking at Orfield's list through the lens of trying to solve the
wicked problem of lack of access to college, one can see the
validity of Rittel's and Webber's point: each of these multiple
obstacles can be seen as an aspect of a different underlying
problem. To which of the underlying problems should a pub-
lic manager allocate the limited available resources? Early
childhood experts propose preschool solutions (e.g., Garcia
et  al. 2020), researchers documenting the value of desegre-
gating poor families propose those solutions (e.g., Bergman
et  al.  2024), and attorneys may favor anti-­discrimination
approaches.
5.10   |   The Social Planner Has No Right
to Be Wrong (i.e., Planners Are Liable
for the Consequences of the Actions They
Generate)
The issue of racial inequities has long been politically fraught
and managers in both the public and private sectors may be
caught in the cross-­fire. As Williamson et al. (2011, 26) note,
“Opposition is concentrated on resentment of perceived federal
government ‘handouts’ to ‘undeserving’ groups, the definition
of which seems heavily influenced by racial and ethnic stereo-
types.” DEI programs are a case in point. After the murder of
George Floyd, organizations increased their adoption of DEI
and other social equity measures. Yet, only a few years later
DEI is under attack, often by well-­funded adversaries, and often
reflecting political partisanship. Social equity efforts, and DEI
in particular, can be attractive targets for well-­funded backlash
that builds on such sentiments. Universities have been a primary
target of anti-­DEI attacks. In addition, numerous states have en-
acted laws banning or limiting the teaching of DEI at schools
or universities (Bolstad 2024; Adams and Chiwaya 2024). In re-
sponse, many organizations have reduced, downplayed, or ended
their DEI efforts (e.g., Patel 2014; Whoriskey and Mark 2024;
Guynn 2024). As always in cases of backlash, public managers
can suffer personal consequences when programs that they have
been managing come under attack.
6   |   Increasing the Public Manager's Chances of
Success
Danken et al. (2016, 18) summarize scholars' conclusions about
ways to address wicked problems: (1) there must be consistent
collaboration across organizational boundaries, including multi-
ple government agencies, involvement of external stakeholders,
and networked governance (Eggers and Kettl 2023). (2) Given
the importance of public leadership, leaders must develop new
capabilities such as negotiation and mediation skills to help bal-
ance the interests and concerns of diverse stakeholders (see also
Conklin 2006; Allen 2017). Leaders also need to understand that
wicked problems tend to become chronic and must be worked on
continuously.
What then are guidelines that can help public managers to ad-
dress wicked problems of social equity? While social equity
initiatives have the potential to cause difficult consequences,
the benefits for each public manager's constituencies and for
the United States as a whole can make a well-­considered effort
worthwhile.
6.1   |   Anticipate Consequences and Shape
Initiatives Accordingly
While public managers may implement their social equity ini-
tiatives in a unique context, there have been many lessons over
the years, often based on painful experience, about types of
second-­order effects that may occur in response to a particular
kind of program. In addition to lessons from the experiences
of public managers, there is a growing body of useful social
science analysis that can help to shed light on the strengths
and limitations and value of particular approaches. One
major lesson is that public managers need to anticipate con-
sequences and shape their initiatives accordingly. Patashnik
suggests that policymakers and public managers can, “‘stress
test’ their proposals and have on their teams a devil's advocate
who tries to think hard of ways that policies could fail to gen-
erate a favorable impression on voters due not only to poor de-
sign and execution but also as a result of political sabotage…”
(E. M. Patashnik 2019, 57).
The result of such a “stress test” may be that the public man-
ager resists a far-­reaching initiative in favor of a lesser one.
This can be difficult when social equity constituencies feel the
accumulated hurt of centuries of discrimination and exploita-
tion and are unwilling to forego making a clear statement of
the injustice and harm caused by that history merely because
of the way that that statement might not tactically be helpful.
Needed, as in other aspects of addressing wicked problems of
advancing social equity, is to recognize the trade-­offs implicit
in such a position.
One major issue to anticipate is the extent that some stakehold-
ers may perceive a particular social equity initiative to present
a zero-­sum situation, whereby gains for one group are seen as

coming at the expense of another, rather than a win-­win that
benefits all groups together. The problem is compounded when
an initiative requires a large initial outlay but provides net pos-
itive benefits only over the long term. Replacing lead drinking
water pipes is one such example; investing in high-­quality early
childhood education is another.
6.2   |   Building Stakeholders' Shared Understanding
of a Wicked Social Equity Problem and Approaches
to Address It
While Patashnik focuses on the issue of backlash, the ap-
proach can be generalized to anticipate broader consequences
of proposed responses to a wicked problem. Conklin  (2006)
suggests convening diverse stakeholders, including those ini-
tially opposed to an initiative, not to seek an ideal solution,
but to foster “shared understanding”. This means ensuring
stakeholders comprehend each other's positions well enough to
engage in intelligent dialogue about varying interpretations of
the problem and to harness collective intelligence for solutions.
According to Conklin, “having a few brilliant people or the
latest project management technology is no longer sufficient”
(Conklin 2006, 29).
Conklin's process for creating “shared understanding” reflects
the view that stakeholders do not always perceive the world
similarly and often see divergent realities with respect to policy
challenges. To increase the chance of shared stakeholder under-
standing, public managers must design policy inquiry, evalua-
tion, and analysis processes that elicit and record these multiple
realities. The data gathered is essential for constructive dialogue
and discussion among the groups affected by policy proposals
and decisions. If equity is included as a goal tied to outcomes,
the public manager should aim to encourage an equitable un-
derstanding of policy effects. This requires the public manager
to assess the environment of those affected and engage in dia-
logue to gain a better understanding of how policy choices im-
pact citizens' lives.
Since differences in perspectives on a policy issue are largely
influenced by one's position relative to that policy, a precon-
dition for creating shared understanding is stakeholders' will-
ingness to listen and understand different viewpoints. Not all
public managers can engage stakeholders in seeking shared
understanding. Public managers must acknowledge the role
of inherent bias in decision-­making. Everyone brings a value-­
laden lens to their analysis, affecting decisions about which
perspectives to include. While there is no strict rule on whose
voices must be included in addressing problems of social eq-
uity, public managers should seek to include a broad range
of perspectives present within and outside the community
served.
Conklin addresses multiple perspectives through what he
calls “Dialogue Mapping.” The public manager assembles rep-
resentatives with varied viewpoints on a wicked problem. A
“Dialogue Mapper” charts each participant's comment on a
whiteboard, showing its relationship to previous comments.
This technique allows everyone to see how their contributions
fit into the overall conversation, promoting coherence and
collaboration. The dialogue mapper actively listens and sum-
marizes the evolving conversation on the map, validating it
incrementally so group members “accept and own the map
as a faithful representation of their thinking” (Conklin, 49).
This process helps participants understand the larger wicked
problems that they are grappling with and move towards a
solution, creating “shared cognition” that offers a path to im-
plementation. Achieving shared understanding is challenging
and time-­consuming but potentially can address social equity
problems more sustainably than a public manager otherwise
might achieve.
Sharma and Sabharwal's  (2025) Conceptual Model of an
Inclusive Leader in Public Organizations identified essential
skills needed by public sector leaders to set the foundation
for an inclusive organization (159). This model moves beyond
traditional studies that focus on the leader's impact on the or-
ganization by expanding their analysis to encapsulate the ten-
dencies necessary for the organization to hold a social justice
and collaborative orientation towards the community served,
as well as to outside stakeholders. These traits of individual
leadership include humility, empathy, emotional intelligence,
ethical conduct, and cognitive complexity. This model postu-
lates that if these traits are imbued by leadership within the
organization, it sets the parameters for a socially conscious
and collaborative approach to policy making by agencies.
Although wicked policy challenges may not be fully addressed
by any one agency or public official, the training of our public
sector professionals by schools and programs of public policy
and administration can strive to incorporate these durable
skills.
6.3   |   Frame Social Equity Initiatives in General
Terms Rather Than as Targeted to Benefit
Specific Groups
Harvard Professor Theda Skocpol (2000) concluded from her
years of study of economic inequality that, “The history of social
policy has a clear lesson. Programs that benefit all citizens do
more to reduce poverty than programs targeted to the poor. So a
new strategy for family security makes more sense than another
War on Poverty.” The same is true of race: “linking public poli-
cies to race is detrimental for support of those policies.” (English
and Kalla 2021).2
This logic led to the 2021 temporary expansion of the Child Tax
Credit, which enjoyed both policy and political success. Some
40% of children, and over half of Black children, were lifted out
of poverty as a result. While the temporary expanded Child Tax
Credit had been authorized for only one year, its demonstrated
success means that the program remains high on the priority
list of congressional advocates of both parties. Another antipov-
erty measure, the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), enjoys bi-
partisan support. Again, this is a benefit for all eligible people
that provides considerable benefits to Black households as well
as white. The trade-­off is that a general antipoverty measure
costs more than a targeted one that helps a specific group people
might care most about.

6.4   |   Take Multiple Systematic Smaller Steps to
Help Mitigate Hazards Posed by Wicked Problems.
Multiple Systematic Small Steps May Be More
Effective and Sustainable Than Trying to Take One
Large One
An important approach to a wicked problem such as achieving
social equity is to define and attack a small piece of the problem
that has more of the attributes of a tame problem, that is, greater
stakeholder consensus on the problem and ways to ameliorate it.
This has many advantages, including fitting a reasonable solu-
tion to available resources and stakeholder support. Also, given
the nature of a wicked problem, it is more likely that an unfore-
seen complication will occur with a major initiative than with a
smaller one.3
On the other hand, the public manager may find that the pros-
pect of small gains provides little inspiration or benefit. King
County, WA, has avoided this dilemma by evolving from a
department-­by-­department approach into a coordinated and
systematic all-­of-­government approach to implementing im-
provements in social equity. “Prior to the first Determinants of
Equity report, we were all just off doing individual, atomized
efforts but we had no overarching strategy, but creating the
county's equity strategic plan was the real turning point” recalls
Michael Jacobson, Deputy Director of the King County Office
of Performance, Strategy & Budget (Personal communication
September 5, 2024). Led by County Executive Dow Constantine
and the County Council, the county initially integrated Equity
and Social Justice concepts with the county's strategic plan
(Valenzuela 2017). The county then developed a baseline of in-
dicators with respect to 13 different elements such as access to
affordable safe housing, early childhood development, and com-
munity and public safety. This baseline then could be used to de-
termine the county's progress in improving social equity (Beatty
and Foster 2015).
The county formally established its Office of Equity and Social
Justice in 2015 and in 2016 issued its Equity and Social Justice
Strategic Plan 2016–2022. The Strategic Plan set implemen-
tation goals in key areas: (1) child and youth development, (2)
economic development and jobs, (3) environment and climate,
(4) health and human services, (5) housing, (6) information
and technology, (7) the justice system, and (8) transportation
and mobility. The Strategic Plan also set standards for making
progress in each goal area. In 2023 and 2024, the King County
Auditor's Office reviewed progress made by the Office (renamed
the Office of Equity, Social, and Racial Justice) and key County
departments, finding progress in multiple areas as well as as-
pects that called for improvement. The all-­of-­government ap-
proach that King County adopted rested on a broad stakeholder
consensus both about the problem of social equity and about the
multifaceted solutions being implemented.
At the federal level, the Biden Administration also has ad-
opted an all-­of-­government approach. The White House issued
Executive Order 13985 (2021) on January 20, 2021, stating the
Administration's policy of implementing, “a comprehensive ap-
proach to advancing equity for all, including people of color and
others who have been historically underserved, marginalized,
and adversely affected by persistent poverty and inequality.”
The Administration required agencies to develop and imple-
ment Equity Action Plans (available at www.​perfo​rmance.​gov/​
equity), and followed up with a second Executive Order 14091
(2023) on February 16, 2023, requiring federal departments
and agencies to establish Equity Action Teams, “to coordinate
the implementation of equity initiatives and ensure that their
respective agencies are delivering equitable outcomes for the
American people.”
In only 3 years, federal departments and agencies achieved no-
table gains. The Biden Administration's February 2024 White
House Equity Action Plan Progress Report notes 630 separate
initiatives, some large and others more modest. Among them,
•	 HUD is restoring its regulations on Affirmatively
Furthering Fair Housing, requiring states and localities
receiving HUD funding to disclose annually the extent of
their residential segregation and their plans for reducing it,
among other provisions (U.S. Department of Housing and
Urban Development, n.d.).
•	 The Administration is implementing a Justice40 initiative,
setting a goal of delivering 40% of “the overall benefits of
certain climate, clean air, clean water, and other federal in-
vestments to disadvantaged communities that have histor-
ically been left behind or overlooked. Over 500 programs
across 19 federal agencies are being reimagined and trans-
formed through the Justice40 Initiative to maximize ben-
efits to disadvantaged communities” (White House Press
Release, February 14, 2024).
•	 The Administration is implementing a provision of the
Bipartisan Infrastructure Law that provides $15 billion in
funding specifically dedicated to replacing lead service lines,
along with an additional $11.7 billion in general-­purpose
funding through the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund,
which can also be used for lead pipe replacement.
One benefit of such a wide-­ranging approach is that, in contrast
to taking a great leap, each agency is proposing and implement-
ing initiatives that build on substantive knowledge—about the
politics of an issue as well as the policies—that they already
possess. The final results of the Biden Administration initiatives
will take time to evaluate, especially given the likelihood of re-
trenchment after President Biden leaves office, but the model
does represent an impressive approach to making progress in
addressing the wicked problem of reducing social inequity in the
United States.
7   |   The Rittel-­Webber Analysis in the Larger
Context of Public Policy Problems and Approaches
The Rittel-­Webber analysis has been increasingly applied to a
wide range of policy problems, perhaps even too much so (Peters
and Tarpey 2019). Indeed, some commentators have extended
the analysis to define “super wicked problems” as applying to
especially pressing matters such as global climate change (Levin
et  al.  2012). Finally, the Rittel-­Webber analysis is sometimes
conflated with other concepts such as “messes” (Norton 2012;
Ney and Verweij 2015), which may be considered as the larger
context from which problems are extracted and addressed, or


## Page 10

Public Administration Review, 2026
55
as “systems of external conditions that produce dissatisfaction
among different segments of the community” (Dunn  2012).
Moreover, “Messy, or poorly structured, problems are character-
ized by a very incomplete understanding how options are linked
to outcomes and by intense conflicts over values” (Metlay and
Sarawitz 2012, 7). Dunn issues a similar warning: “…recognition
of the interdependency, subjectivity, artificiality, and instability
of problems alerts us to the unanticipated consequences that may
follow from policies based on the right solution to the wrong
problem” (Dunn 2012, 71, italics in original).
Of relevance to the practitioner is how analysts who may take
a different conceptual view of wicked problems seem to agree
on the importance of addressing them by considering the views
of stakeholders and moving incrementally (Vincente et al. 1997)
rather than trying a “big bang” approach that disregards stake-
holder perceptions and values and likely second-­order effects
(Norton 2012). Thus, Roberts (2000, 14) contends, as does the
present article, that, “The real challenge is to help [stakeholders]
to build a community of interest where none existed before” (see
also, Ney and Verweij 2015).
In their insightful book, Bridgebuilders: How Government Can
Transcend Boundaries to Solve Problems (2023), William Eggers
and Donald Kettl provide concrete examples of success when tal-
ented leaders bring a wide range of stakeholders together to col-
laborate and coordinate an effective response to problems such as
the need for homelessness prevention and workforce development:
The collision of new boundary-­spanning problems
with boundary-­bound organizations helps explain
why so many problems seem so wicked. It also explains
the large and growing importance of bridgebuilders,
who can connect the players with the problems to be
solved and make the whole much more than the sum
of those parts (Eggers and Kettl, 47).
The question then becomes whether or to what extent issues of
social equity, and race in particular, are more intractable than
other wicked problems. Given the difficult history of U.S. race
relations (Stanton 2020), such a conclusion might be warranted.
The extent of the problem can be seen in the seminal address of
Samuel L. Myers, Jr., as President of the Association for Public
Policy and Management (APPAM). He pointed to the impor-
tance of including race in public policy analysis: “…policy anal-
ysis education, training and scholarship could be enhanced by
a more conscious infusion of analysis of race [and] the analysis
of race can be improved by a more thorough infusion of policy
analysis” (Myers Jr. 2002, 185). His observation was especially
striking given the long prior history of race relations as a major
public policy question. Similarly, and much later, a Brookings
Institution analysis (Briggs and McGahey  2022, 3) contended
that, “…to advance the work and make it endure, we must change
our political culture and grow a broader, more resilient base of
political support beyond the brave leadership of racial justice
advocates.” The present article seeks to respond to these chal-
lenges and contribute to greater understanding of the practical
and policy issues facing public managers as they seek to advance
social equity and ways to include a broad range of stakeholders
in the effort.
8   |   Conclusion
The Rittel-­Webber analysis of wicked problems offers import-
ant insights about difficulties that public managers can face in
trying to advance social equity as a part of their responsibili-
ties. These difficulties occur on multiple levels. In the policy
realm, affected individuals and groups may respond in ways
that undercut the effectiveness of social equity initiatives. On
the political level, difficulties include constraints imposed by
actors such as courts or legislatures as well as the real chance
of backlash that not only can overturn current initiatives but
also threaten the careers of public managers who are perceived
as supporting them. The Rittel-­Webber analysis is especially
important in the way that its exposition of the attributes of
wicked problems can provide guidance about how public man-
agers can reduce the likelihood of disruptive second-­order
effects to advance social equity for their constituents. To ad-
vance social equity, public managers must consider multiple
stakeholder perspectives, and in today's environment of polar-
ization and heightened partisanship, they ignore lessons from
the Rittel-­Webber framework at their peril.

Abstract
The current study contributes to practice in interagency performance manage-
ment through a study of the system of federal grants awarded by the Department
of Housing and Urban Development to local Continuum of Care (CoC). The mixed
methods design synthesizes a multivariate analysis of the relationship between
grantee performance and funding levels, a national survey of CoCs and follow-up
interviews with CoC administrators. Findings reveal little association between
reported improvements in system-level performance and annual funding amounts
awarded to CoCs. Nevertheless, interviews reveal that the system has fostered
commitment to use of data and performance management practices. The study
suggests that intergovernmental performance systems should address issues of
capacity and be more attuned to the context in which grant recipients work to
attain measurable goals. In particular, the value of the performance system is less
a carrot/stick relationship between principals and agents, and more a communica-
tive opportunity for federal entities and localities regarding local achievements
and constraints.
Evidence for practice
• Performance management systems, in addition to promoting accountability,
play a communicative role, aligning priorities across agencies within complex,
multilevel service delivery systems.
• External factors, including capabilities and commitment of network actors, mar-
ket forces, accountability to multiple principals, local context, and the regulatory
environment, may affect organizational incentives and ability to improve mea-
sured performance outcomes.
• Grantors should promote capacity building in grantee organizations and their
sub-recipients through technical assistance, peer-to-peer learning, and capacity
funding.
• Learning in performance management systems requires timely and clear feed-
back and sufficient time to adapt.
• Performance management systems may create administrative burdens, particu-
larly for smaller organizations with less capacity or organizations serving a large
geographic area and should include opportunities for grantees to provide feed-
back about performance management systems to ensure that metrics, priorities,
and process are locally relevant and reflect local conditions.
INTRODUCTION
Among the nation’s most important challenges is the goal
to make homelessness among individuals and families a
rare, brief, and nonrecurring experience in all communi-
ties. A cornerstone of the federal government effort to
achieve this outcome is the Department of Housing and
Urban Development (HUD) program to provide homeless
assistance grants to local Continuum of Care (CoC) organi-
zations. In the mid-2010s, HUD implemented a system of
performance measurement and reporting for grantees
that required collection of data on system-level outcomes

CoCs

the requirements in CFR Title 24, Part 578, which also
defines the responsibilities of a CoC (US Code of Federal
Regulations, 2017). Among the provisions in the Homeless
Emergency Assistance and Rapid Transition to Housing
(HEARTH) Act, signed into law on May 20, 2009, are
requirements that all grant recipients monitor and report
system performance measures (SPMs). This requirement
has been in place since 2012, when HUD issued an
interim program rule, and HUD continues to coordinate
and provide support to CoCs for this effort (HUD, n.d.). In

SPMs annually through the HUD Homelessness Data

(i) length of time a person remains homeless, (ii) the
extent to which persons who exit homelessness to per-

become homeless for the first time (HUD, 2015). HUD
reports progress nationwide on an annual basis to Con-
gress and maintains the data submitted by CoCs at its
public website (HUD, 2024a, 2024b).
While HUD has made strides in advancing perfor-
mance standards in its grants to CoCs, the program faces
obstacles in improving performance, including both the
gravity of the national problem of homelessness and
the complexity of the programs that have evolved to
address it. This paper reports on an assessment of the
HUD CoC performance system. The assessment drew
from the literature on performance management to iden-
tify key contributors to successful implementation and
surveyed CoC applicants regarding their experience with
the program. It provides a “ground-level” perspective on
both the value of using performance measures to signal
federal priorities, and the capacity and environmental
constraints that local agencies confront in trying to
improve performance.
THEORY: CHALLENGES TO COLLABORATIVE
INTERGOVERNMENTAL PERFORMANCE
Performance management systems designed to connect
government decision-making to strategic planning and
performance metrics are now common at all levels of
government (Gerrish, 2015; Harty, 2006; Munteanu &
Newcomer, 2019). At the federal level, the statutory
framework for performance reporting is the 2010 Govern-
ment Performance and Results Act (GPRA) Modernization
Act which requires establishment of agency performance
goals, common federal performance indicators, and regu-
lar reporting (GPRA Modernization Act, 2011). Perfor-
mance management systems have also proliferated at the
state and local levels, with a wide range of approaches to
the collection of data and its use in decision making
(Ammons, 2022; Behn, 2003; Melkers & Willoughby, 2005;
Moynihan, 2008). Models vary in their approach to the
use of data, and the extent to which they embody a focus
on financial incentives for control purposes to ensure
accountability. For example, the “Managing for Results”
approach (Moynihan, 2006) aims to connect strategic
planning within management processes through system-
atic collection and utilization of evidence. In theory, the
approach would both increase managerial autonomy and
accountability, although it has often been the case that
managerial reforms lag the institution of performance
measurement
(Moynihan,
2006).
“PerformanceStat”
reforms, such as the controversial New York City Comp-
Stat policing reform, focus heavily on motivating, learn-
ing, and improving, employing a review of real-time data
on goal attainment through engagement of performance
management teams in regular meetings (Behn, 2014). In
contrast to the models that place measurement at the
forefront, other models emphasize the use of incentives
(Heckman
et
al.,
2002;
Heinrich,
2002;
Musso
&
Weare, 2019). From a principal-agent perspective, pro-
gram improvement requires incentives to change behav-
ior
and
align
agency
activities’
performance
with
organizational
goals.
Examples
of
such
approaches
include performance contracting and the performance-
based budgeting experiments used in federal budgeting
through the GPRA Modernization Act and at the state
level (Heinrich, 2002; Ho, 2018; Lu & Willoughby, 2015;
Melkers & Willoughby, 2005; Moynihan & Kroll, 2016).
While a large literature documents the design and
implementation of performance management systems,
the evidence of success is mixed, in part because many
systems are poorly implemented, and in part because the

performance management approach links strategic plan-
ning to data collection and managerial decision making,
tracking system performance management metrics to
support evidence-based decision-making at the project,
program, agency, and legislative levels (Moynihan, 2008,
p. 39). A successful system performance system requires a
clear set of measurable outcomes or outputs, goal clarity,
and the technical capability to gather information about
outcomes and use data, requisites which many agencies
do not possess (Radin, 2012; Wilson 2000; Poister 2010).
As a result, adoption too often has a compliance flavor,
producing information that is not connected to manage-
ment procedures or is maintained at a level of gover-
nance in which managers lack the authority to adjust
resources (Radin, 2012; Musso & Weare, 2019; Kroll, 2015).
In a systematic review of the literature, Kroll (2015) finds
that six factors facilitate effective use of performance
data, including measurement system maturity, stake-
holder involvement, leadership support, support capacity,
innovative culture, and goal clarity. Musso et al. (2024)
find that success in implementing a performance system
can involve complicated configurations of factors that
include quality metrics, strong leadership, strategic plan-
ning, innovative culture, analytic capacity, and organiza-
tional capacity.
A related challenge, particularly in the intergovern-
mental context, is the difficulty of aligning the incentives
of agencies with those of political principals. While there
is evidence that financial incentives within performance
systems can induce behavioral changes, even well-
intentioned incentives can create perverse outcomes. For
example, the short-run nature of many performance sys-
tems prioritizes short-term gains, which may come at the
expense of long-term benefits (Heckman et al., 2002).
High-intensity incentive structures provide a strong moti-
vation for compliance which could lead to improved out-
comes but also may promote gaming and conflict over
the interpretation or use of performance metrics that
result from ambiguity and require significant investments
in performance information production and oversight. A
successful system must balance the benefits of strong
and
weak
incentives
structures
against
their
costs
(Musso & Weare, 2019). Sanctions that are too strict can
result in gaming or impose prohibitive costs, while pro-
grams that do not provide any financial incentives may
fail to communicate programmatic priorities.
Multi-agent performance management
Another limitation is that many studies of performance
management focus on single agencies or hierarchical
organizations (Musso et al. 2022). This leaves unanswered
how to ensure performance in the highly devolved and
collaborative organizations that increasingly characterize
public service provision. Indeed, performance manage-
ment, a key element of the new public management
(NPM) movement, is in tension with the devolution of
governance that is also associated with NPM. The estab-
lishment of expectations that governments “manage for
results” by aligning incentives and performance metrics
date to the late 1980s and early 1990s, with the NPM
movement that emphasized a more “business-like” and
accountability-focused approach to the management of
public
agencies
(Andrews
&
van
de
Walle,
2013;
Berman, 2007; Berry & Brower, 2005; Moynihan, 2006,
2008). This focus on accountability led to development of
performance management systems across levels of gov-
ernment (Moynihan, 2008; Poister, 2010). At the same
time, NPM emphasized increased competition through
devolution, privatization, and contracting out, which as
observed by Agranoff (2008, p. 315) had the result that:
“Today’s governmental units vertically (federal, state, and
local) and horizontally (interlocal/public and nonpublic
organizations) manage by engaging a host of linkages
with other entities. The result is a myriad of cross-agency
networks, partnerships, consortia, alliances, joint ventures,
contracts, and other collaborative ventures.” This raises
the question considered in this essay: how to ensure
effective performance management systems given the
complexity
of
devolved
governance
that
frequently
involves interagency and intergovernmental networks.
The challenges to implementing performance systems
compound when performance goals move into the more
networked governance structures that have evolved in
the past four decades. Foundational to NPM is a focus on
institutional reforms such as privatization, contracting out
for service provision and public–private partnerships that
increase institutional complexity (Andrews & van de
Walle, 2013; Berry & Brower, 2005). These institutional
reforms seek to increase efficiency through increased
competition, co-production, and the achievement of
economies of scale and scope. Coming on the heels
of devolution that began with the Reagan administration,
increased reliance on contracting for service provision has
resulted in programmatic arrangements that involve com-
plex networks of intergovernmental and intersectoral
actors. As Berry and Brower (2005) argue, these net-
worked approaches to service delivery raise thorny gover-
nance issues, including concerns about transparency and
accountability, institutional identity, challenges to execu-
tive leadership, and diffused responsibility for social
safety net services. A particular challenge to program
improvement is that any learning or improvement must
be at the network level, not merely at the organizational

More successful performance regimes have tended to
be within more hierarchically organized organizations,
which involve less goal conflict than multi-agent collabo-

within

For example, the most well-known “PerformanceStat”

contrast,
within

arrangements for service delivery, there are multiple
agents, often bound together with a combination of for-

principal–agent relationships. This institutional complexity
increases coordination costs and can lead to political and
organizational goal conflict. As Metzenbaum (2009) points
out, there has been too little attention to the possibility
that incentives coupled with measurement systems may
be important means of communicating federal goals
within such complex intergovernmental service systems:
Interestingly,
intergovernmental

In a study of collaboration among and within federal
agencies, Choi and Moynihan (2019, p. 1539) frame the
challenge of multi-agent action as “collaborative perfor-
mance management,” defined as “sharing of resources
and information among different actors for the purposes
of achieving a formal performance goal.” They point out
the tension between collaboration, which tends to be
outward facing, and the need for an internal agency-focus
on performance management goals. Goal salience and
participation in goal setting are associated with success,
but goal salience can be diffuse and participation lacking,
particularly as collaborative networks become more com-
plex. In an analysis of data from a survey of federal
agency managers, the authors find that investment in an
intra-agency performance system is negatively associated
with interagency performance, while goal salience and
participation in goal setting are positively related with
both interagency and inter-agency collaboration in per-
formance management. They conclude: “A fundamental
tension between performance management and collabo-
ration is that performance systems typically reinforce
agency goals, but are less adept at encouraging the solv-
ing of wicked problems that no single agency has com-
plete responsibility for” (Choi and Moynihan, 2019,
p. 1541).
This paper contributes to the emergent literature and
current practice regarding interagency performance man-
agement by assessing performance systems in the com-
plex network of agencies that implement HUD’s CoC
homelessness assistance grant program. As a recent
report by the National Academy for Public Administration
(NAPA) describes, the CoC system involves both vertical
and horizontal networks of federal, state, and local actors.
The report observes: “It is probably safe to say that even
the most knowledgeable local actors cannot get their
arms around the entirety of the resulting system–if a sys-
tem is the right word–for dealing with homelessness”
(NAPA, 2024, p. 8). Within this system, there is high level
of attention paid to performance metrics despite the diffi-
culty of making inroads on the intransigent problem of
homelessness (Musso et al., 2023).
METHODS
This study utilizes a multi-method approach that incorpo-
rates performance and grant-in-aid data from HUD, as
well data from a nationwide survey of CoCs, semi-
structured interviews, and content found on CoC websites
in the public domain. The nationwide survey of CoCs con-
tained a 51-question survey instrument with seven sec-
tions covering CoC structure, leadership, stakeholder
interactions, analytic capacity, culture, access to data,
influence of HUD guidelines, strategic planning, and con-
straints. The research team administered the survey using
Qualtrics.to email it to 413 CoC Collaborative Applicants
(CAs)—the points of contact between HUD and the
CoCs—listed on the HUD Exchange Grantee Contacts list.
Survey invitations were sent in three successive waves in
2022 on October 14–18, October 20–26, and November
4–14 to obtain responses through December. After the
second wave, 73 CoCs were selected at random and
called to request they complete the survey. A follow-up
email was sent on March 8, 2023, to CoCs that had yet to
respond, and additional survey responses were collected
through March 23, 2023. In the last wave, 20 CoCs with
high unsheltered homeless counts received additional
outreach, and HMIS leads and other points of contact
listed on the HUD list were contacted via email and
phone.
In total, 114 CoCs submitted a partially or fully com-
plete response to the survey representing a 30% response
rate. In one case, a CA submitted a response on behalf of
11 CoCs that were counted as a single CoC for sample

for 95% of the total in Fiscal Year (FY) 2022, are referred
to as Tier 1 grants for renewal projects awarded to con-
tinue ongoing operations. While CoCs are required to
upload performance data on an annual basis, the extent
to which these grants might be influenced by perfor-
mance data is unclear. There is greater scrutiny of
reported performance in the competition for the remain-
der of funds, awarded as Tier 2 grants, for new program
activities that address HUD’s policy priorities. But, Tier
2 grants represented only about 5% of the total amount
in FY 2022 (HUD, 2023).
Considering the weak relationship between SPMs
and grant funding, the study nevertheless found broad
acceptance among CoCs of HUD’s performance manage-
ment system. Three in four, or 77%, of the CoC survey
respondents reported their organization has adopted a
multiyear strategic plan. Approximately four in five, or
83%, have adopted measurable goals, while more than
half, or 55%, responded that they reached a high level of
consensus among stakeholders around their goals. Four-
fifths, or 81%, align new and renewal service projects to
their strategic goals in project prioritization decisions,
referred to as the Rank and Review process, during the
development of the annual NOFO application (Musso
et al., 2023, pp. 28–30). Many interviewees stated that
they were “trying to always increase our outcomes and
system performance measures because that’s how we’re
going to get funding” (Interview, major city CoC). Sev-
eral CAs interviewed suggested that setting measurable
goals can also lead to new CoC program initiatives
focusing on local and national priorities. For example, a
chief program officer noted that their CoC board aligned
with HUD, explaining:
…equity has been a major priority…. We just
applied for and received 2 new grants under
DV (Domestic Violence), for funding for our
community… And then, as far as alignment
with health and homelessness, we are work-
ing with the different health care providers in
our community (Interview, major city CoC).
Moreover, HUD priorities are key elements in CoC strate-
gic plans, performance dashboards or scorecards, and
annual progress reports. The vast majority (86%) of survey
respondents review and track progress in system and/or
project performance using HUD SPMs or similar measures
they developed (Musso et al., 2023, p. 34). The survey
responses and interviews suggest that the practice of
goal setting and performance measurement has taken
hold in many CoCs, partly in response to HUD require-
ments for strategic planning and performance reporting.

Grantees as network actors
While there is evidence of progress, the complexity of the
CoC system provides ongoing challenges to implementa-
tion of HUD’s performance management system. In a study
of CoC governance, Mosley (2021) found that many CoCs
are hampered by this governance system, experiencing
challenges related to capacity constraints, conflicting
visions of appropriate service approaches within the net-
works, and differing emphasis on service subpopulations.
The complexity stems in part from the fact that the system
can be seen as one of networks within networks. There is
the federal-local administrative network, consisting of HUD
and the CoCs that coordinate planning, project prioritiza-
tion, and performance reporting for the federal grants.
Also,
HUD
regional
offices
and
technical
assistance
(TA) providers participate in this network. TA providers,
under contract with HUD, provide training and technical
support in such areas as addressing the needs of a diverse
population, reentry services for individuals exiting the car-
ceral system, and the provision of trauma-informed care
(HUD, 2022a, 2022b). At the local or regional level, CoC
organizations coordinate networks consisting of nonprofit
service providers and other local, that is, city or county, or
regional governments. Respondents reported that home-
less service providers and government organizations make
up 98% and 82%, respectively, of the committees that
focus on performance management, while other commit-
tee members include social service providers (68%), advo-
cacy groups (55%), and faith-based organizations (46%).
Among survey respondents, 50% reported that their orga-
nization was structured as a public–private collaborative,
meaning that the entity involves at least one nongovern-
mental entity, but is housed separately from the govern-
ment (e.g., as a nonprofit or a joint-powers agency). The
second-most commonly reported arrangement (27%) is to
house the CoC within a local government agency such as a
county housing department.
The CoCs act as mediating entities who manage up
to federal grant-makers while also coordinating horizon-
tally, and down and through service provider networks.
In other words, the CoC is in a position of being both
principal (to local service providers) and agent (to HUD),
which may pose challenges as the CoC balances com-
peting priorities. As one CoC Program Manager put it:
“HUD does a good job of signaling its priorities in the
NOFO, but how it translates into the community is
another story. The CoC must balance HUD priorities with
the Board priorities.” (Interview, largely suburban CoC).
In some CoCs, there are service providers who do not
wish to take state or federal funds nor participate in
reporting their performance. An additional source of
complexity has to do with variability of funding across
regions. In some smaller CoCs, most services are funded
through HUD, but in larger CoCs, HUD grants may be a
relatively small share of the local homelessness service
budget. This is particularly true for the major city CoCs
that serve most of the nation’s population experiencing
homelessness.
For
example,
while
New
York
City
received $170 million in 2023 through the HUD CoC pro-
gram, its total homelessness services and rental assis-
tance budget was $3.5 billion (Haramis, 2023; US
Department of Housing and Urban Development, 2024a,
2024b). Where there are multiple funding sources, CoCs
must balance differences in reporting requirements
across funding entities. For example, several CoCs noted
that a larger share of their funding came from state or
local taxing sources which do not have similar or, in
some cases, any reporting requirements. This situation
can have the effect of dampening service agency incen-
tives
to
devote
effort
to
HUD-required
reporting.
Respondents also emphasized that smaller or rural CoCs
struggle with service provider capacity. As a CA stated, “I
think most rural CoCs would say… that it’s really tough
to get the data when you have limited service providers”
(Interview, largely rural CoC). Despite these wide differ-
ences in geography and funding streams, all CoCs must
maintain the same level of reporting, and there is little
flexibility given by HUD to explain differences in local
context in annual grant applications. A common sugges-
tion among respondents was that HUD reporting include
more opportunities to explain local contextual chal-
lenges surrounding to data reporting.
Communication and culture
Although there is limited evidence of a clear link between
reported performance and funding levels, interviews with
CAs in several cities nevertheless revealed a sense that
the CoCs took federal reporting requirements seriously
and asserted to their peers within a CoC that poor perfor-
mance could put their funding at risk (2023). This sug-
gests that performance measures do play a role in
helping to shift a mindset among CoC members that
encourages aligning federal and local priorities. These
findings suggest a pattern of mutual reinforcement,
wherein the grant competition draws attention to the
need to measure and manage performance. The majority
of CoCs do establish measurable goals, and interview
respondents by and large reported that they align local
level program goals with HUD policy priorities and perfor-
mance measures over time.
Still, respondents noted some issues related to com-

they received scores on their applications, HUD staff typi-

difficulty debriefing a HUD app, you know, mirrors the dif-

Still others felt that HUD’s performance management
apparatus did not always capture efforts CoCs made in
addressing homelessness: “I prefer not to look at our pro-
grams and their success just through the measures that
HUD wants us to, because I don’t feel like that’s the full
story of what’s going on” (Interview, largely suburban
CoC). For example, a respondent stated that, in addition
to SPMs, they track monthly utilization at the project level
(i.e., percent of voucher holders leased up and percent of
voucher holders matched to a unit) and set monthly per-
formance goals: “we want that number to close to
95-97% utilization all the time” (Interview, major city
CoC). They also track days to move-in—the date from
which a match is accepted to the day the resident moves
into housing—and create monthly dashboards for each
project which they share with all service providers.
While the reporting system appears to be communi-
cating the need to focus on performance, many survey
respondents suggest they nevertheless struggle to cre-
ate and sustain a local culture of innovation and atten-
tion to improving performance. Eighty-three percent of
respondents agreed or strongly agreed that data are
important for achieving goals, while 76% agreed that
readiness to meet new challenges is important. How-
ever, fewer than one-half of survey respondents, or 49%,
agree or strongly agree that employees are motivated
(48%), and committed to innovation and risk-taking
(46%). Only four in 10 respondents, or 39%, agreed that
a dynamic and entrepreneurial culture exists in their
organization (Musso et al., 2023, p. 25). Interviewees
responded that many employees were exhausted due to
the emotional labor involved with service delivery and
the challenges of the recent COVID-19 pandemic.
Capacity and costs
A strong theme in the interviews was the need for CoCs to
develop or acquire analytical capacity to analyze and inter-
pret trends in performance measurement. The CoC survey
respondents agreed that their staff were competent in
basic descriptive statistics (81%) and creating visual dis-
plays (71%) in dashboard-type reports. However, only
about one-third of the CoCs reported having more
advanced skills including complex data analysis (39%),
forecasting (37%), and statistical analysis (32%) techniques,
while a few (12%) agreed that their staff possessed none
of the necessary skills or competencies (2023, p. 26). TA
from HUD fills these gaps and provides long-term support
to CoCs for strategic initiatives outside the day-to-day rou-
tines for tasks such as analyzing subpopulation data for
equity analysis in service provision or training tribal leaders.
Many CoC interviewees remarked that staffing difficulties
related to the fact CoC funding for staff salaries and other
administrative expenses are locked-in based on the origi-
nal agreement award date. This can lead to staff turnover
and shortages, which in turn contribute to low employee
morale and the need to constantly train new staff. When
asked about the capabilities of her staff, a Program Coordi-
nator remarked:
they’re low paid, have no background in this.
So not only are you trying to teach them the
fundamentals of things like HUD priorities,
but you are also trying to teach them all the
successful strategies to help a household.
And more often than not, as soon as they
find something that pays a little bit better,
they’re gone, and then we’re starting over
again
(Interview,
largely
suburban
CoC;
reported in Musso et al., 2023).
Within this context, the ongoing costs of administrative com-
pliance were noted to be a significant burden for smaller,
rural, or suburban CoCs due to lack of staffing and technical
capacity. Many interviewees expressed concern that the
HUD annual competitive cycle imposed substantial adminis-
trative costs related to reporting and grant-writing. There is a
sense that the CAs work year-round on reporting and grant
writing, which detracts from their ability to focus on local sys-
tems improvement. This administrative effort is reportedly
frustrating given that there is limited feedback from HUD
regarding the scoring provided to applicants. Interviewees
suggested that HUD could reduce the administrative burden
for grantees by extending the competitive portion of the
grant application from an annual to a two-year cycle.

In sum, tremendous complexity and constraints charac-
terize the landscape of CoC funding and performance. CAs
must mediate between HUD priorities and the sometimes-
conflicting goals of local service providers. They face severe
obstacles related to housing costs and accessibility in virtu-
ally all areas of the country. And they manage a conse-
quential workload associated with data management,
reporting, and grantmaking with insufficient and under-
paid staff in many areas. In the face of these constraints,
there is nevertheless evidence that, as Metzenbaum (2009)
suggests, performance and grantmaking function together
to align local and federal goals.
CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS FOR
PRACTICE
The focus on NPM and business-like approaches to gov-
ernment and governance has come with a renewed
interest in and attention to performance management
systems. The goal of these systems broadly is to improve
efficiency in resource allocation, internal and external
accountability,
and
system
performance
(Moynihan,
2008). While performance regimes have proliferated at
the federal, state, and local levels, the literature has been
mixed on the extent to which they are implemented fully,
much less have impact on service outcomes (Kroll, 2015;
Moynihan, 2008; Musso et al., 2024). Much of this litera-
ture focuses on a single agency or level of government,
with much less attention to performance management in
an intergovernmental context and the experiences of
recipients of grant-in aid programs.
While the system has had some successes in encour-
aging evidence-based program delivery, recent progress
in achieving system goals by CoCs has been uneven for a
variety of reasons, most notably a lack of affordable rental
housing in almost every geographical area of the country.
Nonetheless, a continued emphasis on achieving perfor-
mance targets and system outcomes has a communica-
tive value that provides a backstop to CoCs confronted
with a lack of political support to make reducing home-
lessness a local priority in some areas. At the same time,
internal constraints such as administrative burden and
staff turnover among service providers can set back
efforts by CoCs to advance performance-based practices.
In this case, the nationwide emphasis on system improve-
ments enhances performance measurement and report-
ing capabilities and encourages the expansion of data
infrastructure and analytical capability among recipients.
Federal grantmaking agencies can take lessons from
HUD’s experience implementing CoC homeless assistance
grants to improve their collaboration, coordination, and
communication with their partners in program implemen-
tation at the local government level.
The study highlights the role of governance complexi-
ties,
environmental
factors,
and
funding
restrictions
within the context of a federal homelessness grant pro-
gram that challenge earlier assumptions of performance
management doctrine. Multilayered and intergovernmen-
tal networked governance complicates stylized principal–
agent models of performance management. This is partic-
ularly the case when actors are accountable to multiple
principals with different priorities and reporting require-
ments, or when agencies alone are unable to impact out-
comes and rely on buy-in from their own set of agents to
carry out the work of tackling wicked problems. Addition-
ally, market forces, in this case upward pressure on wages
and
housing
costs
that
increase
competition
for
employees with requisite technical skills and affordable
units, may stall progress. Federal agencies can support
capacity building and learning among grantees and their
subrecipients by providing TA, opportunities to share suc-
cessful interventions, and capacity funding.
Despite limited evidence that federal grant making is
strongly related to reported performance metrics, there
appears to be widespread adoption and use of HUD

performance measures and processes by CoC grant recip-
ients to address local and federal goals. These findings
suggest that while imperfect, the HUD performance man-
agement system serves a communicate role in aligning
priorities down to CoCs and through complicated multi-
layered organizational networks. As such, the use of per-
formance measures in a networked grant-in-aid system
appear to emulate the role that such systems can play in
aligning priorities within an organization (Moynihan
et al., 2020). They also promote the use of measurement
tools and strategic planning required for system-oriented
thinking (Musso et al., 2023). These achievements suggest
that HUD’s CoC performance system reinforces the impor-
tant role highlighted by Metzenbaum (2024, p. 868), “that
government performance management works best when
it is not about evaluating federal programs, per se, but
rather about federal programs and cross-program goal-
focused teams regularly using data to inform where they
focus.”
The role that performance measures play in aligning
goals does vary, not surprisingly given the variability of
local CoC context, and the challenges to capacity that
many face. Features of the grant application and award
process, such as limited feedback, changing priorities,
grant timelines, and spending limits on CoC capacity, limit
the effectiveness of performance management systems.
Agencies need time to learn from feedback and alter or
create new programs that fit with federal priorities. Addi-
tionally, application and reporting requirements can
become arduous when grantees and their subgrantees
struggle with limited capacity due to high turnover, lack
of training, and operating in geographically dispersed
areas. Some CoC grant recipients appear to treat perfor-
mance as a compliance exercise, while many express con-
cern about the weight of federal requirements. Federal
agencies should create structures to solicit feedback from
subgrantees to ensure metric and priority alignment and
consider complexities in local environment that impact
performance.
From this perspective, it is important to support
grantees as they tailor the program to the needs of the
local population, in this case, people experiencing home-
lessness. Grant program officials should provide policy
guidance and various supports for capacity building to a
heterogeneous group of grantees with different capaci-
ties and needs. This support can take the form of addi-
tional funding for capacity building and training, direct
TA, and sharing of best practices among grant recipients.
Grantors can increase clarity and reduce administrative
burdens among grantees by ensuring timely and detailed
feedback, expanding application cycles, sharing expecta-
tions well in advance of application submission deadlines,
and allowing for more flexibility in application submis-
sions. At the same time, federal departments have an
opportunity to advance national emerging priorities
through the communication of expectations in the fund-
ing cycle, supporting long-term planning and annual
performance reporting among grantees, and encourage
grant recipients to adopt national strategies and perfor-
mance management practices as a precondition for
success.
The study also suggests that the subfield of perfor-
mance management will benefit from increased attention
to the complexity of multi-agent systems for delivery of
services, with the potential for performance management
to complement grantmaking in aligning intra-agency
goals. Performance management, a hallmark of the NPM
movement, can be particularly difficult within the complex
networks of intergovernmental and intersectoral service
providers that have resulted from NPM’s focus on devolu-
tion and contracting out. Yet it is in the context of net-
works that it is particularly important to align priorities and
develop mutual understanding of system goals. While
there is still work to be done to improve HUD’s CoC perfor-
mance reporting system, the experience of grantees sug-
gests that the system is assisting HUD to communicate
priorities and improve mutual understanding among sys-
tem actors. Other agencies may learn from this experience.

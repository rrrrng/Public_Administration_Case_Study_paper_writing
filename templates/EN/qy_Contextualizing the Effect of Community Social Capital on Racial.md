ABSTRACT
While public administration research has made important strides in understanding social capital, less is known about how its
effects vary across populations and contexts. This study investigates how racial segregation and citizen ideology shape the rela-
tionship between community social capital and flu vaccination rates among White and Black Medicare beneficiaries. Findings
reveal that although social capital is associated with higher vaccination rates, its benefits are unequally realized, favoring Whites
over Blacks. Racial segregation weakens the positive effect of social capital for Blacks and exacerbates health disparities. Liberal
ideology amplifies the influence of social capital on vaccination rates for Whites, but this amplifying role is not statistically
significant for Blacks or the racial gap. The results highlight the need for scholars to incorporate social equity more explicitly
into social capital research, to measure group-­specific social capital, and to account for the structural and cultural contexts that
condition its effects.
1   |   Introduction
Over the past two decades, public administration research on
social capital has expanded considerably, with studies exam-
ining individuals (Döring and Madsen  2022; Lee et  al.  2020;
Ryu  2017), organizations (Fredette and Bradshaw  2012;
Kim 2018; Tantardini and Kroll 2015), and especially geographic
entities such as communities (Compton and Meier 2016), coun-
ties (Tantardini et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2020), states (Andrews
and Brewer  2013; Ma  2017), and countries (Doh  2014). Most
findings point to the positive effects of social capital on public
outcomes, though some highlight negative consequences, in-
cluding policy inequities (Hawes and Rocha 2011) and dimin-
ished citizen willingness to co-­invest in public goods (Zhang
et al. 2020). Scholars increasingly acknowledge that the effects
of social capital are contingent not only on its forms—such as
bonding, bridging, and linking (Johnson et  al.  2015; Lofaro
et  al.  2025)—and performance dimensions (Doh  2014), but
also on governmental factors like capacity and commitment
(Andrews and Brewer  2013; Compton and Meier  2016) and
on community characteristics like socioeconomic conditions
(Menahem et al. 2011).
An important line of inquiry examines how the benefits of so-
cial capital are unequally distributed across population groups.
Studies have found that social capital can reinforce inequalities,
especially in racially diverse contexts (Compton and Meier 2016;
Hero and Tolbert  1996; Lofaro et  al.  2025; Lin  2000; Meier
et al. 2014). Hero (2003a) notes that social capital often fails to
bridge racial divides and may even exacerbate disparities, with
high levels of aggregate social capital sometimes correlating
with worse outcomes for minority groups. Some public admin-
istration scholars have explored how racial diversity shapes the
formation of social capital (Letki 2008) and how social capital

This omission is problematic because the formation and con-
sequences of social capital are shaped by the social structures
in which it operates (Albacete 2010), while structural racism is
arguably the most important contextual factor. Structural rac-
ism refers to the interconnected systems that perpetuate racial
inequality across domains such as housing, education, employ-
ment, and healthcare (Bailey et al. 2017), which influence not
only access to economic and cultural capital but also access to
social capital (Gilbert et  al.  2022). Understanding how social
capital interacts with such contextual forces is vital for crafting
effective policies that address community disparities.
This study addresses that gap by examining how racial seg-
regation and citizen ideology condition the effects of commu-
nity social capital on racial disparities in flu vaccination rates
among Medicare beneficiaries. The persistence of lower flu vac-
cination rates among Black older adults relative to their White
counterparts remains a significant public health concern in the
U.S. (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2024). These
disparities contribute to higher rates of flu-­related illness and
mortality in Black communities (Crouse Quinn et  al.  2017).
Because older adults—particularly those aged 65 and over—are
at elevated risk for severe flu complications, increasing vacci-
nation rates in vulnerable populations is a pressing policy goal.
Drawing on the structural-­constructivist model of health dis-
parities (Dressler et al. 2005), we conceptualize racial segrega-
tion as a structural moderator and citizen ideology as a cultural
moderator. We hypothesize that while community social capital
generally promotes higher vaccination rates, its effects vary be-
tween White and Black beneficiaries, and that these effects are
contingent upon racial segregation and ideological climate.
2   |   Social Capital, Inequality, and Health
Disparities
The relationship between social capital and inequality remains
a subject of debate. Defined by Putnam  (2000) as “networks,
norms, and social trust that facilitate reciprocity and civic sol-
idarity,” social capital is often celebrated for its ability to pro-
mote civic engagement and social cohesion. Putnam argued that
states with high levels of social capital tend to exhibit greater
support for racial integration, gender equality, and civil liber-
ties. He built directly on Coleman's (1988) conception of social
capital as a facilitator of coordination and cooperation, inher-
iting Coleman's functional mechanism (trust and cooperation).
However, Putnam largely bracketed the issues of inequality and
exclusion central to Bourdieu (1986), portraying social capital as
a public good that benefits everyone rather than a form of elite
advantage. Later critics contend that Putnam's view overstates
its egalitarian potential (Hero 2003b; Hawes and Rocha 2011;
Lin 2000). Lin (2000) highlights the inherent inequalities em-
bedded in social capital, noting that marginalized groups often
lack equal access to the resources and benefits that social net-
works provide. Hero (2003a, 2003b, 2007), in particular, is skep-
tical of the link between aggregate social capital and positive
economic and political outcomes in racially diverse contexts.
He notes that the benefits of social capital often accrue along
racial lines, depending on the degree of racial heterogeneity in
a community.
Recognizing that social capital can produce both positive and
negative externalities, researchers have shifted from sim-
ply acknowledging the negative externalities of social capital
to examining how its impact varies by form (e.g., bonding vs.
bridging) and context (Panagopoulos et al. 2021). Drawing on
Burt's  (2009) theory of the contingent value of social capital,
scholars have explored how different forms of social capital per-
form differently in different contexts (Berardo and Scholz 2010;
Ryu 2017). Public administration research has also examined
how social capital functions in racially diverse settings (Hawes
and Rocha 2011; Hawes 2017, 2019; Hawes and McCrea 2018),
noting that its effects can diverge across racial groups. More re-
cent health equity studies in public administration extend this
line of inquiry. For example, Lofaro et al. (2025) show that social
capital functions differently across communities with varying
community vulnerability levels and racial compositions, and
Peng et al. (2025) examine how racial socioeconomic inequality
moderates the influence of social capital on COVID-­19 health
disparities. Still, the mechanisms through which social capital
benefits or excludes particular populations have not been fully
unpacked, and no study has used racial segregation or citizen
ideology as moderators.
The insufficient attention to the conditioning role of struc-
tural and cultural contexts has long been recognized in
public health, where researchers remain cautious about
the presumed benefits of social capital (Villalonga-­Olives
and Kawachi  2017; Offer  2021). Pearce and Smith  (2003)
point out that despite Putnam's efforts to link social capital
to public health in Bowling Alone, which considers “bowl-
ing alone represents one of the nation's most serious public
health challenges”, he himself was skeptical in earlier work,
asserting in Making Democracy Work that health is beyond

Admittedly, in the public health literature, there is a growing trend
toward examining different forms of social capital and their dis-
tinct effects on health outcomes (Fraser et al. 2021; Habibov and
Auchynnikava 2025; Linde and Egede 2023). However, relatively
few of these studies focus explicitly on health disparities. Some
studies have investigated how social capital interacts with political
factors, such as citizen ideology, institutional trust, and polariza-
tion, to shape overall health outcomes (Zhang et al. 2022; Carson
et al. 2022), but to date, no studies have examined whether racial
segregation or citizen ideology moderates the effect of social capi-
tal on racial health disparities.
The persistence of racial health disparities, especially between
Black and White Americans, underscores the urgency of this
line of inquiry. Black Americans consistently experience poorer
health outcomes than White Americans across nearly all indi-
cators (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality 2023). In
their influential review, Dressler et al. (2005) identify five the-
oretical models used to explain these disparities: racial-­genetic,
health-­behavior, socioeconomic status, psychosocial stress, and
structural-­constructivist models. Of these, the authors argue
that the structural-­constructivist model—emphasizing how ra-
cially stratified social structures intersect with the cultural con-
struction of aspirations and identities—is particularly useful for
understanding disparities.
Building on Dressler et  al.  (2005), as well as Geronimus
and Thompson (2004) and Phelan and Link  (2015), Hicken
et  al.  (2018) advocate for frameworks grounded in structural
and cultural racism (see also Brown and Homan 2024; Homan
et al. 2021). Structural racism refers to institutionalized policies
and practices that systematically advantage some racial groups
over others (Jones 2000), while cultural racism encompasses the
belief systems and stereotypes—such as assumptions of Black
inferiority—that sustain those structures. Together, they form
a mutually reinforcing system that shapes access to social cap-
ital and health. Hicken et al. (2018) urge scholars to approach
health disparities with an explicit awareness of these interlock-
ing forms of racism. Efforts have started to emerge to develop
comprehensive measures of structural and cultural racism
(e.g., Akinyemiju et al. 2025; Brown and Homan 2024; Homan
et al. 2021).
3   |   Hypothesis Development
3.1   |   Direct Effect of Social Capital
Extensive research suggests that social capital plays a positive
role in improving public health outcomes, including vaccination
uptake (Jung et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2022). Communities char-
acterized by strong social networks, shared norms, and civic en-
gagement are more likely to foster the dissemination of accurate
health information, encourage prosocial behavior, and promote
trust in public health initiatives. These mechanisms are partic-
ularly effective in increasing adherence to preventive measures
such as vaccination. Therefore, we expect that higher levels of
community-­level social capital will be associated with higher flu
vaccination rates.
Hypothesis 1a.  Higher county-­level social capital is associ-
ated with higher flu vaccination rates.
However, while social capital may enhance overall vaccina-
tion rates, it does not necessarily benefit all groups equally.
Literature presents contrasting perspectives on the role of so-
cial capital in narrowing health disparities. On the one hand,
social capital, exemplified by social cohesiveness, can buffer
against stress and health risks, especially for marginalized
groups facing discrimination (Szreter and Woolcock 2004). On
the other hand, social capital may sometimes exacerbate social
inequality and exclusion (Uphoff et al. 2013), as its benefits
are not uniformly accessible to all within a community. Even
when all groups have access—are linked to the community
social networks, different groups may benefit differently from
the access. Recent sociology scholarship emphasizes the con-
cent of “negative ties”—ties that generate stress and worsen
health outcomes for marginalized groups despite their link to
broad social networks (Offer  2021; Adebahr-­Maskow  2022).
Empirical evidence suggests that although social capital is
linked to lower death rates, its effects are more pronounced for
white populations compared to African Americans (Lochner
et al. 2003). This divergence may stem from racially segmented
networks, differential exposure to health-­promoting norms,
or unequal access to influential community institutions. In
heterogeneous counties, social capital may primarily reflect
the norms and resources of majority White communities, thus
producing unequal health gains. As a result, increased social
capital may inadvertently widen racial gaps in outcomes such
as flu vaccination.
Hypothesis 1b.  The positive association between social
capital and flu vaccination rates is stronger for White popula-
tions than for Black populations, thereby exacerbating racial
disparities.

3.2   |   The Moderating Effects of Racial Segregation
and Citizen Ideology
Drawing from the structural-­constructivist model (Dressler
et  al.  2005), health outcomes are shaped by both structural
conditions—such as institutional arrangements and patterns of
residential segregation—and cultural constructions, including
prevailing beliefs, norms, and ideologies that influence health-­
related attitudes and behaviors (see also O'Brian and Kent 2025;
Camobreco and He 2022; Baxter-­King et al. 2022). Social capital
may contribute to reducing racial health disparities in inclusive
environments, but in exclusionary or stratified settings, it can
reinforce existing inequities.
Residential segregation represents a central structural factor in
this dynamic (Brown and Homan 2024; Homan et al. 2021). It
encompasses not only the spatial separation of racial groups but
also the broader institutional and normative systems that sus-
tain racialized inequalities. Segregation has been shown to limit
access to healthcare, quality education, employment, and other
resources, thereby exacerbating disparities in outcomes across
numerous domains (Bailey et  al.  2017; Williams et  al.  2019;
Kramer and Hogue 2009; Fang et al. 1998). For example, seg-
regated neighborhoods disproportionately expose minority res-
idents to environmental hazards, underfunded infrastructure,
and limited access to health-­promoting amenities like recre-
ational spaces and nutritious food (Larson et  al.  2009; Bravo
et al. 2016).
While the direct negative effects of segregation on health are
well documented, its moderating role in shaping the relation-
ship between social capital and health outcomes remains un-
derstudied. Segregation influences not only who resides where,
but also who is included in, or excluded from, the networks
that constitute community social capital. In highly segregated
counties, White residents are more likely to reside in better-­
resourced communities with access to robust social networks
and institutional support. In contrast, Black residents often live
in disadvantaged areas, where distrust in institutions and iso-
lation from mainstream networks limit their ability to benefit
from broader community-­level social capital. In such contexts,
social capital may be spatially and racially fragmented, dispro-
portionately benefiting White residents and leaving Black resi-
dents underserved.
Thus, low levels of residential segregation may provide a more
equitable context in which social capital can improve outcomes
for all groups. In contrast, high segregation can constrain the
flow of social resources to Black communities, weakening the
potential benefits of social capital for Black residents.
Hypothesis 2a.  Residential segregation moderates the re-
lationship between social capital and Black health outcomes.
Specifically, in counties with higher levels of residential segrega-
tion, the positive effect of social capital on flu vaccination rates
among Black Medicare beneficiaries is attenuated.
Conversely, the same dampening effect is not expected for
White residents. Regardless of overall segregation levels, White
residents are more likely to live in resource-­rich environments
and remain well-­positioned to benefit from community social
capital. As a result, the presence of residential segregation dis-
proportionately restricts Black residents' access to and experi-
ence within health-­promoting networks and resources, thereby
widening racial disparities in health outcomes. In other words,
segregation amplifies the disparity-­enhancing effects of uneven
access to social capital.
Hypothesis 2b.  Residential segregation moderates the rela-
tionship between social capital and racial disparities in health
outcomes. Specifically, in counties with higher levels of residen-
tial segregation, the marginal effect of social capital on the ra-
cial gap in flu vaccination rates among Medicare beneficiaries is
amplified.
Ideology functions as a central mechanism of cultural racism,
shaping how racialized outcomes are justified and reproduced
(Akinyemiju et al. 2025; Dressler et al. 2005). As a socially con-
structed and collectively maintained system of meaning, citizen
ideology influences how individuals perceive and interpret so-
cial and political realities. It is perpetuated through ongoing pub-
lic discourse and political communication, ultimately shaping
public attitudes toward health behaviors, including vaccination
(Howard 2024). Research shows that, relative to conservatives,
liberals tend to place greater trust in government and public
health institutions and are generally more supportive of vac-
cination campaigns (Baumgaertner et  al.  2018). Accordingly,
communities with more liberal ideological orientations may
experience better public health outcomes across racial groups—
though the degree of benefit may vary. Liberal ideologies may
also more effectively mobilize community networks in support
of public health initiatives. By contrast, in predominantly con-
servative or Republican-­leaning communities, social networks
are less likely to mobilize in favor of such initiatives and may
instead reinforce skepticism or opposition, thereby contribut-
ing to vaccine hesitancy (Facciani et al. 2023). Community co-
hesion may amplify prevailing ideological orientations, which
may be supportive or critical of public health measures (Zhang
et al. 2022).
Hypothesis 3a.  Citizen ideology moderates the relationship
between social capital and health outcomes. In counties with more
liberal (Democratic-­leaning) populations, the marginal effect of
social capital on Medicare Beneficiaries' flu vaccination rates is
increased.
In more Democrat-­leaning counties, social capital may be
more effectively mobilized to support vaccination uptake—
but does this mobilization benefit Black residents more than
White residents? While Democratic citizens and elected of-
ficials tend to express greater trust in public health institu-
tions and promote equity-­oriented health messaging (Gollust
et  al.  2024; Page-­Tan and Hope  2024), such ideological ori-
entations may not translate into proportionately greater gains
for Black communities in health behaviors. First, citizen ide-
ology may shape attitudinal climates, but it does not neces-
sarily alter the structure or accessibility of social networks
and resources—such as health literacy, care access, or the
strength of interpersonal ties within historically marginalized
communities (Cao et al. 2022). Second, although liberal envi-
ronments are more likely to promote pro-­vaccine norms, they
may not fully mitigate longstanding distrust, misinformation,

4   |   Methods
4.1   |   Data and Sample
This study utilizes the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid
Services (CMS) Mapping Medicare Disparities (MMD) by
Population dataset to construct the primary dependent vari-
ables. While the full MMD database includes 3140 U.S.
counties, our analysis focuses on 2029 counties that report in-
fluenza vaccination rates disaggregated by race (total, White,
and African American populations) under Medicare Fee-­for-­
Service in both 2017 and 2022. After merging these records
with additional data from the U.S. Census Bureau, County
Health Rankings, and other publicly available sources, the
final sample comprises 1575 counties across 50 states plus
the District of Columbia. These counties collectively repre-
sent 90.0% of the total U.S. population, including 83.1% of the
White and 96.8% of the African American population, min-
imizing concerns about missing data bias.1 Given the high
population coverage—particularly for Black Americans—the
absence of data from the remaining counties is unlikely to
pose a significant threat to the validity of the study's findings.2
As shown in Table 1, 859 counties (78.0% of those in the CMS
sample) are classified as urban, characterized by higher popula-
tion densities and more robust healthcare infrastructure. Urban
counties are particularly salient for this analysis, as a large pro-
portion of African American residents reside in urban areas
where disparities in preventive care may be shaped by unique
socioeconomic and institutional factors.

4.2.1   |   Dependent Variables
Racial disparities in influenza vaccination are assessed using
both absolute and relative measures. The absolute mea-
sures reflect vaccination rates for White and Black Medicare
beneficiaries separately, enabling group-­specific analysis. The
relative measure, calculated as the difference between White
and Black vaccination rates, captures the magnitude of the
racial gap. Using both approaches allows us to explore not
only the direct determinants of vaccination coverage but also
the structural mechanisms driving racial disparities (Cheng
et al. 2022).

dissimilarity index from the County Health Rankings, derived
from the 5-­year American Community Survey. This index
ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating greater
segregation—that is, a larger proportion of residents would
need to relocate to achieve an even racial distribution across
neighborhoods (Massey and Denton  1988). This approach
has been widely used in health disparity studies (Brown and
Homan 2024; Homan et al. 2021).
Citizen Ideology is captured by the percentage of county resi-
dents who voted for Democratic candidates in the 2016 and 2020
presidential elections, using data from the MIT Election Data
and Science Lab. Voting preference serves as a timely proxy for
community-­level ideological orientation (Hao and Shao 2023).
4.2.3   |   Control Variables
We include a set of county-­level covariates known to influence
vaccination and health disparities:
•	 Racial diversity: Percent of the non-­Hispanic White resi-
dents (Cheng et al. 2022).
•	 Income inequality: Gini coefficient (ACS, Liao 2006)
•	 Older population: Share of residents aged 65 and over
(Borgonovi et al. 2021).
•	 Health burden: Percent of adults reporting fair or poor
health (age-­adjusted; Borgonovi et al. 2021).
•	 Provider access: Number of users per preventive care pro-
vider (CMS MMD; Borgonovi et al. 2021).
Additional socioeconomic indicators are drawn from the 5-­
year American Community Survey (2011–2016 and 2017–2021),
disaggregated by racial group or expressed as racial dispari-
ties. These include unemployment rate, poverty rate, median
household income (logged), educational attainment (% with a
bachelor's degree or higher), and uninsurance rate. Finally, one
geographic factor is controlled for: Urban is measured based
on whether counties are designated as part of a Metropolitan
Statistical Area (MSA).
4.3   |   Methodology
We employed mixed-­effects multilevel models to examine
county-­level flu vaccination rates. This approach is well-­suited
for the hierarchical structure of the data, the short and balanced
panel design, allowing simultaneous estimation of variation
across counties and over time (Gomes et al. 2022; Rimkutė and
van der Voet 2024). It provides the most theoretically appropriate
and empirically rigorous framework for analyzing multilevel,
short-­panel data in the context of U.S. public health governance.
Vaccination governance in the U.S. is largely state-­directed but
locally implemented, so counties are nested within state policy
contexts. Thus, we include state-­level fixed effects to account
for institutional and regulatory differences across states, and
county-­level random intercepts to account for local heterogene-
ity in implementation capacity and community characteristics.
Since the dataset includes the same 1575 counties in two periods
(2017 and 2022), capturing both pre-­ and post-­pandemic con-
texts as well as two national election cycles, year fixed effects are
included to account for nationwide temporal shocks and broader
secular trends.
To ensure robustness, we compared mixed-­effects estimates
with first-­difference OLS, two-­way fixed-­effects, and random-­
effects models. The direction and significance of key coefficients
remained stable, while mixed-­effects models provided a supe-
rior fit (higher marginal and conditional R2).3

health value of social capital. These findings are consistent with

Anderson and Ray-­Warren 2022).
Hypothesis 2b, that segregation amplifies the effect of social cap-

that segregation moderates not just group-­specific outcomes but
also group differences, exacerbating inequality even when over-
all outcomes improve.
Residential segregation plays a pivotal role in shaping access to
health resources and influences community dynamics. By spa-
tially organizing racial and socioeconomic groups, segregation
creates unequal access to healthcare and other resources across
neighborhoods (Anderson and Ray-­Warren 2022). Within seg-
regated settings, social capital often functions within racially
homogenous networks, strengthening intra-­group cohesion but
failing to address inter-­group disparities. As a result, the bene-
fits of trust, mutual support, and social resources are unequally
distributed, exacerbating racial health disparities like flu vac-
cination gaps (Bailey et al. 2017; Kramer and Hogue 2009). In
stratified communities, social capital may disproportionately
benefit the dominant racial group, widening health disparities
even as overall vaccination rates rise. Policy efforts to mobilize
social capital for public health must therefore be attentive to spa-
tial and racial inequality in its formation and use.

capital. Its overall positive effect on vaccination aligns with
Coleman's (1988) conceptualization of social capital as a collective

Yet the lack of a comparable benefit for Black beneficiaries sup-

of the “dark side” of social capital and exclusionary network ef-

appears insufficient to close racial health disparities (McCabe

6   |   Limitations and Future Directions
This study contributes to the social capital literature by investi-
gating how county-­level contexts shape the relationship between
social capital, health outcomes, and racial disparities in flu vac-
cination. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowl-
edged, each pointing to fruitful directions for future research.
First, the study is subject to important measurement limita-
tions, particularly regarding social capital and structural rac-
ism. While we relied on the most robust available indicators,
our use of aggregate county-­level measures of social capital—
primarily capturing the presence of civic resources—may

civic metrics. This limitation may partly explain the lower ex-

Relatedly, our proxies for structural and cultural racism—resi-
dential segregation and citizen ideology—only capture selected
facets of these complex constructs. Although recent scholarship
in public health has begun developing multidimensional indica-
tors of structural and cultural racism (Akinyemiju et al. 2025;
Brown and Homan 2024; Homan et al. 2021), such data are not
yet fully available at the county level. We employed segregation
and ideology not only for data availability, but also because these
constructs remain theoretically central to public administration
and political science. Still, future work would benefit from inte-
grating more comprehensive and validated indicators of struc-
tural and cultural racism to assess whether these better capture
how racialized contexts condition the effects of social capital on
health disparities.
Second, while this study focuses on social capital, it omits gov-
ernmental factors that may directly influence racial disparities
in health outcomes. Variables such as the scope and equity focus
of local public health campaigns, funding levels, and organiza-
tional mandates likely play critical roles in shaping vaccination
uptake. Future studies should integrate these institutional vari-
ables to provide a more comprehensive view of the intersecting
forces behind racial inequities in public health.
Third, the analysis relies on a short panel with two non-­
consecutive years (2017 and 2022). This limitation stems from
the availability of key predictors—such as social capital, citi-
zen ideology, and contextual variables—which could only be
matched to flu vaccination data in these two periods. While
this structure allows for the examination of both cross-­sectional
differences and limited temporal shifts, the panel provides rel-
atively weak leverage for identifying trends over time. Despite
the use of mixed-­effects models to account for unobserved
county-­level heterogeneity and state-­level clustering, our abil-
ity to draw causal inferences about temporal dynamics remains
constrained. Future studies would benefit from longer panels or
continuous tracking of county-­level health equity outcomes.
7   |   Conclusion
This study investigates how community social capital shapes ra-
cial disparities in health outcomes—specifically flu vaccination
rates among Medicare beneficiaries—and whether its effects are
conditioned by structural and cultural contexts. The findings
show that while higher levels of social capital are generally as-
sociated with improved vaccination rates, these benefits accrue
disproportionately to White beneficiaries, thereby exacerbat-
ing racial disparities. Importantly, the effects of social capital
are contingent on two contextual moderators: racial segrega-
tion and citizen ideology. In more segregated communities, the
positive influence of social capital on Black vaccination rates is
significantly diminished, widening racial gaps. In more liberal
communities, social capital more strongly boosts White vacci-
nation rates, but the moderating effect for Black beneficiaries
remains statistically insignificant. These patterns suggest that
while liberal ideological environments may foster normative
support for public health initiatives, they are insufficient to over-
come deep-­seated institutional distrust or to offset the enduring
structural barriers facing marginalized populations.
These findings underscore the need to more fully integrate a
social equity perspective into public administration scholar-
ship on social capital. While social science research has long
recognized that social capital operates unevenly across pop-
ulation groups, public administration has yet to fully engage
with these distributional dynamics. Future research should
explore how social capital affects diverse groups—defined
not only by race, but also by gender, religion, and their inter-
sections—both across communities and within public orga-
nizations. The next generation of social capital scholarship
should move beyond group-­indifferent measures and toward
group-­specific frameworks, interrogating who can mobilize
social resources, through which networks, and under what in-
stitutional conditions. Distinguishing racialized and gendered
forms of social capital, and mapping the connections between
intra-­group and inter-­group networks, can reveal how differ-
ent patterns of social connection reinforce or mitigate inequi-
ties. Such attention to network structure and positionality is
essential to understanding when, and for whom, community
resources translate into equitable health outcomes.
Finally, this article highlights the importance of contextualiz-
ing the effects of social capital. Structural factors like residen-
tial segregation and cultural forces such as community ideology
shape how social capital operates and for whom it yields bene-
fits. While public administration research has often focused on
managerial moderators, it has paid less attention to how broader
community structures and cultural norms condition the dis-
tributional consequences of social capital. These insights can
inform future organizational-­level studies, where the effects
of social capital may also depend on internal subcultures, net-
work configurations, and the presence or absence—of inclusive
coalitions.

Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) applications transform public sector decision-making. However, most research conceptualizes AI as a form of special­
ized decision-support tool. In contrast, this study presents a different form of human-AI collaboration, the concept of human-AI ensembles,
where public managers and AI tackle the same decision tasks, rather than specializing in certain subtasks. This is particularly relevant for many
public sector decisions, where neither human nor AI predictions have a clear advantage over the other. We illustrate this within the context
of public hiring, focusing on two key areas: (a) the potential of ensembling humans and AI to reduce biases and (b) the willingness of public
managers to implement ensembling. Study 1 uses data from the assessment of profiles of real-life job candidates (n = 695) at the intersection
of gender and ethnicity by public managers compared to AI. The exploratory linear regression results illustrate how ensembled decision-making
may alleviate ethnic biases. The linear regression results of study 2, a preregistered survey experiment, show that public managers (n = 538
with four observations each) put equal weight on AI advice and human advice, and, when reminded of the unlawfulness of hiring discrimination,
may even prioritize AI over human advice.
Key words: human-AI collaboration; decision-making; experiment; bias; public sector.
Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) affects the legitimacy, efficacy, and
legality of public service provision (Dunleavy 2005; Dunleavy
and Margetts 2023). Contemporary AI, typically based on
machine learning approaches, enables AI to learn solutions
from data rather than human programming, differing it from
previous algorithmic decision-support tools (Brynjolfsson
and Mitchell 2017). Public administration research has
begun to explore the changes that AI brings to public sector
­decision-making and policy outcomes. However, current
public administration literature on human-AI collaboration
primarily follows a logic of specialization. Specialization
means that some form of division of labor is assumed, at
least implicitly: AI performs certain subtasks, then the human
performs other subtasks and makes the final decision. AI ei­
ther automates some subtasks or augments them to integrate
algorithmic insights into the decision-making process.
When humans collaborate with AI, however, decision-­
making structures do not have to rely solely on specializa­
tion. As a potential but currently overlooked alternative,
this article conceptually argues that ensembling human and
AI ­decision-making may enhance legitimacy, legality, and
efficacy in the public sector. Following current decision-­
making research, ensembling is defined as the “aggrega­
tion of predictions” from multiple sources applied to the
same issue (Choudhary et al. 2025, p. 537). Unlike special­
ization, ensembling involves a division of labor where both
human and AI tackle the same prediction problem in full,
rather than handling only specific parts. Their predictions
are then aggregated into a final decision (Puranam 2021).
For decisions where neither human nor AI consistently out­
perform the other in prediction accuracy, aggregating di­
verse predictions is advantageous, as it reduces overall error
compared to relying on individual predictions (Page 2007).
The same idea underlies Condorcet’s jury theorem (1785),
which demonstrates that aggregating independent, better-
than-random judgments reduces overall errors. This is par­
ticularly relevant in the public sector, where decision-making
often involves small groups of people operating across units
and hierarchical levels, each with distinct rationales, biases,
and errors (Iaryczower, Lewis, and Shum 2013).
This article illustrates the usefulness of human-AI ensembles
in the context of hiring public employees. Hiring decisions are
illustrative cases where neither humans nor AI have a clear
advantage, and where neither can achieve perfect accuracy
in making predictions due to uncertainty and incomplete in­
formation (Choudhary et al. 2025). Specifically, the article
offers insights based on two studies into critical aspects of
ensembling human and AI in hiring decisions:
(1)	 To what extent do public managers and AI differ in
their biases in hiring, and can ensembling public man­
agers and AI reduce potentially discriminatory biases?
(2)	 To what extent are public managers willing to imple­
ment ensembling, that is, put weight on the advice pro­
vided by AI compared to humans in decision-making?

Both questions concern fundamental but under researched
aspects of the integration of AI into decision-making in the
public sector. Ensembling humans and AI as one form of
human-AI collaboration only have the potential to benefit
decision-making if the predictions of humans and AI are
diverse (study 1), and only if public managers are willing
to implement ensembling, that is, are willing to take advice
of an AI into account like the advice of human colleagues
(study 2).
Study 1 illustrates the potential benefits of ensembling
human and AI predictions in terms of reducing unjusti­
fied biases that can lead to discriminatory outcomes in
hiring public employees. The descriptive findings indicate
that ensembling human and AI person-job fit assessments
of candidates could reduce implicit ethnic bias. The
preregistered survey experiment in study 2 tests how much
weight hiring managers place on the advice of the AI as
compared to the advice of a human. Our findings suggest
that public managers put equal weight on human and AI ad­
vice and appear to exhibit greater receptivity to advice from
AI when they are reminded of the legal prohibitions against
discrimination.
Thus, this article provides three contributions. First, it
advances our understanding of AI for decision-making in the
public sector. We discuss ensembling as a form of human-AI
collaboration within the context of public administration.
Specifically, we examine how and under which conditions
human-AI ensembles can improve the legitimacy, legality,
and efficacy of decision tasks, such as hiring, where neither
humans nor AI have a clear superiority. These insights are
pivotal for informing the development, implementation, and
evaluation of AI in the public sector.
Second, the theoretical contribution is tested in the em­
pirical context of public hiring. A recent review (Jakobsen,
Løkke, and Keppeler 2023) highlights that public adminis­
tration research is limited in its examination of personnel
selection, underscoring the value of our efforts to expand un­
derstanding of the potential of AI in hiring to reduce biases
that can lead to illegitimate, illegal, and ineffective outcomes,
such as discriminatory hiring outcomes. This is important for
research and practice in the light of discussions about repre­
sentative bureaucracy and public sector staff shortages.
Third, the article expands the scope in the emerging field
of research on the impact of AI in public administration and
policy. This article is, to our knowledge, a pioneering effort to
directly and robustly examine the use of AI in public sector
decision-making. It uses real field data and conducts a survey
experiment with notable experimental realism, including an
authentic scenario, high contextual details, and a sample of
real public managers (Brutger et al. 2022). Moreover, we
use the weight of advice (WOA) measurement approach
(Dawes, Faust, and Meehl 1989; Logg, Minson, and Moore
2019), which allows us to move beyond reliance on artifi­
cial scenarios with simplistic choices between human and AI
judgement.
Previous literature on human-AI collaboration
in decision-making
In this section, we review relevant literature concerning
human-AI collaboration in decision-making and describe the
implicit assumption of specialization before discussing the
theoretical foundations of human-AI ensembles.
AI as a tool
Decisions under uncertainty require predictions to guide
choices, even though predictions alone may not resolve them
(Athey 2017). These issues typically require either estimation
or screening. For example, public sector decisions may in­
volve determining how much taxpayer money to invest in a
new project (estimation) or choosing which candidate to hire
for a public sector job (screening).
As an advanced information technology, AI can enhance
decision-making by improving organizational capacities and
providing more accurate, comprehensive, timely, and readily
available information to individual decision-makers (Huber
1990, p. 63). Research shows that AI can support decision
problems involving estimation/regression and screening/
classification, particularly by increasing prediction accuracy
(Jarrahi 2018; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering,
and Medicine 2024). Unlike earlier algorithmic decision-­
making tools, AI based on machine learning algorithms
requires less prior knowledge of problem structure or prede­
fined rules to generate solutions. Instead, AI can approximate
solutions by fitting models to the data. Specifically, AI relying
on deep neural networks and deep learning models assumes a
particular problem structure and uses data to fit and evaluate
a set of potential models (Choudhary et al. 2025). According
to the universal approximation theorems (Goodfellow,
Bengio, and Courville 2016), a neural network architecture
(i.e., an AI algorithm) exists that can achieve at least the same
level of accuracy as any other predictive models, including
those that might underlie human cognition.
However, there are key limitations to AI decision support:
sufficient computing power and sufficient data. In terms of
data, it is important to recognize limitations in volume and
in the type of data available for AI. Not all relevant data can
be captured and codified in ways that can be processed by AI
algorithms (Choudhary et al. 2025). For example, screening
candidates for a public sector job often involves both informa­
tion about the candidate (e.g., a CV that can be translated
into a format accessible to AI) and managers' heuristics—
intuitions and experiences that assess a candidate's compati­
bility with potential colleagues in ways that current algorithms
cannot replicate or access (Luan, Reb, and Gigerenzer 2019).
Automation, augmentation, and human-AI
collaboration
Given these limitations, the focus is less on how AI will replace
decision-makers and more on automating and augmenting
specific tasks (Raisch and Krakowski 2021). In the automa­
tion scenario, AI is expected to outperform humans in spe­
cific subtasks, leading to their replacement in these areas.
However, this shift carries risks, as it may result in the loss of
causal, contextual, and general knowledge within organiza­
tions and their routines (Balasubramanian, Ye, and Xu 2022).
The augmentation research advocates for leveraging the
complementary strengths of humans and AI. However, a recent
meta-analysis found performance losses in ­decision-making
tasks when AI consistently outperforms humans. In such
cases, combining human and AI inputs proved ineffective.
Conversely, when humans outperform AI on their own,
collaborating with AI leads to performance gains (Vaccaro,
Almaatouq, and Malone 2024).
Research into human-AI collaboration examines the inter­
action between humans and AI (for a review, see Hillebrand,
Raisch, and Schad 2025), including studies on their cognitive

interplay. For example, Berente et al. (2021) explore how
human-AI collaboration affects decision-making. They argue
that human-AI collaboration not only automates and informs
decision-making, depending on AI's programmability but
also transforms the role of decision-makers. Unlike tradi­
tional algorithmic support tools, AI can process data in ways
not predetermined by humans, acting independently and
generatively. AI's role in decision-making may lead to a shift
from rule-based to predictive approaches and from value-based
toward more instrumental reasoning. These shifts demand
that humans learn to navigate AI's autonomous and evolving
capabilities—especially as AI sometimes even manages human
employees (Hillebrand, Raisch, and Schad 2025). While ethical
and possibly philosophical questions remain (for an overview,
see Haeri et al. 2022), several studies support the notion that AI
advice can enhance decision accuracy, though it may also lead
to a loss of unique human knowledge (Fügener et al. 2021).
Furthermore, concepts such as hybrid intelligence have
emerged. Hybrid intelligence refers to the integration of special­
ized human and AI capabilities to enhance, rather than replace,
human abilities, enabling the achievement of goals that neither
could accomplish alone (Akata et al. 2020, p. 19). Thus, aug­
mentation extends to contexts such as training AI and approving
AI decisions. In these settings, humans either act as trainers
(human-in-the-loop) or as gatekeepers overseeing AI decisions.
Each role presents distinct challenges: In the gatekeeping sce­
nario, a key concern is automation bias, where humans tend
to uncritically adhere to AI-generated recommendations (Alon-
Barkat and Busuioc 2022; Selten, Robeer, and Grimmelikhuijsen
2023). In the human-in-the-loop scenario, humans contribute to
improving and refining algorithmic accuracy (Slade et al. 2024).
However, recent research suggests that human-AI feedback loops
can unintentionally amplify biases. AI can amplify existing biases
in training data while influencing human perception in ways that
users may not fully recognize. For example, individuals may un­
derestimate the extent to which AI feedback influences their own
decision-making (Glickman and Sharot 2024).
Previous literature on AI in public sector
decision-making
The existing public administration literature on AI can be
broadly grouped into three streams. First, discourses focus
on public values and AI, such as issues of accountability
and trust (Busuioc 2021), inclusion (Moon 2023), justice
(Nagtegaal 2021), or privacy (Willems et al. 2022). Related
to these discussions is a second stream that focuses on govern­
ance in the digital age (Dunleavy 2005), including research on
risk-based frameworks (Wirtz, Weyerer, and Kehl 2022), as
well as digital transformation and AI implementation (Mergel
et al. 2023). The third stream of research draws attention
to the interaction between AI and humans in public sector
contexts, including the perspectives of citizens (Miller and
Keiser 2021) and street-, screen-, or system-level bureaucrats
(Bovens and Zouridis 2002). This includes discussions of how
public managers use AI (Alon-Barkat and Busuioc 2022) and
how their discretion is affected (Busch and Henriksen 2018).
The specialization assumption
Many decisions in the public sector are subject to uncertainty:
public managers have the discretion to evaluate budgets,
projects, job applicants, etc. Such decisions require the eval­
uation of a set of alternatives, the outcomes of which can be
predicted on the basis of their characteristics (Christensen
and Knudsen 2010). As outlined above, current AI can solve
such prediction problems. However, many previous studies
have assumed that decision-makers and AI may be superior at
performing certain tasks, completing certain process steps, or
achieving different outcomes—the specialization assumption.
This is often related to certain limitations such as data availa­
bility, computational power, or the type of data. Although AI
based on machine learning with neural network architectures
can theoretically make any prediction (Choudhary et al. 2025),
public managers may lack the necessary (big) data, computing
power, or simply the skills (Mergel et al. 2023). Moreover, cer­
tain types of data are currently inaccessible to AI, such as the
experience, heuristics, and intuition of public managers.
The literature discourse on AI in the public sector largely
assumes specialization. Public managers and AI are assumed to
perform distinct tasks. For example, existing studies argue that
AI handles certain tasks (automatically), while public managers,
acting as gatekeepers, are responsible for training and/or eval­
uation (e.g., Ranerup and Henriksen 2022). This includes the
view that AI may (partially) replace public managers (Busch
and Henriksen 2018), which carries several risks, such as
concerns over legitimacy (Grimmelikhuijsen and Meijer 2022).
However, there is more than just this form of collaboration,
where humans and AI are divided based on specialization, with
humans assuming the role of (superior) trainers or gatekeepers.
Theory: Ensembling public managers and AI
This manuscript focuses on a division of labor between humans
and AI without relying on specialization. Following Choudhary
et al. (2025, p. 537), we define ensembling as the “aggregation of
predictions” from both humans and AI to address the same pre­
diction problem, without any division of labor. In other words,
ensembling involves both humans and AI independently solving
the same prediction task, with their predictions subsequently
aggregated to produce a final outcome (Page 2007; Steyvers et
al. 2022). This approach may be particularly effective when nei­
ther agent has a clear advantage in performing a task or its sub-
components, and when neither alone can achieve satisfactory
predictive accuracy for the decisions at hand. Such conditions
are common in managerial decision-making, where the division
of labor is arguably unlikely, and these decisions are often left
in human hands due to path dependencies, thereby overlooking
the potential benefits of ensembling (Choudhary et al. 2025).
In the specific context of many public sector decisions, nei­
ther public managers nor AI has a clear advantage over the
other. Under such circumstances, specialization is typically
not pursued, and such decisions are left entirely to public
managers. As a result, the value that AI can bring to the en­
semble remains untapped. While public managers can uti­
lize certain types of information that are hard to collect for
AI, such as experience, intuition, or human heuristics, AI is
typically superior in estimating the best-fitting function that
can describe the available data. Therefore, this article argues
that ensembling public managers and AI promises to improve
public sector decisions in terms of legitimacy, legality, and ef­
ficacy, if public managers are willing to implement ensembles.
This theoretical argument is developed in four steps: the
first section uses the example of the literature on hiring
decisions to show that it leads to suboptimal outcomes if
decisions containing predictions are reserved exclusively
for public managers. The second describes how ensembling
can unfold both via automation and augmentation. The

Hiring decisions and the potential of ensembling
Personnel selection decisions are screening tasks that involve
prediction, for example, of the future success of a candidate
in a job. They are illustrative cases where neither humans
nor AI have a clear advantage, as neither can achieve per­
fect accuracy in predictions due to uncertainty and in­
complete information (Choudhary et al. 2025). Research
on managers' hiring decisions provides strong evidence for
discriminatory outcomes, for example, due to biases related
to gender or race (Galos and Coppock 2023; Quillian et
al. 2017; Schaerer et al. 2023). Comparable concerns have
been raised about AI hiring decisions, where AI assessments
may replicate and perpetuate existing biases or relate to sta­
tistical discrimination (Köchling and Wehner 2020). In the
public sector context, this harms legitimacy of the public
hiring decisions, as it challenges the idea of representative
bureaucracies (Jankowski, Prokop, and  Tepe 2020) and
undermines principles of impartiality and equitable ac­
cess to public sector jobs (Jakobsen, Løkke, and Keppeler
2023). Discriminatory hiring outcomes violate (constitu­
tional) law and undermine fundamental values of public
administration (Bozeman and Johnson 2015; Frederickson
2015). Finally, biases in hiring harm the efficacy of selec­
tion decisions, leading to needless costs for organizations
(Lippens, Vermeiren, and Baert 2023).
Unfolding public manager-AI ensembles
Table 1 illustrates how previous public administration
approaches to AI, which typically focus on specialization,
differ from the presented theoretical argument of ensembling
public managers and AI in public sector decision-making. The
differences are explained across three forms of AI adoption—
human-only decision-making (no AI adoption), automation,
and augmentation with examples from the context of hiring
in brackets. The gray cells illustrate the focus of ensembling
public managers and AI, which can be formed through either
automation or augmentation.
In specialization approaches, AI either automates tasks
such as parsing CVs and presorting documents, replacing
humans in routine functions, or augments human capabilities
by providing complementary information such as exten­
sive online research on applicants—what Wilcox, Damarin,
and McDonald (2022) call “cybervetting.” Then, a spe­
cialized public manager makes the final decision based on
the AI's output. In contrast, many public sector decisions
are made by ensembles in which multiple humans independ­
ently evaluate the person-job fit of candidates and then ag­
gregate their evaluations. Ensembling by automation replaces
a human agent with AI, while ensembling by augmentation
adds AI evaluation to existing humans.
The design theory of human-AI ensembles offers rele­
vant testable propositions. It expects human-AI ensembles
to be superior to human-only or AI-only ensembles when
(a) sufficient data are available for AI to produce models
that are more accurate than pure chance in prediction, and
(b) humans possess sufficient data that is not accessible
to AI (e.g., experience, heuristics, and intuition of public
managers), which enables them to predict more accurately
than pure chance. Empirical research indicates that this is
the case in hiring processes. AI can predict person-job fit
to some extent (Keppeler 2024), but managers' ecological
rationality and heuristics may sometimes be more effective
at predicting success (Luan, Reb, and Gigerenzer 2019).
Furthermore, the theory expects that human-AI ensembles
are more likely to be formed by augmentation (AI addi­
tional) rather than by automation (replacing a human by AI)
if the predictions made by the humans in the ensemble are
more diverse with respect to each other. Inter-human diver­
sity increases the value of ensembles formed through aug­
mentation. Empirical research suggests that people do in fact
rely (too much) on their intuition in hiring, often making
inferences based on gender or ethnicity (Highhouse 2008;
Van Iddekinge, Lievens, and Sackett 2023), even if they have
access to objective, more reliable information (Hoffman,
Kahn, and Li 2017).

because current AI is unable to outperform humans in such

these contexts are often error-prone (Csaszar and Steinberger
2022), as illustrated by examples of ineffective hiring and
discrimination in the labor market. Ensembling is likely to
improve diversity in predictions by leveraging the “structural
complementarities” between human and AI-based prediction
capabilities (Fügener et al. 2021, p. 1528). Moreover, the ag­
gregation approach in ensembling does not require human-AI
feedback loops, which may help avoid negative consequences
of ineffective human-AI collaboration, such as the loss of
unique human knowledge (Fügener et al. 2021), algorithm
aversion (Burton, Stein, and Jensen 2020), or the alteration of
human predictions (Glickman and Sharot 2024).
Thus, ensembling public managers and AI can improve
public sector decisions because it increases the diversity of
predictions. Research shows that diverse predictions are a
necessary condition for more accurate ensembled predictions.
Following the diversity prediction theorem (Page 2007), the
prediction error of an ensemble is lower than the average in­
dividual error of an ensemble member if the diversity across
individuals is positive. Choudhary et al. (2025, p. 546) present
the following expression:
Ensemble error = Average individual error – diversity
(1)
In general, ensemble techniques try to reduce bias and vari­
ance among ensemble members simultaneously by combining
several of them. Theoretically, the ensemble is expected to out­
perform the average accuracy of its members. The benchmark
is a single estimation task, following the assumption that it
is not possible to determine which ensemble member is most
accurate (Choudhary et al. 2025). Notably, an ensemble does
not always outperform its most accurate member, because
increasing diversity does not automatically improve predic­
tion accuracy. This is because average individual error and di­
versity are not independent—an ensemble's accuracy depends
on both diversity and the accuracy of individual members.
For the context of public sector decisions, this article argues
that ensembling public managers and AI can improve legiti­
macy, legality, and efficacy. Regarding legitimacy, ensembling
is in line with the classic idea that decisions can improve
through
error cancellation through
diverse
predictions.
By defining procedures of aggregating predictions in a
formalized way, the quality of decision processes in terms
of accountability, transparency, inclusiveness, and openness
of processes could be enhanced (Schmidt and Wood 2019).
Public managers stay in the loop, and their discretion is not
limited by an algorithm (Grimmelikhuijsen and Meijer 2022).
Regarding legality, ensembling public managers with AI has
the potential to reduce illegal public sector decisions that are
due to discrimination, potential hostility, or biases of public
managers. Human decisions are often ambiguous and at least
as much of a black box as AI (Bonezzi, Ostinelli, and Melzner
2022). Algorithmic decision-making can be seen as not inher­
ently hostile, at least if AI is built according to the principles
of transparency, verifiability, fair and non-discriminatory data
selection, and adequate algorithmic objectivity (Kleinberg et
al. 2018). Even if both the decisions of public managers and
AI may remain opaque to some extent, ensembling should
help to reduce biases that would trigger illegal discriminatory
decisions.
Regarding efficacy, the literature shows that ensembles gain
predictive accuracy with increasing diversity in predictions.
Predictions are more likely to improve with increased diver­
sity in prediction errors made by different models and if the
ensemble members are better than pure chance in predicting
(Steyvers et al. 2022). Arguably, better, that is, more accurate,
predictions lead to more decision efficacy.
In our ensembling approach, humans and AI first solve the
same prediction task independently, and then their predictions
are aggregated. Although aggregation methods vary (e.g.,
averaging, quorum, and plurality), our empirical study uses
simple averaging, which has been shown to be very effective
(Soll and Larrick 2009).
Ensembling public managers and AI in hiring
decisions
In the following, we focus on ensembling public managers
and AI via augmentation in the context of public hiring. In
theory, ensembling should be beneficial and help to equal out
biases on both sides. Previous research shows that both public
managers and AI may exhibit unwarranted biases concerning
candidate gender and ethnicity during the evaluation of job
candidates.
An extensive body of research documents errors and biases
in human recruiting decisions, based on factors such as age,
disability, ethnicity, gender, and other characteristics (Lippens,
Vermeiren, and Baert 2023). Moreover, extensive research
documents the presence of illegitimate disparities, biases, and
discrimination in bureaucratic decision-making in general
(Bell and Jilke 2024; Jilke and Tummers 2018; Pedersen and
Nielsen 2022). Given a persistent reliance on subjectivity and
intuition in the recruitment process (Highhouse 2008), hiring
decisions are often implicitly biased. For example, in line with
taste-based models of discrimination (Hedegaard and Tyran
2018), managers may implicitly harbor negative stereotypes
toward job candidates of a particular gender or ethnicity,
often manifesting unintentionally. Therefore, we posit the fol­
lowing hypotheses:
H1a: Hiring managers' assessment of person-job fit varies
depending on the gender of a candidate.
H1b: Hiring managers' assessment of person-job fit varies
depending on the ethnicity of a candidate.
Regarding biases in AI's evaluations of job candidates, some
research suggests that employing AI in recruitment could po­
tentially result in biases of similar or even greater severity
than those of managers. AI is not immune to biases and po­
tential discrimination, particularly statistical discrimination
(Hunkenschroer and Luetge 2022). For example, an AI might
erroneously deduce that female or ethnic minority candidates
are less suitable for a specific job due to their current un­
derrepresentation. While some scholars express optimism re­
garding this matter (Charlwood and Guenole 2022), others
voice concerns that AI algorithms may sustain or perpetuate
biased decisions. Studies suggest that algorithms can generate
discriminatory and biased outcomes due to reasons such as
inaccurate training, biased or unrepresentative input data,
or unintended consequences of algorithmic optimization
strategies (Köchling and Wehner 2020). Moreover, the quality

­decision-making processes. Research on decision-makers'
willingness to use decision-support tools draws heavily on
human information processing literature, focusing on the
role of cognitive and decision styles. This body of research
aims to describe how humans process information in order
to design systems that better support human decision-making
(Robey and Taggart 1982). From this perspective, receiving
advice from an AI may alter how individuals process avail­
able information and lead them to adjust their beliefs about
the models used by an AI application (Bauer, von Zahn, and
Hinz 2023). However, a review highlights that people's cog­
nitive styles are shaped by situational factors and that relying
solely on intuitive thinking can exacerbate biases rather than
balance intuition with analytical thought (Burton, Stein, and
Jensen 2020). Thus, more recent studies have moved beyond
cognitive styles and information processing theory to focus
on identifying specific biases, as they can prevent the effective
use of AI in decision-making.
The current literature discusses two types of “black
boxes”: the opaque nature of algorithms and human intuition
(Bonezzi, Ostinelli, and Melzner 2022). A recent meta-analysis
identifies two major challenges in human-AI collaboration
(Vaccaro, Almaatouq, and Malone 2024). Overreliance occurs
when people place excessive trust in AI systems, treating their
suggestions as definitive guidelines without seeking or proc­
essing additional information. Under reliance refers to cases
in which humans disregard AI suggestions due to negative
attitudes toward algorithms. In our study, public managers'
reluctance to embrace AI advice could undermine the imple­
mentation of ensembling. Even if ensembling has the potential
to reduce unwarranted biases in public hiring, its success ulti­
mately depends on public managers' willingness to implement
ensembling with AI, that is, to utilize AI advice like advice
from human colleagues. However, empirical research on this
topic remains limited (Burton, Stein, and Jensen 2020).
Regarding overreliance, some research suggests a prefer­
ence for algorithmic advice over human advice, also known
as algorithm appreciation (Logg, Minson, and Moore
2019). In certain contexts, individuals may over-rely on al­
gorithmic insights, a phenomenon also known as automa­
tion bias or selective adherence (Alon-Barkat and Busuioc
2022). This suggests that managers may have developed
favorable attitudes toward AI, leading them to follow AI
recommendations even when the advice is flawed and poten­
tially ignore conflicting information. Alternatively, they may
selectively adhere to AI advice, particularly when it aligns
with their preexisting beliefs and stereotypes (Selten, Robeer,
and Grimmelikhuijsen 2023).
A contrasting perspective highlights the underutilization of
algorithmic insights, often stemming from individuals' reluc­
tance towards algorithms, even when algorithmic forecasts
may outperform human predictions (Burton, Stein, and
Jensen 2020). Research on algorithm aversion describes that
“although evidence-based algorithms consistently outper­
form human forecasters, people often fail to use them after
learning that they are imperfect” (Dietvorst, Simmons, and
Massey 2018, p. 1155). Individuals tend to underutilize algo­
rithmic advice across various domains, such as investing, med­
ical ­decision-making, and other contexts marked by inherent
uncertainty (Dietvorst and Bharti 2020; Vaccaro, Almaatouq,
and Malone 2024). This reluctance extends to situations
where individuals fear that their preferences, circumstances,
or self-expression may not align with the AI model (Longoni,
Cian, and Kyung 2023). In addition, research shows that
under reliance on AI extends to employees. Employees can
display mistrust toward AI (Glikson and Woolley 2020)
and perceive its decisions as overly simplistic or unfair, even
though algorithmic decisions can be objectively fairer or out­
perform human decisions, for example, in evaluating perfor­
mance or providing feedback (Newman, Fast, and Harmon
2020; Tong et al. 2021).
Beyond these divergent viewpoints, it remains unclear
whether underreliance impedes the implementation of public
manager-AI ensembles. Hiring requires decisions under un­
certainty, where moral integrity, trustworthiness, and fair­
ness are perceived as paramount. People might favor human
decision-­making in such decision contexts. If this is the case,
we expect managers to be unwilling to implement ensembling
procedures. We posit the following hypothesis:
H4: Hiring managers will be less responsive to adjust their
initial assessment of a candidate's person-job fit when the
advice comes from an AI compared to when it comes from
a human manager.
Study 1: Illustrating how ensembling can help
to reduce biases
Study 1 uses and expands data from another published article

analysis of the hypotheses H1–H3. The data come from a field
experiment conducted in a German public organizational set­
ting where an AI was deployed in a recruitment process with
the aim of analyzing the effects of disclosing and deploying an
AI in an employer-driven recruitment process. It found that

there are differences between human and AI assessments of
person-job fit assessments, with a particular focus on gender
and ethnicity. We then use simple averaging as an ensembling
strategy to aggregate the manager's and AI's assessments,
based on research showing that equal-weighted averaging
is a highly effective but underutilized method for combining
predictions (Soll, Palley, and Rader 2022).
Design and method
As part of an employer-driven recruitment process for a
German public utility company, profiles of suitable job
candidates for three hard-to-fill positions (Finance Application
Specialist, Utility Application Specialist, Team Leader Power
Grid) were identified on the social media job platform Xing,
a German-language equivalent of LinkedIn. Given that tradi­
tional job postings had not been successful in finding suitable
candidates, a direct-sourcing AI that had been on the market
for around 10 years was tested.1
The AI used machine learning techniques to search for po­
tential candidates using criteria such as job title, competencies,
and skills from profiles on social media job platforms.
Through this process, the application identified 2,000 job
candidates deemed to possess fundamental suitability for the
positions. In addition, the AI computed a person-job fit score
for each identified candidate, ranging from 0 to 100 percent,
and provided the following information: gender, current em­
ployment in the public sector, distance from the candidate's
current place of residence to hiring organization, and dura­
tion of current employment.
After identifying the 2,000 job candidates by using the
AI, a sub-sample of 695 profiles was randomly selected.
The three hiring managers responsible for the job openings
were then provided with the list of these randomly drawn
job candidates. Subsequently, they rated the person-job fit
of each candidate on a scale ranging from 0 to 100 percent.
Importantly, the managers had access to the same candidate
information as the AI (the listed data and the link to the full
profile on Xing). They were not restricted in terms of data ac­
cess or time. However, they were blinded to the job-fit scores
provided by the AI, that is, they did not receive information
on how the AI determined the person-job fit.
We expanded the original dataset (Keppeler 2024) by using
the online tool Namsor (namsor.app) to code the ethnicity
of the candidates based on their full names. Namsor is com­
monly employed in studies and is a recognized tool for name-
based identification tasks (Sebo 2023). We employed the
“Name US Race” feature, which provides a taxonomy in four
classes for non-US residents: “White,” “Hispanic or Latino,”
“Asian non-Latino,” and “Black non-Latino,” based on the
US Census Bureau Taxonomy (2024).
Results
As illustrated in Table 2, descriptive statistics indicate that
the average person-job fit assessment determined by the
managers was overall lower (35.0 percent) compared to the
assessment by the AI (38.9 percent, see also Supplementary
Appendix A, Figures A1 and A2). Due to the average ap­
proach to ensembling, the ensemble lies in between. Notably,
when considering the intersection of gender and ethnicity, fe­
male non-white candidates receive the lowest person-job fit
assessment when evaluated by managers (21.5 percent). The
AI determines a relatively higher person-job fit for women
(40.6 percent) and non-white candidates (41.3 percent) on
average compared to the managers' assessments (31.0 and
29.2 percent).
As shown in Table 3, the results of the ordinary least squares
(OLS) regression analyses show no significant evidence of
a substantial gender bias for managers (P = .165) or the AI
(P = .395). However, person-job fit is negatively related to
candidates of non-white ethnicity (vs. white) for managers
(P = .035) but positively related to the AI (P = .027). The en­
semble shows no significant evidence of bias (see also fig. 1).
Additional subsample analyses by job position (see
Supplementary Appendix A, Table A1) show a negative corre­
lation between person-job fit and (non-white) ethnicity among
managers for the two specialist jobs (Finance Application
and Utility Application), and a negative correlation between
person-job fit and female gender among managers for the
leadership position (Team Leader Power Grid). The person-
job fit determined by the AI is negatively related to the female
gender for one of the specialist jobs (Finance Application) (see
Supplementary Appendix A, Table A2).

To sum up, both human and AI assessments appear to be
at least partially biased, though their biases seem to differ
in nature and direction. In line with previous research, this
divergence may stem from two key sources of variation in
human and AI predictions—and consequently in prediction
errors and biases: differences in models and differences in
data. Regarding models, human prediction in screening and
hiring often relies on heuristics, intuition, and subjectivity
(Highhouse 2008). In contrast, contemporary algorithmic
modeling, such as the proprietary machine learning system
used in this study, employs statistical modeling to generate
predictions (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering,
and Medicine 2024). In this case, the AI assessed person-job
fit by analyzing textual data from job advertisements and so­
cial media profiles. Regarding data, humans and AI differ in
their access: while both can process shared sources like social
media profiles, AI operates on a vastly larger scale. However,
humans have access to tacit, experiential knowledge, such as
insights about team dynamics and how a potential candidate
may fit into an existing team, which remains difficult to codify
and largely inaccessible to contemporary AI (Fügener et al.
2021).
Ensembles typically outperform individual predictions by
leveraging diversity and reducing errors (Hong and Page
2004). This principle holds true even if neither humans
nor AI achieve high accuracy on their own, as long as
their predictions exceed random chance. The diversity of
predictions—including errors—enhances decision quality. As
illustrated in study 1, combining human and AI predictions
can improve accuracy by balancing individual biases with di­
versity. However, such potential benefits of AI in mitigating
biases can only be realized if managers consider implementing
ensembling.
Study 2: Testing the willingness to implement
ensembling
For study 2, we collected data in a survey experiment with
a sample of German managers with hiring responsibilities
(n = 538) to test preregistered hypothesis 4 according to the
preanalysis plan. While the sample of public managers is
fairly representative in terms of gender and age, it shows some
variation at the federal level (see Supplementary Appendix
E, Table A8). By designing our experiment with rich, real­
istic, and contextual nuances, we aimed to provide a com­
prehensive yet conservative test of the expected treatment
effects (Brutger et al. 2022). We examine the extent to which
managers are willing to take AI advice into their personal
assessments of a candidate's person-job fit in comparison to
advice from a human colleague. Participants first evaluated
the person-job fit of four job candidates. Afterward, they
received advice on the candidate's person-job fit, randomly
presented as either AI-generated or human advice, and were
then allowed to adjust their initial person-job fit assessment.
This method enables us to quantify the WOA, measuring the
extent to which participants alter their own judgement based
on exposure to advice (Dawes, Faust, and Meehl 1989; Logg,
Minson, and Moore 2019).
Design and method
The survey experiment proceeded through six steps. First, the
survey presented participants with a real-life job advertise­
ment for the position as finance application specialist from
study 1 (Supplementary Appendix B). Second, participants
were shown four realistic profiles of potential job candidates,
sourced from the Xing profiles sample in study 1. The order
of profiles was random for each participant. The four profiles
(see Supplementary Appendix C, Table A3) varied in gender
(female, male) and ethnic origin (white, non-white) and were
chosen based on the following criteria:
•	 Two female (one white, one non-white) and two male
(one white, one non-white) profiles were chosen.
•	 Complete online profiles were chosen, with profile
pictures and comprehensive CV data, including language
skills. For privacy reasons, we have replaced profile
pictures with stock photos and replaced other identifying
information.
•	 We selected the online profiles for which study 1 found
the greatest difference in the person-job fit ratings be­
tween the public managers and the AI, with neither
rating equal to 100 or 0 percent. This approach aimed to
ensure variability between the participants' ratings and
the AI advice provided. In all four cases, the person-job
fit determined by the AI was higher than the manager's
assessment.
Third, participants were tasked with evaluating the person-
job fit (0–100 percent) of each of the four job candidates
(assessment A1). Fourth, as shown in Table 5, participants
were randomized into four experimental groups, consistent
with a 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial design. On the first
dimension, we manipulated the source of advice (main treat­
ment). All four groups were exposed to an identical numerical

no significant difference between participants employed in
public versus private sectors, and no overall average effect of
either AI advice compared to human advice or the debiasing
treatment.
Auxiliary analyses
We observe a significant negative association among covariates
between self-perceived task expertise and the WOA. We also
see significant positive associations between trust in the advice
and the WOA, and between trust in AI in personnel selection
and the WOA. Additional explorative analyses (Supplementary
Appendix E, Table A7) indicate a significant interaction be­
tween the experimental assignment and trust in AI in personnel
selection: In the AI and debiasing group (relative to the human
and debiasing group), trust in AI in personnel selection is asso­
ciated with an even higher WOA. In other words, participants
who report to agree with the statement “If an artificial intel­
ligence provides me with an assessment of applicants for per­
sonnel selection, I trust this assessment” are more likely to
place a relatively higher weight on the AI advice (see fig. 4).2
Discussion
Are public managers with hiring responsibilities willing
to incorporate AI-generated assessments when evaluating
candidates? Our findings provide no compelling evidence
to suggest otherwise. If anything, they indicate that public
managers may tend to assign greater, rather than less, weight
to AI-generated advice compared to that of a human colleague
when assessing a candidate's person-job fit. This contrasts
with our preregistered hypothesis H4, which was informed
by insights from the algorithm aversion literature. However,
our results challenge this assumption, indicating that public
managers may, if anything, be more receptive to AI-based ad­
vice than to input from human colleagues.
Still, the notion that public managers are more responsive
to AI advice than to human advice should be interpreted with
appropriate caution. Our results indicate a significant differ­
ence only when we compare the “AI” and “Human” experi­
mental groups, which also received an additional debiasing

Concluding remarks
This article presents the theory of ensembling humans and
AI to public administration research and bridges it with the
specificities of public sector decision-making by discussing its
potential in terms of legitimacy, legality, and efficacy. Unlike
the specialization approaches that dominate the current lit­
erature, ensembling lets both public managers and the AI
independently perform complete prediction tasks and then ag­
gregate their results. For public sector decisions where neither
humans nor AI are superior in their prediction accuracy, this
is likely to lead to the described improvements. As research
on biases and noise in human decision-making indicates
(Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein 2021), many public sector
decisions fulfill this criterion and the other conditions for ef­
fective ensembling (Choudhary et al. 2025), namely:
(1)	 Adequate data are available for AI to produce models
that are more accurate than pure chance in predicting
and
(2)	 Humans possess adequate data that is not accessible
for AI (e.g., experience, heuristics, and intuition of
public managers), which enables them to predict more
accurately than pure chance.
Empirically, we illustrated two critical aspects surrounding the
potential of ensembling public managers and AI: the different
biases that public managers and AI may have in the context of
evaluating candidates, and the inclination of public managers
to implement ensembling with AI in their decision-making.
Ensembling can reduce biases (illustrated by
study 1)
Study 1 illustrates what many studies found before (e.g.,
Lippens, Vermeiren, and Baert 2023): Public managers, and
partly AI, may be biased in assessing job candidates. The data
also illustrates previous findings that humans and AI have
different approaches to decision tasks and different biases
(Luan, Reb, and Gigerenzer 2019). Thus, ensembling appears
to be helpful in balancing both human and algorithmic biases.
Despite the limitations of study 1, which analyzed only three
managers and 695 profiles, it effectively demonstrates how
simple averaging of human and AI ratings can produce less
biased decisions than either source alone—particularly valu­
able when neither has a clear advantage, making ensembling
effective. In this context, biased hiring decisions harm legiti­
macy by challenging the idea of representative bureaucracies
and undermining core public values such as impartiality or
equitable access to public sector jobs. Discriminatory hiring
outcomes are also illegal in almost all Western countries. In
addition, biased hiring creates organizational costs through
potential mismatches, turnover, and lower performance. It is
worth noting that ensembling does not require high levels of
decision accuracy—in principle, accuracy above chance is suf­
ficient. Rather, the idea of ensembling is that the diversity in
predictions due to differences in the data and models used
by humans and AI “is likely to (but is not guaranteed to) im­
prove on either alone” (Choudhary et al. 2025, p. 550).

Willingness to implement ensembling with AI
(tested by study 2)
Like other forms of human-AI collaboration, ensembling typ­
ically requires that humans are willing to implement such
procedures, that is, take advice from an AI at least as seri­
ously as advice from a human colleague. Study 2 supports the
potential for adopting ensembling. Decision-makers did not
differ significantly in their WOA when comparing human and
AI advice. However, when reminded of existing unlawful dis­
crimination—and particularly when they placed greater trust
in AI in the decision-making context—participants assigned
greater weight to algorithmic advice than to human advice.
What might explain the moderating influence of the
debiasing cue? One possible explanation is “algorithm appre­
ciation,” a term coined by Logg, Minson, and Moore (2019),
which suggest that people, in certain situations and for cer­
tain tasks, tend to prefer algorithmic advice. In our study,
participants may have interpreted the debiasing cue about
labor market discrimination as a signal that human advice
could be biased and thus placed less weight on human advice
(which had the lowest average WOA across all experimental
groups). Conversely, because AI in public hiring remains rel­
atively novel (with only 1.7 percent of our sample reporting
prior AI use in hiring), participants may not have associated
the debiasing cue with AI. As a result, they may have placed
greater weight on AI advice.
Ensembling theory suggests that human-AI collaborations
are more likely to form through augmentation rather than
automation when human predictions show diversity. The
premise that “augmented human-algorithm decision systems
outperform both single human and single algorithm deci­
sion makers” (Burton, Stein, and Jensen 2020, p. 229) seems
plausible. Our comparison of human-human and human-AI
collaboration reflects realistic multi-agent public sector
decision-­making. These collaborative decisions raise account­
ability issues that become more complex when AI is involved,
extending beyond decision-makers and political leaders to in­
clude AI developers and owners (Yuan and Chen 2025).
Implications for policymaking
The results imply that policy approaches to the introduction
of AI tools should not only be based on the assumption of
specialization but should allow for ensembling approaches.
This perspective is often overlooked and appears to be insuf­
ficiently considered in risk-based regulation approaches, such
as the European AI Act. The presented study assessed the use
of an AI in the context of employment; a so-called “high risk”
application requiring registration in an EU database and a pre-
market assessment (Footnote 14, European Parliament 2024,
p. 12). This rather strict regulatory approach might limit en­
semble scenarios to a very narrow set of cases, infringing the
outlined potential of improving decision outcomes.
Furthermore,
when
public
organizations
consider
ensembling their public managers with AI for certain
decisions, it is advisable not to compare the potential use
of AI to an idealistic scenario, such as a world without dis­
crimination in hiring. Arguably, the relevant counterfactual is
the status quo, with its pressing problems of discrimination
in hiring and other public sector decisions. Taking into ac­
count the status quo of the specific context allows us to assess
both the opportunities and risks of, for example, ensembled
decision-making. Otherwise, relevant potential for improving
equity and fairness in decision-making processes may be
overlooked.
Limitations and future research perspectives
The present research has some limitations. First, it analyzes
a specific AI tool and setting (three specific positions in
Germany). While this helps to illustrate a theoretical mech­
anism and guide future research, a broader research agenda
is needed to fully understand AI and its impact on adminis­
trative decision-making. Future research could explore how
human-AI ensembling can reduce bias and noise in both au­
tomated and augmented decision-making, as study 1 only
combines isolated data collections and does not capture the
full decision-making process. Future research should compare
human-AI ensembles against human-only ensembles (the cur­
rent status quo) in real-world settings. In this context, it is
important to investigate human-AI collaboration in public or­
ganizations, regardless of whether decision-making structures
in which humans and AI collaborate follow ensembling or
specialized approaches.
Second, our study used conventional machine learning
rather than newer generative AI systems, which are now rap­
idly evolving. These models can also contribute to highly com­
plex, strategic decisions (Doshi et al. 2024). Future research
should explore whether strategic decisions in the public sector
could benefit from ensembling with generative AI, especially
as recent fieldwork suggests that these systems significantly
impact human-AI collaboration and expertise (Dell’Acqua et
al. 2025).
Third, while study 2 was preregistered, the hypotheses and
analyses of study 1 are exploratory and only illustrative. Study
1 cannot be seen as robust evidence for the design theory of
human-AI ensembling, but rather as an illustrative example
and starting point for a research agenda on ensembling in
public sector decision-making. Future research could focus
not only on empirical tests, but also on the philosophical
implications of ensembling, other forms of human-AI col­
laboration, and the ethical dilemmas and challenges (Haeri
et al. 2022) or regulatory issues related to AI (Nitzberg and
Zysman 2022). It is crucial to move beyond potentially mis­
leading comparisons to idealized scenarios (e.g., ignoring
existing unlawful hiring discrimination) and provide more
ecologically valid evidence. To this end, research could make
greater use of WOA and similar approaches to better under­
stand the ensembling of managers and AI. Future public ad­
ministration research would benefit from exploring various
forms of human-AI collaboration and the associated changes
in organizational context, collective agency, systemic interac­
tion, and outcomes in public service provision (Hillebrand,
Raisch, and Schad 2025).
Fourth, our results do not prove that discriminatory
differences in ratings may reflect legitimate CV differences as­
sociated with gender and ethnicity. Nevertheless, our studies
illustrate the potential benefits of ensembling and are con­
sistent with existing research. Fügener et al. (2021) found that
AI advice improves individual and small group performance
on predictive tasks with rich data, such as hiring, while recent
meta-analyses confirm that human-AI ensembles can out­
perform either component alone (Vaccaro, Almaatouq, and
Malone 2024). Future research should explore human-AI col­
laboration where humans possess critical knowledge that is
inaccessible to AI, such as team dynamics.

Finally, a key question for future research is whether

knowledge about how to design, deploy, and disclose AI in a

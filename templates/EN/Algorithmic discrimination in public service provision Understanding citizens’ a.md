Abstract
As public bodies increasingly adopt AI technologies in their work, there is simultaneously growing attention to the risk that the reliance on the
technology may introduce biases and produce discriminatory administrative outcomes, as demonstrated by multiple real-world cases. Our
contribution addresses a core theoretical puzzle: with AI algorithms being increasingly embedded across public services, we lack crucial knowl-
edge about how citizens assign responsibility to public organizations for algorithmic failures and discrimination in public services compared to
human discrimination. This speaks to key questions as to whether organizational responsibility attribution mechanisms and public demand for
consequences fundamentally change in the context of algorithmic governance. Addressing this gap, we examine whether individual citizens are
less likely to attribute responsibility for algorithmic versus human discrimination in public service provision. Building on psychology literature,
we further theorize potential mechanisms that underlie these effects and shape citizens’ responses. We investigate these research questions
through a pre-registered survey experiment conducted in the Netherlands (N = 2,483). Our findings indicate that public organizations are not
held to a lower responsibility standard for algorithmic compared to human discrimination. Technological delegation to AI does not allow public
bodies to bypass responsibility for discriminatory outcomes. However, we find that citizens assign more responsibility for algorithmic discrimi-
nation when the algorithm is developed in-house rather than externally. This could lead to the emergence of accountability deficits pertaining to
technological outsourcing.
Key words: artificial intelligence; accountability; responsibility attribution; discrimination; algorithmic discrimination.
Introduction
In the Netherlands, an investigative journalism report
(Lighthouse Reports 2023a; Wired 2023) revealed that the
municipality of Rotterdam had relied for years on a dis-
criminatory AI algorithm to predict welfare fraud. Primarily,
residents of migrant origin were flagged for highly intru-
sive fraud investigations; supposedly, “mathematical proof”
migrants were more likely to commit fraud. Upon investi-
gation, it turned out that the algorithm used, among others,
Dutch language skills as a risk indicator for fraud. The
system’s training data collapsed deliberate fraud and genuine
mistakes into one category. Residents with poor language
skills were simply more likely to commit paperwork mistakes.
By conflating genuine mistakes and actual fraud, the algo-
rithm wrongly learnt that poor Dutch language skills are a
useful predictor for fraud, essentially “convert[ing] ‘honest
mistakes’ into learned associations between poor Dutch skills
and propensity for fraud” (Wired 2023). This is not an isolated
incident but comes in the aftermath of the “childcare benefits
scandal,” where the reliance on an AI algorithm by the Dutch
tax authority similarly resulted in structural discrimination,
with nationality used as an indicator in fraud determinations.
Over 30,000 victims, wrongly flagged by the algorithm,
faced benefits termination and forced repayments, resulting
in severe financial problems, bankruptcies, mental health is-
sues, and children removed from their families (Geiger 2021;
Volkskrant 2020). Similar cases of discrimination related to
the use of AI algorithms have been documented across a mul-
tiplicity of jurisdictions, among others in the US, UK, France,
and Sweden, with sweeping effects (e.g., Le Monde 2023;
Lighthouse Reports 2023b; Lighthouse Reports and Svenska
Dagbladet 2024; Obermeyer et al. 2019; The Guardian 2024).
These examples are illustrative of the impactful yet less dis-
cernible ways in which AI algorithms, by virtue of their com-
plexity and associated opacity, can come to encode biases and
perpetuate them, giving rise to significant concerns about AI
tools’ potential to exacerbate disparate outcomes, discrimina-
tion, and social inequity (Eubanks 2018; Peeters 2023; Ruijer
et al. 2023). These concerns become especially problematic in
administrative contexts involving high-stakes, life-changing
decisions. When such tools fail, by virtue of their automated
nature, failures get replicated and affect large portions of the
public all in one sweep, with serious negative consequences of
a large magnitude (Busuioc 2021).

In this connection, our contribution seeks to advance a core
theoretical puzzle: as AI algorithms are increasingly embedded
across public services, we lack crucial knowledge about how
citizens assign responsibility to public organizations for al-
gorithmic failures and discrimination in public services
compared to human discrimination. This is important as the
extent to which individual citizens perceive public organiza-
tions as responsible and attribute responsibility affects public
demands for accountability. We ask: when AI algorithms
fail, how do citizens respond to discriminatory administra-
tive outcomes linked to algorithm use in public services? Are
they likely to view public organizations less responsible for
algorithmic discrimination in public services, compared with
human-based discrimination? Essentially, how does the al-
gorithmic nature of the agent shape citizen perceptions of
responsibility attribution and ensuing demands for account-
ability of public bodies deploying suboptimal systems? This
crucially speaks to key questions as to whether blame attribu-
tion mechanisms for organizational failures become severed
in the context of algorithmic governance. We seek to under-
stand to what extent adverse administrative outcomes trans-
late into organizational responsibility attribution and public
demand for consequences, or to the contrary, whether this
fundamental accountability link is disrupted in this setting.
Resolving this theoretical puzzle gains high relevance in a
context where modern governance is increasingly one of gov-
ernance by algorithm (Grimmelikhuijsen and Meijer 2022;
Keppeler 2024; Peeters 2020; Schiff, Schiff, and Pierson 2021;
Yeung and Lodge 2019), with AI adoption reshaping the na-
ture of public administrations in non-negligible and pro-
found ways (Busuioc 2021; Peeters and Widlak 2023; Young,
Bullock, and Lecy 2019). Increasingly, AI algorithms take
automated or partially automated decisions that used to be
the sole domain of civil servants and street-level bureaucrats
(Bullock 2019). This is the case not only for so-called “pro-
duction agencies” (Wilson 1989)—large-scale bureaucracies
that routinely make standardized decisions but also increas-
ingly traditionally high discretion tasks that are impactful and
consequential for citizens are being “colonized” by automa-
tion and AI solutions in pursuit of efficiency and performance
gains (Bullock, Young, and Wang 2020; Busuioc 2021).
What is more, the relevance of these questions is fur-
ther compounded in view of recent research in psychology
conducted in the private sector context, which suggests
individuals are less concerned about discriminatory outcomes
when caused by AI algorithms compared with human discrim-
ination, and accordingly less likely to assign responsibility in
the former case (Bigman et al. 2023; Feier, Gogoll, and Uhl
2022; Shank, DeSanti, and Maninger 2019; Wilson, Stefanik,
and Shank 2022). These studies theorize that this may be
largely explained by a mechanism of reduced attribution of
prejudicial motivation to algorithms compared to human ac-
tors. Essentially, a perceived lack of intent of algorithms to
discriminate underlies these effects.
To date, there is no empirical research that elucidates
whether these findings also extend to a public policy set-
ting, yet if they were to similarly hold for the public sector
and to the attribution of responsibility to public organiza-
tions, the consequences thereof would be highly problem-
atic from a public accountability and organizational point
of view, potentially letting public bodies “off the hook” for
discriminatory outcomes. It would effectively amount to a
relegation of accountability, allowing public bodies to effec-
tively re-write the parameters of their own accountability and
blame through technological delegation, as well as de facto
reward and incentivize the continued adoption of suboptimal
algorithmic systems, reinforcing patterns of inequality in
public service provision. Such patterns would not necessarily
need to be intentional either. Reduced responsibility attribu-
tion could serve to disincentivize organizations from being
mindful of these matters and expend organizational resources
on overseeing, redressing, and anticipating them.
To address these significant research gaps, we preregistered
and conducted a randomized survey experiment in the
Netherlands (N = 2,483). We use a vignette design to ex-
amine whether citizens attribute less responsibility to public
organizations for discriminatory outcomes resulting from the
reliance on algorithmic systems compared to human-based
discrimination, and whether they are less likely to demand
accountability and tangible consequences. We also com-
pare between algorithms developed in-house and those de-
veloped externally by a private party to test the hypothesis
that individuals are less likely to attribute responsibility for
organizations in the outsourced case. The effect of the latter
on mechanisms of responsibility attribution is an important
aspect for investigation in a context where public bodies
often rely on private vendors for AI technologies deployed for
core governmental tasks. We further develop and test several
mediating mechanisms—namely, perceptions of the inten-
tion to discriminate, and of control over the discriminatory
outcome—to gain theoretical understanding about what
underlies these differences.
Our findings indicate that public organizations are not held
to a lower responsibility standard for algorithmic compared
to human discrimination. However, we do find that citi-
zens assign more responsibility for algorithmic discrimina-
tion when the algorithm is developed in-house, rather than
outsourced to a private company. Our findings further sug-
gest that perceptions of reduced influence of the public organ-
ization over the outcome underlie this effect. This raises the
risk that outsourcing may nevertheless allow public organi-
zations to sidestep accountability for detrimental outcomes.
The adoption of AI algorithms in public service
provision
Advocated as low-cost, scalable technological solutions that
can improve the efficiency of administrative processes and
streamline citizen-state interactions, AI tools’ popularity is
bringing about a technological revolution in public govern-
ance, as their adoption reaches out to an ever-broader array
of administrative tasks. Broadly speaking, “a set of tools that
display (or can even surpass) human-level performance on
given tasks traditionally associated with human intelligence”
(Alon-Barkat and Busuioc 2023, 153), AI systems are set
to become a staple of public service provision in the years
to come. Beyond efficiency considerations, one of the core
promises driving AI adoption across public sectors is that the
reliance on such tools should purportedly lead to more objec-
tive and accurate decision-making, doing away with the many
“deficits” of human judgment. According to proponents, AI
algorithms will “prevent unequal treatment,” “reduce error,”
and “counteract or even eliminate cognitive biases,” with
advocates advancing AI adoption as a solution to biases and

discrimination already present in our institutions (Sunstein
2022, 1175).
The current realities of algorithm use in public sector
decision-making have thus far however, proven to be consid-
erably less glamorous and a lot more harmful, with recurrent
examples of failure and discrimination linked to algorithm
deployments arising across jurisdictions (see e.g., Amnesty
International 2021; Citron 2008; Lighthouse Reports 2022,
2023a, 2023b). Studies have established that AI algorithms
can learn and replicate a whole range of biases—ranging from
(historical) biases present in our society and its institutions,
to the biases and heuristics of their designers, compounded
by specific model choices and training data skewness (e.g.,
Barocas and Selbst 2016; Bender et al. 2021; Benjamin 2023;
Buolamwini and Gebru 2018)—or introduce new biases alto-
gether such as biases arising in human–algorithm interactions
(Green and Chen 2019; Alon-Barkat and Busuioc 2023; cf.
Keppeler et al. 2025). Paradoxically, algorithms can exacer-
bate and reinforce some of the very biases they were meant
to remedy; this time, however, on a large scale, coupled with
intractable difficulties in diagnosing failures. Often, it is the
already marginalized and vulnerable communities that bear
the brunt of harmful deployments.
This points at the need for effective oversight of the use
of algorithms in administrative contexts. However, this is
often easier said than done, as it has been established that
there are serious challenges for accountability in relation to
the use of AI algorithms in public governance (Busuioc 2021;
Cobbe, Veale, and Jatinder 2023; Elliott and MacCarthaigh
2025; Grimmelikhuijsen and Meijer 2022; Wieringa 2020)
for a variety of intractable reasons. Algorithmic opaque-
ness, the sheer size of the parameter space, and the technical
complexity of advanced AI algorithms effectively render
these so-called “black boxes,” obscuring the reasons behind
decisions (Burrell 2016; Pasquale 2015; Rudin 2019). These
specificities of AI algorithms—opacity, convoluted decisional
pathways, coupled with their self-learning nature—severely
limit our ability to retrieve and understand how an algo-
rithmic decision came about. These challenges are addition-
ally compounded by secrecy—the typically proprietary nature
of AI systems, including when deployed across public sectors
(“intentional opacity,” Bloch-Wehba 2021; Burrell 2016). At
the same time, potential technical solutions to AI opacity—
be they in the form of algorithmic audits, explainable AI
approaches, or reverse engineering of models—come with se-
rious limitations, failing to adequately address these concerns
(e.g., Birhane et al. 2024; Bordt et al. 2022; Busuioc et al.
2023; Lakkaraju and Bastani 2020; Obermeyer et al. 2019).
A fundamental and related concern in this connection is
whether the public actually perceives public organizations to
be responsible for algorithmic discriminatory outcomes and
whether citizens—as the ultimate “principals” within dem-
ocratic delegation chains (Strøm 2000)—have the drive to
hold government organizations accountable for algorithmic
failures. Normatively, any exercise of public power demands
meaningful checks and safeguards of that power—whatever
form it may take, whether automated or analog. Public ac-
countability pertains to matters of public concern: the ex-
ercise of public powers, the conduct of public bodies and
institutions, and the implementation and discharge of public
services (Bovens, Goodin, and Schillemans 2014). Public ac-
countability is fundamentally about overseeing—and placing
checks on—the exercise of public authority (see more broadly,
Bertelli 2012; Bovens 2007; Busuioc and Schillemans 2015;
Mulgan 2000; Overman and Schillemans 2022). Chains of
delegation (from citizens to elected political representatives
all the way to public servants) are to be mirrored by chains
of accountability running in the opposite direction (Strøm
2000).
Accountability is closely linked to notions of responsibility
(Bovens 1998), in that accountability should ensue when
actions and behaviors that fall short of mandated expecta-
tions are attributable or imputable to specific actors or fall
within what is perceived as their (formal) responsibility. In
other words, accountability is enacted within “parameters of
blame and responsibility,” follows actions and behaviors that
are deemed blameworthy in view of established mandates
and prevailing societal norms and public expectations (Black
2010). When public actions and decisions fall short of ex-
pectations, negative consequences should ensue: “account-
ability relations are important venues for delivering blame
in case things go wrong. Being accountable means being re-
sponsible, which, in turn, means having to bear the blame”
(Bovens 2005, 189). Such consequences can take a variety of
forms, formal or informal in nature, and with more or less
“bite” (from a slap on the hand, “naming and shaming” to
budget cuts or dismissals), reflecting the perceived gravity of
the transgressive behavior.
The algorithmic context, however, stands to complicate
attributions of responsibility. The act of technological dele-
gation introduces a new link in the traditional chain of del-
egation, as administrative actors essentially delegate part of
the tasks or the decision-making process to algorithmic sys-
tems whose functioning is often a technical “black box,” as
discussed above, and as such, challenging to oversee. Added
to this, the distributed and modular nature of AI technologies,
with multiple actors contributing to system functionality, in-
tricately complicates matters of responsibility attribution
for failure, fragmenting responsibility and reducing visibility
(Cobbe, Veale, and Jatinder 2023). Moreover, the proprietary
nature of many algorithmic systems outsourced from private
vendors obscures system functioning from both citizens and
public bodies deploying them alike (Bloch-Wehba 2021). This
can seriously confound and blur lines of responsibility for
failures or for discriminatory outcomes, as chains of delega-
tion become more muddled, dispersed, and hybrid in nature.
Notions of responsibility attribution and blame allocation po-
tentially become tangled up and confused with the inclusion
of opaque synthetic agents in the decision-making process,
with the potential to obfuscate accountability for failures.
In this context, investigating public perceptions of respon-
sibility attribution becomes especially relevant: if the public
were, as a result, to become apathetic to discrimination in its
algorithmic iteration, this becomes a systemic failure of ac-
countability in view of their role as the ultimate “principals”
in accountability chains. Citizen perceptions are ultimately
the key concern from a democratic government perspective,
and indeed, citizen (“third party”) perceptions are critical for
policy support (Langer and Landers 2021) and fundamen-
tally, what makes public accountability “tick.”
Attribution of responsibility for algorithmic
versus human discrimination
To the best of our knowledge, this topic has not been directly
studied in the public sector context. Yet, several psychology

studies have examined this in private sector contexts. To date,
the most comprehensive research on individuals’ responses to
algorithmic versus human discrimination has been conducted
by Bigman et al. (2023). In a series of experimental psy-
chology studies, the researchers postulate that people tend to
be less “morally outraged” by discrimination caused by an al-
gorithm, so-called “algorithmic outrage deficit.” They further
suggest that a motivation attribution mechanism underlies
these differences (Gray, Young, and Waytz 2012; Malle,
Guglielmo, and Monroe 2014). In the case of human dis-
crimination, people may assume the discriminatory outcomes
are linked to decision-makers’ prejudiced motivation, that
is, ingrained prejudices leading to intentional actions and
outcomes that are discriminatory. By contrast, algorithms
lack agency and thus people are less likely to ascribe their
outputs to prejudiced motivation (Bigman and Gray 2018;
Shank and DeSanti 2018; Shank, DeSanti, and Maninger
2019). By extension, they theorize that people are also less
likely to attribute prejudiced motivation to the corresponding
organization and more likely to assume the discrimination
was unintentional, which reduces the organization’s perceived
responsibility.
Bigman et al. (2023) put these expectations to test across eight
studies using a vignette survey-experimental design, comparing
between human and algorithmic discrimination conditions.
They find consistently that participants are less likely to ascribe
prejudiced motivation to the AI algorithm compared with a
human specialist. Their empirical findings with respect to how
algorithms affect people’s moral judgements toward the com-
pany are somewhat more mixed. While in one study (study 3),
they do not find participants’ moral outrage toward the com-
pany is affected by the fact that discrimination is caused by
an algorithm, subsequent studies (studies 4 and 8) found that
participants in the algorithmic condition were less likely to
perceive the company as acting out of intentional prejudice.
Correspondingly, participants were also less likely to view the
company as blameworthy and to demand punishment and
legal liability. Altogether, based on these findings, this research
concludes that individuals “are less likely to hold the organi-
zation responsible” for algorithmic versus human discrimina-
tion (Bigman et al. 2023, 4) and warns that “people’s defenses
against injustice might be lowered when the agent is an algo-
rithm, making it easier for discrimination to go unnoticed and
unopposed” (Bigman et al. 2023, 24).
Additional experimental studies similarly conducted in
mostly non-public settings, also tend to suggest that people
judge AI algorithms less strictly than humans in instances of
moral violation outcomes and attribute less responsibility
(Shank, DeSanti, and Maninger 2019; Wilson, Stefanik,
and Shank 2022). In line with Bigman et al. (2023), these
studies similarly find that individuals tend to perceive human
decision-makers as more intentional, responsible, and blame-
worthy compared to AI algorithms for wrongdoings in real-
world situations.
Taken jointly, this emerging psychology literature indicates
that people tend to attribute less intentionality and, accord-
ingly, less responsibility to algorithms. While perhaps not that
surprising that algorithms are perceived as lacking intention-
ality in discriminatory outcomes (we are talking of artifacts,
after all), from a public administration and public accounta-
bility perspective, how this links back to responsibility attri-
bution at the organizational level in a public policy context
becomes especially relevant and in need of investigation.
Based on this previous literature, we hypothesize that:
H1: Citizens view public organizations as less respon-
sible for discriminatory outcomes if these discriminatory
outcomes result from algorithms (“algorithmic discrimina-
tion”), compared to humans (“human-based discrimina-
tion”).
H2a: The reduction in perceived responsibility attributed to
public organizations for algorithmic discrimination (H1) is
mediated by citizens’ perception of a lack of intent of the
algorithm to discriminate (compared to humans).
H2b: The reduction in perceived responsibility attributed to
public organizations for algorithmic discrimination (H1) is
mediated by citizens’ perception of a lack of intent of the
public organization to discriminate.
Whether citizens believe the discriminatory outcome was
intentional or unintentional, their judgment about the
organization’s responsibility will also be shaped by their
perceptions of the organization’s capacity to prevent it
(Malle, Guglielmo, and Monroe 2014). In other words, it
depends on the degree to which they believe organizations
have influence over the discriminatory outcome. Public ad-
ministration studies on responsibility attribution for public
organizations in cases of delegation similarly highlight the
role of the perceived influence of the public organization over
the outcome as a relevant consideration in shaping people’s
judgements of organizational responsibility (Nielsen and
Moynihan 2017; Van der Voet and Rimkutė 2023).
The reliance on AI algorithmic systems, as noted above, can
be conceptualized as an instance of delegation, this time in its
technological iteration (“technological delegation”), where
critical tasks are entrusted to algorithmic systems. In the case
of AI algorithmic systems, given their technical complexity and
opacity, as noted above, as well as gaps in technological knowl-
edge among managers, overseeing their functioning becomes
especially challenging. At the same time, by virtue of their au-
tomated nature, algorithms have scaling effects (a discrimina-
tory algorithm will impact many more decisions at once than
one discriminating civil servant). Failure in this context comes
with significant systemic and even cascading effects, potentially
posing serious control challenges for deploying organizations.
Thus, we hypothesize that citizens attribute less responsi-
bility to organizations for algorithmic versus human discrim-
ination also because they may view organizations as having
less control over algorithmic systems and accordingly, less in-
fluence over the discriminatory outcome.
Thus, on this theoretical basis, we postulate a third
mediating mechanism:
H2c: The reduction in perceived responsibility attributed to
public organizations for algorithmic discrimination (H1) is
mediated by citizens’ perception of a reduced influence of
the public organization over the outcome.

the fact that AI algorithms used by public bodies are not
necessarily developed in-house but routinely also devel-

Dimand et al. 2023; Dutch Data Protection Authority/

In other words, there can be controllability effects arising
not only from technological delegation but also due to the
outsourcing of the AI algorithm development itself outside
the organization.
Contracting out comes with widely recognized account-
ability challenges (Johnston, Romzek, and Wood 2004;
Mulgan 2006; Romzek and Johnston 2005, 2010) and re-
lated challenges of blame attribution (Marvel and Girth
2016; Piatak, Mohr, and Leland 2017). Outsourcing places
service delivery outside organizational hierarchy, resulting
in information asymmetries (Dickinson and Yates 2023)
and reduced direct organizational control over task execu-
tion, compared to public service delivery, where services are
subsumed in traditional hierarchies and chains of command:
“Contracting out (…) imposes a definite break in this chain
of general oversight and accountability. Responsibility for
service provision is placed in the hands of people who are
not under the day-to-day control of department managers
and are not subject to open-ended direction from ministers
or their officials” (Mulgan 1997, 108; see also Marvel and
Girth 2016).
Relatedly, this can be expected to impact perceptions of
responsibility, blame attribution, and culpability. Blame at-
tribution theories underscore that perceptions of an actor’s
influence over an outcome affect attributions of blame
(Alicke 2000; Schlenker et al. 1994). Findings on this in
the context of outsourcing are not straightforward, how-
ever. For instance, Marvel and Girth (2016) find that while
citizens view political actors as having more control over
public service delivery when services are provided by a
public organization (as opposed to a private body), the
difference between sectors only has a modest and indirect
causal effect on attributions of blame and responsibility. By
contrast, Piatak, Mohr, and Leland (2017) find that “if cit-
izens are given information about the context and who is
in control of the service, they attribute blame accordingly”
(976); and reiterated: “controllability plays a large role in
who is to blame for public service delivery failure” (986).
Relatedly, in a recent cross-national study, Walker et al.
(2025) find that information cues about outsourcing to a
private firm decrease citizens’ blame attribution to political
actors for public service failure.
In this context, the reliance on external AI algorithms de-
veloped by outside vendors is a form of outsourcing of the
technology that similarly results in reduced organizational
control. Hence, following blame attribution theories and
notions of controllability, we hypothesize that:
H3: Citizens view public organizations as less responsible
for algorithmic discrimination when the algorithm was de-
veloped by a private party, compared to when the algo-
rithm was developed by the organization itself.
H4: The reduction in perceived responsibility of the public
organization for algorithmic discrimination when the al-
gorithm was developed by an external private party (H3)
is mediated by citizens’ perception of a lack of influence of
the organization over the outcome.
Research design
To test our theoretical hypotheses, we employed a pre-
registered1 vignette-based survey experiment. We presented
participants (Dutch citizens) with a realistic scenario of dis-
crimination against citizens with a migration background
by a public body, and randomly assigned them to different
information regarding the human/algorithmic source of the
discrimination, in line with our hypotheses. The Netherlands
specifically makes for a good research setting for our
study, with AI algorithms widely deployed by Dutch public
authorities (Dutch Data Protection Authority/Autoriteit
Persoonsgegevens 2024; TNO 2024), which renders our ex-
periment highly reliable and realistic. Relatedly, serious cases
of discrimination in relation to AI use in Dutch public services
have also been explicitly documented in this context (Amnesty
International 2021; Dutch Data Protection Authority 2020,
2024; Lighthouse Reports 2023a; Wired 2023).
Within the Dutch context, we employed two equivalent
scenarios of discrimination in public service provision: dis-
crimination in welfare fraud detection by a municipality
(hereafter: welfare fraud case) and discrimination by the
police resulting in over-policing of minority neighborhoods
(hereafter: policing case). Both cases were inspired by recent
real-world examples of discrimination associated with algo-
rithmic bias. We purposefully selected domains representing
different administrative functions that have witnessed the
deployment of AI algorithms on a large scale in recent years,
including in the Netherlands. By putting our theoretical
hypotheses to the test in two distinct scenarios, we aimed
to enhance the study’s external validity beyond specific
topics. We tacitly expected our hypotheses to similarly apply
to both discrimination cases, irrespective of policy context
(i.e., we did not formulate specific interaction hypotheses),
and thus, for our main hypotheses tests, we pooled together
the two scenarios and compared our outcome variable (re-
sponsibility attribution) across conditions, leveraging statis-
tical power. In addition to our main hypothesis tests, we also
present results per case and examine pertinent differences
between the two policy domains as post-hoc exploratory
findings. The research was conducted in accordance with the
research ethics standards of the Faculty of Social Sciences
of Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, ethics review approval
2022-11-28-843.

scenarios: welfare fraud (n = 1,256) and policing (n = 1,227).
As noted in the pre-registration, we based our main models
and prior power analysis on the pooled dataset. This sample
is sufficiently powered for detecting a small effect size of
Cohen’s d = 0.15, which we defined a priori as the smallest
effect size of interest (SESOI) (Lakens 2017). Our power
analyses refer to power = 0.8, P = .050, one-sided test for
directional hypotheses. However, different to our preregis-
tration, one could argue that power may be assessed at the
scenario level, given the random assignment into two cases.
Power analyses per case subsamples would provide 51%
power to detect an effect of d = 0.15 and 80% power for
d = 0.22, an effect size still considered small, but larger than
the pre-registered SESOI.
The sample is fairly representative of the Dutch adult pop-
ulation, albeit it underrepresents citizens of young age and
with a migration background and those with high household
income (see Table 1). To ensure that this does not affect the
generalizability of our results, we also examine the robustness
of our findings to survey weights, with no meaningful changes
to the main results (Supplementary Appendix, 4.6).
Experimental procedure
After providing informed consent, participants were first
asked a battery of general questions regarding their trust
in institutions (national government, the civil service, local
government and the police; four items in total) as well as a
question on their prior attitudes toward AI, on par with other
societal and technological changes (to disguise our focus on
AI algorithms and avoid priming). The wording of these pre-
manipulation items is available in Supplementary Appendix,
6.1. We next informed participants that they will be presented
with a hypothetical local news article and asked questions
about it. Participants were then randomly assigned to one of
the two cases.
Within each case, participants were randomly assigned to
one of five conditions: a control condition or one of four treat-
ment conditions presenting different information regarding
the source of the discrimination, namely: human-based; AI
algorithm (neutral); AI algorithm developed in-house (algo-
rithm developed by the organization); outsourced AI algo-
rithm development (algorithm developed by a private party).
In the control condition, we did not provide any specific in-
formation as to the source of the discriminatory outcome. In
the human-based condition, the news article notes that the dis-
criminatory outcome can be attributed to a team of analysts
deployed by the police/municipality. We chose the wording
“team of analysts” in the human-based condition to signal
that the outcome is based on relevant human expertise and
knowledge, as a counterfactual for outcomes attributable to
algorithmic systems. In the AI algorithm condition (neutral),
the news article notes that the discriminatory outcome can be
attributed to an AI algorithmic system deployed by the police/
municipality (without specifying whether the algorithm was
developed in-house or externally). The comparison between
the AI algorithm condition (neutral) and the human-based
condition in the pooled dataset of the two cases is our main
test for the causal effect of algorithm-based versus human dis-
crimination (H1 and H2), while the control condition serves

Variables
Our main outcome variable is citizens’ attribution of respon-
sibility to the public body for the discriminatory outcome.
This variable is measured through two items, adopted from
Nielsen and Moynihan (2017): “The police/municipality is
responsible for the discriminatory outcome to a great ex-
tent,” “The police/municipality is to blame for the discrimi-
natory outcome to a great extent” (Cronbach’s α = 0.91, r =
0.84). The two items were presented in random order imme-
diately after the hypothetical article. We also accounted for
participants’ demand for consequences as a more tangible ex-
pression of their perception of responsibility. In accordance
with the public accountability literature, we assume that the
attribution of responsibility should reflect higher demands
for accountability and consequences for the public body
for the discriminatory outcome. We included demands for
consequences as a secondary outcome variable, as noted in
our pre-registration. This variable is measured through the
following two items, similarly presented in a random order,
immediately after the responsibility attribution items: “The
police/municipality should be held accountable for the dis-
criminatory outcome.” “The police/municipality should face
consequences for the discriminatory outcome” (Cronbach’s α
= 0.91, r = 0.83). In two open-ended questions, respondents
were also asked to briefly explain, in their own words,
the reasoning behind their quantitative assessments (of
organization’s responsibility and demand for consequences).4
Our first mediating variable is the perceived intent of the
“source” of discrimination (algorithm/team of analysts) to
discriminate. This variable was measured with the following
item: “The analysts/algorithm intended to discriminate against
people with a migration background.” Our second mediating
variable is the perceived intent of the public organization to
discriminate, measured through the following item: “The po-
lice/municipality intended to discriminate against people with
a migration background.” Our third mediating variable is the
perceived influence of the public organization over the dis-
criminatory outcome, measured through the following item:
“The police/municipality had a high degree of influence over
the discriminatory outcome.” The mediating variables were
presented after the outcome variables in a random order.
All items above were measured on a seven-point Likert
scale, ranging from 1 (totally disagree) to 7 (totally agree).
To facilitate the interpretation of the results, we transformed
all variables to a 0–1 continuous scale (min–max normali-
zation) and created composite indices for our outcome and
mediating variables. We further assessed our measurement
model and the reliability of our measures via confirmatory
factor analyses (CFA) (Supplementary Appendix, 2).
Manipulation checks
To ensure the effectiveness of our experimental design, we
included pre-registered factual manipulation checks. We
assessed whether participants correctly understood the pres-
ence of discrimination and recalled the source of the dis-
crimination (human vs. algorithm). Additionally, for those
in algorithmic discrimination conditions, we checked their
recall of whether the algorithm was developed in-house or
by a private party. The question wording and full results are
presented in Supplementary Appendix, 3.
Our results indicate that 71% of the respondents correctly
identified the discriminatory outcomes. As for the source of
the discrimination, among the algorithm conditions, 82%
correctly recalled the AI algorithm as the source of discrim-
ination, and 81% of those also correctly stated whether the
algorithm was developed in-house or by a private party. In the
human discrimination condition, 58% correctly stated that
the discrimination is caused by a team of analysts, and 15%
mistakenly stated that the discrimination is attributed to an
algorithm. Our main models are based on the full analytical
sample and should be regarded as intention-to-treat (ITT)
estimates, as some respondents failed our factual manipulation
checks, introducing potential experimental non-compliance
(Harden, Sokhey, and Runge 2019; Montgomery, Nyhan, and
Torres 2018). In our robust analyses, in line with established
practice, we partly address this by filtering out participants
who did not pass the factual manipulation checks5 and by

Analytical approach
Congruent with our pre-registered analytical plan, we esti-
mate regression models controlling for the cases as our main
analyses. Our main hypothesis tests pertain to our regression
models for responsibility attribution, which is our primary
outcome measure. We also estimate models for demand for
consequences, as a secondary outcome measure. For testing
H1 and H2, we estimate regression models testing the effect
of the AI algorithm condition versus the human-based con-
dition. For testing H3 and H4, we estimate regression models
testing the effect of the algorithm in-house versus algorithm’s
external condition.
Given our multiple testing across two outcomes and two
reference groups, we further statistically account for mul-
tiple comparisons (four comparisons per hypothesis). We

Responsibility attribution for algorithmic versus
human discrimination
Figure 3 graphically shows the descriptive comparison of our
two outcome variables between the AI algorithm condition,
the human condition, and the control condition (serving as
an additional reference group), across the two cases. Table 3
depicts the OLS regression results, testing the effect of algo-
rithmic versus human discrimination. Model 1.1 represents
our main model for testing our first hypothesis.
The left panel of Figure 3 shows that participants do not as-
cribe lower responsibility to the public body in cases of algo-
rithmic discrimination compared to the human discrimination
or control conditions across both case types. The algorithmic
discrimination’s treatment effect is positive, in the opposite
direction to our hypothesis, and not statistically significant
in our regression model for responsibility attribution nor for
demand for consequences. Regarding the secondary outcome
variable of demand for consequences, we found differences
between the two cases as indicated by the significant interac-
tion terms in model 1.4. We observed no significant effects in
the welfare fraud case, whereas in the policing case, contrary
to our expectation, participants in the human discrimination
condition demanded fewer consequences than those in the
algorithmic discrimination (p < .001, Bonferroni-adjusted
p = .008, two-sided test).
Taken together, these results do not allow us to reject our
null hypothesis in relation to our first hypothesis. We found
no substantial evidence that citizens are inclined to attribute
less responsibility to public organizations for algorithmic
versus human discriminatory outcomes. Based on an equiv-
alence test (Lakens 2017), we confidently reject the presence
of an ITT effect more extreme than our predefined SESOI
of 0.15 standard deviations (t[989.48] = 2.18, p = .010). We
conclude that the effect, if any, is below our predefined SESOI
(full statistical test is reported in Supplementary Appendix, 5).
The robustness of these findings is upheld in supplemen-
tary analyses, where we control for additional covariates and
account for experimental non-compliance (detailed in the
Supplementary Appendix, 4).
Our analyses did not reveal statistically significant
differences in responsibility attribution between algorithmic
versus human discrimination. Thus, we did not establish
a causal relationship, and therefore, we do not proceed to
formally test our secondary mediation hypotheses (H2a–c).
Nonetheless, for the sake of transparency, and in accordance
with our pre-analysis plan, we have included statistical me-
diation analyses in the Supplementary Appendix, 4.5. In a
nutshell, the algorithmic condition did not significantly in-
fluence perceptions of the participants regarding the public
body’s control over the outcome or the intention of the al-
gorithm/human analyst team to discriminate. We did find
that participants in the algorithmic discrimination condition
viewed public bodies as having less intention to discrimi-
nate, albeit the effects are relatively small and not consist-
ently observed in all robustness checks. To reiterate, these
differences did not translate into reduced attribution of re-
sponsibility and diminished demand for consequences.
We estimated additional exploratory interaction models
to examine whether the difference in responsibility attribu-
tion between algorithmic and human discrimination varies
by socio-demographic characteristics, prior perceptions of
AI, or trust (heterogeneous treatment effects). These explor-
atory models are reported in Supplementary Appendix, 7.
Overall, we found no robust, statistically significant inter-
action effects across both scenarios, suggesting that our null
findings in relation to H1 are stable and robust. One exception
emerged in the welfare case: participants with lower trust in
the civil service assigned less responsibility and demands for
consequences for algorithmic discrimination (compared with
human discrimination), while those with higher trust assigned
more responsibility and higher demands for consequences.
This may tentatively suggest that in some policy contexts,
citizens with higher expectations of civil servants may hold
public agencies to a higher responsibility standard for failures
attributed to the use of AI.

responsibility for the discriminatory outcome to the munic-
ipality, while in the policing case, they assign lower levels of
responsibility—irrespective of whether the discrimination
is algorithmic or human-based. In other words, our study

Conclusion
We conducted a survey experiment to examine citizens’ attri-
bution of responsibility for discriminatory outcomes in public
service provision, resulting from reliance on AI algorithmic
outputs compared to human-based discrimination. In all, our
study serves to advance an emergent and increasingly important
stream of research on the evolving landscape of public sector
accountability in the age of algorithmic governance, including
some of the ways that new and emerging technologies may dis-
rupt existing accountability frameworks. Research in this vein
is highly relevant for our field in a context where AI is being
adopted at a fast pace across public domains, and where the
track record of harmful deployments by public bureaucracies,
as acutely illustrated by the examples in our introduction, is
mounting. Understanding to what extent adverse administra-
tive outcomes linked to the use of suboptimal AI technologies
translate into organizational responsibility attribution and
public demand for accountability, or to the contrary, whether
this fundamental accountability link is severed becomes critical.
Our findings indicate that technological delegation to AI
does not allow public bodies to bypass responsibility for dis-
criminatory outcomes. These findings are theoretically mean-
ingful and allow us to theorize that the algorithmic nature of
the “agent” in technological delegation relationships does not
defuse organizational responsibility attribution for discrimi-
natory outcomes. As we move toward “governance by algo-
rithm” and growing automation in government, where public
tasks are increasingly implemented through reliance on AI,
these findings are theoretically and normatively important—
and somewhat reassuring. Our empirical findings are also
consistent with insights from real-world cases of algorithmic
discrimination, such as the Dutch childcare benefits scandal
mentioned above or the UK grading algorithm scandal, where
an algorithm downgraded students’ A-level results, with pro-
nounced negative effects for students from disadvantaged
backgrounds (Broussard 2020). In these anecdotal examples,
we saw algorithmic discrimination spark intense public scru-
tiny, public investigations, and respectively, public outcry and
protests (Amoore 2020).
At the same time, our experimental findings indicate that
citizens assign more responsibility to public bodies when
algorithms are developed in-house as opposed to by external
private parties. This could lead to the emergence of accounta-
bility deficits pertaining to technological outsourcing that re-
quire further investigation in future work.
Outsourced algorithms are often legally opaque—that is,
subject to proprietary protections imposed by vendors, in-
cluding when used in the public sector (Bloch-Wehba 2021),
severely limiting information availability that could inform
adequate monitoring of their functioning. Recent cases
have highlighted the many harms caused by the adoption
of a host of suboptimal outsourced systems across crucial
policy areas. For instance, the now infamous MiDAS algo-
rithm that wreaked havoc with its inaccurate unemployment
fraud determinations (it is assessed the algorithm was wrong
93% of the time, with 40,000 incorrect fraud determinations;
Angwin 2022) was proprietary (Elyounes 2020, 492), as
are predictive systems used in criminal justice (Citron 2016;
Rudin, Wang, and Coker 2020) as well as numerous predic-
tive policing systems of dubious quality mushrooming across
jurisdictions (Richardson, Schultz, and Crawford 2019). This
can render it difficult for public bodies to adequately oversee
the functioning of such systems, and relatedly, to diagnose
failures across public domains (see also Dickinson and Yates
2023).
In the Netherlands, investigations by the Court of Audit
found that when algorithms are developed by, or their
functioning is outsourced to, external providers both the re-
sponsible ministry as well as the public body concerned have
little insight into algorithm functioning (Netherlands Court
of Audit 2022, 37; Netherlands Court of Audit 2021, 42).
Inscrutability stands to exacerbate informational asymmetries
and obfuscate accountability across public services. In this
context, reliance on external algorithmic systems where
public administrators lack insight into the actual functioning
and quality of such systems raises risks of arbitrary and
opaque administrative decision-making (Busuioc 2023). This
becomes especially problematic when such algorithms are
relied on in administrative contexts affecting individuals in
life-altering ways.
Indeed, we theorize that the differentiated patterns of re-
sponsibility attribution for in-house versus outsourced algo-
rithmic development that we diagnosed in our study could
give rise to perverse incentives: discourage internal algo-
rithmic development, encouraging public bodies instead to
outsource algorithm development to private actors, as a way
to bypass responsibility for potential failures and adverse
outcomes arising from algorithmic deployment. This points to
a risk that outsourcing can incentivize the continued adoption
of suboptimal algorithmic systems by government agencies.
Reduced organizational responsibility attribution for adverse
outcomes produced by systems developed externally creates
less incentives for public organizations to pay attention to,
and spend resources to identify and correct, potential biases
and errors of such systems, thereby allowing existing sys-
temic biases to perpetuate. What is more, incentives to out-
source algorithmic development can also disaffect in-house
technical expertise acquisition in the bureaucracy. Yet, this is
precisely an area where public sector expertise already seri-
ously lags behind the private sector, and where the acquisi-
tion of in-house expertise is regarded as critical to its ability
to keep pace with a rapidly shifting technological context
(Pahlka 2023). Our research thus suggests existing oversight
mechanisms in contracting and procurement processes need
to be carefully evaluated to potentially prevent outsourcing of
critical technological tasks from undermining accountability
mechanisms. As public governance turns “algorithmic,” these
aspects require careful consideration and will affect citizens’
day-to-day experience of government in non-negligible ways.

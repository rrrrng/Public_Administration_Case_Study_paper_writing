Abstract
In complex, polycentric environmental governance systems, actors may choose to collaborate with one another to reduce their collective vul-
nerability and enhance system function. However, collaboration can be costly, and little evidence exists for how particular collaborative forums
impact the broader governance system in which they are embedded. To address this gap, we investigate the role of intermediate collaborative
forums, which support collaboration among a subset of system actors, in polycentric governance systems. Empirically, we analyze the structural
and functional role of an intermediate collaborative forum called the Arizona Municipal Water Users Association (AMWUA) within the municipal
surface water governance network for the Phoenix Metropolitan Area (PMA) in Arizona, United States. To do this, we draw from 21 interviews
with water professionals in the PMA, which we analyze through a combination of network analysis and qualitative coding. We find that AMWUA
facilitates strong bonding capacities among members, allowing for streamlined bridging to the rest of the network that enhances information
processing and advocacy of member needs. Our findings advance theory on the role of collaboration in polycentric systems and inform the de-
sign of collaborative institutions to improve environmental governance.
Introduction
Modern environmental governance systems are highly com-
plex, involve many overlapping actors and decision-making
forums, and face myriad challenges associated with overuse,
development, and climate change (Herzog and Ingold 2019;
Lubell 2013; York et al. 2021). Policy actors may choose to
collaborate within these systems to share information and re-
sources, develop common management strategies, and other-
wise coordinate to reduce their collective vulnerability and
improve system function (Bodin 2017; Lubell et al. 2014).
Collaboration can be costly and potentially ineffective how-
ever (Koebele 2015; Koontz and Thomas 2006; Margerum
and Robinson 2016). Thus, understanding how collabora-
tive efforts impact the broader governance system in which
they occur is critical for improving environmental governance
outcomes.
Empirical studies of collaborative environmental gov-
ernance tend to conduct either detailed analyses of isolated
collaborative processes (e.g., Berardo, Heikkila, and Gerlak
2014; Koebele 2020; Sullivan, White, and Hanemann 2019)
or system-wide assessments that obscure the impacts of
specific collaborative forums (e.g., Berardo 2014; Bodin,
Sandstrom, and Crona 2017; Henry 2021; Li and Mostafavi
2021; Lubell, Robins, and Wang 2014). Consequently, these
studies often fail to reveal how multiple scales of govern-
ance interact and influence one another. Moreover, because
scholars have only begun to characterize coordination
structures within multilevel governance systems (Morrison
et al., 2023) and unpack the mechanisms underlying actors’
motivation to collaborate within specific forums (Angst et al.
2022; Fischer and Maag 2019; Fried, Hamilton, and Berardo
2022; Lubell and Morrison 2021), we have an inadequate
understanding of the systemic impacts of collaboration
(Herzog and Ingold 2019; Mancilla García and Bodin 2019;
Scott and Thomas 2017).
To address this knowledge gap, we combine structural net-
work analysis with qualitative interview data to elucidate the
role of intermediate collaborative forums, which support col-
laboration among a subset of system actors, in polycentric
governance systems. Empirically, we analyze the Phoenix
Metropolitan Area (PMA) municipal surface water govern-
ance system in Arizona, United States, which is facing severe
water supply challenges characteristic of other systems in the
region (Hondula et al. 2019). We specifically analyze the role
of a key intermediate collaborative forum called the Arizona
Municipal Water Users Association (AMWUA), guided by the
following research questions:
1.	 What role do intermediate collaborative forums, such as
AMWUA, play in the PMA municipal surface water gov-
ernance system?
2.	 What motivates system actors to participate in AMWUA?
In addition to mapping the PMA water governance system
and analyzing AMWUA’s structural position within it, we as-
sess actors’ motivations for collaborating within AMWUA to

Theoretical Background
Polycentric Governance and Decision-Making
Forums
Like many other service delivery systems, urban water systems
are governed through complex, polycentric arrangements.
In contrast to more centralized governance arrangements,
polycentric arrangements are characterized by (i) multiple
decision-making forums with a degree of formal independ-
ence that (ii) take each other into account to varying extents
through competition, cooperation, conflict, and conflict res-
olution processes (Carlisle and Gruby 2019; Ostrom et al.
1961), often across scales and jurisdictions (Andersson and
Ostrom 2008; York et al. 2019). Thus, polycentric govern-
ance is not a monolithic exercise; it involves interactions
among multiple actors navigating institutional complexity in
pursuit of their policy goals (Lubell and Morrison 2021).
Polycentric systems have been touted for their ability to
promote innovation and learning, bolster adaptive capacity,
improve the “fit” of governance efforts, achieve economies
of scale, and produce equitable and sustainable outcomes
(Andersson and Ostrom 2008; Carlisle and Gruby 2019;
Ostrom 2010; Pahl-Wostl et al. 2012; Pahl-Wostl and Knieper
2014; Scott and Greer 2019). However, given that nearly all
governance systems are polycentric to some degree (Lubell
and Robbins 2021), empirical studies find that they vary
widely in their ability to deliver these purported benefits
(Berardo and Lubell 2019; Bissonnette et al. 2018; Sovacool
2011). This suggests a critical knowledge gap regarding how
the characteristics of a polycentric governance system—espe-
cially the arrangement and roles of forums within it—impact
its function (Carlisle and Gruby 2019; Thiel, Garrick, and
Blomquist 2019).
This concern is central to Ecology of Games Theory (EGT),
which provides a lens for examining the interplay between
the structure and function of a polycentric system (Berardo
and Lubell 2019; Lubell 2013), typically by analyzing the
arrangement of diverse decision-making forums within it
(Berardo, Olivier, and Lavers 2015; Fischer and Maag 2019;
Fried, Hamilton, and Berardo 2022; Mancilla García and
Bodin 2019; Olivier and Berardo 2022). Forums can vary
along multiple dimensions, including size, inclusiveness, de-
cision-making modes, and leadership (Fischer and Leifeld
2015), and actors may participate in multiple forums to
satisfy various policy needs (Angst et al. 2022; Fischer and
Maag 2019; Lubell 2013). Forums have been studied using
both theoretical (e.g., Scott, Thomas, and Magallanes 2019;
Smaldino and Lubell 2014) and empirical approaches (e.g.,
Lubell, Robins, and Wang 2014; Mewhirter, Lubell, and
Berardo 2018; Olivier and Berardo 2022) to understand
how forum attributes, network context, and actor partici-
pation are linked. Many EGT-informed studies suggest that
polycentric arrangements with sufficient coordination among
decision-making forums, including those across scales, are
better able to achieve system-level goals (Bodin 2017; Carlisle
and Gruby 2019; Ekstrom and Young 2009; Heikkila,
Villamayor-Tomas, and Garrick 2018; Lubell 2013; Pahl-
Wostl et al. 2012), while highly fragmented or centralized
arrangements may lead to activities that conflict with one an-
other to undermine system goals, increase transaction costs,
and reduce accountability (Andersson and Ostrom 2008;
Carlisle and Gruby 2019; Mudliar 2021). Identifying effec-
tive coordination mechanisms and structures, and under-
standing how and why actors participate in them, is therefore
critical for enhancing the performance of polycentric govern-
ance (Morrison et al. 2023).
The Role of Intermediate Collaborative Forums in
Polycentric Systems
An increasingly ubiquitous type of forum recognized for its
potential to enhance coordination in polycentric systems is
the collaborative forum (Margerum and Robinson 2016;
Newig et al. 2019), based on the principles of collaborative
governance (e.g., Ansell and Gash 2008, 2018; Bodin 2017;
Emerson, Nabatchi, and Balogh 2012). Collaborative forums
engage public and private actors in consensus-oriented delib-
eration around shared problems, typically by fostering the
development of social capital, trust, and learning through
face-to face dialogue over extended periods of time (Emerson,
Nabatchi, and Balogh 2012; Koebele 2019; Leach and
Sabatier 2005; Leach et al. 2014). Whether implemented from
the “top down” or forged from the “bottom up”, collabo-
rative forums share the goal of crafting mutually acceptable
policies or management actions, which may be easier to imple-
ment and perceived as more legitimate (Gerlak, Heikkila, and
Lubell 2013; Koontz and Newig 2014; Sabatier et al. 2005).
Participating in collaborative forums can be costly for ac-
tors, however, as they are often time- and resource-intensive,
can lead to “lowest-common-denominator” solutions, and
may fail to engage all relevant participants, among other
challenges (Gerlak, Heikkila, and Lubell 2013; Kenney 2000;
Koontz and Thomas 2006). Engaging in a new collaborative
forum (sometimes called a “collaborative institution” in EGT
scholarship) may also limit an actor’s ability to participate ef-
fectively in other existing forums (Berardo and Scholz 2010;
Hileman and Bodin 2019; Lubell et al. 2017; Mewhirter,
Lubell, and Berardo 2018).
While several EGT studies have sought to understand
the outputs of very large or even system-wide collaborative
forums (e.g., Berardo, Olivier, and Lavers 2015; Mancilla
García and Bodin 2019), fewer studies have focused deeply
on how collaborative forums that engage a smaller subsets
of system actors in collaboration impact system function. We
call these forums intermediate collaborative forums. EGT re-
search has found that these types of forums often serve as
key hubs of network activity and decision-making, espe-
cially when they are perceived to improve learning or equi-
table resource distribution (Fischer and Maag 2019; Fried,
Hamilton, and Berardo 2022; Lubell 2015; Lubell, Robins,
and Wang 2014). Intermediate collaborative forums can also
facilitate both bonding and bridging interactions among ac-
tors in the broader system (Berardo & Lubell, 2016). Bonding
refers to strengthening relationships among likeminded ac-
tors—through
trust-building,
information-sharing,
and
resource-pooling, among other activities—which can re-
duce their individual and collective risk when engaging in
system governance (Bodin 2017; Lubell, Henry, and McCoy
2010; Lubell, Mewhirter, and Berardo 2020). Bridging refers

Actors’ Motivations for Engaging in Collaboration
In addition to investigating the structural relationship be-
tween intermediate collaborative forums and the governance
system, understanding what motivates actors to participate
in a given collaborative forum can provide additional insight
into its role in the system (Angst et al. 2022; Fischer and Maag
2019; Herzog and Ingold 2019; Mancilla García and Bodin
2019). Bodin (2017) argues that to understand when collab-
oration is useful for solving socio-ecological problems, we
must direct “attention to who the actors are, with whom they
collaborate, and how the structures [they engage in] . . . re-
late to the actors’ abilities to address different environmental
problems” (p. 2). This approach aligns with recent calls from
Lubell and Morrison (2021) to place individuals, embedded
within the polycentric system, at the forefront of analyses, as
well as from Ostrom (2005) to understand how participants
animate decision-making situations. In short, expanding our
characterization of actor participation in a forum beyond a
binary “yes/no” variable can provide important insight into
the specific roles that different forums play in a polycentric
system.
The intermediate collaborative forum at the heart of this
study involves collaboration primarily among municipal
entities, often called intergovernmental, intermunicipal, or
interlocal collaboration. Such collaboration may be institu-
tionalized in quasi-regional governments, metropolitan plan-
ning forums, and service delivery organizations or agreements,
among other types of partnerships (Hulst and van Montfort
2012; Leon-Moreta and Totaro 2022; Rickabaugh 2021;
Rubado 2021; Warm 2011). While such forums vary in the
tasks they undertake, their authority, and the degree to which
their members are formally bound to one another (Blair and
Janousek 2013), individuals typically participate in them
on behalf of their organizations or governments, sometimes
under the facilitation of professional staff (Rayle and Zegras
2013; Rickabaugh 2021).
Myriad rational and institutional explanations exist for
why governments or other organizations are motivated to en-
gage in collaboration (Williams et al. 2009), despite it being
recognized as a costly or risky governance strategy (Hansen,
Mullin, and Riggs 2020; Rubado 2021; Terman, Feiock,
and Youm 2020). Rational explanations center around an
organization’s desire to gain resources, including information,
in order to enhance efficiency or performance (Agranoff and
McGuire 2003; Lubell 2015; Muraoka and Avellaneda 2021).
In the case of water resources, external drivers like perceived
supply risks have been found to be positively correlated with
collaborative participation (Bell, Fencl, and Mullin 2022;
Herzog and Ingold 2019). Low-capacity governments, those
experiencing austerity, or those located in highly fragmented
regions may also be motivated to collaborate with others to
gain capacity or create economies of scale (Bel and Warner
2015; Deslatte and Feiock 2019; Terman, Feiock, and Youm
2020). However, organizations with high managerial and
organizational capacity may be better prepared to engage
in productive collaboration (Bell, Fencl, and Mullin 2022;
McGuire and Silvia 2010).
Institutional explanations for collaboration tend to focus
on organizational norms or beliefs as motivators, such as
the desire to “do what’s right” or strengthen one’s legitimacy
(Mosley and Wong 2021). In this vein, organizations may be
more motivated to collaborate with other organizations that
are similar in structure or professional norms and with whom
they already share a network or trust (LeRoux, Brandenburger,
and Pandey 2010). Some organizations may be required or
coerced to join a collaborative initiative through regula-
tion or other mandates, which may help incentivize them to
overcome differences with their collaborators (Monroe and
Butler 2016), while others may collaborate to avoid coercion
or combat a common opponent (Henry, Lubell, and McCoy
2011; Koebele 2020). Additionally, factors such as shared
beliefs (Calanni et al. 2015), greater interdependence (Hulst
and van Montfort 2012), high problem severity (Herzog and
Ingold 2019; McGuire and Silvia 2010), and observing one’s
neighbors collaborate successfully (Rubado 2021) may also
motivate engagement in collaboration.
Given the plethora of potential reasons why actors may
choose to collaborate, as well as the recognized costs of col-
laboration, it is necessary to identify why an actor is motivated
to engage in a specific collaborative forum within the broader
polycentric system. To do this, we integrate qualitative insight
about actors’ motivations for participating in an intermediate
collaborative forum based on the benefits they perceive with
a structural network analysis of the forum’s position in the
system. This analysis allows us to provide a fuller picture of
the role of intermediate collaborative forums in a polycentric
governance system.
Case Context: Municipal Water Governance in
Phoenix
We analyze collaboration in the case of municipal provision
of surface water in the PMA, the 10th largest urban area in
the United States (U.S. Census Bureau 2022). The PMA is
located in the central Arizona desert region, which prompted
a long history of infrastructure and institutional develop-
ment to manage its scarce water resources (Larson, Wiek,
and Withycombe 2013; Sullivan et al. 2017). Today, PMA
municipalities rely heavily on two surface water sources:
the Salt and Verde Rivers, provided by the Salt River Project
(SRP), and the Colorado River, provided by the Central
Arizona Project (CAP). SRP water rights are tied to previ-
ously irrigated lands from the start of the twentieth century,
well before the PMA’s urban growth, while CAP water rights
were allocated in the 1980s. Figure 1 shows the location of
PMA cities in relation to these water sources.
As of 2019, water use in the PMA was primarily agricul-
tural (43%) and municipal (38%), with about 75% of mu-
nicipal supplies coming from surface water (ADWR 2022).
Municipal demand continues to expand; however, the City of
Phoenix is experiencing the largest population growth of all
large US cities (Healy 2021), and PMA cities like Buckeye are
among the fastest growing in the United States (Hing 2021).

Thus, PMA municipal water providers are primarily con-
cerned with securing renewable and reliable water resources,
which have largely already been allocated or over-allocated.
The Arizona Municipal Water Users Association (AMWUA)
was formed in 1969 by five PMA cities—Phoenix, Scottsdale,
Mesa, Glendale, and Tempe—to promote cooperation among
cities who hold rights to both SRP- and CAP-provided water.
By 1989, AMWUA reached nine members, adding Chandler,
Goodyear, Peoria, and Gilbert. Avondale was the last city
to join in 2007. AMWUA’s mission is to protect “members’
ability to provide assured, safe, and sustainable water
supplies to their communities,” further adding, “working col-
laboratively, we advocate responsible water stewardship that
supports economic prosperity and safeguards Arizona’s water
supplies for future generations” (AMWUA 2022).
AMWUA, which has its own executive staff, convenes
member cities in three ways: (i) the Board of Directors,
which includes a representative from each member city who
is also an elected city official; (ii) the Management Board,
which consists of utility directors from each member city
and makes recommendations to the Board of Directors;
and (iii) Technical Advisory Groups, which are comprised
of specialists from each member city that advise the execu-
tive staff and Board of Directors. The Management Board
and the Board of Directors meet publicly, but the advisory
groups often hold closed-door meetings. AMWUA also
interacts with PMA cities who have CAP rights but lack
SRP rights (e.g., non-AMWUA cities), as well as federal and
state agencies, water providers, and stakeholders from other
sectors.
Scholars have long called for a paradigm shift in the PMA
toward more adaptive water management and proactive navi-
gation of uncertainty (Gober et al. 2010; Sampson, Quay, and
White 2016). They have also revealed the unique perspectives
of its water resource managers and the public (Larson et al.
2009; White et al. 2015a; White et al. 2015b). However, a gap
remains in understanding how actors in the PMA collectively
manage their water supplies, as well as how intermediate col-
laborative forums such as AMWUA facilitate collaboration
among a subset of actors to improve governance and sup-
port water sustainability. We seek to address this gap using
the methodology described next.

Interview Coding
We coded interview transcripts according to an a priori
codebook developed from the study context and literature
(see supplementary appendix 3), though additional codes
were added over time based on new information from
interviewees. The coding process links themes from narrative
interview responses to theoretical concepts of interest. Ours
featured three broad categories: (1) collaboration structure,
(2) planning process and information, and (3) motivations
and strategies. This analysis relies on a subset of collabora-
tion structure codes, including those identifying forums and
interactions among actors, as well as motivation codes for
forum participation.
Two coders coded two complete transcripts together, one
with a bulk water provider and one with a city-level actor,
to gain shared understanding. Then, the coders coded four
transcripts (three cities and one state-level actor) separately
and compared all coded statements to ensure the robustness
of the shared understanding. Finally, the two coders divided
the remaining 15 transcripts, making sure that each sub-
group of actors (e.g., AMWUA cities or state agencies) was
also evenly split. The coders regularly met during the coding
process to discuss any questions or nuances uncovered in
coding. Due to the noticeable but analytically insignificant
tendency of one coder to code more text for the same code

Analysis: Network Mapping
Network mapping is a popular tool for characterizing and
evaluating the structure of polycentric systems (e.g., Hileman
and Bodin 2019; Lubell et al. 2017; Lubell, Robins, and Wang
2014; Scott and Thomas 2017) and connecting the struc-
tural features of governance systems to system activities (e.g.,
Berardo 2014; Berardo and Scholz 2010; Bodin, Sandstrom,
and Crona 2017; Scott 2016; Ulibarri and Scott 2017). From
the coded transcripts, we derived information for assembling
an undirected network diagram of surface water govern-
ance in the PMA. Nodes include (1) actors, defined as rep-
resentatives of organizations involved in the management
of surface water in the PMA, and (2) forums, defined per
Fischer and Leifeld (2015) as having (i) a clear organizational
boundary of forum members, (ii) an issue-based purpose, (iii)
actor diversity, (iv) repeated interaction of actors. We used
a unipartite network as opposed to a bipartite network be-
cause we compare connections between actors mediated by
a forum (captured with a bipartite representation) with di-
rect unmediated actor connections (not captured with a bi-
partite representation), which were frequently mentioned in
our interviews. Edges are nonweighted and represent direct
collaborative relationships mentioned in the interviews, in-
cluding built infrastructure partnerships (e.g., shared inter-
connect), one-on-one meetings for water resources planning,
and direct flows of information.
While we detected multiple actor types in the PMA, in-
cluding universities, federal agencies, agricultural districts, and
developers, we limited the actors in this analysis to bulk water
providers, state agents, municipal water providers, and tribes,
who also receive water directly from bulk water providers in
the PMA. Excluded actors typically lacked formal jurisdiction
over or involvement in municipal water supply provision, or
were unspecified. Similarly, we excluded forums not directly
related to municipal surface water provision.
We use the network analysis and visualization software
Gephi (version 0.9.5) to map the PMA surface water gov-
ernance system and calculate centrality metrics like degree,
closeness, and betweenness to highlight central nodes in
the PMA surface water governance network, their rela-
tive positions and ability to reach other nodes quickly, and
their bridging capacities, respectively. We normalize the
betweenness centrality metric by the number of node pairs
not including the node of interest to ensure that the metric
is in the range [0,1].
Our network analysis involved a constant dialogue be-
tween the static network structure and the qualitative con-
text from which the nodes and connections were derived. This
approach provides a richer picture of a system’s structure
(Ahlstrom and Cornell 2018; Bixler et al. 2020; Caceres et al.
2022; Riggs, Langston, and Phann 2020) and helps to trian-
gulate static network insights (Kapucu, Hu, and Khosa 2017).
Critically, rather than using the interview data to “comple-
ment” or “confirm” quantitative insights often drawn from
large survey-derived networks, we use the interview data as
both a source for quantitative network construction and anal-
ysis, and as a stand-alone source for qualitative insight on
how that network operates, as will be described next.
Analysis: Motivations
In addition to coding the transcripts for actors, forums, and
connections, we coded for eight types of motivations or
reasons why actors say they participate in AMWUA, derived
from the literature: capacity, common goals, externalities,
information sharing, deliberation, requirement, trust, and
uncertainty (see supplementary appendix 3). We identified
376 statements discussing motivations for collaborating
through AMWUA and noted the number and types of ac-
tors with statements for each type and subtype. We also in-
ductively identified subtypes of motivations within each of
the categories (e.g., lobbying as a specific type of capacity)
as necessary. All eight theoretically derived motivation types
were identified in the interview data; however, we reduced
manifested redundancy among types by aggregating our
results into two broad categories that capture actors’ most
commonly cited motivations for participating in AMWUA:
internally directed actions and externally directed actions.
Internally directed actions are actions AMWUA performs
with the 10 AMWUA member cities (e.g., facilitating inter-
city coordination), while external actions are actions directed
outward from AMWUA and its member cities to others in the
network (e.g., lobbying the state legislature).
Although other mixed-methods studies of polycentric gov-
ernance have used interview data and qualitative analysis to
extract motivational insight (Bixler et al. 2020; Caceres et al.
2022), they often (i) do not narrow the motivational focus
to a particular forum, instead referring to broader network
participation, and (ii) do not locate identified motivations
within the broader theoretical literature. By making our con-
nection to theory explicit and contextualizing that analysis to
a particular collaborative forum, we gain further insight into
AMWUA’s role within the governance system beyond what
can be derived from the structural network analysis alone.
In short, by understanding why participants are motivated
to participate in AMWUA, we learn more about what role
AMWUA plays in practice in the governance system.

Surface Water Governance Network in the PMA
First, we present the structure of the PMA surface water gov-
ernance network (figure 2a), which has 29 nodes, 137 edges,
and a graph density of 0.337. The average degree (number of
connections per node) is 9.448, and the distribution of degrees
is right skewed, primarily due to the presence of some very
high-degree actors like CAP and ADWR. CAP and ADWR
also have the highest betweenness centralities (0.218 and
0.188, respectively) and very high closeness centralities (0.93
and 0.90, respectively), suggesting that they bridge many
actors in the network (betweenness centrality) and serve as
“broadcasters” (closeness centrality) who can quickly reach
the whole network. Their unique connections are captured

AMWUA’s ability to foster trust and open perspective sharing
among members, often by ensuring they interact repeatedly.
The interview participants explained that member cities in-
teract formally in required AMWUA meetings, which then
support more informal, spontaneous conversations among
members that both deepen trust and inspire additional col-
laborative opportunities. As trust develops, individuals are
further motivated to engage in open information sharing,
even comparing AMWUA to a “sounding board” where
individuals can experiment with ideas and get feedback. This,
in turn, enriches each city’s awareness of other city contexts
and potential mutually beneficial resources. However, this
motivation also speaks to the balancing act that interme-
diate collaborative forums like AMWUA must navigate to
create intimate and trusting spaces while also ensuring mul-
tiple perspectives are included to make information sharing
worthwhile.
Another commonly mentioned motivation refers to
AMWUA as a “think tank” for its members. AMWUA
employs its own staff, who aid in the development of policy
ideas by, for instance, collecting and processing data from each
of the cities. In this way, the intermediate collaborative forum
allows members to pool their resources to achieve economies
of scale, which is more efficient than each city maintaining
its own equivalent capacities, while remaining under the pur-
view of member interests. This motivation suggests that in-
termediate collaborative forums can improve the efficiency of
the learning and solution search process for members with
common interests compared with one-on-one coordination.
The third internal motivation, which builds on the pre-
vious two, highlights AMWUA’s role in facilitating coordi-
nation on intercity actions. This motivation reflects the fact
that beyond information sharing, AMWUA also facilitates
deliberation among its members regarding how informa-
tion can be translated into collaborative action. At the time
of the interviews, one salient example was activation of city
drought management plans. While each city has its own plan,
the fact that the cities share water sources and infrastructure,
as well as the desire to avoid citizen and industry confusion,
led them to coordinate within AMWUA around when their
plans should be activated. Many cities also directly interact
with one another to coordinate on shared projects, and major
cities like Phoenix interact with cities throughout the PMA, as
revealed in the network analysis; however, AMWUA, as an in-
termediate forum, provides invaluable coordinating capacity
around multicity actions that affect the shared interests of a
subset of network actors.
Next, we turn to actors’ externally directed motivations,
which help to articulate the streamlined bridging role played
by AMWUA as an intermediate collaborative forum in the
governance system. As uncovered in the network analysis,
AMWUA is an effective broadcasting node, able to reach many
actors in the network in short paths, and the three categories
of external motivations speak to this capacity. The most fre-
quently cited external motivation for actors to participate in
AMWUA was its ability to gather and process information
from non-AMWUA forums and actors. Even though multiple
AMWUA and non-AMWUA actors directly interact with each
other, AMWUA plays a valuable role in streamlining relevant
communication from both higher-level actors (e.g., ADWR)
and other user groups (e.g., SAWUA) to member cities. This
capacity benefits member cities, who can be short-staffed and
may lack the resources to attend all relevant meetings held
by other actors. This motivation demonstrates the potential
value of intermediate collaborative forums to mitigate the
problem of participant fatigue often cited in the Ecology of
Games literature (e.g., Hileman and Bodin 2019) where too
many forums can increase the transaction costs of participa-
tion to a degree that performance suffers.
Second, multiple participants emphasized AMWUA’s advo-
cacy, lobbying, and negotiation actions pursued on behalf of
member cities with external actors (e.g., CAP or SRP) and
forums (e.g., Colorado River renegotiation). As an agent of its
members, AMWUA only acts under unanimous consent of its
members, and as noted in interviews, this capacity affords the
ten cities, together, more influence over regional, statewide,
and national issues. In other words, intermediate collabora-
tive forums like AMWUA can advance the common interests
of their strongly bonded members in the broader network
through activities like collective lobbying with greater influ-
ence than members may have individually.
Finally, two participants highlighted that AMWUA
also provides a valuable space for member cities to craft
common messaging for the media. Just as unanimous advo-
cacy strategies strengthen the collective influence of member
cities in state forums, so too does shared media messaging
strengthen the image of member cities in the public conversa-
tion about PMA municipal water. Even though each city has
its own water system, their citizens consume common media
outlets, so if the messaging is different, citizen confusion can
harm trust in municipal provision broadly. Thus, intermediate
collaborative forums like AMWUA can both provide a ben-
efit to members and streamline communication within the
broader system by efficiently developing common messaging
around collective strategies.
Taking the insights gained from the network and
motivations analyses together, AMWUA’s role as an inter-
mediate collaborative forum may be best described as pri-
marily creating strong internal collaboration among its
members (bonding) and secondarily advocating for their col-
lective needs within the broader network by connecting to,
communicating with, and sharing information from other ac-
tors (bridging and broadcasting). AMWUA bonds members
by regularly facilitating a space for open perspective sharing
to develop trust and coordination while also providing a full-
time staff to process data and develop actions relevant for
all cities. It then bridges the AMWUA members as a collec-
tive to the rest of the governance network by streamlining
information collection and broadcasting member needs to
higher governance levels (e.g., state or regional) and adjacent
actors (e.g., WVWUA and SAWUA actors) through its advo-
cacy and strategic media messaging efforts. AMWUA’s role
as an intermediate collaborative forum thus reflects the in-
teractive coordination and collaboration processes occurring
at multiple scales in polycentric governance systems, from
individual city needs to regional needs (Berardo and Scholz
2010; Berardo and Lubell 2016). This, in turn, suggests that
the role of intermediate collaborative forums is shaped by
both the needs of the subset of governance actors who par-
ticipate in the forum and the governance system structure in
which it is nested, the implications of which we will discuss
in the next section.

Discussion and Conclusion
We analyzed the role of a key intermediate collaborative
forum, AMWUA, in the PMA municipal water governance
system using an innovative methodological approach that
puts qualitative data and network analysis in dialogue. The
results reveal several important lessons about the role of in-
termediate collaborative forums in polycentric governance
systems broadly, especially concerning their bonding and
streamlined bridging capacities. This analysis of AMWUA
thus offers a proof-of-concept for the promise of intermediate
collaborative forums in mitigating costs associated with col-
laboration and enhancing coordination in complex environ-
mental governance arrangements. Here, we discuss several
broader takeaways from the findings presented above.
First, water providers and higher-level regulatory entities
constantly seek information to guide decision-making. While
state-level government organizations often serve as cen-
tral knowledge brokers (Koontz 2021), municipal providers
cannot meet all their strategic needs by engaging with those
central actors alone. Thus, our findings suggest a niche role
for intermediate collaborative forums (Research Question 1),
which can process, tailor, and broadcast information in ways
that meet the collective needs of a subset of network actors.
However, this is only possible when members of the interme-
diate collaborative forum are strongly bonded through activi-
ties such as repeated face-to-face discussions on challenges of
mutual interest (Ansell and Gash 2008; Berardo and Lubell,
2016). Put differently, intermediate collaborative forums can
act as a two-way conduit between actors across scales to facil-
itate the kind of multilevel information flow necessary to ad-
dress water resource challenges that transcend jurisdictional
boundaries (York et al. 2019). In a governance context where
information about both future water supply and demand is
valuable, information cannot simply flow top-down, but must
flow in both directions as users and suppliers navigate uncer-
tainty together.
For this to happen, however, local actors must be willing
to continually participate in the intermediate collabora-
tive forum, which must provide capacities beyond what the
members have on their own. From an institutional transaction
costs perspective (Feiock 2013), such capacities must exceed
the well-cited costs associated with participation in collab-
orative forums. Our motivations analysis delineated several
example capacities provided by AMWUA (Research Question
2). Broadly, our results suggest that intermediate collabora-
tive forums must be both “inward” and “outward” facing
to motivate actors to participate, allowing them to facilitate
both bonding and streamlined bridging on behalf of the col-
lective. This unique role positions AMWUA to increase the
efficiency and innovativeness of cities’ information-gathering,
decision-making, and management processes while simulta-
neously advocating for and communicating on their behalf in
a way that has greater influence than the simple sum of their
parts.
Second, the delicate balance of bonding and bridging
capacities demonstrated by AMWUA also speaks to a trade-
off in designing collaborative governance: achieving common
understanding versus respecting individual members' needs
and interests. Regarding the former, the PMA is situated
within an arid environment that relies primarily on surface
water delivered by two bulk water providers. Emphasizing
these common infrastructure and water sources can help
AMWUA cities feel a strong sense of shared understanding
and goals—a frequently cited feature of successful collabo-
rative arrangements (Ansell and Gash 2008; Hulst and van
Montfort 2012; LeRoux, Brandenburger, and Pandey 2010;
McGuire and Silvia 2010). However, AMWUA must also sup-
port the diverse needs and perspectives of individual member
cities to some extent by facilitating open deliberation around
member-specific needs. This balance may be best struck in in-
termediate collaborative forums that focus on small subsets
of actors, as larger collaborative efforts must often overlook
individual needs at the expense of making progress on collec-
tive goals, limiting opportunities for members to learn from
and support one another (Berardo and Lubell 2019; Bodin,
Sandstrom, and Crona 2017; Mewhirter, Coleman, and
Berardo 2019; Siddiki, Leach, and Kim 2017). However, this
may also increase or perpetuate fragmentation in the broader
network, as discussed further below.
Third, AMWUA demonstrates that the theoretical dis-
tinction between formal and informal collaboration can be
unnecessarily illusory and even unproductive at the “inter-
mediate” scale of governance. Our interviews highlighted
that AMWUA provides a fruitful space to share information
horizontally among cities and vertically from higher to lower
governance levels in both formal and informal environments
(Rubado 2021; Terman, Feiock, and Youm 2020), and that
these mechanisms may be mutually reinforcing (Blair and
Janousek 2013; Park et al. 2021). By providing formal
opportunities for engagement, intermediate collaborative
forums can spur informal interactions, which may encourage
new partnerships that further reduce the risks and costs as-
sociated with collaboration and enhance coordination in
broader system governance (Feiock 2013; Lubell 2013; Lubell
et al. 2017; Mewhirter, Coleman, and Berardo 2019).
Despite these benefits, our analysis did detect a potential
negative externality associated with intermediate collaborative
forums. AMWUA’s success in providing multiple capacities to
its well-bonded members may prevent those members from
developing capacities in other forums. This can hurt non-
AMWUA municipal providers in the PMA who do not have
access to the same resources that AMWUA provides (Berardo
2014; LeRoux, Brandenburger, and Pandey 2010), as was in-
dicated by interviewees when discussing how joint members
of AMWUA and WVWUA perceive AMWUA to be their pri-
mary collaborative forum. Consequently, non-West Valley
and non-AMWUA PMA cities (like Queen Creek) lack access
to the capacities of other actors in the network and may be
impacted by decisions influenced by better-resourced actors,
especially when they act collectively. Moreover, the strong
homophily in AMWUA may prevent actors from gaining di-
rect insight into the wider policy context outside member city
interests and thereby reduce learning about broader system is-
sues (Berardo and Lubell 2019; Bodin, Sandstrom, and Crona
2017; Mewhirter, Coleman, and Berardo 2019; Siddiki,
Leach, and Kim 2017). While making intermediate collabo-
rative forums more broadly inclusive could help solve some
of these challenges, increasing participant diversity may also
disrupt the strength of the forum and undermine its bonding
capacity as preference heterogeneity increases (Lubell 2013).
Future research should investigate how intermediate col-
laborative forums can be (re)designed to reduce the negative
externalities and maximize the systemic benefits discussed
above. Doing this will require understanding the interactions

operate across multiple scales of interest.

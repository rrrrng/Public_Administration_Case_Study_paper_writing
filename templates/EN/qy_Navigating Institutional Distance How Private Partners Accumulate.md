ABSTRACT
Institutional distance shapes how private partners participate in cross-­country public–private partnership (PPP) projects, yet
its effects remain underexplored. Drawing on organizational institutionalism, we argue that institutional distance generates
adaptation pressures that prompt private partners to engage in sustained collaborations. Such collaborations lead private part-
ners to build relationships and connect into networks, within which positional advantages are accumulated. Using data on 3223
PPP projects across 91 countries from 1998 to 2022, we establish longitudinal networks and measure three types of positional
advantages: linkage, brokerage, and independence. Our results show that greater institutional distance is associated with the ac-
cumulation of these advantages over time. Moreover, host countries' involvement in economic-­political alliances moderates these
relationships in contrasting ways, weakening the effect on linkage and brokerage advantages while strengthening the effect on
independence advantages. We highlight implications for how governments can better support private partners' participation in
cross-­country PPP projects.
1   |   Introduction
Networks are carriers of institutional effects.
(Owen-­Smith and Powell 2008).
Over the decades, research on institutional environments and
networks in public management has largely developed along
separate paths. The former typically stems from organizational
sociology (Scott  2004), discussing topics such as institutional
isomorphism of public sector organizations (Frumkin  2004)
and nonprofit sector support organizations (Liu 2024). The lat-
ter, by contrast, emerges from economic sociology and follows a
structural tradition (Granovetter 1985), examining topics such
as the structure of effective governance of disaster response
networks (Nowell et al. 2018) and clean energy governance net-
works (Yi 2018). Although often treated separately, institutional
environments and networks are closely related (Owen-­Smith and
Powell 2008). Institutional analysts contend that organizations
have to adapt to institutional environments to survive and thrive
(DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Meyer and Rowan 1977). By occu-
pying advantageous positions in networks, organizations gain
better access to and control over the resources needed to nav-
igate these environments (Ma et al. 2025). Network structures
are adaptive to environments (Kraatz 1998). However, through a
literature review of network research in public management, Hu
et al. (2023) find that most studies focus on networks at a single
point in time, with few exploring the relationship between insti-
tutional environments and network structures over time.
In parallel, the rise of new public management has spurred
research into flexible, resource-­rich networks, which are used
to cope with “wicked problems” (Isett et al. 2011). Against this
context, public–private partnership (PPP) provides an important

arena for network research. PPP can be viewed as collaborative
networks formed through public–private linkages, reducing
public sector monopolies and enhancing private sector com-
petitiveness (Hu et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025). With the contin-
ued emergence of economic-­political alliances, PPP networks
increasingly span countries, connecting private partners from
different institutional environments. The differences in institu-
tional environments between countries, known as institutional
distance (Kostova 1999), present challenges for private partners.
While previous studies have analyzed the effects of institutional
environments in PPP projects' host countries (Casady  2021;
Fleta-­Asín et al. 2025; Song et al. 2023), few have examined the
effects of institutional distance between host countries and pri-
vate partners' home countries.
Taken together, we examine the effects of institutional dis-
tance in PPP networks. The argument of Owen-­Smith and
Powell  (2008), introduced earlier, is rooted in organizational
institutionalism and emphasizes that institutional effects are
transmitted through networks rather than acting on organiza-
tions in isolation. Although this argument is developed at the
level of organizational fields, it highlights a general mechanism
through which organizations respond to institutional environ-
ments via networks. This mechanism can inform cross-­country
analysis (Phillips et  al.  2009), where organizations operating
across different country-­level institutional environments rely on
network relationships to navigate unfamiliar rules, norms, and
expectations. These relationships connect private partners into
PPP networks through which they secure access to resources,
reduce uncertainty, and strengthen coordination (Alinaghian
and Razmdoost 2018). Based on this theoretical framing, we aim
to make three aspects of contributions.
First, PPP projects exist in recurring collaborations, where
project participants “gel” into networks after a series of proj-
ects that accumulate over time (Siemiatycki  2011). Previous
studies have explored PPP network structures (Hu et al. 2024;
Siemiatycki  2011; Singh and Prakash  2010; Wu et  al.  2025),
yet they are largely confined to single-­country contexts, with
limited analysis of larger-­scale networks in cross-­country con-
texts. More importantly, as indicated earlier, existing network
research in public management often examines networks at a
single point in time (Hu et al. 2023). Building on this, our study
shifts attention to how institutional distance influences the po-
sitions of private partners in PPP networks over time. Rather
than framing adaptation as a one-­time reaction, we analyze
how sustained collaborations shape private partners' positions
in networks.
Second, the relationship between institutional environments
and the participation of private partners has received extensive
attention in PPP studies (Casady 2021; Fleta-­Asín and Muñoz
2021; Jiménez et al. 2022; Panayides et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2019).
For instance, Casady  (2021) finds that private partners are
highly sensitive to conditions such as control of corruption,
government effectiveness, as well as voice and accountability.
The greater the institutional distance between home and host
countries, the more challenging it becomes for private partners
to learn and adapt to the host environment, thereby increasing
the need for their adaptation (Kostova et al. 2020). Conversely,
when the institutional environments of home and host countries
are similar, private partners seem to have a form of “immunity”
to host country environments. Despite this, the extent to which
institutional distance affects private partners' adaptations re-
mains unclear, particularly as reflected in their network posi-
tions. Building on this, we examine the relationship between
institutional distance and private partners' network positions.
Third, beyond the institutional distance between home and host
countries, it is also important to consider how host countries are
positioned within broader cross-­country contexts. Economic-­
political alliances, such as the Belt and Road Initiative, the Asia-­
Pacific Economic Cooperation, and the European Free Trade
Association, differ in governance regimes. However, these al-
liances share common features that shape how countries en-
gage in cross-­country cooperation (Lu et al. 2020). In our study,
economic-­political alliances are defined as cross-­country coop-
eration arrangements that, regardless of governance differences,
reduce institutional uncertainty by providing supportive condi-
tions for cross-­country economic activities (Buckley et al. 2013).
These features enhance private partners' perceptions of co-
ordination ease and institutional predictability, helping them
navigate institutional distance in cross-­country PPP projects.
Drawing on these insights, we examine how host countries'
involvement in economic-­political alliances moderates the re-
lationship between institutional distance and private partners'
network positions.
For the empirical analysis, we curate a longitudinal dataset cap-
turing the network participation of private partners across 3223
PPP projects across 91 countries from 1998 to 2022. The follow-
ing sections present the theoretical framework and hypotheses.
Next, we discuss strategies for data collection and variable mea-
surement. The results are then reported. The article concludes
with a summary of findings, implications for practice, limita-
tions, and directions for future research.

2.1   |   Institutional Distance and Network
Positional Advantages
Research on institutional environments spans multiple levels
(Scott  1995), including the global, field, industry, and organi-
zational levels. Among these, institutional distance is broadly
defined as the extent of differences between the institutional pro-
files of two countries, typically home and host (Kostova 1999). It
captures the challenges and frictions that arise when organiza-
tions operate across heterogeneous institutional environments.
This concept has informed cross-­country comparative studies
not only in international business but also in public manage-
ment (Kaufmann et  al.  2018). Prior reviews highlight three
main theoretical strands of institutional distance, including or-
ganizational institutionalism, institutional economics, and com-
parative institutionalism (Kostova et al. 2020), as summarized
in Table 1.
To ensure clarity, Kostova et  al.  (2020) emphasize the impor-
tance of grounding research in a specific theoretical strand,
cautioning that blending ideas across strands could obscure the-
oretical coherence and weaken explanatory power. Our study is
anchored in the strand of organizational institutionalism for the
following reasons. Institutional economics conceptualizes insti-
tutional distance primarily in terms of institutional quality and
transaction costs, focusing on how differences in institutional
arrangements create frictions that hinder economic exchanges.
While this strand offers valuable insights into market opera-
tions, it does not sufficiently address the legitimacy challenges
and adaptation processes organizations encounter when oper-
ating in unfamiliar institutional environments. Comparative
institutionalism, in contrast, centers on historically rooted,
system-­level complementarities and the diversity of institutional
configurations shaped by national trajectories. Although this
strand is useful for explaining country-­level institutional diver-
gence, it is less applicable to the organization-­level dynamics
emphasized in our study.
Our study examines how private partners develop positional ad-
vantages in networks through sustained participation in cross-­
country PPP projects. Greater institutional distance heightens
adaptation pressures, as foreign organizations often lack famil-
iarity with rules, norms, and expectations in the host country
(Kostova and Zaheer 1999; Xu and Shenkar 2002), and may also
encounter additional barriers arising from their “foreignness”
(Mezias  2002). Thus, greater institutional distance generates
substantial challenges as organizations confront switches in
institutional environments (Kostova and Roth  2002; Phillips
et al. 2009). These challenges prompt private partners to rede-
ploy resources, cultivate relationships, and secure advantageous
positions in PPP networks. Such positions provide private part-
ners with greater access to diverse resources, as well as the ca-
pacity to coordinate across disconnected organizations. Given
the focus on private partners' adaptation to unfamiliar environ-
ments, organizational institutionalism offers a coherent theoret-
ical foundation for our study.

in organizational institutionalism, showing how institutional
distance operates as an external stimulus that generates ad-

through structural embedding in networks and strategic posi-
tioning to gain legitimacy and navigate institutional complexity
(Alinaghian and Razmdoost  2018). In this process, networks
provide access to resources and external validation that can
compensate for limited local experience, facilitate broader col-
laboration across organizations, and enhance perceptions of

2.2   |   Classification of Network Positional
Advantages
In public management research, centrality is a key concept for
analyzing how organizations position themselves in networks
(Hager et al. 2004; Huang and Provan 2007; Schalk et al. 2010;
Villadsen 2011), as it provides the basis for classifying positional
advantages that organizations derive from their network embed-
ding (Yi  2018). The mainstream view of centrality stems from
economic sociology, which argues that the structure of inter-­
organizational networks shapes economic actions by influencing
access to and control over resources (Granovetter 1985; Uzzi 1996).
These resources, ranging from information and knowledge to col-
laborative opportunities (Huggins 2010), can be more effectively
leveraged when organizations occupy central positions, thereby
enhancing their capacity to accumulate advantages (Teece 2007).
Freeman (1978) proposes multiple indicators to measure central-
ity, among which degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality
have become the most widely used indicators for characterizing
network positions in public management studies (Hu et al. 2023;
Kapucu et al. 2017; Ma et al. 2025; Medina et al. 2022).
As shown in Figure 2, we classify these positional advantages
into two main categories. Resource-­dependence advantages em-
phasize positions built on external access and control, including
linkage advantage (degree centrality) and brokerage advantage
(betweenness centrality). Resource-­independence advantage
emphasizes positions that preserve autonomy and flexibility, re-
flected in independence advantage (closeness centrality). This
classification highlights the distinct ways in which organiza-
tions accumulate positional advantages when adapting to insti-
tutional distance.
First, degree centrality reflects the positional advantages of
a node based on the number of direct linkages it has to other
nodes in the network (Freeman 1978), thereby indicating link-
age advantages. It is often used to assess whether a node oc-
cupies a more central or peripheral position (Hu et al. 2023).
In public management research, organizations located at the
core are often crucial for network functioning, making them
more visible and perceived as essential by policymakers and
the public (Ma et  al.  2025). Such well-­linked organizations
engage in interactions with a wide range of partners, which
enhances their visibility and relational stability. This link-
age advantage not only broadens access to resources but also
strengthens influence (Provan et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2026),
enabling organizations to cope with unfamiliar institutional
environments.
Second, betweenness centrality reflects the positional advan-
tages of a node based on its frequency of lying on the shortest path
between pairs of nodes in the network (Freeman 1978), thereby
indicating brokerage advantages (Guler and Guillén  2010).
This shifts the analytical focus from direct linkages to bridg-
ing positions (Everett and Valente 2016). In public management
research, a high betweenness centrality indicates that an orga-
nization plays a broker role in the network (Horning et al. 2016).
Such brokerage positions generate two key advantages: access-
ing novel, non-­redundant resources by connecting otherwise
disconnected groups (Hu et al. 2023) and exerting control over
resource flows across the network (Lan et al. 2020). Together,
these advantages enable organizations to more effectively adapt
to unfamiliar institutional environments.

Third, closeness centrality reflects the positional advantages of a
node based on the average shortest distance to all other nodes in
the network (Freeman 1978), thereby indicating independence
advantages (Powell et al. 1996; Rowley 1997). A node is glob-
ally central if it has the shortest average distance from all other
nodes. In other words, it is close to all other nodes. Specifically,
closeness centrality is often calculated as the inverse sum of geo-
desic distances from one node to all others (Borgatti and Li 2009;
Wasserman and Faust 1994). In public management research, a
high closeness centrality contributes to greater effectiveness in
achieving collaborative goals (Zheng et al. 2025). Organizations
with such positions are less constrained by brokers and main-
tain broader autonomy in collaboration (Brandes et  al.  2016).
This independence advantage allows them to preserve flexibility
in structuring their connections, ensuring adaptability in unfa-
miliar institutional environments.
2.3   |   Institutional Distance and Linkage
Advantage
An organization's linkage advantage reflects the extent to which
it is directly linked with other organizations in the network (Hu
et  al.  2023). Greater institutional distance creates legitimacy
pressures and resource constraints, which push organizations
to extend partnerships in order to demonstrate reliability and
compensate for unfamiliarity (Kim and Kim 2018). In response,
private partners adapt by cultivating linkages with host gov-
ernments and local partners, embedding in PPP networks to
build trust and secure resource support (Hu et al. 2024; Velotti
et al. 2012). Network linkages are the conduits for assets, infor-
mation, and status (Yang et al. 2011). Linkage advantages imply
resource flows, high visibility, and power (Ibarra 1993). On the
one hand, central organizations that are well linked can better
tap into resources and remain better informed than peripheral
organizations (Shi et al. 2012). On the other hand, central or-
ganizations enjoy high visibility and power, attracting outside
organizations seeking to increase their own centrality through
collaborations (Gnyawali and Madhavan  2001). Over time,
these adaptations consolidate into stronger linkage advantages
that signal credibility in host country environments (Koka and
Prescott 2008; Siemiatycki 2011). Accordingly, we propose the
following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1 (H1).  Institutional distance is positively re-
lated to the linkage advantage of private partners participating
in PPP projects.
2.4   |   Institutional Distance and Brokerage
Advantage
An organization's brokerage advantage reflects the extent to
which it lies on the shortest path between other pairs of orga-
nizations (Guler and Guillén 2010). The linkage advantage only
considers the direct linkages that an organization has, whereas
the brokerage advantage takes into account both direct and in-
direct linkages in the network. Thus, linkage advantage char-
acterizes local centrality, whereas brokerage advantage reflects
global centrality (Hu et al. 2023). Greater institutional distance
fragments relational channels among host governments and
private partners from different countries, creating coordina-
tion challenges and information barriers. In response, private
partners adapt by taking on bridging roles that connect discon-
nected groups of organizations, thereby gaining access to diverse
and non-­redundant resources while facilitating coordination
across organizations (Hu et al. 2024; Kim and Kim 2018; Wu
et al. 2025). Over time, these adaptive bridging actions consoli-
date into stronger brokerage advantages, providing both control
over resource flows and access to novel information (Koka and
Prescott 2008; Meschi and Wassmer 2013). A broker position is
not likely to be built up in a short period of time but rather re-
quires the accumulation of non-­redundant resources over time
(Gilsing et al. 2008). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 2 (H2).  Institutional distance is positively re-
lated to the brokerage advantage of private partners participating
in PPP projects.

2.5   |   Institutional Distance and Independence
Advantage
An organization's independence advantage reflects the extent to
which it is free from the control of others (Brandes et al. 2016).
An organization is more independent when it has access to
many other organizations and relies on only a minimal number
of brokers (Gómez et al. 2003). Distinguished from brokerage
advantage, which emphasizes control, independence advantage
emphasizes autonomy and flexibility. Greater institutional dis-
tance heightens the risks of over-­reliance on specific brokers or
unstable partners, creating pressures for organizations to pre-
serve autonomy and flexibility. In response, private partners
adapt by cultivating dense and overlapping connections that
shorten access paths to diverse partners, thereby reducing de-
pendence on any single one (Siemiatycki 2011; Wu et al. 2025).
Over time, these adaptive actions consolidate into stronger in-
dependence advantages, enabling organizations to sustain col-
laboration autonomy in complex institutional environments
(Lin et al. 2009; Zheng et al. 2025). It is unlikely that these cen-
tral positions can be established in a short period of time but
rather emerge gradually as organizations adjust to host country
environments (Kim and Kim  2018; Koka and Prescott  2008).
Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 3 (H3).  Institutional distance is positively re-
lated to the independence advantage of private partners partici-
pating in PPP projects.
2.6   |   The Moderating Effects of Economic-­Political
Alliances
Economic-­political alliances, such as regional trade agreements
and bilateral or multilateral treaties, have significant effects on
the institutional environments of their members (Davis 2011).
Specifically, these alliances provide a context of socialization
in which sustained contacts and resource sharing help shape
external perceptions of alliance members' institutional envi-
ronments, making them appear less exclusive (Keukeleire and
Hooijmaaijers  2014). When PPP host countries participate in
multiple economic-­political alliances, the institutional differ-
ences perceived by private partners from home countries tend to
be narrowed (Buckley et al. 2013). As mentioned earlier in our
integrated framework, alliances moderate the relationship be-
tween institutional distance and network positional advantages.
Based on the classification of these advantages, alliances are
expected to affect them differently. When alliances expand the
pool of viable partners and channels, they are likely to diminish
reliance on direct linkages or specific brokerage bridges while
increasing the value of maintaining autonomy and flexibility.
Accordingly, these dynamics are expected to produce differenti-
ated moderating effects on network positional advantages, and
the specific hypotheses are presented below.
Linkage advantage refers to the number of direct links an orga-
nization maintains, and its underlying intuition is dependency.
Private partners with numerous linkages can access many re-
sources (Rowley 1997). However, these resources are often re-
dundant, as directly linked organizations may belong to the
same group, creating a tendency toward homogenization (Hu
et al. 2023). Regardless of whether these resources are redundant,
they can still help private partners adapt to the institutional envi-
ronments of host countries (Kostova et al. 2020). In PPP networks,
direct linkages enable private partners to align their actions with
environmental changes and foster dependency frameworks for
resource sharing (Velotti et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2025). The involve-
ment of host countries in economic-­political alliances can, on
the one hand, narrow the perceived institutional distance and,
on the other hand, enrich the pool of resources available to pri-
vate partners (Buckley et al. 2013). Consequently, the need for
private partners to rely on redundant resources is reduced. As
such, the stimulus of institutional distance and the motivation of
private partners to leverage linkage advantages tend to weaken.
Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 4a (H4a).  The involvement of PPP projects' host
countries in economic-­political alliances negatively moderates the
effect of institutional distance on the linkage advantage of private
partners.
Brokerage advantage captures how much an organization is in-­
between others (Burt 1992). Similar to the linkage advantage,
the intuition behind brokerage advantage is also dependency.
Linkage advantages are measured using degree centralities,
which reflects local dependence and focuses on a group of di-
rectly linked organizations (Provan et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2026).
By contrast, brokerage advantages are measured using be-
tweenness centralities, which reflects global dependence and
emphasizes bridging different groups of organizations that are
not directly connected (Hu et al. 2023). Resources from discon-
nected groups of organizations are more likely to be novel (Guler
and Guillén 2010). In PPP networks, private partners occupying
broker positions are able to acquire and control non-­redundant
resources (Wu et al. 2025). The involvement of host countries in
economic-­political alliances serves to narrow perceived institu-
tional distance and extends the pool of resources available to pri-
vate partners (Buckley et al. 2013). The need for private partners
to rely on non-­redundant resources diminishes. Consequently,
the stimulus of institutional distance and the motivation of pri-
vate partners to harness the brokerage advantage are inclined to
weaken. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 4b (H4b).  The involvement of PPP projects' host
countries in economic-­political alliances negatively moderates the
effect of institutional distance on the brokerage advantage of pri-
vate partners.
Independence advantage reflects an organization's indepen-
dent access to others in the network. Those organizations with
a low independence advantage are highly dependent on oth-
ers (especially brokers) for access to other parts of the network
(Rowley 1997). Conversely, organizations with a high indepen-
dence advantage can reach others with a minimum number of
brokers (Gómez et  al.  2003). Distinguished from linkage and
brokerage advantages, independence advantages emphasize the
ability to accumulate autonomy (Brass 1984; Powell et al. 1996).
In PPP networks, private partners occupying positions of high
closeness centralities can better reduce dependence on specific
organizations (Hu et al. 2024). The involvement of host coun-
tries in economic-­political alliances provides a pool of resources
and facilitates market access for the private sector of its member

3   |   Methods and Data
3.1   |   Research Setting and Data Sources
PPP has gained global prominence as an important model for
delivering infrastructure. Each private partner's participation
in a PPP project can essentially be regarded as a transaction
(Casady et al. 2020). PPP networks are formed through the ac-
cumulation of sustained transactions (Siemiatycki 2011). By re-
ferring to previous studies in the field of PPP (Fleta-­Asín and
Muñoz 2021; Fleta-­Asín et al. 2025; Jiménez et al. 2022; Song
et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2019), we collect data from the World
Bank's Private Participation in Infrastructure (PPI) database,
World Development Indicators (WDI), Worldwide Governance
Indicators (WGI), and Orbis (a comparable data resource on pri-
vate companies).
3.2   |   Measures
3.2.1   |   Dependent Variables
Grabher (2002) points out that “projects apparently operate in
a milieu of recurrent collaboration that, after several project
cycles, fills a pool of resources and ‘gels’ into latent networks.”
The positional advantages of private partners in networks are
not formed in the short term but instead develop cumulatively
over time. Accordingly, to capture these positional advantages,
we analyze networks that accumulate year by year (e.g., 1998,
1998–1999, 1998–2000, 1998–2001, and so on). Specifically,
we calculate three types of centralities for private partners
embedded in networks over time, including linkage advan-
tage (LA), brokerage advantage (BA), and independence ad-
vantage (IA). Although the term PPP dates back to the 1960s,
modern PPP emerged in the 1990s alongside the rise of new
public management, with a focus on improving infrastructure
management (Casady et al. 2020). Given data availability, our
sample covers the period from 1998 to 2022. The process for
establishing PPP networks is detailed in Appendix A.
3.2.2   |   Independent Variable
The independent variable is derived from WGI. The WGI data-
set combines surveys of households and businesses, as well
as assessments by a variety of business information provid-
ers, non-­governmental organizations, multilateral organiza-
tions, and other public sector agencies. This dataset has been
widely used in PPP research to characterize institutional en-
vironments (Fleta-­Asín and Muñoz 2021; Song et  al.  2023;
Wang et al. 2019). Importantly, within the organizational in-
stitutionalism tradition, WGI provides a theoretically grounded
operationalization of the regulatory aspects of institutional
environments (Kostova et al. 2020), as it captures rule-­based
governance conditions that underpin legitimacy expectations
and shape organizational adaptation pressures. Specifically,
our measure of institutional environments is based on six indi-
cators: voice and accountability, political stability and absence
of violence, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule
of law, and control of corruption.
Drawing on organizational institutionalism, we capture the
extent rather than the direction of institutional differences be-
tween home and host countries (Kostova et al. 2020). Whether
host countries' institutional environments are stronger or
weaker than those of home countries, such differences invari-
ably impose greater adaptation pressures on private partners.
As detailed in Appendix  A, Table  A2 shows that our sample
is concentrated in low-­ and middle-­income host countries. In
these contexts, institutional distance tends to heighten adap-
tation pressures, as private partners face unfamiliar and less
predictable institutional environments, making organizational
adaptation especially salient. Following Eden and Miller (2004),
we calculate institutional distance as the absolute difference in
the institutional environment between home and host countries,
yielding our first measure of institutional distance (ID1).
For robustness checks, we adopt two alternative measures. First,
while retaining the WGI indicators, we replace the absolute dif-
ference method with the Euclidean distance approach, which is
widely used in institutional distance research (Berry et al. 2010),
yielding institutional distance 2 (ID2). Second, we retain the ab-
solute difference method but replace the WGI with the Economic
Freedom Index (EFI), which also captures the regulatory as-
pects of institutional environments (Kostova et al. 2020), yield-
ing institutional distance 3 (ID3). The calculation procedures for
institutional distance are provided in Appendix A.
3.2.3   |   Moderating Variable
We examine the economic-­political alliances (EA) in which
PPP projects' host countries are involved. Following Jansen
et al. (2020), we identify a set of alliances that have been influ-
ential in regional trade and investment, such as North American
Free Trade Agreement, European Free Trade Association,
Belt and Road Initiative, Asia-­Pacific Economic Cooperation,
Caribbean Community Single Market and Economy, and East
African Community. Given the distribution of host countries in
the PPI database, we focus specifically on alliances involving
emerging economies. The measurement details of this moder-
ating variable and the list of relevant alliances are provided in
Appendix A. To alleviate the potential multicollinearity prob-
lems, we apply a mean-­centering procedure before generating
the interaction terms used to examine moderating effects (Aiken
and West 1991; Lin and Germain 2003).

3.2.4   |   Control Variables
A set of control variables is included, including project-­, orga-
nization-­, and country-­level variables (Wang et al. 2019). At the
project level, we account for the way in which a PPP project is
initiated, capturing whether a project involves unsolicited pro-
posals. PPP projects initiated by government agencies are typ-
ically procured through a structured public-­planning process
involving competitive tendering (Siemiatycki  2009). By con-
trast, under the unsolicited proposal approach, private partners
identify and propose projects to governments without formal
invitation (Osei-­Kyei et al. 2018). This variable equals 1 if the
PPP project is developed through an unsolicited proposal and
0 otherwise. We also control for the project contract period and
total investment to capture differences in project scale and com-
plexity (Zou et al. 2014). Total investment consists of physical
assets and payments to government. As Bertelli  (2019) notes,
this helps account for the possibility that projects of different
sizes and scopes may be associated with distinct participation
patterns of private partners due to unobserved legal or business
conventions.
At the organization level, because past investment experience
is an important factor shaping subsequent cross-­country de-
cisions (Collins et al. 2009; Galavotti et al. 2017), we include a
variable capturing the private partner's cumulative investment
in the host country, reflecting its experience in that market. At
the country level, we include GDP, GDP growth, and foreign di-
rect investment (FDI) net inflows and outflows, as these factors
strongly influence private partners' cross-­country investment
decisions (Forssbæck and Oxelheim 2008). Country-­level con-
trol variables are drawn from WDI, which provides global de-
velopment data covering economic, social, and environmental
indicators. Because our focus is on institutional distance, we
calculate the differences between home and host countries in
GDP, GDP growth, FDI net inflows, FDI net outflows, and pop-
ulation. To account for the distributional characteristics of total
investment, GDP, and population, we apply natural logarithms
(Becker et al. 2019). This approach helps isolate the role of in-
stitutional distance by reducing potential confounding effects
from broader country-­level disparities. Consistent with institu-
tional distance, all country-­level difference variables are lagged
by 1 year.
After eliminating missing values, the final sample comprises
3223 projects across 91 countries, covering 6099 transactions.
Each transaction represents a private partner's investment in a
host country through participation in a PPP project in a given
year. Table 2 reports descriptive statistics for these variables and
Table 3 presents their correlations.
4   |   Model Specifications
In examining how private partners accumulate their network
positional advantages through cross-­country transactions,
we face a potential endogeneity concern stemming from self-­
selection bias. This bias arises when the key explanatory vari-
able is not randomly assigned but instead determined by private
partners themselves (Wolfolds and Siegel  2019). In our study,
private partners with stronger network positional advantages
may be more inclined to invest in countries with greater insti-
tutional distance to extend their competitiveness. Such non-­
random selection into host countries could bias estimates.
To address this potential bias, we employ a two-­stage Probit se-
lection model for instrumental variable (IV) estimation (Yi and
Liu 2022). IV estimation allows the use of exogenous variables to
mitigate selection bias in the absence of a randomized experimen-
tal design. For an IV to be valid, it is required to meet two condi-
tions: correlation with the endogenous independent variable and
no correlation with the error term. In other words, the IV should
influence the dependent variable only through its effect on the in-
dependent variable.
Based on prior literature and theoretical reasoning, we select three
IVs. First, we use host countries' PM2.5 exposure concentration,
as higher pollution levels raise operational costs (e.g., employee
health expenses) (Chang et al. 2016; Hanna and Oliva 2015) and
reduce the host country's attractiveness for cross-­country trans-
actions. Second, following Kling and Weitzel (2011) and Basuil
and Datta (2015), who show that industry characteristics influ-
ence cross-­country transactions, we include the industry-­level
entry rate of other private partners from the same industry into
the host country (excluding the focal private partner). This vari-
able captures industry-­specific attractiveness that may affect the
likelihood of engaging in cross-­country transactions. Third, we
use the share of tourism in the host country's GDP as an indica-
tor of investment attractiveness, as tourism-­oriented economies
often have more developed infrastructure and favorable invest-
ment conditions (Aradhyula and Tronstad 2003).
Since cross-­country investment decisions are generally based on
prior conditions, all IVs are lagged by 1 year, consistent with the
treatment of the independent variables. We also include year and
region fixed effects to capture time-­specific shocks and time-­
invariant regional characteristics. Further details on the selec-
tion of IVs are provided in Appendix B.

5.4   |   Robustness Checks
To further ensure the robustness of our results, we conduct sev-
eral additional tests. First, recognizing that the effects of inde-
pendent variables may take longer to materialize, we extend the
lag between the independent and dependent variables from 1 to
2 years (Park and Liang 2021). Second, as described earlier, we
recalculate institutional distance using an alternative formula
and re-­estimate the models accordingly. Third, acknowledging
the limitations of the WGI, we replace it with the EFI. The de-
tailed results are reported in Appendix C. Overall, the robust-
ness tests confirm our key findings.

Organizational

understand the effects of institutional environments, it is
necessary to shift the level of analysis from organizations to
the inter-­organizational relationships that characterize the
networks in which organizations are embedded (Vasudeva
et  al.  2013). Despite the important role of institutional envi-
ronments in shaping inter-­organizational networks, relevant
research in public management remains underdeveloped (Hu
et  al.  2023). One important reason is that longitudinal and
large-­scale network data are required to statistically assess how
environments affect networks over time. By analyzing 3223 PPP
projects across 91 countries, our study promotes the integration
of public management and international business research. In
particular, we construct a dataset mapping longitudinal and
large-­scale PPP networks, enabling us to trace how network
structures accumulate over time. Drawing on organizational
institutionalism, our study articulates a framework linking in-
stitutional distance to organizational adaptation and, in turn,
to network positional advantages. Building on this framework,
we make three contributions.
First, the participation of private partners is highly sensitive to
institutional environments, such as freedom from corruption
and quality of regulations (Casady 2021). Prior studies have ex-

of the PPP literature has regarded institutional distance as a
“disadvantage.” Similarly, in international business research,
existing studies generally conclude that institutional distance
negatively affects the survival and thriving of organizations
engaged in cross-­country transactions (Donnelly et  al.  2024;
Kostova et al. 2020; Mingo et al. 2018; Wu and Salomon 2016).
By contrast, our study reveals the “bright side” of institutional
distance.
We show that institutional distance acts as a stimulus that
drives private partners to adapt to unfamiliar environments
by accumulating positional advantages in networks. In other
words, abstract institutional distance at the country-­level
can be translated into concrete relational strategies at the
organization-­level over time. While institutional distance can
be viewed as an external constraint or barrier, organizations
can endogenize it by leveraging resources. In international
business research, recent studies have begun to explore how
disadvantages of institutional distance can be turned into
advantages. For example, Donnelly et  al.  (2024) argue that
institutional distance can motivate organizations to exploit
managerial and financial slack resources to survive and thrive.
Extending this line of research, we argue that organizations
occupy advantageous network positions to enhance their ac-
cess to and control over resources, thereby coping with unfa-
miliar institutional environments.
Second, no organization possesses sufficient resources to ad-
dress wicked problems alone. PPP networks are particularly im-
portant when neither the public nor the private sector can deliver
goods or services independently (Agranoff and McGuire 2001).
Recent public management studies have begun to trace PPP net-
work evolution (Hu et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025), but their evi-
dence remains largely confined to single-­country or subnational
settings. In our study, we map PPP networks over a broader spa-
tial and temporal scope.
Our study responds to the call by Hu et al. (2023) to examine
how contextual factors shape networks over time. Our findings
show that institutional distance significantly influences posi-
tional advantages in cross-­country PPP networks. Greater insti-
tutional differences encourage private partners to accumulate
direct linkages, act as brokers bridging disconnected groups,
and establish shorter paths that reduce reliance on third parties.
These outcomes extend PPP network research beyond single-­
country settings and demonstrate that institutional environ-
ments are not merely background variations but determinants
of network adaptation.
Third, economic-­political alliances are conceptualized as
cross-­country cooperation arrangements that facilitate eco-
nomic interaction and investment among member coun-
tries, particularly by providing supportive conditions for
foreign direct investment (Buckley et al. 2013; Keukeleire and
Hooijmaaijers  2014). Rather than constituting unified gover-
nance regimes, such alliances operate by lowering institutional
uncertainty and easing access to host environments, thereby
mitigating frictions arising from institutional differences (Zhu
et al. 2015). We find that the involvement of PPP host countries
in economic-­political alliances negatively moderates the effect
of institutional distance on the linkage and brokerage advan-
tages of private partners. Intriguingly, it positively moderates
the effect of institutional distance on independence advantages.
This dual pattern underscores that alliances act as a double-­
edged mechanism. The strengthening of supportive conditions,
which aids private partners in adapting to unfamiliar environ-
ments, reduces their reliance on the utility of direct linkages
and brokerage bridges, while simultaneously elevating the
value of independent routes.
In public management research, degree centrality, between-
ness centrality, and closeness centrality are often treated as
analogous. For example, Ma et al. (2025) show that centrally
positioned organizations, regardless of whether they are mea-
sured by degree, betweenness, or closeness centrality, are better
able to survive and thrive as institutional environments change
over time. In contrast, we make a nuanced distinction by char-
acterizing three forms of positional advantages corresponding
to three types of centralities. Degree centrality and between-
ness centrality emphasize dependence on redundant and
non-­redundant resources, respectively (Kim and Zhu  2018),
whereas closeness centrality emphasizes autonomy and inde-
pendence in resource access (Powell et al. 1996; Rowley 1997).
Economic-­political alliances provide member countries' pri-
vate sectors with a resource pool and market access (Buckley
et al. 2013). The involvement of host countries in such alliances
enriches the pool of resources available to private partners, re-
ducing their dependence on specific partners. Consequently,
the moderating effects for linkage advantage (degree central-
ity) and brokerage advantage (betweenness centrality) are
negative. Conversely, the involvement of economic-­political
alliances enhances private partners' need for autonomy in ac-
cessing a wider pool of resources. Accordingly, the moderat-
ing effect for independence advantage (closeness centrality) is
positive.
7   |   Conclusions
7.1   |   Summary of Findings
First, the existing PPP literature examines the effects of host
countries' institutional environments on the participation of
private partners (Casady  2021; Fleta-­Asín and Muñoz 2021;
Jiménez et al. 2022; Panayides et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2019). We
extend PPP research by incorporating the concept of institutional
distance, which captures institutional differences between pri-
vate partners' home countries and PPP projects' host countries,
and by examining how these differences shape private partners'
participation. In doing so, our study enriches the understand-
ing of organizational institutionalism in cross-­country PPP
projects. Moreover, unlike prior studies of institutional distance
(Donnelly et al. 2024; Kostova et al. 2020; Mingo et al. 2018; Wu
and Salomon 2016), which largely highlight its disadvantages,
we reveal its “bright side,” showing how institutional distance
can positively shape the accumulation of positional advantages
among private partners in PPP networks.

Second, network research has grown rapidly in public manage-
ment, yet most studies analyze networks at a given point in time.
Moreover, existing PPP network research is largely confined to
specific countries (Hu et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025), which con-
strains its ability to capture the effects of cross-­country contex-
tual factors. Our study advances this line of research by using
longitudinal data to map network structures over time and
across countries, echoing the call to investigate how contextual
factors shape networks dynamically (Hu et al. 2023).
Third, prior studies have indicated the relevance of economic-­
political alliances in shaping PPP projects (Murphy  2008;
Siemiatycki  2013; Yang et  al.  2020). Our findings reveal the
“double-­edged” effects of economic-­political alliances. As host
countries participate in more alliances, institutional distance
perceived by private partners from home countries narrows,
while the pool of accessible resources expands. As a result, alli-
ances negatively moderate the effect of institutional distance on
linkage and brokerage advantages. These two advantages cor-
respond to degree and betweenness centrality respectively, and
they prioritize resource dependence. Conversely, alliances posi-
tively moderate the effect on independence advantage, which is
derived from closeness centrality and emphasizes resource au-
tonomy. By distinguishing among three types of centrality and
their corresponding positional advantages, we extend research
on network positions (Hu et al. 2023) and provide a nuanced
understanding of organizational institutionalism (Kostova
et al. 2020).
7.2   |   Implications for Practice
Our study provides threefold implications for practice. First,
institutional distance can stimulate private partners to embed
themselves in PPP networks. For policymakers in host coun-
tries, because institutional environments are relatively sta-
ble and unlikely to change in the short term (Kingston and
Caballero  2009), efforts should focus less on eliminating in-
stitutional differences and more on leveraging them as cata-
lysts for collaboration. Previous studies highlight economic
tools, such as subsidies (Wang et  al.  2019) or anti-­corruption
reforms (Jiménez et  al.  2017), to attract PPP investments. By
contrast, our findings suggest expanding tools related to collab-
orative support, such as digital PPP investment platforms (Shor
et  al.  2024) and cross-­country information channels (Coche
et al. 2024), to streamline partner matching and standardize in-
formation sharing. They reduce search and coordination costs
and transform institutional differences into opportunities for
learning and cooperation. The feasibility of these tools depends
on balancing inclusiveness and efficiency. Centralized platforms
may improve transparency and coordination but risk privileging
better-­resourced organizations while marginalizing smaller and
less-­resourced ones. Policymakers therefore need to incorporate
features such as simplified registration, training modules for
small and medium-­sized organizations, multilingual interfaces,
or tiered participation schemes to safeguard inclusiveness while
reaping efficiency gains.
Second, for policymakers in home countries, our findings cau-
tion that institutional similarity, that is, a condition of low in-
stitutional distance, may create “institutional insensitivity.” In
this case, private partners lose incentives to adapt, which in
turn weakens their accumulated network positional advantages.
This underscores the need to foster private partners' adaptive
capabilities to navigate diverse institutional environments.
A critical dilemma arises from the “double-­edged” effects of
economic-­political alliances. While alliances lower entry barri-
ers and enhance legitimacy (Buckley et al. 2013; Keukeleire and
Hooijmaaijers  2014), they may also diminish opportunities to
build linkage and brokerage advantages, thereby constraining
strategic flexibility. Policymakers need to balance these trade-­
offs by adopting measures that leverage the alliances' legitimacy
benefits while mitigating their constraints. Measures, such as
broad cross-­country matchmaking and partner coordination ini-
tiatives, are more effective in open host country environments,
as they capitalize on alliance-­based legitimacy and easier entry
while counteracting reduced incentives to accumulate linkage
and brokerage advantages. In exclusive environments, however,
such measures may tend to be symbolic or fail to deliver substan-
tive outcomes, given that alliances' ability to alleviate legitimacy
pressures is limited and their tendency to restrict strategic flex-
ibility becomes more pronounced. Accordingly, concentrating
resources on a few trusted anchor partners is practical, as these
partners often possess local knowledge, established relational
networks with host governments, and a proven track record of
navigating exclusive environments. These capabilities enable
them to capitalize on alliances' legitimacy benefits and enhance
their independence advantages, facilitating genuine adaptation
to host environments.
Third, for the private sector, Donnelly et  al.  (2024) argue
that slack resources can help firms endogenize institutional
distance. Building on this view, we show that private part-
ners can further enhance adaptation by occupying central
network positions that extend access to and control over re-
sources. These resources, such as information, knowledge,
and collaboration opportunities, exist not only within indi-
vidual organizations but also across inter-­organizational con-
nections (Huggins 2010). Yet private partners face trade-­offs
when prioritizing different types of positional advantages.
Overprioritizing linkage or brokerage advantages can strain
coordination and increase dependency risks, while focusing
predominantly on independence advantages may diminish
collaboration opportunities. Larger private partners with
abundant resources may be better positioned to pursue in-
dependence advantages, whereas smaller and medium-­sized
ones may find it more practical to build linkage or brokerage
advantages through coalitions or consortia. This highlights
the need for private partners to dynamically align their net-
work strategies with their resource endowments.
7.3   |   Limitations and Future Research
Despite these contributions and implications, our study has sev-
eral limitations that call for further research. First, our analy-
sis relies on the PPI database, complemented by private partner
information collected from Orbis. While PPI provides standard-
ized project records at financial closure, its coverage is limited.
It primarily focuses on low-­ and middle-­income countries, and
small or subnational initiated PPP projects are less consistently
captured. These features narrow the range of institutional

environments observed and constrain the generalizability of our
findings. Future research could expand by using alternative PPP
databases such as IJGlobal, GlobalData, or Preqin to broaden
both country and project coverage. Nevertheless, for the pur-
poses of this study, we rely on PPI because of its public avail-
ability, consistent definitions, and long time span across many
countries, which together allow us to establish longitudinal and
cross-­country PPP networks. To mitigate potential selection bias,
we follow Yi and Liu (2022) in constructing a dyadic panel two-­
stage probit selection model, introducing instrumental variables
and incorporating the IMR to obtain more reliable estimates.
Second, our measure of institutional distance is based on WGI.
Although widely applied in PPP research, WGI is perception-­
based and therefore carries inherent limitations. We emphasize
regulatory aspects because PPP projects are organized through
public–private linkages, and the regulatory environment is
crucial to shaping private partners' adaptation. To address this
limitation, we conduct robustness tests using EFI. Compared
with WGI, EFI provides an alternative measure by emphasizing
market-­oriented institutional features such as property rights,
business freedom, trade freedom, and investment freedom.
Future research could incorporate normative and cognitive
aspects of institutional environments to deepen understand-
ing of how institutional distance influences private partners'
adaptation in cross-­country PPP projects. Relative to general
country-­level measures, issue-­specific approaches offer greater
sensitivity in assessing institutional distance and represent a
valuable direction for exploring different aspects of institutional
environments (Kostova et al. 2020).
Third, our study adopts the “structuralist” approach to net-
work research in public management (Berry et  al.  2004; Hu
et al. 2023; Isett et al. 2011; Kapucu et al. 2017). It assumes that
organizational adaptation in PPP networks primarily unfolds
through sustained cooperation, with linkage, brokerage, and in-
dependence advantages captured by degree, betweenness, and
closeness centrality. While these are widely accepted structural
proxies, they inevitably leave out non-­structural forms of adapta-
tion. Future research could therefore complement our structural
analysis by employing qualitative case studies or alternative net-
work measures, offering a more nuanced understanding of how
organizations adapt.

Abstract
As the bureaucratic policymaking process has frequently deviated from conventional procedures contemplated by administrative law stat­
utes, recent research suggests that bureaucrats strategically use rulemaking procedures to pursue their own goals and circumvent political
interventions. However, the literature has often neglected implementation issues that bureaucrats confront in the policymaking process. Building
on a bureaucratic reputation perspective that explicitly recognizes bureaucrats’ concern for implementation failure and reputational damage,
this study examines when and why U.S. federal agencies issue rules without prior notice and comment instead of proposing rules through the
conventional notice-and-comment process. Using logistic regressions with fixed effects, based on over 16,000 rules published between 2000
and 2020, we find that agencies are more likely to solicit prior public comment when making more complex and stringent rules. However, they
tend to bypass it when making new rules and joint rules with other agencies. This study also shows that the positive effect of rule stringency
on agencies’ use of the conventional notice-and-comment process tends to be more pronounced in agencies with higher proportions of pro­
fessional bureaucrats. Overall, our findings indicate that bureaucrats’ choices of rulemaking procedures might be shaped by their incentives to
prevent implementation failure and preserve agency reputation, which can be compatible with the norms of democratic governance.
Key words: bureaucratic reputation; procedural politicking; implementation uncertainty; professionalism; Administrative Procedure Act.
Introduction
As elected officials delegate widespread policymaking au­
thority to the bureaucracy, often without clear guidelines, un­
elected bureaucrats have considerable discretion in making
and implementing policies (Rourke 1984; Yackee and Yackee
2016). While such delegations occur with the expectation
that the bureaucracy accommodates various societal interests
in knowledgeable and legitimate ways (Rosenbloom 2000),
ironically, bureaucratic discretion has been largely considered
a threat to democratic governance (Meier and O’Toole 2006;
Spence 1997). Accordingly, presuming that bureaucrats are
agents who have incentives to use their expertise in policies
and procedures for their own goals, research on political con­
trol of the bureaucracy has focused on how to ensure bu­
reaucratic compliance with the mandates and preferences of
political principals (Weingast and Moran 1983; McCubbins,
Noll, and Weingast 1987, 1989; McCubbins and Schwartz
1984; but see Waterman and Meier 1998).
As “unorthodox rulemaking” practices that depart from the
original intent of the 1946 Administrative Procedure Act
(APA) have increased over time (Gluck, O’Connell, and Po
2015), recent research suggests that bureaucratic agencies en­
gage in “procedural politicking”—“using procedures in stra­
tegic ways so as to insulate policies that are at risk of political
interventions and ensure that bureaucrat-preferred policies
endure” (Potter 2019, p. 6; Nou 2012; Raso 2015). For ex­
ample, Potter (2019) provides evidence that federal agencies
tend to set public comment periods to overlap with congres­
sional recesses when unfavorable congressional oversight is
expected. Likewise, Nou and Stiglitz (2015) demonstrate that
agencies are less likely to disclose their rulemaking agendas
during periods of divided government than during unified
government. While these studies provide important insights
into bureaucrats’ strategic use of administrative procedures
for their own preferences and the limitations of procedures as
a major tool of political control, both the political control and
procedural politicking literature have focused on institutional
tensions, often implying ideological or partisan politics, but
have largely treated policy implementation as a black box.
A reputation-based perspective explicitly recognizes that
bureaucrats’ use of discretion is heavily shaped by task-
specific concern for implementation failure (Carpenter 2002;
Moffitt 2010, 2014). In order to avoid policy failure and sub­
sequent reputational damage, bureaucrats are less likely to
make policy decisions without taking the technical and po­
litical feasibility of implementation into account (Rourke
1984). This indicates that it is necessary for agencies to build,
maintain, and enhance the support and cooperation of mul­
tiple stakeholders affected by their policies (Carpenter 2001,
2010; Long 1949; Simon, Smithburg, and Thompson 1950).
Such roles of bureaucrats in building political consensus
among various interests—that are partly driven by their pur­
suit of effective implementation—have long been considered
desirable for achieving a pluralist democracy (Appleby 1949;
Herring 1936; Long 1949). Thus, exploring bureaucratic dis­
cretion through the lens of bureaucratic reputation allows
us to appreciate its implications for democratic governance
in a more balanced way, accommodating the necessity of

Focusing on bureaucrats’ incentives to preserve agency
reputation, this study examines when and why U.S. federal
agencies issue their rules through interim, direct, or tempo­
rary final rulemaking without prior notice and comment,
as opposed to using the conventional notice-and-comment
process that provides the public with an opportunity to com­
ment on proposed rules. While the former procedures expe­
dite the rulemaking process and afford flexibility to agencies,
the latter more faithfully embodies the intent of the APA to
inculcate democratic values—such as procedural due process,
representation, and openness—in the rulemaking process
(Asimow 1999; Noah 1999; Rosenbloom 2000). Given that
these rulemaking procedures reflect different levels of tradeoffs
between bureaucratic and democratic values, looking at
agencies’ choices of rulemaking procedures can offer insights
into the compatibility of bureaucratic policymaking with
democracy.
In explaining which rulemaking procedures agencies em­
ploy, this study mainly focuses on the levels of uncertainty
in rule implementation. To prevent implementation failure
and subsequent reputational damage, bureaucrats need to
reduce implementation uncertainty by acquiring implemen­
tation knowledge and eliciting cooperation from affected
groups (Carpenter 2002; Moffitt 2010, 2014; O’Toole and
Meier 2003). Since inviting public comments at an early
stage of rule development might be more procedurally legiti­
mate and effectively facilitate learning and cooperation from
stakeholders, we argue that bureaucrats are more likely to use
the conventional notice-and-comment process when making
rules with greater levels of implementation uncertainty. To
test this argument, this study captures multiple dimensions of
rule implementation uncertainty, such as rule novelty (making
a new rule versus revising an existing one), complexity (word
count in a rule’s abstract), stringency (the number of restric­
tive words in a rule and its industry relevance), and joint
rulemaking (a joint rule with other agencies).
This study also explores how the relationship between
agencies’ choices of rulemaking procedures and rule imple­
mentation uncertainty varies by bureaucratic professionalism
and anticipated responses from interest groups. Bureaucratic
professionalism is measured by the proportion of professional
bureaucrats at agency headquarters, while expected interest
group responses are operationalized by the ideological diver­
gence between agencies and their interest groups. By exploring
how task-level decisions are shaped by agency- and political
environment-level contexts, we shed light on bureaucrats’
reputational incentives in a more integrated way (Jilke et al.
2019; Lynn, Heinrich, and Hill 2000).
Using a dataset of over 16,000 rules published between
2000 and 2020, this study provides evidence that agencies
are more likely to use the conventional notice-and-comment
process when dealing with more complex and stringent rules,
which might require greater efforts to acquire implementa­
tion knowledge and cooperation from affected interests for
effective rulemaking. On the other hand, we demonstrate
that agencies are less likely to use such a standard procedure
for new rules and joint rules with other agencies, perhaps
reflecting reputational incentives to establish less disputed
jurisdictions. Second, this study finds that the increased use
of the conventional notice-and-comment process for more
stringent rules tends to be more pronounced in agencies with
higher proportions of professional bureaucrats, though the
evidence for this tendency is not robust. Finally, we demon­
strate that agencies’ choices of rulemaking procedures are
largely responsive to the priorities of political institutions in
a nonideological manner and appear insensitive to the ide­
ological or partisan (un)favorability of political institutions
and interest groups toward agencies. These findings indicate
that reputational concerns might outweigh ideological or
partisan politics in bureaucrats’ strategic use of the notice-
and-comment process, offering insights into the democratic
implications of bureaucratic reputation management.
APA notice-and-comment process
Among various procedural options that bureaucratic agencies
can use in the rulemaking process (see Potter 2019, Chapter
3), this study focuses on their use of the conventional notice-
and-comment process versus interim, direct, or temporary
final rulemaking procedures.1 The APA requires agencies
to issue a notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) in the
Federal Register and provides interested stakeholders with
an opportunity to present opinions, evidence, critiques, and
perspectives on it (Kerwin and Furlong 2019; Rosenbloom
2000). Submitted comments are considered before finalizing
the rule, though how seriously agencies take those comments
can vary by agency and type of commenters (e.g., business
interests, public interest groups, and citizens) (Yackee, and
Yackee 2006). The APA also prescribes the conditions under
which agencies can be exempted from going through the con­
ventional notice-and-comment process: “interpretative rules,
general statements of policy, or rules of agency organiza­
tion, procedure, or practice” or “when the agency for good
cause finds that notice and public procedure thereon are im­
practicable, unnecessary, or contrary to the public interest”
(5 U.S.C. § 553). The former categories of rules are gener­
ally published through guidance documents (Yackee 2020;
Header & Yackee 2020a), while the latter, known as the good
cause exemption, are issued via interim, direct, or temporary
final rulemaking we focus on.2

to be applied narrowly, agencies have issued a considerable

difficulties of political institutions in monitoring rulemaking

rulemaking procedures (Raso 2015). While such discretion

the APA, agencies can use interim, direct, or temporary
rulemaking procedures to pursue their own policy preferences

have already taken effect might discourage stakeholders’ par­

comments seriously (Asimow 1999). Agencies can also re­

without the withdrawal of the rule (Noah 1999).3

whether each rule is published in a legally and procedurally
valid manner. Rather, it seeks to find systematic patterns of
when and why agencies issue interim, direct, or temporary
final rules without prior comment through a large-N analysis.
By doing so, it explores how agencies’ choices of rulemaking
procedures can be shaped by bureaucrats’ concern for agency
reputation and appreciates their compatibility with the norms
of democratic governance.
Implementation uncertainty and bureaucratic
reputation
The extent to which agencies can avoid policy failure and
reputational damage depends on how adequately they ad­
dress technical and political issues related to rule implemen­
tation. Since elected officials delegate policymaking authority
to agencies in part due to their lack of policy expertise (Bawn
1995), the mere publication of a rule might not be enough
to maintain bureaucratic reputation. As Kerwin and Furlong
(2019, p. 90) point out, “[t]he preoccupation with production
of rules obscure[s] the fact that rules are worse than useless
if they result in an inconsistent, incoherent regulatory pro­
gram that cannot be understood by the regulated community
or enforced by government agencies in the field.” Even when
politicians leave controversial issues to agencies for blame-
shifting purposes (Fiorina 1982; Weaver 1986), agencies need
to avoid being a scapegoat for policy failure. Thus, agencies
should demonstrate their ability to make a rule that can
achieve policy goals. Policy failure and reputational damage
can lead to the loss of discretion and resources in making
and implementing policies and even agency termination
(Carpenter 2001, 2010; MacDonald and Franko 2007).
To avoid policy failure and subsequent loss of reputation,
it is necessary for agencies to reduce implementation uncer­
tainty (O’Toole and Meier 2003).4 To do so, they should seek
knowledge about how a rule can be effectively implemented.
Without such knowledge, seemingly straightforward rules
could easily fail to achieve their goals (Mazmanian and
Sabatier 1983; Pressman and Wildavsky 1984). Since bureau­
cratic agencies do not inherently possess technical expertise
in their policy areas, they need to invest substantial time and
effort in developing policymaking and implementation ex­
pertise relevant to new policies (Gailmard and Patty 2013;
Hirsch 2016; Turner 2017; Waterman and Meier 1998). Even
if they possess expertise, policies and their implementation
should be adapted to new needs and issues arising from eco­
nomic, political, social, and technological changes. Therefore,
agencies should solicit information on implementation from
diverse stakeholders, such as affected industries, state and
local governments, private and nonprofit implementers, pro­
fessional organizations, and the mass public (Moffitt 2010,
2014). The standard notice-and-comment process (i.e.,
NPRMs) would be one means of acquiring such information
at an early stage of rule development.
Implementation uncertainty depends on not only technical
expertise but also the cooperative and coordinated efforts of
affected stakeholders (Mazmanian and Sabatier 1983; Moffitt
2014; O’Toole and Meier 2003; Pressman and Wildavsky
1984). If a rule regulates private actions, implementation
success or failure might be determined by the extent to
which regulated entities cooperate with the rule. If a rule is
implemented by state and local governments, their imple­
mentation efforts might substantially affect policy outcomes.
Given the limitations of formal authority in monitoring and
sanctioning noncompliance, establishing the legitimacy and
trustworthiness of the authority is likely to be more effective
at eliciting cooperation and reducing implementation uncer­
tainty (Miller 1992). As cooperation and compliance can be
fostered through “two-way reciprocity” between superiors
and subordinates (Barnard 1938; Miller 1992; Simon 1947),
as well as between political principals and bureaucrats
(Carpenter and Krause 2015; Krause 1999), the legitimacy
of rulemaking agencies vis-à-vis affected stakeholders can be
established through formal and informal negotiation and bar­
gaining processes. In this regard, the conventional notice-and-
comment process might help agencies protect their reputation
by enabling them to identify the degrees of stakeholders’
support and opposition and to seek compromise and mutual
agreement (West 2004).

from the fairness of its procedures. Langbein and Kerwin

It is noteworthy that a reputation-based perspective does
not deny bureaucratic motives to manipulate administra­
tive procedures for their own policy preferences. Unlike the
political control and procedural politicking literature, how­
ever, it suggests whether or not agencies engage in procedural
politicking depends on reputational benefits and costs that
such manipulation can bring about. For example, agencies
might bypass the conventional notice-and-comment process
when its reputational costs are expected to be insignificant.
On the other hand, they might refrain from such avoidance if
its reputational costs are expected to be significant. As such,
agencies’ use of administrative procedures might be strategic
not in terms of their own goals but in terms of reputational
incentives that are shaped by task, organizational, and polit­
ical contexts (Carpenter 2010; Carpenter and Krause 2012;
Krause and Corder 2007; Krause and Meier 2003).
Hypotheses
Implementation uncertainty
Knowledge of rule implementation is likely to be accumulated
in proportion to agencies’ implementation experiences. As
Pressman and Wildavsky (1984, p. xviii) point out, “imple­
mentation is the only source of experiences that managers can
use to test and improve their programs.” When agencies make
a new rule rather than revising an existing one that is in effect
and codified in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), they
may have limited information about policy effectiveness and
implementation challenges. As agencies actually implement
the rule and interact with affected stakeholders over time,
they can acquire rule-specific knowledge of how the regulated
interests respond to the rule and how effective and fair it is in
comparison to its desired outcomes. By doing so, a new rule
could be updated to a better rule based on a deeper under­
standing of its implementation issues (Wagner et al. 2017).
Although agencies can acquire information informally and
privately (West 2004, 2009), making new rules through the
conventional notice-and-comment process might be more
beneficial because it enables agencies to identify implemen­
tation challenges and gauge the extent of support for new
regulations from a wide range of stakeholders. Conversely,
the necessity of such technical and political learning might
diminish when agencies update routine and frequent rules.
Thus, we expect that
H1.1 (Rule Novelty): Agencies are more likely to solicit
prior public comment when making more novel rules.
Implementation uncertainty also depends on the degree of task
complexity. The more complex issues agencies deal with, the
less likely rulemakers are to know how to design an effective
rule and what its consequences would be (Mazmanian and
Sabatier 1983; O’Toole and Meier 2003). Thus, the marginal
benefits from acquiring implementation knowledge through
the conventional notice-and-comment process might become
greater as agencies make more complex rules. Moreover, even
if agencies possess sufficient expertise in addressing com­
plex issues, their rules should be clearly understood by both
implementers and affected groups. Accordingly, complex
rules often require detailed and extensive explanations to
ensure their compliance for effective implementation (Potter
2019; Yackee and Yackee 2010). As Moffitt (2014) suggests,
“learning in public”—by which knowledge is distributed and
legitimized—plays a crucial role in preventing implementa­
tion failure as much as “learning from the public.” Given that
the conventional notice-and-comment process can serve both
roles, we expect that
H1.2 (Rule complexity): Agencies are more likely to solicit
prior public comment when making more complex rules.
The degree of regulatory stringency can be understood as a
function of both the range of behavioral changes required
and the scope of target groups (Judge-Lord, McDermott, and
Cashore 2020; Mazmanian and Sabatier 1983). The more
restrictive a rule is, the more cooperation and compliance
efforts from affected interests are necessary to achieve its
policy goals. Thus, agencies need to seek greater legitimacy of
their rulemaking processes to elicit approval of, or at least ac­
quiescence to, more restrictive rules from those interests who
bear higher costs of compliance (Carpenter 2010). For ex­
ample, Rashin (2022) shows that the SEC tends to cite more
academic studies to justify its rules as they contain more re­
strictive words and phrases. In a similar vein, the higher ne­
cessity of legitimizing agency rulemaking in response to more
restrictive rules might encourage agencies to provide affected
interests with a fair opportunity to comment at an early stage
of rule development.

Likewise, as the number of groups affected by a rule
increases, the broader interdependence between an agency
and groups is likely to form. Given the nature of regulation
that inevitably generates winners and losers, the increased
regulatory scope might make agencies more cautious about
their rulemaking. If an agency explicitly favors one group
over another, it might face potential reputational threats from
those who are suspicious about the legitimacy of its decisions
(Carpenter 2001, 2010). To elicit deference to agency
rulemaking and cooperation from diverse groups, it is nec­
essary for agencies to build consensus and mutual agreement
through bargaining and negotiations (Carpenter and Krause
2015). Given that federal agencies usually communicate with
key stakeholders in informal ways during the early stages of
rule development (West 2004, 2009), the marginal benefits
from following the conventional notice-and-comment process
for a few affected groups (i.e., low regulatory scope) might
be small because reaching mutual agreements through in­
formal bargaining could be more effective. As the number of
affected groups increases, however, the conventional notice-
and-comment process—which can effectively accommodate
diverse and often conflicting interests—might become a more
attractive venue than informal bargaining processes and
soliciting public comments near the final stage of rulemaking.
Moreover, the need for procedural justice afforded by a
formal comment period might be greater when a consensus
is harder to reach as well as when a rule is more restrictive.
Although regulatory restrictiveness and scope are concep­
tually distinct, we conceptualize regulatory stringency as a
function of these two variables. In other words, regulatory
stringency might be established by a combination of both
restrictiveness and scope rather than by each variable sepa­
rately. For example, a rule that requires significant behavioral
changes from regulated entities but applies to a few groups, or
a rule that regulates numerous groups but mandates minimal
changes, might be much less stringent than a rule imposing
heavy restrictions on a wide range of industries. This insep­
arable relationship between regulatory restrictiveness and
scope in shaping regulatory stringency can be well reflected in
the aggregated conceptualization (Judge-Lord, McDermott,
and Cashore 2020; Mazmanian and Sabatier 1983).
Since affected groups include not only industries but also
state and local governments, implementation interdependence
can arise when federal rules affect other levels of government.
Like corporations and trade associations, these governments
also actively submit comments on federal rules (Ban and
You 2019). Moreover, economically significant rules—those
estimated to have an annual impact of more than $100 mil­
lion on the economy—inherently involve a considerable de­
gree of regulatory stringency. Given our discussion above, we
expect that
H1.3 (Rule stringency): Agencies are more likely to solicit
prior public comment when making more stringent rules.
Implementation uncertainty does not necessarily arise from
the direct interface between agencies and affected interests.
Joint rulemaking among federal agencies can also increase
implementation uncertainty in which potential outcomes
range from turf war to committed collaboration (Freeman
and Rossi 2012). Since both rulemaking and implementation
should be coordinated among participating agencies that have
their own policy perspectives and priorities, the probability
of failure might increase substantially even under mutual
agreement on policy goals (Pressman and Wildavsky 1984).
Focusing on the increasing implementation uncertainty in
joint action, we expect that
H1.4 (Joint rulemaking): Agencies are more likely to solicit
prior public comment when making rules jointly with other
agencies.
Implementation uncertainty and bureaucratic
professionalism
Bureaucratic reputation, as an overarching organizational
value, emerges through the identification process by which
bureaucrats’ identity and esteem are tied to agency reputa­
tion (Carpenter 2010). However, the degree of bureaucrats’
identification with agency reputation might vary by the type
of bureaucrats. According to Wilson (1980), bureaucrats can
be categorized into three types in terms of their different
motives: careerists, professionals, and politicians (political
appointees). Among them, professional bureaucrats whose
reference group is their peers outside their agency are more
likely to be sensitive to agency reputation than other types of
bureaucrats. Given their greater concerns for peer recognition
and expertise, their agencies’ failure to bring out the desired
policy outcomes might damage peers’ beliefs in their com­
petence as well as agency reputation (Miller and Whitford
2016). Although careerists also care about agency reputation,
their focus is likely to be more towards their agencies’ survival
rather than performance (Wilson 1980). Thus, their efforts to
reduce implementation uncertainty in making a rule might be
relatively weaker than professionals. For political appointees
who have relatively short tenure and are motivated by po­
litical ambitions rather than professional expertise, agency
reputation might not be substituted for their own personal
goals in guiding their behavior as much as professionals and
careerists. Their approach to rulemaking might be oriented to
the publication of rules that help their career moves outside
the agency in the future.
Considering that organizational reputation might be­
come a collective goal of organizational members through
socialization processes among three types of bureaucrats
(Carpenter 2010; Simon 1947; Wilson 1980), agencies with
higher proportions of professional bureaucrats are likely
to be more sensitive to reputational benefits and costs of
their rulemaking activities. Accordingly, how agencies use
rulemaking procedures in response to the varying degree of
rule implementation uncertainty can be shaped by their levels
of professionalism. For example, if a rule requires more be­
havioral changes of affected groups as well as regulates a
larger number of groups (i.e., high regulatory stringency),
the expected reputational costs of bypassing the conven­
tional notice-and-comment process might be greater to
agencies with higher proportions of professional bureaucrats.
On the other hand, agencies with smaller proportions of
professional bureaucrats are less likely to appreciate such
costs. Considering that most rulemakings are conducted by
employees in agency headquarters, we expect that

increase, agencies with higher proportions of professional
bureaucrats at their headquarters are more likely to solicit
prior public comment.

Implementation uncertainty and interest group
opposition
Presuming explicitly or implicitly that bureaucratic agencies
pursue their own goals that are not matched with those of
political institutions and interest groups, the procedural
politicking literature suggests that agencies are likely to avoid
the conventional notice-and-comment process when unfavor­
able interventions from those actors are expected (Nou and
Stiglitz 2015; Potter 2019; Raso 2015). Such avoidance might
occur more frequently in response to the increasing level of
rule implementation uncertainty. For example, when agencies
propose rules that newly expand their regulatory areas or
introduce more stringent rules, which are likely to attract
more unfavorable responses from affected groups (Yackee
2006), avoidance could increase because it allows agencies
to preoccupy rulemaking agendas without accommodating
the interests of those groups. Assuming that interest groups
whose ideologies are more distant from an agency’s ideology
are more likely to be unfavorable to its rules (Potter 2019), the
procedural political literature might argue that agencies are
more likely to avoid the conventional notice-and-comment
process in response to the increasing ideological divergence
between agencies and their interest groups as their rules’ im­
plementation uncertainty increases.
While the political control literature has overlooked var­
ious options that agencies can use to avoid the conven­
tional notice-and-comment process (Spence 1997), control
mechanisms such as “fire alarm” monitoring can be applied
to the rulemaking process (McCubbins and Schwartz 1984).
Since an agency’s avoidance can be revealed to political
institutions through complaints of affected interests, it may
refrain from using rulemaking procedures without prior com­
ment under their watchful eyes. With the assumption that af­
fected groups whose ideologies diverge more from an agency’s
ideology raise more complaints, the political control litera­
ture might suggest that agencies are more likely to use the
conventional notice-and-comment process in response to the
increasing ideological mismatch between agencies and their
interest groups. When the degree of rules’ implementation
uncertainty is relatively high, such willingness might become
greater because affected groups’ influence on implementa­
tion success or failure becomes more significant. Although the
procedural politicking and political control literature might
expect different patterns of agencies’ procedural choices in
rulemaking, their focus is primarily on goal conflict between
agencies and interest groups.
According to a reputation-based perspective, however,
agencies’ choices of rulemaking procedures are less likely to
be driven by partisan or ideological politics (Carpenter 2002).
Given the presence of multiple stakeholders and even mul­
tiple political masters, attending to the demands of interest
groups in partisan or ideological ways might significantly
deteriorate the legitimacy of agency rulemaking (Carpenter
and Krause 2012). Being responsive to them in those ways
might increase the possibility of implementation failure be­
cause affected interests become suspicious of agencies’ cred­
ible commitment, which makes them reluctant to provide
information and cooperate with agencies’ rules (Gailmard
and Patty 2013; Miller and Whitford 2016). Moreover, the
APA gives all affected groups the right to seek judicial review
of rulemaking procedures (5 U.S.C. § 704). Even if a majority
of interest groups are ideologically aligned with an agency
and expected to respond to its rules favorably, excluding
the interests of smaller groups from the rulemaking process
could significantly damage agency reputation through diverse
channels, such as the courts and the national media. Although
the hypothesis is presented following the logic of procedural
politicking for hypothesis testing, we expect that we will not
reject the null hypothesis based on the logic of bureaucratic
reputation.
H3: As levels of rule implementation uncertainty increase,
agencies that anticipate more unfavorable responses from in­
terest groups are less likely to solicit prior public comment.
Data, measures, and methods
Our data collection starts with identifying federal rules (i.e.,
NPRMs and interim/direct/temporary final rules) that were
published in the Unified Agenda of Federal Regulatory and
Deregulatory Actions (Unified Agenda) between 2000 and
2020.5 Since several dimensions of rule implementation un­
certainty are measured based on the rule texts, we link those
rules in the Unified Agendas to their corresponding rules
published in the Federal Register by using Federal Register
citations specified in the Unified Agenda. After setting the an­
alytic sample of rules, various data on the characteristics of
rules, agencies, and political environments are subsequently
attached, as explained below.
It is important to note that we focus on the rules published
both in the Federal Register and the Unified Agenda. Although
a large number of rules that are not published in the Unified
Agenda but in the Federal Register are recurring rules (e.g., the
Federal Aviation Administration’s rules that amend airspace
across various regions), agencies may strategically not disclose
a few salient rules in the Unified Agenda to avoid political
monitoring of rule development (Nou and Stiglitz 2015). Since
we do not examine agencies’ procedural choices between rule
publication in the Unified Agenda and the Federal Register but
those between a NPRM and an interim/direct/temporary final
rule, our sample is restricted to the rules published both in the
Federal Register and the Unified Agenda.6

Implementation uncertainty
We use several variables to capture multiple sources of im­
plementation uncertainty, such as rule novelty, complexity,
stringency, and joint rulemaking. New rules are measured
by figuring out whether a rule contains new CFR parts that
are not codified in the previous year’s CFR. For instance, in
October 2020, the Consumer Product Safety Commission
(CPSC) proposed a safety standard for crib mattresses by
creating a new CFR part (16 CFR Part 1241, 85 FR 67906).
Meanwhile, the Commission proposed its standard for the
flammability of mattresses by revising an existing CFR part
(16 CFR Part 1632, 85 FR 68803). Like the former rule, the
rules that contain new CFR parts are treated as new rules. To
capture low levels of rule novelty in recurring rules, we also
measure whether rules are designated as routine and frequent
rules in the Unified Agenda.
Assuming that more complex rules might entail more expla­
nation of their basis and purpose, the logged number of words
in each rule’s abstract is used as a proxy measure for rule com­
plexity (Yackee and Yackee 2010). Alternatively, we measure the
length of a rule’s preamble—where its background and major
issues are discussed—by counting the number of preamble pages
(Potter 2019). As “the preamble’s length often varies based on
the complexity of the issues considered and the anticipated con­
troversy surrounding the rule” (Carrigan and Mills 2019, p.
729), this measure captures rule stringency as well as complexity.
To account for the correlations between preamble length and
other measures of implementation uncertainty, we conduct prin­
cipal component analysis (PCA) and include its factor scores in
our models. The results from such models are consistent with the
results presented below (see Online Appendix C6).
Leveraging the “industry regulation index” from RegData,
which combines the restrictiveness of a CFR part with its
relevance to specific industries classified by North American
Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes (Al‐Ubaydli and
McLaughlin 2017), we operationalize rule stringency in terms
of both how restrictive a rule is and how many industries are
likely to be affected by a rule. We measure rule restrictiveness
by counting the number of restrictive words and phrases (e.g.,
shall, must, may not, prohibited, and required) in each CFR
part attached at the end of a rule (Yackee 2021). The regula­
tory scope is measured by linking each CFR part of a rule to
its corresponding industry relevance probabilities for four-digit
NAICS industries in RegData, which range from 0 to 1 for
each industry. These probabilities are derived using machine-
learning algorisms that are trained on the Federal Register
publications specifying the NAICS codes affected by rules
(McLaughlin, Nelson, and Powers 2022).7 Since rule stringency
can be well captured by a function of both regulatory restric­
tiveness and scope, it is calculated by multiplying the number
of restrictions by the industry relevance probabilities at the
CFR part level—which represent the restrictiveness of CFR
parts applied to each industry—and then summing them at the
rule level (logged).8 A rule that imposes many restrictions on
various industries with high relevance probabilities has a larger
value than a rule that applies the same number of restrictions
to fewer industries. We also capture the levels of regulatory
stringency by measuring whether or not rules are expected to
be economically significant and affect other levels of govern­
ment, such as state, local, and tribal governments, using the
Unified Agenda.
Measures of joint rulemaking are divided into internal
and external joint rulemaking. The internal joint rulemaking
indicates that multiple bureaus within an agency are engaged
in proposing a rule. The external joint rulemaking indicates
that multiple different agencies are involved in proposing a
rule. Our focus lies on external joint rulemaking because in­
ternal joint rulemaking often deals with “rules of agency or­
ganization” that the APA notice-and-comment requirement is
not likely to be applied (5 U.S.C. § 553).
Professionalism
Using the FedScope data, bureaucratic professionalism is
measured as the proportions of professional employees at fed­
eral agencies’ headquarters.9 Compared to other occupational
types of employees, such as administrative, technical, and
blue-collar employees, professional employees are required
to have expertise acquired through specialized education or
training, which might make them more sensitive to agency
competence and reputation (Lewis 2008). Given that there are
large variations in the proportions of professional employees
across bureaus within an executive department (e.g., National
Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration [about 45 percent]
and Patent and Trademark Office [about 80 percent] within
the Department of Commerce), the bureau-level measures are
applied if possible.10

Control variables
Several other characteristics of the rulemaking context are
included as control variables. At the rule level, we include
whether rules address emergencies, which might inevitably
necessitate bypassing the conventional notice-and-comment
process for prompt implementation. Using interim or tem­
porary rulemaking processes to respond to emergencies
is likely to be justified through the good cause exemption,
deeming prior public comment “impracticable” or “contrary
to the public interest.”12 Such immediate actions can also help
agencies preserve their reputation, despite potentially higher
levels of implementation uncertainty. Emergency rules are
identified by searching for keywords like “emergency,” “dis­
aster,” “crisis,” and ‘outbreak’ within the title, Action field,
and abstract of the rules. We also control for rules related to
contracts, grants, benefits, and loans because these rules are
exempt from the conventional notice-and-comment process
under the APA (5 U.S.C. § 553). Following the same method
used for emergency rules, contract-related rules are identified
by using keywords, such as “acquisition,” “grant,” “procure­
ment,” and “fee.”
At the agency level, we control for the rulemaking burden
of agencies by measuring the logged number of rulemaking
agendas published in the biannual Unified Agenda (lagged six
months). The growing rulemaking burden can shift agencies’
reputational focus from quality rules to the publication of
rules. If more and more rulemaking agendas are delayed,
agencies’ inability to publish and implement rules might
incur more reputational costs than their inability to make
quality rules at some point. Also, the logged total number
of employees is included as a proxy measure for agency re­
sources (size).
At the political environment level, we include variables that
control for political institutions’ influences on agencies’ pro­
cedural decisions. Following previous research (Haeder and
Yackee 2018; Potter 2019), an unaligned president is coded 1
when the ideological positions of the presidents and agencies
are different from each other (e.g., a Republican president
and liberal agencies) and 0 otherwise. Congressional oppo­
sition is measured by the ratio of the number of ideologi­
cally mismatched party members of Congress to the number
of ideologically matched party members of Congress (Potter
2019). Given the growing partisan polarization in Congress,
the size of each party is weighted by the ideology estimate of
the median member of each party.13 Agencies’ expectations
about the courts’ response to their rulemaking are measured
by counting the annual number of APA-related cases that
are filed in all federal circuit courts (court cases, logged and
lagged one year).14 To control for agency responsiveness to
political institutions’ non-ideological demands, we include
whether NPRMs and interim/direct/temporary final rules
are published in the first and last year of a presidential ad­
ministration and whether a statutory or judicial deadline is
imposed on a rule.15 Descriptive statistics are presented in
Online Appendix C1.
Estimation
Given the dependent variable is binary, we use logistic
regressions with bureau- and presidential administration-
fixed effects.16 This approach allows the variables related to
implementation uncertainty to be sufficiently isolated from
unobserved characteristics of agencies and the presidency. We
also run the models adding year-fixed effects to further con­
trol for unobserved time-specific factors (see Online Appendix
C3). However, we present the results from the models
without year-fixed effects to retain interesting variables, such
as those indicating the first and last years of presidential
administrations, even though the results are largely consistent
across both sets of models. Standard errors are clustered at
the agency level. To test interactions established in H2 and
H3, the baseline model is augmented by including interac­
tion terms between seven variables related to implementation
uncertainty and bureaucratic professionalism, as well as be­
tween these variables and the ideological mismatch between
agencies and interest groups, respectively.

Discussion and conclusion
With the emergence of bureaucracy as the “fourth branch”
of government in a separation of powers system, how to rec­
oncile bureaucratic discretion and power with the norms of
democratic governance has long been a central question in
the fields of public administration, political science, and ad­
ministrative law (Mosher 1982; Redford 1969; Rosenbloom
2000; Waldo 1948). In response to recent literature on pro­
cedural politicking, this study has tackled this question by
examining when and why U.S. federal agencies use or bypass
the conventional notice-and-comment process, particularly
focusing on task-level implementation uncertainty that is at
the core of reputation management (Carpenter 2002; Moffitt
2010, 2014; see also Christensen and Lodge 2018). Although
the empirical context of this study is federal rulemaking, our
findings provide several insights into bureaucratic discretion
and democratic governance more broadly.
First, this study indicates that the bureaucratic reputation
perspective might offer a complementary lens to the proce­
dural politicking and political control literature for exploring
how bureaucrats exercise discretion and its compatibility with
democracy. Our findings suggest that bureaucrats’ pursuit of
effective rulemaking—that requires implementation knowl­
edge and cooperation from affected stakeholders—can lead
to democratic rulemaking that upholds representation and
procedural due process. Conversely, rulemaking procedures
without prior comment might be used more frequently when
such procedures are less likely to compromise both effective
and democratic rulemaking, allowing bureaucrats to deal
with immediate and minor issues more flexibly. These findings
indicate that bureaucrats’ strategic use of administrative
procedures is significantly shaped by managerial concerns,
which encourage them to balance bureaucratic values—such

When it comes to agencies’ responsiveness to political
institutions, our findings align well with the bureaucratic
reputation perspective that argues that the presence of mul­
tiple audiences might encourage agencies to refrain from
committing to certain audiences and to appear respon­
sive and impartial (Carpenter 2001, 2010; Carpenter and
Krause 2012). Our findings demonstrate that agencies’ use
of rulemaking procedures without prior comment is as­
sociated not with ideological mismatches with electoral
institutions but rather with their non-ideological priorities
(i.e., the presidents’ first year and statutory deadline). This
indicates that agencies’ procedural decisions might be less
about circumventing political oversight and more about
fulfilling the regulatory agendas of both the presidents and
Congress, regardless of ideological differences between them
(see Header & Yackee 2020b). Moreover, their decisions
might be shaped by potential judicial review that guarantees
access for all affected interests, consistent with the purpose
of the APA that aims to inculcate bureaucratic agencies with
democratic values, such as representation, participation, and
fairness (Rosenbloom 2000).
Of course, both the procedural politicking and tradi­
tional political control literature provide valid explanations
of bureaucratic decisions and behavior. In line with the pro­
cedural politicking literature, agencies might seek to preoc­
cupy rulemaking agendas for new rules to pursue their own
policy preferences and avoid interventions from political
institutions. Given that revising existing rules are likely to in­
volve incremental changes in the rules (Wagner et al. 2017),
setting a rulemaking agenda through interim or direct final
rulemaking first and then adjusting the rule subsequently
through the conventional notice-and-comment process can
enable agencies to insulate their preferences from political in­
terference. Likewise, as the political control literature argues,
the increasing use of rulemaking without prior comment in
response to statutory deadlines suggests that agencies might
be responsive to procedural constraints imposed by Congress.
Also, the courts seem to directly or indirectly compel agencies
to comply with the conventional notice-and-comment
process, which can reduce politicians’ monitoring and en­
forcement costs of ensuring agency compliance (McCubbins,
Noll, and Weingast 1987, 1989).
However, the bureaucratic reputation perspective can
also offer an alternative to the above interpretations from
the theories of procedural politicking and political control.
Making a new rule can be understood as a jurisdictional
claim on a new policy area. Although bypassing the con­
ventional notice-and-comment process can raise the risk of
implementation failure, the reputational costs of regulatory
inaction and the potential loss of an undisputed jurisdiction
might be deemed greater than the costs due to implementa­
tion failure (Maor 2010; Moynihan 2012). By the same token,
the increasing use of rulemaking without prior comment for
joint rules with other agencies might be driven by agencies’
motivation to protect their own jurisdictions (Wilson 1989).
By clarifying jurisdictional boundaries before attracting
comments from the entire regulated community, participating
agencies can make their jurisdictional uniqueness less dis­
putable. In sum, bureaucratic discretion and its implications
for democratic governance might be more comprehensively
explained by considering the reputational imperatives of bu­
reaucracy: effective task management (i.e., policy implemen­
tation), a sensible balance among multiple administrative
values (i.e., bureaucratic and democratic values), the appear­
ance of fairness and responsiveness with respect to multiple
stakeholders, and undisputed jurisdictions for reputational
uniqueness.
Second, this study contributes to the literature exploring
the implications of bureaucratic reputation for democratic
governance. As discussed above, bureaucrats’ pursuit of
agency reputation through effective rule implementation and
non-ideological responsiveness to the demands of political
institutions seems compatible with democratic values such
as procedural due process and representation (Moffitt 2010,
2014). As opposed to the concern about bureaucratic claims-
making to build new coalitions with interest groups for their
own preferences (Bertelli and Busuioc 2021), our findings
suggest that agencies, at least procedurally, allow affected
interests—whether they are expected to favor or oppose
their rules—to be represented in the policymaking process.
As Carpenter (2001, p. 367, 2010) notes, these findings indi­
cate that bureaucratic reputation-building occurs “not at the
expense of democratic participation but in a symbiotic rela­
tionship with it.”
However, bureaucrats’ pursuit of an uncontested jurisdic­
tion through rulemaking without prior notice and comment
seems to pose a threat to democratic governance. Moreover,
our findings suggest that their motive to establish reputational
uniqueness might be particularly strong, not being affected by
higher levels of interest group opposition. This is consistent
with Maor, Gilad, and Bloom (2013), who find that agencies
tend to actively respond to criticisms of their jurisdictions
whose distinct reputations are not yet fully established. As
Wilson (1989, p. 195) points out, “turf problems are large
and largely insoluble.” Accordingly, the potential risk that
reputation-minded bureaucrats strategically disguise their
expertise and representativeness from political principals
likely lies in their motive to preserve reputational uniqueness
(Bertelli & Busuioc, 2020). As such, the democratic deficits of
bureaucratic reputation-building might be driven by their en­
gagement in turf battle rather than in ideological or partisan
politics.
Third, this study indicates that agencies with greater levels
of professionalism might be more sensitive to agency reputa­
tion, which can be compatible with democratic governance.
Our findings suggest that agencies’ choices of rulemaking
procedures are not directly related to their levels of profes­
sionalism.18 Instead, while less robust, agencies with higher

with higher proportions of professional bureaucrats might be
more adept at calculating reputational benefits and costs of
rulemaking procedures in response to varying levels of im­

that the lack of professionals’ attention to the general interest
and open politics can pose a threat to democratic govern­
ance (Mosher 1982), professional bureaucrats might be more
committed to upholding, or at least not undermine demo­
cratic values (Miller and Whitford 2016). While our findings
are consistent with previous literature suggesting that tasks
involving professional attention and review, which might be
more uncertain in their implementation, tend to appear more
frequently on the agendas of public advisory committees
(Moffitt 2014, Chapter 6), more systematic research on the
reputational sensitivity and democratic implications of bu­
reaucratic professionalism is needed.
This study has several limitations that need to be addressed
in future research. First, while this study presents arguments
based on the bureaucratic reputation perspective to comple­
ment the procedural politicking and political control litera­
ture, additional empirical evidence is necessary to generalize
our findings. The notice-and-comment process is relatively
visible to external stakeholders, which provides a favorable
context relevant to bureaucrats’ reputational incentives.
However, agencies can employ various esoteric procedural
tools during the entire stages of rulemaking (Potter 2019).
Thus, looking at less visible procedures, such as the timing of
rule finalization, the delay of the compliance date, and the use
of guidance documents (Potter 2019; Yackee 2020; Yackee
and Yackee 2010), is necessary to test and integrate different
theoretical approaches to bureaucratic rulemaking.
Second, this study could not adequately consider the dereg­
ulatory nature of rulemaking. Since deregulatory rules usually
remove CFR parts without specifying their sections, entirely
deregulatory rules are excluded from our sample. For partially
deregulatory rules, the restrictiveness of removing CFR parts
is treated as zero. Although deregulatory rules may involve
little of the implementation uncertainty we focus on, they still
carry high stakes for political institutions and interest groups
(Header and Yackee 2018). As the Trump administration ac­
tively advocates deregulation, taking Covid-19 crisis as an
opportunity to do so (Raso 2020), comparing agencies’ proce­
dural decisions on regulatory rules with those on deregulatory
rules might be another way to explore bureaucrats’ concern for
implementation uncertainty and procedural politicking.
Finally, our measurement approach can be improved to
delve into the political roles of bureaucrats in aggregating
and resolving diverse interests at the task level. While we use
the aggregated ideology or partisanship measures of agencies,
political institutions, and interest groups, these measures are
limited in capturing the degree of policy conflict between
agencies and political institutions, between agencies and in­
terest groups, and even among interest groups, which can vary
from one rule to another (​Lowande and Potter 2021). Thus,
it might be promising to develop or leverage rule-level meas­
ures of policy conflict and examine their relationships with
agencies’ substantive and procedural decisions. Given the im­
portance of managing organizational reputation among mul­
tiple audiences (Carpenter 2001, 2010; Carpenter and Krause
2012), such an approach might allow us to better understand
the reputational motives of bureaucrats and their democratic
implications.

ABSTRACT
Illegitimate tasks are duties that violate established role expectations, such as assigning urban planning officers to manage traffic
hotlines. In the public sector, bureaucratic structures, resource constraints, and leadership limitations often contribute to the
proliferation of such tasks, yet their impact on public servants' behaviors remains unclear. This study examines how and when
illegitimate tasks inhibit public servants' innovative work behavior through the mediating role of cognitive flexibility and the
moderating roles of different forms of trust in leaders. Hierarchical model analyses using multi-­wave data from public servants
and their managers in Vietnam reveal that illegitimate tasks indirectly reduce innovative work behavior via cognitive flexi-
bility. Additionally, relationship-­based trust amplifies the negative effect of illegitimate tasks on cognitive flexibility, whereas
character-­based trust does not. These findings highlight the need to minimize illegitimate task assignments and reveal the po-
tential drawbacks of relationship-­based trust in such adverse work conditions.
1   |   Introduction
In the public sector, where work boundaries are often unclear
and the environment is uncertain, employees may frequently
find themselves assigned tasks that do not align with their roles
or are illegitimate. Illegitimate tasks refer to job assignments that
are either unreasonable or unnecessary (Semmer et al. 2010).
Unreasonable tasks—for instance, directing urban planning
officers to manage citizen hotlines—fall outside the employee's
core role and therefore should not be assigned to them (Semmer
et al. 2010). Unnecessary tasks, such as preparing extensive and
detailed reports that are seldom read, lack clear purpose, waste
valuable time, and are considered superfluous for the job at hand
(Semmer et al. 2010). Like other job stressors—such as red tape
(Davis and Pandey 2024)—illegitimate tasks have both objec-
tive and subjective dimensions, shaped by the nature of the work
and employees' perceptions of their task assignments (Ding and
Kuvaas 2023). The present study focuses on employees' percep-
tions of illegitimate tasks assigned by their managers, as such
perceptions often influence workplace attitudes, emotions, and
behaviors (Bliese et al. 2017).
Several factors may contribute to the prevalence of illegiti-
mate tasks in the public sector. Constrained budgets, which
reduce staffing levels and limit new hires, often force remain-
ing employees to unreasonably take on additional responsi-
bilities beyond their formal job roles (Björk et al. 2013). The
growing volume of policies and regulations can further in-
crease administrative burdens, leading to the assignment of
tasks that are not directly related to employees' core respon-
sibilities (Fernández-­i-­Marín et al. 2024; George et al. 2021).
In addition, managers—who play a central role in task allo-
cation—may, due to inadequate leadership or even abusive
supervision, assign duties that are either unnecessary or

Furthermore, the public sector's rigid bureaucratic structures

Illegitimate tasks are recognized work stressors (Ding and
Kuvaas 2023) that can undermine positive attitudes and be-

has hitherto been limited research on the consequences of
illegitimate tasks and their influential mechanisms in public

impact and thereby limits effective management interventions

This study examines the effects of illegitimate tasks on public
sector employees' innovative work behavior, which involves
generating, introducing, and implementing new ideas, pro-
cesses, and practices that enhance an organization's effec-
tiveness (Janssen  2000; Scott and Bruce  1994). We aim to
understand how this dark side of the job challenges employ-
ees' engagement in innovation, which is vital for public sec-
tor organizational success in rapidly changing environments
(Hall  2023; Zou  2024). Based on conservation of resources
(COR) theory (Hobfoll 1989; Hobfoll et al. 2018), we propose
that illegitimate tasks negatively impact innovative work be-
havior. Furthermore, cognitive flexibility—the ability to think
adaptively and fluidly (De Dreu et al. 2008)—is proposed as a
key mediator for this relationship due to its resource-­intensive
nature and its fundamental role in human functioning
(Ionescu 2012). While public administration research exten-
sively examines negative cognitive aspects, such as biases
(Afzal and Panagiotopoulos 2025; Nagtegaal et al. 2020) and
uncertainty (Bernards 2024), there is a significant gap in ex-
ploring positive cognition, including cognitive flexibility and
its role in fostering innovative work behavior.
Furthermore, we integrate COR with cognitive dissonance
theory (Festinger 1957) to offer nuanced insights into how two
forms of trust in leaders—relationship-­based trust (stemming
from emotional connections and genuine concern between
leaders and employees) and character-­based trust (founded on a
leader's competence, reliability, and ability to fulfill job-­related
responsibilities) (Dirks and Ferrin 2002; Legood et al. 2023)—
moderate the effect of illegitimate tasks. While trust in leaders
is widely acknowledged in the public administration literature
for its positive influence on public sector employees and organi-
zations (Bentzen 2022), there has been limited exploration of the
different forms of trust (Miao et al. 2014; Park 2012), or how they
shape public servants' responses to negative job features, such
as illegitimate tasks. Adopting a multi-­based trust perspective
offers valuable insights into the complex workplace dynamics in
the public sector.
Our research makes four significant contributions to the pub-
lic administration literature. First, we pioneer the examination
of the adverse effects of illegitimate tasks on public servants'
innovative work behavior, shedding light on this critical yet
underexplored issue within the public sector. In doing so, we
address recent calls to investigate how negative job aspects,
such as illegitimate tasks, impact innovative work behavior in
both public administration and general management contexts
(AlEssa and Durugbo  2022; Srirahayu et  al.  2023). Second,
our study extends the stream of research on illegitimate tasks
by investigating cognitive flexibility as a mediation pathway
through which illegitimate tasks undermine innovative work
behavior among public servants. Examining this cognitive
mediation mechanism, this work extends the existing studies
of illegitimate tasks that focus on psychological (Mäkikangas
et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2022) or affective pathways (Wang and
Jiang 2023; Wang and Zong 2023). Third, we expand the pub-
lic administration literature on trust in leaders by examin-
ing how two distinct forms of trust—relationship-­based and
character-­based—moderate the effects of illegitimate tasks.
Although existing research indicates that different forms of
trust in leaders can influence employees' cognitive processes
in different ways (Dirks and Ferrin 2002; Legood et al. 2023;
McAllister  1995), the literature remains ambiguous regard-
ing how these trust mechanisms operate, particularly in the
context of negative job aspects such as illegitimate tasks. Our
research sheds light on the nuanced ways in which different
types of trust can shape employees' reactions to adverse work
conditions. Lastly, by integrating cognitive dissonance the-
ory, our study contributes to a more precise understanding
of resource dynamics within COR theory, emphasizing that a
resource's utility is contingent upon its qualitative character-
istics and contextual relevance. Taken together, our study pro-
vides a novel understanding of employee cognitive processes
and innovative work behavior in the context of the interplay
between job-­related stressors and trust in leaders. It also offers
meaningful advice for practitioners on managing job stressors
such as illegitimate tasks and fostering innovative behavior
among public servants.
The article proceeds as follows: The next section reviews the
literature and develop our hypotheses. Following this, we pro-
vide a detailed description of the research methodology and
key findings. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical
implications, address the study's limitations, and suggest future
research directions.
Summary
•	 Assigning illegitimate tasks that do not form part of
the established role expectations of public servants
can impede their essential functions, including cog-
nitive flexibility (i.e., the capacity to think adaptively
and fluidly) and innovative work behavior.
•	 Public sector organizations should implement proac-
tive measures to identify, mitigate, and prevent illegit-
imate tasks by clearly defining job duties and fostering
open communication and transparency. Policymakers
should develop regulations that prevent the assign-
ment of illegitimate tasks.
•	 Immediate managers, who often assign tasks directly
to public servants, should ensure tasks are meaning-
ful and relevant to their duties.
•	 Public servants who place strong trust in their lead-
ers may react more negatively to illegitimate tasks.
Therefore, public sector managers should exercise
caution in leveraging this trust, as its misuse may
backfire.

2.1   |   Illegitimate Tasks
In the dynamic and rapidly evolving public sector, public ser-
vants may encounter tasks they perceive as illegitimate (Semmer
et al. 2010). These illegitimate tasks—those falling outside an
employee's expected role—can pose significant workplace chal-
lenges. Research in the general management field has shown
that such tasks can lead to frustration, negative emotions, inef-
ficiency, and even unethical behavior, ultimately undermining
both individual performance and organizational effectiveness
(Ding and Kuvaas 2023). However, their impact within the pub-
lic sector remains underexplored.
A well-­recognized challenge in public sector work is red tape
(George et al. 2021), which shares certain characteristics with
illegitimate tasks yet remains conceptually distinct. Both re-
flect perceived inefficiencies within organizations (Ding and
Kuvaas  2023; George et  al.  2021), yet they differ in import-
ant ways. Red tape refers to organizational rules, procedures,
or regulations that lack functional value and are perceived as
obstacles to work effectiveness (Bozeman 1993; Pandey 2021).
It operates at a systemic level, embedded within bureaucratic
structures, and typically affects multiple employees through
uniform procedural constraints. In contrast, illegitimate tasks
are role-­incongruent tasks perceived as unjustified and inap-
propriate based on an individual's job identity. These tasks are
assessed at the individual level and often stem from direct man-
agers when they assign unsuitable duties to employees (Ding
and Kuvaas 2023).
Although conceptually distinct, the two constructs can be inter-
related. Red tape may indirectly contribute to the emergence of
illegitimate tasks by creating inefficiencies and excessive proce-
dures that managers must navigate. As a result, they may inad-
vertently assign inappropriate tasks to employees due to the lack
of viable alternatives created by these procedural constraints.
Nevertheless, illegitimate tasks can also arise independently of
red tape—particularly when poor managerial judgment leads
to misaligned assignments. For example, asking an urban plan-
ning officer to manage a traffic hotline would likely be seen as
illegitimate due to the misalignment with their role—but would
not be perceived as related to red tape, since it does not originate
from formal procedural excess but from ineffective manage-
ment, especially in job analysis and assigning right tasks to right
people. Recognizing this distinction is crucial, as interventions
aimed at reducing systemic inefficiencies—such as red tape—
may not effectively address the root causes of illegitimate work.
2.2   |   Illegitimate Tasks and Innovative Work
Behavior
COR theory (Hobfoll 1989; Hobfoll et al. 2018) posits that in-
dividuals are motivated to protect, acquire, and retain valuable
resources. When faced with actual or potential resource loss due
to work conditions, individuals tend to adopt a defensive pos-
ture, prioritizing the preservation of their limited resources over
investing them in discretionary or high-­effort activities (Hobfoll
et al. 2018). This resource-­conserving tendency is particularly
evident when employees encounter job demands such as ille-
gitimate tasks. Such tasks drain both cognitive and emotional
resources by requiring employees to divert their energy toward
non-­role-­related activities and evoking feelings of resentment,
frustration, and burnout (Semmer et al. 2015). In such contexts,
resource-­depleted employees are more likely to protect their
remaining resources than to expend them on challenging be-
haviors such as innovative work activities, which require sub-
stantial investment in idea generation, experimentation, and
long-­term engagement without immediate returns. Empirical
research grounded in COR theory generally supports this per-
spective, demonstrating that resource-­depleted employees
under stressful conditions are less inclined to participate in dis-
cretionary behaviors (Bani-­Melhem 2020; Liu et al. 2019). Thus,
we hypothesize:
Hypothesis 1.  Illegitimate tasks are negatively related to in-
novative work behavior.
2.3   |   The Mediating Role of Cognitive Flexibility
Cognitive flexibility—the capacity to think adaptively and flu-
idly (De Dreu et al. 2008)—is fundamental to adaptive decision-­
making (Laureiro-­Martínez and Brusoni  2018), navigating
complex and unforeseen challenges, and fostering the genera-
tion of innovative solutions (Kiss et al. 2020). In this context,
according to Hobfoll (1989), cognitive flexibility serves as a re-
source for achieving other critical resources, such as superior
performance (Wang et al. 2023). Thus, individuals strategically
invest in cognitive flexibility to facilitate the acquisition of fur-
ther valuable resources.
Prior research has emphasized the mediating role of cognitive
flexibility in translating the influence of contextual factors on
individual outcomes, such as creativity (Zhang and Zhang 2025)
and well-­being (Koesten et al. 2009). Building on this empiri-
cal foundation and the COR theory's principle of resource in-
vestment, our study explores how cognitive flexibility, as a
resource, mediates the relationship between illegitimate tasks
and innovative work behavior. According to COR theory, when
employees encounter work conditions that threaten or deplete
their valued resources, they are likely to adopt defensive strat-
egies to conserve remaining resources, which can subsequently
shape their work-­related attitudes and behaviors (Hobfoll
et al. 2018). Drawing on COR, illegitimate tasks serve as signifi-
cant job demands, depleting employees' resources, leading them
to conserve their remaining resources rather than investing in
developing resources for higher-­order functions like cognitive
flexibility.
Employees with limited cognitive flexibility, due to resource con-
servation triggered by the negative effects of illegitimate tasks, en-
counter significant obstacles in engaging in innovative behavior.
Cognitive flexibility is a vital resource for fostering innovation, as
it enables individuals to explore diverse approaches or generate
novel ideas (Kiss et al. 2020; Su et al. 2025). Additionally, cogni-
tive flexibility facilitates the integration of multiple perspectives,
a key element in gaining support for new ideas (Al-­Ghazali and
Afsar 2021), and helps individuals adapt to unforeseen challenges,

2.4   |   The Moderating Roles of Relationship-­Based
and Character-­Based Trust
Interpersonal trust is commonly defined as “a psychological state
comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based on positive
expectations of the intentions or behaviors of another” (Rousseau
et al. 1998, 395). When trust is present, individuals are more likely
to engage in positive work behaviors that contribute to organiza-
tional success (Legood et al. 2021). In the public administration
literature, interpersonal trust has been examined in relation to
various focal points, such as co-­workers, immediate leaders, senior
leaders, and citizens (Taylor 2018). Among these, trust in imme-
diate leaders is the most extensively studied, as the frequent in-
teractions between employees and their immediate leaders make
this form of trust a critical mechanism through which leaders in-
fluence employees' attitudes and behaviors (Legood et al. 2021).
Trust in leaders is typically explored through two distinct bases:
relationship-­based trust and character-­based trust, which align
closely with McAllister's  (1995) affective and cognitive bases of
trust (Dirks and Ferrin 2002; Legood et al. 2023). Relationship-­
based trust emerges from emotional connections, personal care,
and genuine concern for others' welfare, developing within the in-
terpersonal dynamics between employees and their leaders (Dirks
and Ferrin 2002; Legood et al. 2023). This base of trust emphasizes
mutual understanding and reciprocal obligations that extend be-
yond mere economic transactions (Legood et al. 2021). Character-­
based trust, on the other hand, is rooted in employees' belief that a
leader possesses the competence and reliability to fulfill job-­related
responsibilities (Dirks and Ferrin 2002; Legood et al. 2023). Due
to their different origins, relationship-­based and character-­based
trust may coexist to varying degrees (Tomlinson et al. 2020). For
instance, employees may develop character-­based trust in a leader
due to admiration of the leader's knowledge, competence, and
skills, while simultaneously developing relationship-­based trust
through the leader's support, empathy, and care.
The public administration literature has long emphasized the
positive effects of trust in leaders on both employee and orga-
nizational outcomes (Cho and Park  2011; Perrier et  al.  2025;
Taylor 2018). Hence, trust is typically viewed as a psychological
resource for achieving other valuable resources (Nguyen and
Luu 2020). It is, therefore, logical to suggest that relationship
and character based-­trust in leaders, as resources, may serve as
a moderator to buffer the negative impact of illegitimate tasks
on public servants' attitudes and behaviors. However, existing
public administration research may have overlooked the com-
plex role trust plays when interacting with other management
practices, as COR theory suggests: “how resources operate de-
pends on the ecological context, such that in one context a re-
source might be salient and positive, and in another, it might be
salient but negative” (Hobfoll et al. 2018, 113). For instance, con-
scientiousness is often seen as a resource that helps individuals
manage other resources; yet, under frequent email disruptions,
highly conscientious individuals may experience a faster deple-
tion of their affective well-­being compared to those with lower
conscientiousness, as they may feel more stressed by the poten-
tial delay of competing tasks due to disruptive emails (Russell
et al. 2017). As COR theory remains ambiguous in explaining the
complexity of the moderating role of resources under stressful
conditions, Hobfoll et al. (2018) suggest integrating COR with
other micro theories to better understand the role of resources.
The present study will combine COR with cognitive dissonance
theory (Festinger 1957) to offer a more nuanced understanding
of how relationship-­ and character-­based trust interact with ille-
gitimate tasks to influence employee cognitive flexibility.
Cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger 1957) posits that individu-
als experience psychological discomfort when they simultaneously
hold contradictory beliefs, values, or attitudes. This discomfort
motivates them to reduce the dissonance, with the urgency to re-
solve it increasing as the inconsistency intensifies (Festinger 1957).
In workplace settings, employees may experience cognitive disso-
nance when assigned tasks they perceive as illegitimate, as these
tasks clash with their role expectations or professional identity.
Employees who have high levels of trust in their leaders—whether
grounded in perceived competence (character-­based trust) or in
benevolence and goodwill (relationship-­based trust) (Dirks and
Ferrin 2002; Legood et al. 2023)—may experience this dissonance
more intensely. The assignment of illegitimate tasks contradicts
their expectations of trusted leaders, leading to stronger feelings
of disappointment or even betrayal, and to a greater preoccupation
with resolving the internal conflict caused by illegitimate tasks.
Thus, they may experience greater depletion in their cognitive
and affective resources. As resource loss intensifies, employees
become more inclined to conserve their remaining resources,
thereby reducing their motivation to invest in cognitively demand-
ing functions like cognitive flexibility. In contrast, those with
lower levels of trust may perceive less dissonance and are therefore
less negatively affected by illegitimate tasks than their high-­trust
counterparts. The argument that trust in leaders amplifies the neg-
ative effects of illegitimate tasks on cognitive flexibility aligns with
the growing body of research indicating that inconsistent leader-
ship—such as alternating between abusive and transformative
styles—can increase stress and negative emotions in employees
(Klebe et al. 2022; Mullen et al. 2011; Suurd Ralph and Barling
2023; Schilling et al. 2023). Thus, we propose:
Hypothesis 3(a).  Relationship-­based trust moderates the
relationship between illegitimate tasks and cognitive flexibility
such that the negative effect is intensified when relationship-­based
trust is high.
Hypothesis 3(b).  Character-­based trust moderates the rela-
tionship between illegitimate tasks and cognitive flexibility such
that the negative effect is intensified when character-­based trust
is high.
The distinction between relationship-­based and character-­based
trust suggests that each form may uniquely shape how employ-
ees react to illegitimate tasks. Relationship-­based trust, grounded
in emotional bonds and interpersonal connection (Tomlinson

3   |   Methods
3.1   |   Data Collection
Data collection for this study was conducted in two distinct
waves during 2023, targeting public servants and their de-
partment managers within Vietnam's public sector. Vietnam
presents a compelling context for this research due to the
growing need to promote innovative work behavior amid
the increasing prevalence of illegitimate tasks. As one of
the fastest-­growing economies with ambitious development
goals, Vietnam's government faces mounting pressure to meet
dynamic service demands and improve service standards
(Vu  2023). In response, fostering innovation among public
servants has become a national priority (Vu 2023). However,
the potential rise of illegitimate tasks—stemming from on-
going administrative reforms, reductions in the public sector
workforce, and poor managerial practices—threatens to un-
dermine these efforts. Understanding how illegitimate tasks
affect innovative work behavior is crucial, as it can inform
policies and managerial strategies aimed at reducing these
tasks to support public service reform.
We contacted public sector departments that work in different
public service areas, including healthcare, broadcasting, and
public administrative service provision, for data collection.
With the help of these departments, we distributed surveys to all
public servants and their managers at two different time points,
three months apart in 2023. Participants were informed that
their participation was voluntary and anonymous.
In the first wave, we surveyed public servants regarding their
perceptions of illegitimate tasks, relationship-­based trust,
character-­based trust, cognitive flexibility, and demographic
variables. This wave yielded 461 usable responses from public
servants working across 40 departments.1 Three months later,
the second wave of data collection was conducted, during which
department managers were contacted and asked to evaluate the
innovative work behavior of each employee in their department.
This second phase received responses from 24 out of 40 depart-
mental managers of the public servants in wave 1 (a response
rate of 60%).
Managers' evaluations of employee innovative work behavior
offer several advantages. First, they mitigate common method
bias—often associated with reliance on a single data source—
by combining managerial assessments of the outcome with em-
ployee self-­ratings of the antecedent, mediator, and moderators

they evaluated. These numbers were then used as codes by the
research team to match employee and manager responses. After
matching the two waves, we had 301 dyadic responses comprising
public servants from the first wave fully matched with 24 manag-
ers from the second wave. We removed 7 responses due to missing
data, resulting in a final dataset that includes 294 dyads of public

an average organizational tenure of 13.18 years. 37% of the partic-

3.2   |   Measures
All main variables, except for the controls, were measured
using items with 5-­point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree;
5 = strongly agree) and from previously validated measures
which were translated to Vietnamese through a back-­translation
approach (Schaffer and Riordan 2003).
Illegitimate tasks were assessed using the 8-­item Bern
Illegitimate Tasks Scale (BITS; Semmer et al. 2010), compris-
ing two facets: unreasonable tasks and unnecessary tasks. We
treated illegitimate tasks as a single factor construct since our
focus is on investigating the overall effect of illegitimate tasks
on public servants. Additionally, the two facets were highly cor-
related (r = 0.75), suggesting that they may represent the same
construct (Ding and Kuvaas  2023). Standardized CFA factor
loadings of all items ranged from 0.65 to 0.83.
Cognitive flexibility was measured using the scale developed
by Martin and Rubin  (1995). We retained 5 items with stan-
dardized CFA factor loadings greater than 0.50 (ranged from
0.51 to 0.82).
Innovative work behavior was measured using a 5-­item scale
adapted from Scott and Bruce  (1994) and Janssen  (2000), as
utilized in Ma Prieto and Pérez-­Santana  (2014). We retained
3 items with standardized factor loadings greater than 0.50
(ranged from 0.54 to 0.99).
Relationship-­based trust was measured using the 5-­item affect-­
based trust scale developed by McAllister (1995). Standardized
CFA factor loadings of all items ranged from 0.81 to 0.84.
Character-­based trust was assessed using a 6-­item scale developed
by McAllister (1995). We retained 5 items with standardized CFA
factor loadings greater than 0.50 (ranged from 0.78 to 0.82).
Control variables included gender, age, education, and tenure,
consistent with prior research in innovative work behavior
among public servants (Kousina and Voudouris 2023; Lapuente
and Suzuki 2020). Table 1 presents details of these measures.
3.3   |   Reliability, Convergent Validity
and Discriminant Validity
We evaluated the reliability of our five latent constructs using
construct reliability (CR) and Cronbach's alpha. As detailed in
Table 1, the CR values ranged from 0.80 to 0.91, and Cronbach's
alpha values ranged from 0.77 to 0.91. These figures confirm
that the constructs are reliably measured (Hair et al. 2009).
The latent constructs demonstrate acceptable convergent validity
due to the following: First, all constructs showed high reliability
(Hair et al. 2009). Second, standardized CFA factor loadings for
all constructs exceeded 0.50 (Hair et al. 2009). Third, the av-
erage variance extracted (AVE) values for each construct were
above 0.5, ranging from 0.52 to 0.67 (Hair et al. 2009).
Regarding discriminant validity, the five-­factor CFA model ex-
hibited good fit indices (χ2[289] = 622.77, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.95,
RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.08). All inter-­construct correlations
were below or equal to 0.7 (Cheung et al. 2024). Furthermore,
each AVE was significantly higher than the squared correlation
coefficients, as shown in Table 1. These findings confirm that
the latent constructs maintain discriminant validity (Cheung
et al. 2024).
3.4   |   Common Method Bias and Variance
We implemented several procedural and statistical remedies
to mitigate the potential influence of common method bias
on our findings. We collected data at two different times
with different raters to reduce common method bias risks
(Podsakoff et al. 2012). We also ensured participant anonym-
ity and randomized the survey questions to minimize social
desirability and other potential biases (Podsakoff et al. 2012).
Furthermore, we performed Harman's single-­factor test to as-
sess common method variance, which revealed that a single
factor accounted for only 28.86% of the variance—suggesting
that common method variance is not a significant concern.
Lastly, identifying a significant moderation effect further sup-
ports the robustness of our findings, indicating that common
method bias did not unduly influence the results (Bozionelos
and Simmering 2022).

3.5   |   Analytic Procedure for Hypothesis Testing
The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient for innovative work be-
havior was 0.73, indicating that 73% of the variance in this be-
havior could be attributed to the departmental membership of
the employees. To manage the influence of group membership
and the nested structure of our data (public servants nested
within 24 departments), we employed the random intercept
model of hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) (Garson 2013).
This model accommodates variations in baseline character-
istics among public servants nested within different depart-
ments, thereby isolating the effects specific to individuals
while adjusting for inter-­departmental differences in innova-
tive work behavior.
We conducted mediation analysis of the two-­level random in-
tercept (hierarchical) model, accounting for hierarchical data
structures. The indirect effect was calculated using the product
of coefficients method, where the effect of the independent vari-
able on the mediator (a path) and the effect of the mediator on
the dependent variable (b path) were multiplied (a × b). To assess
statistical significance, we used a bootstrap resampling proce-
dure with 5000 replications, repeatedly resampling the dataset
with replacement to estimate the indirect effect (a × b). This gen-
erated bootstrap standard errors, percentile and bias-­corrected
95% confidence intervals (CIs) to account for variability and po-
tential skewness. The mediation effect was considered signifi-
cant if the confidence interval did not include zero, indicating
a meaningful indirect pathway. The analyses were conducted
using Stata version 17.0.

4.2   |   Robustness Analysis
Initially, we examined the moderating effects of relationship-­
based trust and character-­based trust separately to clarify their
contributions to the relationship between the independent and
dependent variables. Including multiple moderators in the same
model may create overlapping effects that complicate interpreta-
tion (Becker et al. 2016). This can also reduce degrees of freedom
and increase standard errors, thereby decreasing the statistical
power of the tests for individual variables (Becker et al. 2016).
Nonetheless, to further validate our results, we subsequently
analyzed both moderating effects simultaneously within a hi-
erarchical model, presented in Model 7 (Table  2). The results
indicate that the interaction between illegitimate tasks and
relationship-­based trust is negative and statistically significant
at p = 0.05, whereas the interaction between illegitimate tasks
and character-­based trust is negligible and not statistically sig-
nificant3. These additional analyses confirm that our conclu-
sions regarding Hypotheses 3a, 3b, and 4 remain consistent with
our findings as discussed above.
5   |   Discussion
This study draws upon COR theory and cognitive dissonance
theory to postulate a study model of how and when illegitimate
tasks discourage innovative work behavior among public ser-
vants. It observed that illegitimate tasks undermine innovative
work behavior. The study further found evidence for the role of
cognitive flexibility as a mediating path between illegitimate
tasks and innovative work behavior. Furthermore, while the
results support relationship-­based trust as a moderator that in-
tensifies the negative association between illegitimate tasks and
cognitive flexibility, there is insufficient evidence to support
character-­based trust as a moderator for this negative relation-
ship. Nonetheless, these findings provide support for a stronger
moderating effect that relationship-­based trust plays in the re-
lationship between illegitimate tasks and cognitive flexibility
than character-­based trustdoes.

5.1   |   Theoretical Implications
This study provides crucial theoretical insights for the public
administration literature. First, by empirically validating the
negative effect of illegitimate tasks on public sector employ-
ees' innovative work behavior, the present study adds to the
inquiries in the public administration literature that attempt
to understand how rules and procedures (e.g., red tape) under-
mine employees' engagement in innovative activities (Moon
and Bretschneider 2002; van Acker et al. 2018; Van Dijck and
Steen 2023). It also extends an emerging stream of research in
public administration that calls for a better understanding of
managers' assignment of illegitimate tasks that drain resources
among public sector employees who even need more resources
to cope with and address increasing demands from citizens
(Bani-­Melhem et al. 2025; Fältén et al. 2024). We draw scholarly
and practitioner attention to illegitimate tasks that can discour-
age public sector employees from generating and implementing
novel and useful ideas or solutions to provide better services for
citizens.
This inquiry also responds to recent systematic literature re-
views on employee innovative work behavior in both pub-
lic administration and general management (AlEssa and
Durugbo  2022; Srirahayu et  al.  2023), which have called for
further research into the effects of specific job aspects, such
as illegitimate tasks, on innovative work behavior, beyond the
focus of the current studies on general job demands (AlEssa and
Durugbo 2022; Srirahayu et al. 2023). Simultaneously, this study
challenges the stream of research on illegitimate tasks that has
largely examined how illegitimate tasks stimulate negative be-
haviors among employees, such as unethical behavior and work-
place deviance, as well as suppressing a few positive behaviors
like organizational citizenship (reflected in the review by Ding
and Kuvaas 2023) by breaking the silence on how illegitimate
tasks inhibit employee innovation. Therefore, our study offers
new insights into the broader consequences of illegitimate tasks
that extend beyond the previously studied negative and positive
outcomes.
Second, the study finds evidence for employee cognitive flexi-
bility as a mediating conduit for the negative effect of illegiti-
mate tasks on employee innovative work behavior. Albeit this
finding is partly consonant with prior findings on the links be-
tween illegitimate tasks and cognitive processes (e.g., psycho-
logical detachment, Zhou et al. 2020; attention residue, Parker
et al. 2024) as well as Al-­Ghazali and Afsar's (2021) report of
cognitive flexibility and innovative work behavior, it extends the
current literature by being the first to examine the mediating
role of cognitive flexibility underlying the adverse effects of ille-
gitimate tasks on public servants' behavior. This inquiry extends
the stream of task duties–innovative work behavior research by
unearthing a fine-­grained cognitive process, namely cognitive
flexibility, through which illegitimate tasks undermine innova-
tive work behavior among employees. This finding challenges
the current assumptions that the main mechanisms through
which illegitimate tasks influence employee behaviors or per-
formance are psychological (meaning of work [Mäkikangas
et al. 2023], moral disengagement [Zhao et al. 2022], psycho-
logical contract breach [Dong et al. 2025]) or affective pathways
(shame/anger [Wang and Jiang 2023]; negative emotion [Wang
and Zong 2023]). Adding cognitive flexibility as a mechanism to
the current assumptions on psychological/affective mechanisms
can help further explain how illegitimate tasks can inhibit extra-­
role behaviors (e.g., innovative work behavior) beyond job per-
formance or in-­role behaviors, thereby increasing managers'
understanding of more adverse effects of illegitimate tasks and
motivating them to minimize such tasks to unleash cognitive
flexibility for innovation.
The third significant contribution of the study entails the rev-
elation of the role of relationship-­based trust as a moderator
that accentuates the detrimental effect of illegitimate tasks on
employee cognitive flexibility, as well as a more pronounced
effect of relationship-­based trust compared with character-­
based trust on this adverse effect. These results are somewhat
consistent with Muntz et al. (2019) findings on the interactive
effect of supervisors' relational transparency and illegitimate
tasks. Nonetheless, while Muntz et al.'s (2019) work examined
the moderating effect of relational transparency, our study
marks a pivotal advancement in the literature by uncovering
the critical role of fine-­grained forms of trust in leaders, namely
relationship-­based and character-­based trust, and being among
the first to examine these moderators in the context of illegit-
imate tasks. Albeit these two forms of trust in leaders may in-
fluence employees' cognitive processes in different ways (Dirks
and Ferrin 2002; Legood et al. 2023; McAllister 1995), it is un-
clear in the literature how differently the moderating mecha-
nism of these two trust forms operates, especially in the context
of illegitimate tasks. We extend the literature by demonstrating
that relationship-­based trust is stronger than character-­based
trust in accentuating the detrimental effect of illegitimate tasks.
This also challenges the assumption that trust in leaders or their
leadership serves to nurture employees' cognitive flexibility
(Chen et al. 2025) and innovative behaviors (Zafar et al. 2024)
by highlighting that some forms of trust in leaders, particularly
relationship-­based trust, may even undermine these employee
outcomes by exacerbating the detrimental effects of job-­related
factors such as illegitimate tasks. Furthermore, by examining
the moderating effect of employees' psychological state (i.e.,
trust in leaders), our findings also question the prevailing view
that the influence of illegitimate tasks is largely bounded by
supervisors' behaviors, such as relational transparency (Muntz
et al. 2019), support (Fila and Eatough 2020), developmental
feedback (He et  al.  2024), or paternalistic dimensions (Wang
and Zong 2023).
In addition, it was unexpectedly found that character-­based
trust does not exert a moderating effect on the adverse impact
of illegitimate tasks. A potential explanation for this finding is
that, although employees are likely to experience cognitive dis-
sonance when they perceive incongruence between their beliefs
in the manager's character or benevolence and his or her assign-
ment of illegitimate tasks, employees who are high in character-­
based trust are less likely to perceive that such a dissonance is
critical in influencing their relationship with the manager and
undermining their cognitive process for innovation. Employees
with character-­based trust view that a dissonance in relation to
competence (Legood et al. 2023) can be adjusted through mu-
tual learning, leading to a weak or even non-­significant moder-
ating effect of character-­based trust on the negative association
between illegitimate tasks and cognitive flexibility. Nonetheless,

employees who base their trust in the manager's benevolence
(i.e., relationship-­based trust) are more sensitive to a dissonance
linked with caring values (Legood et al. 2023) and experience
stronger psychological dissonance, resulting in a more pro-

negative association between illegitimate tasks and cognitive

Lastly, by integrating cognitive dissonance theory, we respond
to Hobfoll et al.'s (2018) call to expand COR theory through the
incorporation of complementary micro-­level perspectives. This
theoretical triangulation enables a more nuanced understand-
ing of how resources function under adverse work conditions,
such as illegitimate tasks. Our framework reveals that, in such
contexts, resources typically regarded as beneficial—such as
trust in leaders—can heighten cognitive dissonance, ultimately
constraining individuals' cognitive flexibility. This finding con-
tributes to an emerging stream of COR research that challenges
the assumption of resource universality, emphasizing instead
that the utility of a resource is contingent upon its contextual
relevance and the psychological meanings attributed to it. Our
findings also extend COR theory by differentiating between two
types of trust-­based resources: relational and character-­based
trust. We demonstrate that these forms of trust exert different
effects under adverse conditions, such as illegitimate tasks. This
highlights the importance of considering not only the presence
of resources but also their qualitative nature and situational
alignment.
5.2   |   Practical Implications
Our findings provide important implications for public admin-
istration. First, public sector organizations should implement
proactive measures to identify and prevent illegitimate tasks, en-
suring alignment with employees' responsibilities (Bani-­Melhem
et al. 2025). Clear job descriptions with explicit key performance
indicators (KPIs) can minimize ambiguity in role expectations.
Additionally, fostering open communication is crucial (Wang
and Jiang 2023). Creating a work climate where public servants
feel safe raising concerns about task assignments without fear of
repercussions can help promptly address illegitimate task issues.
Second, immediate managers, who often directly assign tasks to
employees, should be cautious to ensure that they assign tasks
that are meaningful and relevant to employees' duties. They can
help employees reframe their tasks by providing context and
explaining the significance and relevance of these tasks to the
employees' core duties, thereby reducing the misperception that
tasks are illegitimate (Bani-­Melhem et al. 2025). Third, as trust
can amplify the negative impact of illegitimate tasks, manag-
ers should avoid exploiting employee trust by assigning such
tasks. Employees with high levels of trust in their leader may
be less resistant to accepting these assignments, which can lead
to cognitive dissonance and further hinder their cognitive flexi-
bility and innovative work behavior. Lastly, when avoiding ille-
gitimate tasks is impossible—such as when a colleague falls ill
and urgently needs others to temporarily take on tasks outside
their usual responsibilities—managers must take appropriate
steps to mitigate the negative impacts (Wang and Jiang 2023).
Employees' reactions to these tasks depend largely on their
understanding of why they were assigned, so it is crucial for
managers to clearly explain the reasoning to prevent feelings of
resentment or frustration (Wang and Jiang 2023). Furthermore,
when managers show respect, appreciation, and recognition for
employees taking on seemingly “unreasonable” tasks, it plays
a crucial role in mitigating the negative effects of illegitimate
tasks (Bani-­Melhem et al. 2025).
5.3   |   Limitations and Future Research Directions
We acknowledge several limitations in our study. First, our
methodology relies on self-­reported measures from pub-
lic sector employees and their immediate leaders to assess
key variables, which may not accurately reflect true levels of
these variables. For instance, manager evaluation of employee
innovative work behavior may be influenced by their subjec-
tive views, expectations, or their relationship with employees.
Future studies should incorporate more objective measures,
such as tracking the number of implemented new ideas (Banks
et al. 2023). Second, our approach of measuring the key vari-
ables at two different time points, while insightful for testing
direct mediating effects as well as avoiding common method
bias, is not a truly longitudinal design. Future research should
adopt a cross-­lagged design, with repeated measurements over
extended periods to enhance the robustness of findings and
provide deeper insights into temporal dynamics (Murdoch
et al. 2023). Third, as national cultural values can significantly
affect how employees perceive and respond to illegitimate tasks
(Ding and Kuvaas 2023), using a sample from only one coun-
try may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Thus, it is
crucial for future research to examine the effects of illegitimate
tasks and their mechanisms across countries. Lastly, our study
focuses on a limited set of mediators, moderators, and outcomes
related to illegitimate tasks. Expanding this scope would offer a
more comprehensive understanding of the impact of such tasks
in the public sector.
6   |   Conclusion
Our research highlights the detrimental effects of illegitimate
tasks on public servants' cognitive flexibility and innovative
work behavior, presenting significant challenges to the public
sector's ability to adapt in dynamic environments. We stress the
need to raise awareness of these tasks and implement proactive
measures within public administration. Policymakers, manag-
ers, and leaders should develop strategies to mitigate these tasks
and establish robust policies to prevent their recurrence. Our
findings also reveal that relationship-­based trust can exacerbate
the negative impact of illegitimate tasks on employees' cognitive
flexibility. While trust is typically regarded as a positive force
within organizations, our research suggests that its effects are
more nuanced and context-­dependent than often acknowledged
in prior literature. This underscores the need for a more refined
theoretical approach that considers the potential unintended
consequences associated with trust.

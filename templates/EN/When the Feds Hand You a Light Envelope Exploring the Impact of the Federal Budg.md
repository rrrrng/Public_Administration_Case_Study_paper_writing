ABSTRACT
In 2009, the US federal government created the Build America Bond (BAB) program to improve access to the capital markets for
state and local governments. While the federal government typically subsidizes subnational capital financing indirectly through
tax exemption, the BAB program provided direct cash subsidies to governments. The Budget Control Act of 2011 implemented
a sequester that reduced BAB subsidies by 5.7 to 8.7% each year since 2013. This paper estimates previous and potential future
sequestration losses on all outstanding BABs as of the end of 2022. It delves into BAB use and sequestration by two issuers
(governments in the state of Texas and City of Chicago). The paper concludes with a discussion of the current state of BABs as
it relates to recent litigation and refinancing attempts. The loss estimates provide lessons for the provision and management of
federal direct subsidies available to state and local governments.

1   |   Introduction
It has now been over 15 years since President Obama signed
the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA) which
aimed to stimulate the US economy in the context of the Great
Financial Crisis (GFC) and subsequent Great Recession. As part
of this legislation, the ARRA created several new types of mu-
nicipal bonds, most notably Build America Bonds (BABs). BABs
were intended to provide state and local governments greater
market access to cost-­efficient capital for infrastructure financ-
ing during this time of extreme market upheaval. Unlike typical
tax-­exempt bonds that represent an indirect subsidy, BABs were
taxable bonds through which the federal government provided
a generous direct cash subsidy to municipal issuers to offset the
higher cost of capital associated with taxable bonds. The subsidy
was 35% of the annual interest cost of the bonds.
BABs were a popular financial tool as evidenced by state and
local government entities selling $181 billion of these bonds be-
tween March 2009 and December 31, 2010, upon the program's
legislative sunset. President Obama proposed several reau-
thorizations of BABs and even presidential candidate Donald
Trump's economic advisors indicated an interest in instituting
a direct subsidy bond program (Luby et al. 2021). The Biden ad-
ministration also initially advocated for a BAB type of capital
financing in its infrastructure plans (Pierog 2021). However, to
date, no reauthorization of BABs has occurred as Republicans in
Congress have been unsupportive, and many government issu-
ers have soured on BABs (Devitt 2024).

mental issuers have ex-­post misgivings about the BAB program

in most BABs made it difficult for governments to refinance their
bonds for interest cost savings (Salwati and Wessel 2021). This
turned out to be unfortunate as interest rates declined to his-

most importantly, many governments unfortunately learned
what The Sopranos TV character Richie Aprile warned about

promised BAB subsidies not for a year or two but by 5.7 to 8.7%
each year since 2013 as a result of budget sequestration included
in the 2011 Budget Control Act (Internal Revenue Service 2023).
These “light envelopes” in annual federal bond subsidies, as well
as the inability to refinance early, have significantly narrowed
the cost of capital benefit of BABs relative to traditional tax-­
exempt bonds (Luby et al. 2021).
In terms of Richie Aprile's graver concern of “…it is just the
beginning”, such 5.7% reductions are scheduled to continue
through 2030. However, most concerning to state and local gov-
ernments was the recent threat to delay or completely eliminate
the 35% subsidy in 2023 and thereafter as a result of the federal
government not enacting a PAYGO waiver for BABs due to the
2021 American Rescue Plan being passed via budget reconcil-
iation (Sowers 2022a). This would have resulted in billions of
lost subsidies to state and local governments. In the short-­term,
this crisis was averted at the last minute when such a waiver
was ultimately included in the omnibus budget package passed
by Congress in late December 2022 (Hilltop Securities  2023).
However, such a waiver will need to be approved in future
years to ensure that municipal issuers receive their BAB subsi-
dies although still at reduced levels due to the scheduled budget
sequester.
This paper explores the impact of the federal budget sequestra-
tion on the BAB program. We begin with a brief recap of the
policy justification for a direct subsidy bond program as well as
offering some considerations of such a program. We contextual-
ize the BAB program in the larger academic literatures on fiscal
federalism and federal budget dysfunction. We then turn to our
analysis by detailing the amount of BABs outstanding at the end
of 2022 and estimating previous and potentially future seques-
tration losses for municipal issuers on such bonds to get a sense
of the “lightness” of the federal government's envelope. Next, we
delve into the BAB use, sequestration impacts and other admin-
istrative challenges on two of the heaviest users of these bonds,
issuers in the State of Texas and the City of Chicago. The paper
concludes with a brief discussion of the current state of BABs
related to recent litigation and attempts to refinance these bonds
through extraordinary redemptions provisions potentially trig-
gered by the federal budget sequester. The paper is not just
backward-­looking at a federal program that has expired. Rather,
the loss estimates and developments discussed in this paper
offer lessons for the provision and management of federal di-
rect subsidies available to state and local governments through
President Biden's recent landmark Inflation Reduction Act.
2   |   The Cases for and Against Federal Direct
Subsidy Bonds
State and local government entities use debt to finance their cap-
ital activities. In the context of the GFC and Great Recession,
the BAB program aimed to give these issuers a new financing
tool to help them fund infrastructure projects in the midst of
an economic downturn. The 35% subsidy was viewed as more
generous than what state and local governments receive indi-
rectly through tax exemption (Ang et al. 2010; US Department
of the Treasury  2010; Liu and Denison  2014). Thus, with the
BAB program structured as a temporary one scheduled to sun-
set on December 31, 2010, issuers were incentivized to sell BABs
quickly to help finance infrastructure projects already on the
docket, with such funded projects having a stimulative effect on
the macroeconomy.
It was also expected that municipal issuers would be able to fi-
nance these projects at a lower net cost of capital due to the tax
status of BABs. That is, taxable BABs attract a wider range of
investors, including non-­profit foundations, foreign entities, and
pension funds that normally do not purchase municipal bonds
since they cannot avail themselves of the tax exemption bene-
fit. Basic theories of market competition argue that the greater
demand for BABs would cause investors to accept lower inter-
est rates on these bonds, which would redound to the benefit of
state and local government entities.

and the economy in 2009 and 2010, the creation of a direct

subsidy bond program was a policy goal advocated for decades
by many economists and policy analysts (Fortune 1973; Galper
and Peterson 1973; Congressional Budget Office 2004). These
observers preferred the direct subsidy approach relative to the
indirect subsidy approach through bond tax exemption for sev-
eral reasons. First, tax exemption was viewed as inefficient in
terms of the cost to the federal government in foregone tax rev-
enues compared to the benefits received by state and local gov-
ernments via lower borrowing costs. The diversity of marginal
tax rates of municipal bond investors in which lower marginal
income tax investors effectively “set” the interest rate on the
tax-­exempt bonds ensured that the federal government would
pay more for the subsidy in foregone tax revenues than state and
local governments would receive in benefits from lower inter-
est rates.
Second, since municipal bond tax exemption is generally un-
capped in terms of an investor's income level, it provides the
largest benefit to investors in the highest marginal tax bracket.
These analysts viewed such tax preference as inequitable, un-
necessarily exacerbating income inequality in the United States.
Finally, a federal budgetary benefit provided as a direct subsidy
is more transparent than one provided indirectly. In the case of
the BAB program, the subsidy is technically part of the appro-
priations process subject to annual review and legislative action,
whereas municipal bond tax exemption is part of the tax code
(i.e., tax expenditure) and not subject to regular review. In fact,
significant changes to most tax expenditures are relatively rare
as benefitting constituencies assemble and rally to protect their
tax benefits.
All three of these justifications for a direct subsidy bond pro-
gram are perfectly reasonable and would make sense to the neu-
tral observer. However, there are other considerations from the
state and local government perspective when moving from an
indirect to a direct approach in the context of bond subsidies.
First, there is a concern about the future “federalizing” of subna-
tional infrastructure development. This would arise if Congress
decided to provide varying levels of direct bond subsidies based
on bond project type as aligned with the policy preferences of
federal legislators. This would result in a loss of financial au-
tonomy for state and local governments (Johnson et al. 2021).
Senator Ron Wyden (D-­OR) exemplified this concern when he
said the following in 2010:
I would like to see different flavors of BABs created.
That would allow us to adjust the subsidy and give, for
example, transportation infrastructure investment a
larger subsidy than other types of projects because
transportation projects typically create more jobs and
other public benefits. (Seymour 2010)
Second, managing a direct subsidy bond program is different
from a traditional tax-­exempt bond. Since the federal subsidy
is indirect through lower interest rates demanded by investors
because of the tax exemption, tax-­exempt bonds do not require
regular interactions post bond issuance with the federal govern-
ment by the municipal issuer. This is not the case with federal
direct subsidy bonds. In the case of BABs, the issuer must file an
8038-­CP in paper form (not electronically) no more than 90 days
before each interest payment date to formally request the sub-
sidy. The Chicago case detailed later in this paper will illustrate
some of the administrative challenges with this requirement.
Third, and most germane to this paper, moving to a direct
subsidy approach exposes state and local governments to the
whims of the annual federal budget process. As mentioned
above, this is not the case with tax expenditures like municipal
bond tax exemption since the tax code is changed infrequently.
Unfortunately for these governments, this precisely is what has
happened since 2013. State and local governments received the
full 35% interest cost subsidy between 2009 and 2012. However,
in 2011, Congress passed the 2011 Budget Control Act, which
established caps on discretionary spending for fiscal years 2012
through 2021 and required automatic spending cuts—known
as sequestration—if the government exceeded these caps. The
sequestration required the government to make spending cuts
across a range of federal programs, including subsidies for
BABs. Table 1 shows the BAB subsidy sequestration reduction
for each fiscal year between 2013 and 2030.
While the BAB program aimed to provide more direct financial
benefits and transparency over traditional tax-­exempt bonds,
the larger implications of shifting fiscal responsibility to the
federal government were not fully appreciated until the bud-
get sequester. The reductions displayed in Table 1 highlight the
long-­term risks of federalizing state and local financing. The fol-
lowing section explores the theoretical significance of the BAB-­
related losses faced by state and local governments in the wake
of a budget sequester.

and governance. The effects of sequestration on the BAB pro-
gram offer a powerful illustration of the implications of fed-
eralization in the context of state and local finance. The bond
subsidies are susceptible to the whims of federal budgetary pol-
itics. Naturally, this is relevant to the larger discussion on fiscal
federalism as it raises concerns about the potential for shifting
fiscal incentives and the risk of increasing federal control over
state and local finances.
Historically, the theoretical framework of fiscal federalism em-
phasized the balance between central and local fiscal authority
over resource allocation and accountability. Decentralized fis-
cal systems are generally expected to enhance local preference-­
matching with service provision, efficiency, and accountability
(Tiebout 1956; Oates 1972). On the other hand, more recent the-
oretical developments highlight the risk of greater fiscal ineq-
uities. Intergovernmental aid may distort local incentives and
increase public spending inefficiencies, and inequitable service
provision across jurisdictions (Oates 1999; Weingast 2009). In
the US, for example, the financing of public infrastructure is
largely under the domain of local authorities. Increased federal
involvement in infrastructure finance may undermine the bene-
fits of decentralization, with local governments still responsible
for the allocation of infrastructure funds, while increasing fed-
eral dependency for revenue generation. The BAB program em-
phasizes a potential “vertical fiscal imbalance” (VFI), in which
the federal government may become increasingly involved in
the financing of local service provision, while leaving spend-
ing authority under the responsibility of state and local govern-
ments (Oates 1999). Essentially, VFIs distort incentives, because
spending authority and revenue responsibility are misaligned.
Another apparent issue with VFI in the context of the BAB
program is that it makes local infrastructure spending suscep-
tible to changes due to federal budgetary dysfunctions. Second-­
generation theories of fiscal federalism elaborate on changes to
incentive structures and governance resulting from federal de-
pendency. Reliance on intergovernmental aid during economic
crises may lead to a softening of budget constraints which incen-
tivize riskier fiscal behavior and less usage of innovative fiscal
tools in periods of poor economic health because subnational
governments expect to be cushioned by federal aid (Kornai 1986;
Weingast 2009).
A major risk of federalization in the US context is that federal
aid is highly volatile to the turbulent nature of the US federal
budget process. This volatility, compounded by the burden of
managing such a program under federal regulations, can un-
dermine congruence between spending and public preferences
(Joyce 2008). Additionally, the decline of the federal budget pro-
cess, by centering on partisan conflict rather than budgeting
efficiency, can unleash administrative chaos and downstream
effects to lower levels of government (Rubin 2007; Meyers 2014;
Brien et al. 2023). If federal aid is subject to budget dysfunctions
such as shutdowns, continuing resolutions, and central to this
paper, sequestration, state and local governments risk an imbal-
ance between their expenditure authority and funding for those
decisions.
The BAB program is a practical case to examine how central-
ized economic aid allows federal budgeting dysfunctions such
as sequestration to create uncertainty and financial losses for
state and local governments. The promises of the BAB program
increase state and local dependency on the federal government
for municipal bond interest payments, while leaving spending
authority at the state and local level. This alleviates financial
pressures on state and local governments, but also subjects
funding to the unpredictability of the federal budgeting process.
Relative to the protection of municipal bond tax exemption safe-
guarded in the tax code, there is weak protection of direct bond
subsidies in the face of a sequester. If state and local government
bond subsidies are subject to the whims of federal budgeting,
issuers assume the risk of navigating an unpredictable fiscal en-
vironment with insecure resources. How much of this risk was
assessed by state and local governments when considering the
use of BABs is unclear.
Furthermore, this risk leaves state and local governments ex-
posed during economic downturns. As discussed before, issuers
were incentivized by the generous direct subsidy to take advan-
tage of BABs, but state and local governments were left to fend
for themselves when they received “light envelopes” to manage
their budgets. Not only does this impact infrastructure financ-
ing, but it also limits their ability to manage periods of economic
downturn, putting their long-­term fiscal health at risk and po-
tentially mitigating the financing benefits of BABs. The remain-
ing sections of the paper will illuminate some of these concerns
by exploring the dollar impacts of the budget sequester and other
federal administrative impacts on the provision of BAB subsi-
dies to state and local governments.
4   |   Build America Bonds Outstanding
As stated earlier, the BAB program was initially very popular
with municipal issuers. There were $64.1 billion and $117.3 bil-
lion in BABs initially sold in 2009 and 2010, respectively.
This comprised 16% (2009) and 27% (2010) of the long-­term
municipal bond market in these 2 years (Luby 2012). The sell-

ing BABs as of this date.

5   |   Build America Bond Sequestration Loss
Estimation
As previously discussed, the sequestration of federal bond subsi-
dies has been widely noted in the financial press and in state and
local government finance circles. However, we are not aware of
any updated publicly available estimates of the potential revenue
losses to state and local governments under different sequester
scenarios.2 This section of the paper aims to provide such an
analysis by making the following three budget sequester loss
estimates for issuers in each state based on the $112.1 billion in
BABs outstanding as of October 2022:
1.	 The historical estimated loss between March 1, 2013 and
September 30, 2023 based on the actual sequestration re-
duction percentages by year as shown in Table 1 (“histori-
cal loss estimate”)
2.	 The hypothetical estimated loss between January 1, 2023
and September 30, 2026 assuming the federal government
reduced the entire 35% subsidy as was the concern at the
end of 2022 when the PAYGO waiver was in jeopardy (“hy-
pothetical loss estimate”)
3.	 The future estimated loss between January 1, 2023 and
September 30, 2030 based on a 5.7% annual budget seques-
ter. The 5.7% reduction for this period was the BAB seques-
tration reduction established in 2020 scheduled to apply
to all BAB interest payments through September 30, 2030
assuming BABs received the PAYGO waiver (“future loss
estimate”)
The calculation of the loss is straightforward and can be
seen through an example. As previously stated, the full BAB
subsidy was 35% of the interest payment on the bonds. The
federal budget sequester percentage reduction (as shown in
Table  1) represents the percentage of the BAB interest pay-
ment subsidy reduced in any given year. In 2013, the se-
questration rate was 8.70%. Now, let's assume that there was
a $10 million par amount BAB outstanding that carried a
coupon interest rate of 6%. The 2013 loss estimate from the
federal budget sequester for that bond would be computed as
follows:
1.	 The scheduled total annual interest payment payable by
the issuer to investors would be $600,000 (i.e., $10,000,000
* 6.00%)
2.	 The full annual BAB subsidy the issuer expected to re-
ceive from the federal government to offset a portion
of annual interest payment would be $210,000 (i.e.,
$600,000 * 35%)
3.	 The annual amount of the full BAB subsidy the issuer
would not receive as a result of the federal budget seques-
ter (i.e., estimated loss) would be $18,270 (i.e., $210,000 *
8.70%)

A few estimation assumptions and caveats are made. First, the
“historical loss estimate” is only based on the BABs outstand-
ing as of October 2022, which was $112.1 billion. Since the total
issuance of BABs was $181 billion, $68.9 billion either already
matured or were called early prior to October 2022. As such, the
historical loss estimate should be viewed as conservative since it
does not include bonds outstanding as of 2013 but that matured
before October 2022. This missing information is due to a lack
of data on these matured/called bonds available from Bloomberg
as of October 2022. Second, the computations of the “hypotheti-
cal” and “future” loss estimates are made so long as the bond is
expected to be outstanding. For example, a bond that matures
on July 1, 2025 would have its “hypothetical” and “future” loss
estimates computed only to July 1, 2025 not September 30, 2026
or September 30, 2030, respectively, since the bond is scheduled
to be redeemed in full on July 1, 2025 and not outstanding the
full time period under those two scenarios. Third, we assume
that no BABs outstanding will be called early in computing the
“hypothetical” and “future” loss estimates. This is a reasonable
assumption, as was noted previously in terms of the difficulty
in refinancing BABs before their maturity date which will be
detailed in more depth in the last section of this paper. Finally,
the future loss estimate only goes through September 30, 2030
since this is the date that the current budget sequester cov-
ers. However, since many of these BABs have maturities after
September 30, 2030 and the budget sequester may be extended
past that date, the future loss estimate should be viewed as a
low-­end estimate.

7   |   Build America Bonds: The Chicago Experience
The City of Chicago offers an opportunity for an even deeper ex-
ploration of the impact of the federal budget sequester on a local
government issuer. As shown in Table 10, the City of Chicago
was an active seller of federal direct subsidy bonds during the
BAB program time period. The city sold eight bond issues be-
tween January 2010 and December 2010 for a total par amount
of $1.61 billion. The city sold these bond issues under four differ-
ent bond credits: general obligation, O'Hare airport, wastewater
and water. Six of these bond issues were BABs ($1.45 billion), one
was a BAB Recovery Zone Economic Development financing
($133.18 million) and one was a Qualified Energy Conservation
bond ($29.67 million). As of December 31, 2022, $1.33 billion of
these federal direct subsidy bonds were still outstanding with a
portion of the O'Hare bonds called early and a portion of the GO
2010B (Moden Schools) bonds maturing as scheduled.
Table 11 details the historical loss estimate for these eight federal
direct subsidy bond issuances by year between 2013 and 2023.
The city expected to receive $416.67 million in federal bond sub-
sidies assuming the full 35% subsidy would be received. Based
on the actual budget sequester reduction percentages between
2013 and 2023, the city should have had its subsidies reduced
by $27.07 million. However, the city's bond subsidies were fur-
ther reduced each year (with the exception of 2020) by another
$3.1 million total for employment taxes that were unpaid. The
timing and form of the notice of the additional holdback for un-
paid taxes often was scattershot with such acknowledgments
made via physical mail just before the interest payment date,
after the interest payment date or, at times, not at all. Moreover,
the IRS calculation of unpaid taxes was not detailed on any of
the notices, with just a line-­item amount noted as “amount ap-
plied to other taxes.”
The city received many subsidies in the 2018–2023 period
late, sometimes substantially late. The IRS compensated the
city for such timing delinquencies by providing late interest in
2018, 2020, 2022, and 2023 of $450,104. The Internal Revenue
Service warned municipal issuers of the potential late payments
during the pandemic. Since federal direct bond subsidies are
filed in paper form and not electronically, the IRS was delayed
in processing these paper filings due to IRS workers mainly
working remotely during this time (Murray and Finister 2020).
Nevertheless, the timing delays represent an additional admin-
istrative burden that the city absorbed in making its interest pay-
ments on the bonds. As shown in Table 11, the city's net reduction
in subsidies was $29.73 million (i.e., $27,073,936 + $3,104,029–
$450,104) between 2013 and 2023.4

8   |   Build America Bond Current Developments
The frustration with the sequestration of BAB subsidies has re-
sulted in an uptick in recent actions taken by municipal issuers.
One recent development was a 2020 lawsuit against the United
States government filed by six public power agencies that issued
over $4 billion in BABs. The lawsuit claimed that BABs should
never have been subject to the federal budget sequestration and
thus demanded that these issuers receive refunds for their se-
questered subsidies (Devitt  2023a). The plaintiffs argued that
Congress intended BABs to be exempt from sequestration and
that the Treasury Department had the ability to make such an
exemption. However, in February 2023, the Court of Federal
Appeals ruled that sequestration did apply to BAB subsidies
(Devitt 2023a). In August 2023, the plaintiffs appealed and sev-
eral other state and local policy groups urged the US Supreme
Court to take up the case (Devitt 2023b).

pal issuers to refinance their outstanding BABs by utilizing the
bonds' extraordinary redemption provisions (ERPs). ERPs were
included in many BAB bond indentures allowing the bonds to
be redeemed before their scheduled maturity date triggered by
a one-­time “extraordinary” event. The benefit of the ERPs is the
allowance to call the BABs early at a price of 100% of their par

to refinance the bonds early but only at a make-­whole call price.
Make-­whole call prices are based on current market conditions
and are usually valued at the present value of the future sched-

In order to “make investors whole”, a make-­whole call will usu-

provision (Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board 2024). Thus,
while the make-­whole call provision does not readily allow issu-
ers to economically refinance BABs, triggering the ERPs would.
In the case of BABs, municipal issuers are claiming that the one-­
time extraordinary event to trigger its ERP is the federal bud-
get sequester that has reduced subsidies for over a decade. In
February 2024, the law firm Orick issued a report arguing the
2023 Federal Court of Appeals ruling supports the claim that
the budget sequester would qualify as an ERP triggering event
on many BABs (Dockerty 2024). Upon the release of such a re-
port, many issuers considered refunding5 their BABs using their
ERPs. However, investors subsequently threatened lawsuits
challenging the use of ERPs to refund BABs at par. Some issu-

their refundings given the investor concerns and threatened
lawsuits (Dockerty 2024). Only time will tell what lies ahead on
a more market-­wide basis in terms of refinancing BABs, but it
is nonetheless unfortunate that issuers need to exert time and
energy exploring these legal and refinancing paths. One thing is
clear from such efforts is that many municipal issuers feel that
shedding their BABs from their bond portfolio would be finan-
cially advantageous.
9   |   Conclusions
This paper explored various issues related to the federal budget
sequester on the Build America Bond program.6 In this sense,
the paper offers unique contributions to the academic literature
on fiscal federalism and federal budget dysfunction in terms of
federal budget impacts on state and local governments. Based
on the amount of BABs outstanding at the end of 2022, we esti-
mated the aggregate historical dollar impact on municipal issu-
ers from the budget sequestration over the last 10 years. We also
made aggregate estimates for future federal direct bond subsidy
losses to these entities under two different scenarios. We are
not aware of any studies that have provided both historical and
pro forma estimates of these costs to state and local government
entities. While such aggregate historical and future losses are
not overwhelming, such reductions in federal aid are certainly
not helpful in the building and maintaining of the nation's in-
frastructure. Moreover, a full reduction of federal bond subsi-
dies, as was threatened at the beginning of 2023, would be much
more detrimental to state and local governments based on our
estimates.
The loss estimates in this study affirm key concerns in the fis-
cal federalism literature about the downstream effects that can
result from increased centralization. The structure of the BAB
program, reliant on federal funding while municipalities hold
spending authority, clearly exemplifies a vertical fiscal imbalance
that can undermine local fiscal autonomy and sustainability. The
losses municipalities incurred due to federal budget sequestra-
tion provide a clear reminder: relying on federal subsidies makes
local governments volatile to any federal budget dysfunction.
Sequestration, an external shock outside of state and local con-
trol, has induced BAB subsidy losses for issuers across all 50
states between 2013 and 2023, and the losses are only expected
to grow by 2030. Municipalities increased their dependence on
the federal government for infrastructure funding but were left
to deal with the consequences of sequestration on their own, with
already limited resources. These losses are significant when the
extant infrastructure gap and fiscal strain municipal govern-
ments are already facing are taken into consideration.
Through a deeper-­dive analysis, we also explored the impacts on
two of the most active users of federal direct subsidy bonds, the
State of Texas and Chicago. The experiences of Texas issuers and
the City of Chicago reinforce the risks of federalization; all types
and sizes of issuers became susceptible to unexpected disrup-
tions in the federal budget process. Municipalities in both cases
relied on the federal government for expected lower-­cost infra-
structure financing but were left to deal with the consequences
of sequestration on their own, with already limited resources.
In the case of Texas, our estimates showed the diversity of gov-
ernments and bond types impacted by the reduction of federal
bond subsidies in one of the largest US states. The Chicago expe-
rience illustrated both the explicit cost of a direct bond subsidy
program to a large local government from being exposed to the
federal budget process but also elucidated some of the “hidden”
challenges/costs in managing such a program relative to simply
issuing traditional tax-­exempt bonds. In our view, such adminis-
trative challenges have not been discussed enough in the assess-
ment of federal direct subsidy bonds.
Given our cost estimates and case studies analyzing the impact
of federal budget sequester, it is unsurprising that many mu-
nicipal issuers have sought to shed their BABs in recent years.
Nevertheless, there are members of Congress and some public
policy organizations that still would like to see a reboot of the
BAB program (Lorenzo  2021). In the context of the need for
greater financing tools to fund our nation's infrastructure, these
advocates tout the efficiency, interest cost, and equity benefits
of a direct subsidy program relative to traditional tax-­exempt
bonds. Given greater investor acceptance in the last decade, they
believe that a future BAB program would likely see bonds being
sold with par instead of make-­whole calls. They also advocate for
any new BAB program to include protections of bond subsidies
from future budget sequestration. Whether it is possible to bind
future Congresses with such iron-­clad guarantees is unclear.
Regardless of whether issuers are successful in refinancing their
BABs or receiving refunds on the previous sequestrations, or
whether Congress creates a new BAB program, the estimates
and experiences explored in this paper are certainly relevant
in an emerging area of federal financial policymaking. As
Marlowe (2022) describes, President Biden's signature Inflation
Reduction Act (IRA) provides direct pay “tax credits” to non-­
federal taxpayers such as state and local government entities.
Just like direct pay bond subsidies, the IRA essentially allows
the federal government to write checks to state and local govern-
ments to subsidize energy-­related infrastructure projects. In this

sense, IRA payments look very similar to federal BAB subsidies.
The future of the IRA and these direct subsidies is unclear given
the new Trump administration's policy priorities. Nevertheless,
the experiences described in this paper offer some lessons for
federal, state, and local policymakers as they implement the IRA
or other direct subsidy programs.
The concerns addressed here, especially regarding distorted in-
centive structures and downstream effects of federal budget dys-
function, may not have been sufficiently considered in state and
local governments' decisions to use BABs. Policymakers should
pay careful attention to how federal payments to state and local
governments may again shift the balance of fiscal authority and
responsibility as the IRA or other federal subsidy programs are
implemented. Like implementation in all policy areas, it is often
an iterative process in which all levels of government learn from
past successes and failures. Let us hope that is the case with the
BAB and any other future direct subsidy programs. If not, mu-
nicipal issuers would be wise to heed Richie Aprile's warning on
“light envelopes.”

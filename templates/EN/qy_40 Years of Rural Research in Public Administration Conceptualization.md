ABSTRACT
This manuscript systematically reviews 221 peer-­reviewed studies on rural public sector organizations published in 15 leading
public administration journals between 1980 and 2022. It addresses three core questions: (1) How has rurality been conceptual-
ized and measured? (2) What outcomes are attributed to rurality? and (3) Where is further research most needed? Key trends in
publication patterns, geographic focus, citation networks, research design, and measurement strategies are analyzed. Findings
reveal growing academic interest and increasingly sophisticated approaches to defining and studying rurality. However, the
literature remains fragmented, often relying on inconsistent definitions and underrepresenting non-­US contexts. For both schol-
ars and practitioners, the review highlights the need for clearer conceptual frameworks and more consistent measures to better
understand how rural settings shape governance, service delivery, and administrative capacity. Improved conceptual clarity and
global inclusion are critical for building a more cohesive, policy-­relevant research agenda for rural public administration.
In recent years, scholars have criticized the marginalization of
rural issues within public administration, describing the field as
one of the “least researched” and most “neglected” areas of in-
quiry (Hall 2022; Helpap 2019). Gabehart (2023) extends this cri-
tique, arguing that rurality is often examined only superficially,
frequently reduced to a binary opposite of urbanism in quantita-
tive models. In response, this review systematically assesses the
rural public administration literature to evaluate the validity of
these concerns, synthesize existing rural administrative knowl-
edge, and identify persistent gaps in the field (Cooper 2017).
Recent attention to rural issues has been fueled by a range of
developments, including divergent political behavior in rural
regions (Cramer 2016; Lunz Trujillo and Lin 2025), disparities
in COVID-­19 outcomes (Mueller et al. 2021), and evidence of
structural disadvantages such as limited broadband access,
weak local government capacity, and under-­provision of pub-
lic services (Lobao and Kelly 2019; Jensen 2024, 2025). While
rural areas, in the aggregate, often face more adverse conditions
than urban ones (Accordino 2019), the treatment of rurality as
a deficit-­based or monolithic category obscures internal het-
erogeneity and positive variation. Research shows that rural
regions are not uniformly disadvantaged and that urban–rural
boundaries are often fluid and better understood as a continuum
than a dichotomy (Dabson 2019; Lichter and Ziliak 2017). These
conceptual complexities underscore the need for more rigorous
and consistent approaches to defining and measuring rurality in
public administration research.
Growing awareness of rural challenges has prompted re-
newed academic and policy interest, especially as crises such
as the opioid epidemic and heightened political polarization
since 2016 have drawn attention to rural disparities. Yet, in-
consistencies in how rurality is operationalized continue to
affect the reliability and comparability of findings (Lichter
and Brown  2011; Lichter and Johnson  2023). For instance,

empirical research. Additionally, rural public administration
research has long underscored the distinct challenges rural
governments face and the importance of tailoring policy and
management strategies to their unique contexts. Early work,
such as Cigler (1993), highlighted how reduced federal fund-
ing, economic shifts, and structural constraints like low pop-
ulation density and reliance on part-­time officials limited the
capacity of rural governments to meet growing responsibili-
ties. Four decades later, recent studies continue to document
these persistent capacity and revenue limitations, while also
drawing attention to emerging issues such as broadband ac-
cess, affordable housing, and substance abuse (Helpap 2023).
Together, this scholarship demonstrates both the enduring
and evolving nature of the difficulties confronting rural local
governments, reinforcing the need for continued research
that not only identifies challenges but also explores strat-
egies, including partnerships, resource development, and
capacity-­building, that can strengthen governance in rural
communities.
The trajectory of rural-­focused research in public administra-
tion reveals both progress and persistent gaps. The 1980 Public
Administration Review special issue on rural governance marked
an early effort to draw attention to rural contexts, though at the
time the field lacked conceptual clarity and often conflated ru-
rality with small or under-­resourced governments. Through the
1990s, rural research appeared only sporadically in top public
administration journals, and over the past two decades, these
outlets have largely neglected rural public administration.
Exceptions include State and Local Government Review and a
few other journals with a natural focus on local governance,
which have provided some continuity for rural scholarship.
Despite these gaps, recent studies demonstrate measurable prog-
ress: most now distinguish rurality from related concepts, cap-
ture variation within rural contexts, and explore mechanisms
beyond resource limitations, including local culture, historical
trajectories, and civic norms. Nevertheless, the field remains
predominantly US-­focused, with limited attention to global
rural governance, indicating both the strides made and the need
for further research.
Despite these insights, the literature remains fragmented (see
Figure 4), with relatively few studies building on one another
or systematically testing common variables (see Table 6). This
gap means that local governments often must make decisions
without a solid evidence base, risking inefficient resource al-
location and suboptimal policy outcomes (e.g., Partridge and
Rickman 2006). Moreover, as rural contexts continue to change
demographically, economically, and technologically, older find-
ings may no longer fully capture current realities. Expanding
rural-­focused research can address these gaps, providing both
practical guidance for policymakers and administrators and op-
portunities to develop cumulative knowledge that strengthens
governance across diverse rural communities.
This review has two primary aims. First, it assesses how ru-
rality has been conceptualized and measured in public ad-
ministration scholarship from 1980 to 2022. Although rural
public sector organizations have long been of interest (e.g.,
Selznick  1949), the literature is fragmented and lacks con-
sistent definitions (Seroka 1989). This problem of rural defi-
nition is not unique to public administration, but the field
notably lacks a systematic review or meta-­analysis of rural
research (Bennett et al. 2019; De Marco and De Marco 2010;
Hart et  al.  2005; Halfacree  1993; Long et  al.  2021; Nelson
et  al.  2021; Nemerever and Rogers  2021). As public admin-
istration aspires to build cumulative knowledge, conceptual
clarity on rurality is essential.
Second, this review identifies outcome areas linked to rural
contexts. By systematically cataloging how rurality is asso-
ciated with administrative challenges or performance, this
review provides evidence for practitioners and identifies re-
search needs. While much literature focuses on rural decline
(Hall 2022), this review seeks to clarify where and how those
challenges manifest and what responses might be effective.
Like Cucciniello et al. (2017), this review also aims to contrib-
ute to a more coherent rural public administration research
agenda.
Methodologically, the study employs the Preferred Reporting
Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-­Analyses (PRISMA)
approach to analyze 221 academic articles. Findings suggest
significant conceptual fragmentation in how rurality is de-
fined and measured. However, the past decade has seen the
most growth in rural-­focused research and a shift toward more
representative, empirical methods, moving beyond case study–
driven work.

1   |   Methodology
This review employs a systematic approach to evaluate the state
of rural public administration scholarship from 1980 to 2022. As
noted in the Introduction, concerns about the field's conceptual
inconsistency and empirical neglect necessitate a transparent
and replicable method for assessing what is known and where
gaps remain. Systematic reviews offer a rigorous framework
to collect, screen, and synthesize relevant literature through
clearly defined research questions, inclusion criteria, and coding
strategies (Cooper 2017).
The guiding research question for this review is: What is known
about rural public administration, and how has scholarship in
this area developed over time? To address this question, a sys-
tematic search was conducted using three academic databases:
ISI Web of Science, JSTOR, and Public Administration Abstracts,
along with keyword searches on the websites of 15 leading pub-
lic administration and management journals.1 The search terms
included “rural,” “nonmetropolitan,” and “non-­metropolitan.”
Books and grey literature were excluded to ensure consistency
with established standards for high-­quality, peer-­reviewed
scholarship similar to other systematic literature reviews in pub-
lic administration (Ritz et al. 2016). In total, 1885 records were
identified and screened using the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting
Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-­Analyses) process (see
Figure 1). The final search was completed in January 2024.
Publications were included if they were published between
1980 and 2022. The review period begins in 1980, the year
Public Administration Review (PAR) released a special issue on
rural administration, signaling the field's early interest in the
topic (Zody  1980). However, even this issue revealed concep-
tual ambiguity. Of the eight featured articles, only four explic-
itly addressed rural areas (Brown 1980; Fetter and Stott 1980;
Giles et al. 1980; Zody 1980), while others examined small or
low-­capacity governments without clearly distinguishing rural
contexts. The issue also largely focused on US cases and lacked
a coherent conceptualization of rurality, conflating it at times
with poverty, isolation, or scale.
Despite these limitations, the 1980 PAR issue helped foreground
rural issues within the discipline. Subsequent decades saw a lim-
ited but notable expansion of rural research. Journals published
additional special issues (Slack 1990), and scholars contributed
both empirical and theoretical work (Agranoff 1998; McGuire
et al. 1994; Ostrom et al. 1993; Radin 1996). Nonetheless, recent
commentary has highlighted the need for renewed attention
to rural administration, both to better understand US-­specific
developments and to improve global conceptual inclusivity
(Accordino 2019; Hall 2022; Gabehart 2023; Helpap 2019, 2023).
Calls for greater conceptual precision have become increasingly
urgent. Scholars have critiqued the use of “rural” as a catch-­all
term for small, poor, or peripheral jurisdictions and have urged
public administration researchers to move beyond simplistic cat-
egorizations, such as binary dummy variables (Gabehart 2023).
A systematic review is especially valuable at this time to clarify
how rurality has been defined, operationalized, and linked to
administrative outcomes.

passing initial screening were coded based on key characteristics:

1.1   |   Coding and Eligibility Criteria
Eligibility criteria were designed to ensure a focused and repli-
cable review of rural public administration scholarship. Records
were included if their title or abstract referenced “rural,” “non-
metropolitan,” or “non-­metropolitan,” and if the full text men-
tioned these terms more than once (excluding references). Only
peer-­reviewed journal articles published in English between
1980 and 2022 in 15 leading public administration journals were
considered. Book reviews and brief commentaries were excluded
unless they offered comparative analysis or original theoretical
insight. Each eligible article was coded by research methodol-
ogy (theoretical, case study, literature review, quantitative, qual-
itative, or ethnographic). Case studies were distinguished from
other empirical work based on the author's intent to generalize
findings beyond the cases examined. Of the 1885 records ini-
tially identified, 540 duplicates and 992 ineligible articles were
removed based on journal, date, or keyword exclusions. Full-­
text screening of the remaining 353 articles resulted in 131 addi-
tional exclusions, primarily for lacking substantive discussion of
rurality (i.e., mentioning a rurality-­related term more than once
in the manuscript) or being under one page in length. The final
dataset includes 221 articles.
To ensure inter-­coder reliability, a graduate assistant inde-
pendently screened and coded a random subsample of 100 ar-
ticles using the same criteria. Interrater reliability was assessed
using Cohen's Kappa (κ = 0.73), indicating substantial agree-
ment (Landis and Koch 1977), with minimal bias and moderate
prevalence effects. Each article was then coded for key vari-
ables, including geographic focus, conceptualization of rurality,
and methodological approach. Articles were also assessed for
whether they compared rural areas to urban/metropolitan con-
texts or to other rural settings. The coding strategy aims to illu-
minate trends in how rurality is defined, measured, and studied,
and to identify whether the field is sufficiently diverse in meth-
odological approaches to support theory-­building and triangu-
lation (Hendren et al. 2018). It also helps uncover whether rural
public administration research is culturally bounded or analyt-
ically fragmented.
2   |   Findings
From the 1885 journal records initially collected across 15 top
public administration and management journals, 221 articles
met the inclusion criteria. Of the 221 articles included in this
systematic review, over 58% of the studies focused on the United
States, and nearly 75% of the non-­US-­focused work appeared
in just four journals. This suggests a limited geographic scope
and raises concerns that rural public administration scholarship
may be culturally or regionally bound. The data used to create
Figure  2 reveals that 39 countries and 7 multicountry cases
were studied, but the United States alone accounted for 58% of
the research. Rural Africa and South America remain signifi-
cantly underrepresented. For comparison, US-­authored studies
comprise only 27.5% of the work in public service motivation
research (Ritz et al. 2016). This geographic imbalance reveals
a need to diversify the contexts examined in rural public admin-
istration scholarship (see also: Table S2).
Table 1 shows that although 14 of the 15 journals published rural
research between 1980 and 2022, four journals: Voluntas, State
and Local Government Review (SLGR), Nonprofit and Voluntary
Sector Quarterly (NVSQ), and International Journal of Public
Administration (IJPA) accounted for 56% of all rural-­focused
publications over the last decade. Only Perspectives on Public
Management and Governance (PPMG) lacked rural-­focused ar-
ticles, in part due to its recent launch in 2018. Temporal trends
indicate renewed interest: the last decade produced the most
rural-­related publications since the 1980s. This could reflect
either increased scholarly attention or an overall rise in jour-
nal output. Table 2 shows that three of these journals (PPMG,
ARPA, and SLGR) did not publish internationally focused rural
research. Among those that did publish rural public adminis-
tration research, just four journals accounted for 74% of non-­US
publications, suggesting limited venue diversity. Despite this
concentration, global research on rurality has increased signifi-
cantly since 2010 (Table 2).
Methodologically, rural research is increasingly representative
and empirical (Table 3). The proportion of articles using gener-
alizable empirical methods rose from 59% in the 1980s to 82% in
the 2010s. Meanwhile, reliance on case studies has declined, and
only one study employed ethnography. While this shift aligns
with broader trends in the field (Pitts and Fernandez 2009), it
highlights a simultaneous decline in theoretical contributions,
underscoring the need for further conceptual development.

need for clearer differentiation and more consistent concep-

risks modifiable areal unit problems (MAUPs), in which the
geographic level of analysis (i.e., how a geography is defined)
affects outcomes. This was evident in conflicting findings by
Kraus (2012) and McFarland (2019): one questioned the effec-
tiveness of intergovernmental collaboration based on limited
participation; the other highlighted the benefits of regional
connectivity in rural economies. In both cases, unclear or in-
consistent definitions of rurality hindered interpretability and
comparison.
To assess the conceptual generalizability of rurality, this review
also examined whether studies compared rural areas to urban/
metropolitan counterparts or conducted within-­rural com-
parisons. Most studies engaged in urban–rural comparisons,
suggesting a tendency to define rurality in opposition to urban
norms rather than as a unique context in its own right. This an-
alytical habit may obscure variation within rural settings and
limit the development of rural-­specific theory. In sum, while ru-
rality is an increasingly salient concept in public administration,
it remains inconsistently defined and under-­theorized. Greater
conceptual clarity, attention to intra-­rural variation, and cross-­
national empirical work are needed to advance the field.
2.2   |   Measurement
Rurality in public administration is most often measured
through categorical distinctions between metropolitan and
non-­metropolitan areas (e.g., Gimpel and Karnes  2006).
The dominant approach dichotomizes or trichotomizes
areas using national classifications (e.g., “metro,” “metro-­
adjacent,” and “non-­metro”), a strategy similar to Torgerson
and Edwards (2013). While this method is simple and widely
used in statistical modeling, it homogenizes rural contexts
and fails to capture within-­rural variation. Moreover, national
definitions vary significantly, limiting cross-­national com-
parability. None of the 221 articles in this review employed
internationally harmonized systems such as functional urban
areas (FUAs) or the OECD's rural typology, further limiting
external validity. This strategy also poses risks in longitudi-
nal studies. Rural areas absorbed into expanding metropoli-
tan zones are reclassified, introducing confounder bias and
obscuring developmental trajectories. Over time, this demo-
graphic “winnowing” may selectively remove higher-­growth
rural areas from analysis (Lichter and Brown  2011), leav-
ing behind a more disadvantaged baseline group. Other fre-
quently used measurement strategies include US census-­based
schemes such as the Rural–Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC)
and Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes, which in-
troduce gradations of rurality based on population density,
urbanization, and commuting patterns. While more nuanced,
these rely on subnational geographies (e.g., counties, tracts)
and are susceptible to MAUPs. Few authors explicitly justify
their choice of geography or measurement system based on
theoretical alignment.
Empirical studies also use simple proxies such as population
size, density, or distance from urban centers (e.g., Alm and
Witt 1995; Propheter 2019). Population thresholds vary widely,
for example, 10,000 (Propheter 2019) versus 50,000 (Reed and
Blair 1993), without clear justification. This variability reflects
the absence of a uniform definition, making transparency in
operationalization essential. Theoretical justification and sen-
sitivity checks are rarely provided but would greatly improve
interpretability and replication. Several studies incorporate ru-
rality as a social construct. These measures emphasize norms,
values, and civic identity (e.g., reciprocity, local traditions, or
community narratives) and typically rely on archival, qual-
itative, or ethnographic methods (Lee and Blackford  2020;
Paarlberg et al. 2022). Though more resource-­intensive, these
methods offer thick description and are particularly useful for
uncovering rural-­attributed causal mechanisms.
Despite the range of strategies, at least a dozen articles failed to
define or justify their rural measures. In some cases, rurality
was included only as a control variable and left undefined (e.g.,
Snow and Williamson 2015). Others offered vague descriptions,
such as calling Kentucky “relatively rural” without clarifying the
source or basis for this claim (Kukla-­Acevedo et al. 2012). These
omissions hinder comparability and limit the cumulative value
of findings. Ultimately, a clearer connection between theory
and measurement is needed. Researchers must attend not only
to how rurality differs from urban or metropolitan areas but also
to how rurality itself varies across time and place. Conceptual
clarity, transparency in operationalization, and justification of
measurement strategy are essential to advancing rural public
administration research.
A key step in advancing rural public administration research
is greater clarity and consistency in how rurality is measured.

many rural communities also lack a robust network of prox-

for intergovernmental collaboration. Regional partnerships
(McFarland  2019; Rickabaugh  2021) and alternative service

effectiveness of contracting (Bel and Fageda  2011; Mohr
et al. 2010).
Second, cultural and social distinctions between rural and
urban communities are frequently cited in the literature,
meaning differences between rural and urban areas extend
beyond institutional capacity. Rural communities exhibit dis-
tinct civic cultures, including higher levels of volunteering
(Paarlberg et al. 2022), stronger community identity (Lee and
Blackford 2020), and locally embedded norms (Arsneault 2006).
These cultural traits can shape public expectations, administra-
tive behavior, and policy implementation, and underscore the
importance of considering community history (Painter  2022;
Uden 2016).
Third, research highlights the value of leveraging rural assets.
Despite facing complex policy challenges, rural areas may bene-
fit from uniquely rural strengths. Rural identity, sense of place,
and social cohesion can be mobilized to foster collaboration and
enhance service outcomes (Lee and Blackford 2020; Uden 2016).
Cross-­boundary coordination and partnerships grounded in
shared social norms can generate access to resources and knowl-
edge networks (McFarland 2019).

structural and contextual variation across rural communities.
Differences in broadband access, transportation networks, and
public service infrastructure (Chicoine et al. 1989), along with
divergent policy environments and local leadership capacities
(Creek and Karnes 2010; Hartley et al. 2019), all contribute to
performance outcomes. These findings emphasize that rural-
ity is not monolithic but a context-­dependent construct that
interacts with institutional, cultural, and geographic factors.
This indicates that rurality, while context-­dependent, pro-
vides meaningful explanatory power across diverse admin-
istrative settings. Together, these themes demonstrate that
rurality is not only a descriptive category but also a concept
with practical relevance for understanding public administra-
tion outcomes.
3   |   Discussion and Future Research
This systematic review reveals an expanding body of research on
rural public administration, both in volume and empirical rigor.
This renewed scholarly attention appears to be linked to grow-
ing concerns over urban–rural disparities in governance, polit-
ical expression, service provision, and economic outcomes. For
example, the growing political divide between urban and rural
communities, especially visible in voting patterns and perceptions
of democratic legitimacy, has become a focus of recent inquiry
(Cramer 2016; Mettler and Brown 2022). Although much of this
attention originated in political science and related disciplines, its
influence has extended into public administration, with scholars
investigating rural public health administration (Bagchi 2019),
local governance (D. J. Helpap 2019), and election management
(Rinfret and Barsky 2020). The findings of this review further
suggest that rural jurisdictions able to harness collaborative net-
works, leverage rural cultural identities, and capitalize on local
amenities tend to achieve more favorable administrative out-
comes. Taken together, these insights highlight several practical
implications. First, they point to the importance of strengthening
cross-­sector partnerships, particularly in health, emergency man-
agement, and service delivery, where rural resource constraints
can be mitigated by regional or interlocal collaboration. Second,
they suggest that administrators and policymakers should rec-
ognize and incorporate local cultural identities and civic norms
when designing and implementing programs, rather than relying
solely on models developed in urban contexts. Finally, the evi-
dence underscores the need for practitioners to frame rural assets
(e.g., natural amenities, civic traditions, and local networks) not
as deficits but as strategic resources that can support governance
innovation and policy success. By integrating these lessons, rural
administrators and policymakers can better navigate structural
challenges while enhancing both the effectiveness and legiti-
macy of rural governance. However, the review also highlights
enduring fragmentation in the literature, particularly regarding
definitions and measurements of rurality. This lack of cohesion
limits theoretical development and complicates the translation of
research into practice. Together, these trends suggest three criti-
cal directions for future scholarship: refining conceptual clarity,
articulating and investigating causal mechanisms, and expand-
ing research beyond the US context.
Although there is growing interest in rural governance, the lack
of conceptual integration remains a major barrier. The field is
characterized by a wide range of definitions and operationaliza-
tions of rurality, but few studies clearly justify their choices or
engage prior work. Citation network visualization (see Figure 4)
reveals that many rural-­focused articles operate in isolation,
drawing little on previously published rural research. Without
more consistent and theoretically informed definitions, rural
public administration risks falling into a cycle of conceptual
reinvention that stifles cumulative knowledge building. Clearly
articulated, context-­sensitive definitions, informed by theory
and supported by citations to prior work, are essential for a more
cohesive and impactful research agenda.
The diversity of topics examined reflects the broad scope of
rural public administration scholarship (see Table S1). However,
relatively few variables appear consistently across studies

Beyond clarifying definitions, scholars must also specify why
rurality matters to their research design. Rurality is a multi-
dimensional concept encompassing features such as organi-
zational and population density, physical and social isolation,
infrastructure deficits, and cultural identity. These dimensions
will not be relevant to every research question, so a contingency-­
based approach is needed. Researchers should identify the
specific facets of rurality that are germane to their study and
explain how these characteristics plausibly affect administrative
outcomes. This will support more nuanced theory development
and aid practitioners in identifying which rural factors matter
most in their contexts.
Additionally, more work is needed on rural governance outside
of the United States. While non-­US rural research has increased
since 2010, over 70% of those articles appeared in just four of
the 15 journals included in this review. Nearly 60% of all rural
public administration studies still focus on the United States,
threatening to culturally bind the literature and constrain its
global applicability. Editors and researchers should encourage
broader geographic representation, not only to enhance external
validity but also to capture the diverse institutional and political
environments in which rural governance occurs.
Many rural governments face persistent fiscal and workforce
constraints, yet they also serve populations with disproportion-
ate needs in areas such as health, infrastructure, and emergency
management. Moving forward, scholars could ask how rural ad-
ministrators continue to adapt to conditions of limited resources
or capacity through technology adoption, cross-­sector or re-
gional partnerships, or innovative practices, and whether these
adaptations generate or mitigate inequities across communities.
Examining these dynamics with field experiments, comparative
case studies, and administrative data on service performance
would shed light on the conditions under which shifts to the sta-
tus quo of rural governance enhance, rather than undermine,
administrative performance in rural areas.
Looking back, the 1980 Public Administration Review special
issue on rural governance serves as an instructive benchmark.
At that time, the field lacked conceptual clarity, and rural was
often conflated with small or under-­resourced governments.
This review finds measurable progress: most recent studies dis-
tinguish rurality from related but distinct concepts and avoid
equating it solely with resource scarcity. Moreover, a growing
share of studies now incorporates dimensions of within-­rural
variation, an improvement over the earlier reliance on binary
urban–rural classifications. Still, the field continues to face
challenges similar to those noted over 40 years ago. As in the
1980 special issue, most rural research remains US-­centric,
and only limited space has been given to global rural gover-
nance contexts. Nonetheless, there has been some progress in
explicating the mechanisms through which rurality affects
outcomes. While early work emphasized resource limitations,
contemporary research increasingly recognizes the influence of
local culture, historical trajectories, and civic norms.
Collectively, these findings suggest that the field is evolving
in meaningful ways, yet continued refinement is needed. The
future of rural public administration research will depend on
stronger theoretical foundations, greater international engage-
ment, and a clearer articulation of the causal relationships that
underpin observed rural–urban disparities. By addressing these
challenges, scholars can help develop a more coherent, inclusive,
and practically relevant body of knowledge on rural governance.
4   |   Conclusion
Whereas the preceding section synthesizes what the literature
has established about rural public administration, this final sec-
tion focuses explicitly on unresolved questions and directions
for future research. This systematic review sought to assess
the current state of rural public administration research and
identify directions for its continued development. While early
characterizations described rural public administration as un-
derstudied (Zody 1980; Gabehart 2023; Hall 2022), this review
finds that publication activity has increased significantly over
the past 12 years. However, greater volume has not yet translated
into a cohesive research agenda. Rurality remains a broad and
inconsistently defined concept, with limited theoretical integra-
tion across studies and little cumulative refinement. As a result,
rural research in public administration continues to suffer from
conceptual fragmentation. Despite these challenges, the find-
ings underscore rurality's relevance to a wide range of adminis-
trative issues from service provision and capacity constraints to
identity, governance structure, and interorganizational collabo-
ration. To advance the field, this review identifies three priori-
ties for future research.
Improving the conceptualization and measurement of rurality is
an essential step for advancing the field. Future research should
offer clear definitions, connect those definitions to prior work,
and justify measurement strategies within well-­articulated
theoretical frameworks. Too often, rurality is treated as a self-­
evident variable, which limits the accumulation of generalizable
knowledge and impedes theoretical progress. Equally import-
ant is a more nuanced, contingency-­based approach. Given the
multidimensional nature of rurality, encompassing geogra-
phy, demography, culture, location, and infrastructure, not all
dimensions are relevant to every research question. Scholars
should identify which aspects are most salient in their studies
and articulate the mechanisms by which these characteristics
influence governance outcomes.
Another major concern is the literature's geographic concen-
tration. Rural public administration research remains heavily
US-­focused. More than 70% of non-­US studies were published
in just 4 of the 15 journals reviewed. This concentration limits
the global applicability of rural public administration knowl-
edge and calls for more inclusive research that reflects diverse
institutional and geographic contexts. This narrow scope risks
producing culturally bounded knowledge that lacks interna-
tional applicability. Expanding the field's geographic and in-
stitutional diversity will be key to developing a more globally

relevant research agenda. Public administration journals can
play a critical role by encouraging submissions that examine
rural contexts comparatively or across underrepresented re-

port this effort by offering rural-­focused submission tracks,
dedicated subgroups or sections, keynote presentations, and
symposiums that highlight rural governance challenges and
innovations. Instructors also contribute by incorporating rural
examples, case studies, and assignments into their courses,
fostering early exposure to diverse rural contexts among stu-
dents. Scholars themselves can advance geographic and insti-
tutional diversity through collaborative projects across regions
and countries, sharing data and methodologies, and mentoring
early-­career researchers to engage with rural-­focused studies.
Collectively, these strategies can help build a more integrated,
globally informed, and policy-­relevant body of rural public ad-
ministration research.
Looking ahead, additional directions are also worth exploring.
Greater clarity is needed around which rural measures best pre-
dict public management outcomes. Research must also move
beyond simply acknowledging the importance of rurality to
empirically identifying which of its dimensions drive variation.
Meanwhile, revisiting case study methods and within-­rural
comparisons can offer deeper insights into successful practices
and local innovations. Finally, while large-­N studies dominate
the field, rich qualitative research remains crucial for uncover-
ing context-­specific mechanisms that broader data trends may
obscure.
In conclusion, while rural public administration research is ex-
panding, its theoretical and methodological foundations must be
strengthened. By refining definitions, clarifying mechanisms,
and broadening the global lens, scholars can ensure that future
work offers actionable insights for both academics and practi-
tioners. Finally, the aim of future rural research should not be to
replicate existing interdisciplinary findings but to adapt and ex-
tend them within the frameworks of public administration. By
adapting insights from other disciplines, public administration
scholarship can bridge existing gaps and bring rural governance
into the core of the field's debates.

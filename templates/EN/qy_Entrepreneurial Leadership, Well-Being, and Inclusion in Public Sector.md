ABSTRACT
Entrepreneurship is increasingly promoted as a way to make public sector organizations (PSOs) more effective. However, there
is little evidence on how it impacts the working lives of public employees. Therefore, this study investigates whether entrepre-
neurial leaders in PSOs enhance organizational effectiveness while promoting employee inclusion and well-­being. Based on a
large survey of Australian Government employees (n = 127,436), we found that entrepreneurial leaders significantly increase
effectiveness and promote inclusion and well-­being. Furthermore, by comparing the various components of entrepreneurial lead-
ership, we found that factors associated with entrepreneurship and general leadership both separately influence PSOs. However,
while entrepreneurship factors have a stronger impact on organizational effectiveness and the promotion of well-­being, the more
generic leadership factors are more strongly associated with inclusion promotion. Amid increasing demands on PSOs, this study
highlights the value of training leaders in entrepreneurial and good leadership practices to improve organizational performance
and employee support.
1   |   Introduction
Public sector organizations (PSOs) are increasingly challenged
by financial austerity, complex socio-­economic demands, and
heightened citizen expectations (Stanton and Oliver  2025;
Bagirova et al. 2025; Seidemann et al. 2025). These pressures
have strained service delivery capacities and placed consider-
able psychological and organizational burdens on public em-
ployees (Cinar et  al. 2021; Raadschelders 2011). As a result,
mental health concerns have intensified, and staff turnover has
increased, undermining workforce stability and organizational
performance (Ravalier et al. 2023; Strandell 2020; van der Meer
et  al.  2022). Furthermore, in diverse democratic societies, re-
search shows that a lack of inclusion worsens the effectiveness
of PSOs and increases organizational challenges, such as stress
and burnout (Barboza-­Wilkes et al. 2023; Olumekor et al. 2024).
To address these complex issues, PSOs are promoting entrepre-
neurial leadership (EL) traits to inspire effective and creative
solutions among employees and stakeholders (Australian Public
Service Commission 2021b).
Therefore, due to the growing importance of public sector
entrepreneurship (PSE) in PSOs, academic interest has also
increased. However, a majority of prior PSE studies have an-
alyzed the antecedents of entrepreneurial behavior rather
than its impact on organizational performance. These stud-
ies revealed that leadership behavior can influence the entre-
preneurial attitude of public sector employees (Demircioglu
and Chowdhury 2021; Vivona and Lewis 2023) and enhance
their alertness to new opportunities (Arnold 2019). More re-
cently, public sector EL has been linked to organizational ef-
fectiveness, workplace democracy, and innovation (Kousina

Research indicates that creating an inclusive working envi-

clusion by arguing that ideas are just as important as socio-

Simply, hiring individuals from diverse backgrounds, includ-
ing women or people with disabilities for mid-­level and senior
positions, is insufficient if their views are not valued within
the organization (Jana and Diaz Mejias  2018). In addition,
since public agencies are funded by and responsible to peo-
ple of all backgrounds, creating inclusive environments that
harness diverse lived experiences and ideas can enhance orga-
nizational effectiveness and reduce the risks and costs associ-
ated with policy failures (Olumekor et al. 2024). Furthermore,
promoting well-­being can improve both the quality of life of
employees and the performance of organizations (Bellingan
et al. 2024).
Using a large dataset from the Australian Public Service
Commission (2023) (n = 127,436), this article examines the in-
terdependent relationship between EL, well-­being promotion,
inclusion promotion, and organizational effectiveness. More
specifically, this study aims to answer the following two key
research questions. First, how does EL influence the effec-
tiveness of PSOs? Second, to what extent does the promotion
of well-­being and inclusion in PSOs mediate this relation-
ship? To answer these two research questions, we adapt the
measurement scale of EL developed and validated by Renko
et al. (2015) to a public sector setting. While this scale cap-
tures distinct EL traits, it also includes dimensions that over-
lap with other leadership styles, such as transformational and
adaptive leadership. Consequently, a further objective of this
research is to provide a more nuanced understanding of the
role of EL by comparing the influence of factors closer to en-
trepreneurship with factors related to general leadership. We
specifically seek to examine how entrepreneurial dimensions
and general leadership dimensions separately influence orga-
nizational effectiveness and the promotion of well-­being and
inclusion in PSOs. The following sections outline the theoreti-
cal background and hypotheses, followed by the methodology,
results, discussion, and conclusions.
2   |   Theoretical Background
2.1   |   EL: Perspectives and Measurements
Public administration research increasingly highlights en-
trepreneurship as a key driver of improved efficiency, effec-
tiveness, and service quality in the public sector (van der
Meer et al. 2024; Hayter et al. 2018; Bernier and Hafsi 2007).
Schumpeter's  (1939) foundational work positioned entrepre-
neurs as people engaged in the pursuit of innovative ideas and
the creation of new opportunities. Building on this early in-
sight, pioneering studies on PSE have similarly emphasized
innovation as a defining characteristic of the public sector en-
trepreneur (Roberts 1992). Inspired by these studies, and the
definitions proposed in recent literature (Olumekor 2024), we
define the public sector entrepreneur as an individual engaged
in generating and pursuing innovative ideas under uncertain
conditions to either self-­improve, improve the internal working
processes of public institutions, or improve the delivery of public
services.
Closely related to this, the study of EL has recently gained
prominence as an important research topic as scholars and
practitioners seek to understand how leadership behaviors
drive innovation and opportunity creation in organizations,
including public organizations (Kousina et  al.  2024). EL
combines concepts of organizational leadership, which in-
volves influencing followers to achieve organizational goals
(Yukl  2008), and elements of entrepreneurship, which em-
phasize innovation and proactiveness to take advantage of
opportunities (Schumpeter  1934, 1939). Accordingly, entre-
preneurial leaders are often viewed as innovative leaders who
inspire innovation and the proactiveness to recognize and ex-
ploit opportunities among followers (Leitch and Volery 2017;
Renko et al. 2015; Miao et al. 2018). Therefore, entrepreneur-
ial leaders serve as both enablers and practitioners of entrepre-
neurship (Newman et al. 2018).
Within public administration, there was a shift from the tradi-
tional style of leadership, that emphasized hierarchy, structure,
stability, and efficiency, to transformational leadership, which
promoted relationships, empowerment and managing change
(Moynihan et al. 2012; Nguyen et al. 2022). However, in recent
years, organizations have begun to emphasize an EL style that
prioritizes autonomy, adaptability, proactiveness, innovative-
ness, and risk-­taking (Kousina et al. 2024; Vivona 2024). In this
sense, a first “unified” or aggregated perspective on entrepre-
neurial leadership (i.e., an “EL” perspective) has claimed that EL
reflects a distinct construct that represents more than the sum of
its parts. Moreover, scholars treating EL as a unified construct

Despite this growing consensus around EL as an integrated
construct, some scholars have questioned whether its concep-
tual boundaries are sufficiently distinct from related constructs
such as transformational leadership (see e.g., Ravet-­Brown
et al. 2024). From this “disaggregated” perspective on entre-
preneurial leadership (i.e., an “E + L” perspective), EL may be
understood not as a singular style, but as the co-­occurrence
of two distinct behavioral domains: general leadership (L)
and entrepreneurial behavior (E), which are often, but not al-
ways, integrated. This disaggregated perspective stems partly
from concerns about construct overlaps in the leadership lit-
erature. As Anderson and Sun (2017) noted, many contempo-
rary leadership constructs (e.g., transformational, adaptive,
and EL) share overlapping dimensions, raising questions on
their uniqueness. Within public administration, studies have
identified as many as 20 leadership styles, many of which, al-
though unique, share some conceptual similarities (Backhaus
and Vogel  2022). Disaggregating EL into these two compo-
nents may offer diagnostic clarity and enable researchers to
assess whether a component is driving specific outcomes more
than the other.
However, disaggregating EL is not without risks, as it may sac-
rifice the configurational coherence of the construct (as seen in
the unified perspective) and potentially underestimate the inter-
active effects that emerge when entrepreneurial and leadership
behaviors are enacted together. To reconcile these perspectives,
this study adopts a dual-­lens approach that recognizes both the
conceptual coherence of EL as a unified construct and the em-
pirical utility of examining its two core dimensions separately.
This approach offers both theoretical and practical benefits.
The unified model reflects the holistic conceptualization of EL,
capturing the synergistic nature of combining entrepreneurial
vision with leadership influence. In contrast, the disaggregated
model offers analytical granularity, allowing for more fine-­
tuned insights about the differential effects of entrepreneurial
versus leadership behaviors.
2.2   |   EL in Public Administration: Well-­Being,
Inclusion, and Effectiveness
The influence of leaders within the organization has been
widely studied across multiple disciplines, including public
administration (Moynihan et  al.  2012; Schuster et  al.  2024;
Grøn 2025; Choi and Ko 2024). While studies on EL were ini-
tially dedicated to private sector entrepreneurship, an exten-
sive body of literature shows that it is also present in nonprofit
organizations and PSOs (Currie et al. 2008; Ruvio et al. 2010).
Research shows that organizational leaders significantly influ-
ence the behavior of employees and the success and effective-
ness of organizations (Yukl 2008). In addition, entrepreneurial
leaders develop clear visions, promote innovation, absorb un-
certainty, and create dynamic working environments (Gupta
et al. 2004; Ruvio et al. 2010), and these factors can enhance
the effectiveness of public organizations. Studies find that EL
improves the performance and effectiveness of community-­
based nonprofit organizations, the innovativeness of schools,
the effectiveness of PSOs, and the promotion of workplace
democracy in public organizations (Pihie et al. 2014; Santora
et al. 1999; Vivona 2024).
Therefore, we hypothesize that:
H1.  Entrepreneurial leadership (as a unified construct [EL])
has a positive impact on the effectiveness of PSOs.
H1a.  The entrepreneurial dimension (E) of EL has a distinct
positive impact on the effectiveness of PSOs.
H1b.  The leadership dimension (L) of EL has a distinct positive
impact on the effectiveness of PSOs.
Studies in organizational psychology have long connected the
character of leaders to the well-­being of subordinates in both
organizations and groups. For example, several review studies
have shown that supportive and transformational leaders sig-
nificantly improve the well-­being of their followers, whereas
destructive leaders significantly weaken them (Montano
et al. 2017). This finding is backed up by research in public sector
settings showing that leaders influence the well-­being of public
municipality employees regardless of whether they work from
home or from site (Lundqvist et  al.  2022). Because entrepre-
neurial leaders are concerned with meeting work-­related goals
(Vivona  2024), and the well-­being of employees is crucial for
achieving organizational goals (Bellingan et al. 2024), we pos-
tulate that entrepreneurial leaders will be motivated to create an
environment that supports the well-­being of their subordinates.
H2.  Entrepreneurial leadership (as a unified construct [EL])
has a positive impact on the promotion of employee well-­being in
PSOs.
H2a.  The entrepreneurial dimension (E) of EL has a distinct
positive impact on the promotion of employee well-­being in PSOs.
H2b.  The leadership dimension (L) of EL has a distinct positive
impact on the promotion of employee well-­being in PSOs.
While well-­being relates to supporting employees' physical, men-
tal, and emotional health, workplace inclusion ensures employ-
ees feel respected, valued, and fully integrated in the workplace
culture (Vivona and Lewis 2023). According to Mor-­Barak and
Cherin (1998), inclusion is the feeling employees get that they
fully belong to a work group, and are valued within the organi-
zation. This includes a feeling that they can meaningfully con-
tribute to decision making, have access to resources, and possess
some influence over other areas of work. Studies show that so-
ciodemographic characteristics have historically been a source
of exclusion in the workplace, leading to discrimination based
on age, gender, race, or ethnicity (Roberson and Scott  2024).
Research shows that a lack of inclusion significantly influ-
ences the success of organizations, as it ensures that important
groups of people cannot give their maximum talents and efforts
toward achieving the organization's objectives (Randel  2023).
Accordingly, creating a workplace culture that treats all employ-
ees fairly regardless of their demographic background, and one

that values the inputs of all employees, is often recommended
(Mor-­Barak and Cherin  1998; Sabharwal  2014). Therefore, for
the purpose of this study, we define workplace inclusion as the
creation of working environments that value the ideas and con-
tributions of all employees regardless of sociodemographic back-
ground, and where all employees are treated with respect and
dignity. This definition includes the welcoming of ideas in work-
places, the participation of all employees in workplace activities,
and the promotion of an inclusive workplace culture. This ap-
proach differs from those taken by other associated areas such
as DEI, representation, and social/organizational justice (Prasad
and Śliwa 2024; Roberson and Scott 2024).
There is some consensus in academic research that leaders are
crucial for developing inclusive working environments (Shore
and Chung 2022; Korkmaz et al. 2022). Studies demonstrate that
leaders can foster the psychological safety and empowerment of
social minorities, strengthen belongingness within teams, show
appreciation to all team members by recognizing their efforts and
contributions, and support the organization's mission of promot-
ing inclusion (Shore and Chung 2022; Veli Korkmaz et al. 2022).
For example, a recent study finds that EL within the school man-
agement system led to better inclusion outcomes for students
with special education needs (Stavrou and Kafa  2024). These
findings are buttressed by research on public sector leadership.
For instance, a longitudinal study of a public child welfare orga-
nization in the United States found that leaders influenced the
feeling of inclusion within the organization (Brimhall et al. 2017).
Also, a separate study of 45 diverse public sector teams in the
Netherlands showed that leaders positively influenced the oth-
erwise negative relationship between diversity and inclusion
(Ashikali et al. 2021). In addition, through role modeling and a
reward system that remunerates inclusive and respectful behav-
ior, leaders can inspire and motivate followers to act in inclusive
ways (Boekhorst 2015). As a result, we hypothesize the following:
H3.  Entrepreneurial leadership (as a unified construct [EL])
has a positive impact on the promotion of inclusive (IP) workplace
culture in PSOs.
H3a.  The entrepreneurial dimension (E) of EL has a distinct
positive impact on the promotion of inclusive (IP) workplace cul-
ture in PSOs.
H3b.  The leadership dimension (L) of EL has a distinct posi-
tive impact on the promotion of inclusive (IP) workplace culture
in PSOs.
Finally, some studies have analyzed the indirect impact of EL
on several outcomes. For instance, studies show that individual
and team creative efficacy mediates the relationship between
EL and creativity (Cai et  al.  2019), and innovative environ-
ments mediate the relationship between EL and the innovative
behavior of employees (Li et al. 2020). However, these studies
did not investigate how EL mediates public sector effectiveness.
Public sector studies assessing the indirect effect that other fac-
tors exert on EL have so far been scarce. Consequently, inspired
by the findings of previous studies on the mediating influence
of other factors on EL (Cai et al. 2019; Li et al. 2020), and the
conclusions of studies showing that inclusion and well-­being
significantly influence other factors (Jaiswal and Dyaram 2019;
Ribeiro et al. 2021), we postulate that the promotion of inclusive
workplace culture and employee well-­being will mediate the
influence of EL on public sector effectiveness. The full list of
hypotheses is presented in Table 1.
H4.  Well-­being promotion (WP) mediates the positive relation-
ship between EL and public sector effectiveness.
H5.  Inclusion promotion (IP) mediates the positive relationship
between EL and public sector effectiveness.

the APS has increasingly emphasized empowering public
sector leaders to foster innovative and proactive behaviors
that align with the entrepreneurial mindset (Australian
Government 2009, 2010). The APS has indeed recently identi-
fied EL as a core capability for public sector leaders to navigate
rapid change and meet future challenges (Australian Public
Service Commission  2021b). However, despite the growing
recognition of EL as an essential driver of innovation and one
of the six leadership capabilities based on the APS Leadership
Capability Framework, its development within the APS re-
mains limited (Australian Public Service Commission 2021b,
2024). This is not surprising, as the public sector has only re-
cently begun to embrace the value that entrepreneurship has
to offer, both in practice and in scholarly discourse (Kousina
et al. 2024; Vivona et al. 2024).
Moreover, the APS has recognized the need to enhance orga-
nizational culture and performance, linking it explicitly to its
efforts to promote diversity, inclusion, and overall well-­being.
For example, in 2016, the establishment of the Secretaries
Equality and Diversity Council marked a commitment to
breaking down barriers to create an inclusive and respect-
ful workplace for all employees (Australian Public Service
Commission 2019, 64). Similarly, a 2024 report highlights that
“the APS has long been focused on the well-­being of its employ-
ees … [and] high performance of employees and organizations
cannot be sustained without adequate levels of employee well-­
being” (Australian Public Service Commission 2024, 14). In
this context, EL is seen by the APS as a key capability to “build
a positive and collaborative team environment” (Australian
Public Service Commission 2021a). Nevertheless, despite the
recent APS strides in promoting EL, the focus on leadership
qualities such as inclusivity and well-­being still remains na-
scent. The connection between EL and these elements is in its
early stages, and its influence on leadership practice within
the APS is still evolving. As such, the Australian context pro-
vides a timely and relevant setting to explore the role of EL
in enhancing public sector performance, particularly with re-
gard to inclusion and well-­being.
3.2   |   Analytical Process
The constructs of entrepreneurial leaders examined in this
study were adapted from the measurement scale developed by
Renko et al. (2015). We examine EL across five major constructs
using nine measurement instruments (Table S1). The first EL
construct (EL1) is the engagement of followers in creating a vi-
sion of the future; this is based on the following construct: My
supervisor engages with staff on how to respond to future chal-
lenges. According to Kuratko and Audretsch (2009), the ability
of leaders to make strategic plans, particularly on the long-­term
challenges and opportunities surrounding an organization, is
a core part of EL. In addition, entrepreneurial leaders success-
fully influence followers to buy into a clear vision of the future
(Renko et al. 2015), and mobilize followers to become commit-
ted to this future vision (Gupta et al. 2004).
The second construct (EL2), my supervisor ensures that my
workgroup delivers on what we are responsible for, is focused on
the responsibility or persistence of entrepreneurial leaders to
ensure that followers achieve results within the unit or organi-
zation, in spite of internal or external challenges. According to
Kuratko (2007), entrepreneurs possess some form of tenacity to
meet goals and objectives, and entrepreneurial leaders display a
high level of commitment in ensuring that subordinates achieve
their goals (Hitt et  al.  2011; Kuratko and Audretsch  2009;
Bagheri and Harrison 2020).
Third, the commitment of entrepreneurial leaders to improv-
ing and supporting the performance of employees is another
important construct of EL. This is based on the following two
measurements (EL3, EL4): my supervisor encourages my team
to regularly review and improve our work, and my supervisor
provides me with helpful feedback to improve my performance.
Research shows that a commitment to improving the perfor-
mance of employees is an important trait of entrepreneurial
leaders (Vivona 2024). They set positive and high expectations
of performance, and support employees in achieving them
(Kuratko and Audretsch 2009).
Fourth, entrepreneurial leaders inspire innovation and cre-
ativity. While creativity and innovation are complementary
ideas, there are small but important differences between them.
According to Hughes et  al.  (2018, 3) “Workplace creativity
concerns the cognitive and behavioral processes applied when
attempting to generate novel ideas. Workplace innovation

ity often refers to idea generation, whereas innovation refers to
the implementation of ideas to achieve better practices, proce-
dures, or products. The association between entrepreneurial
leaders and workplace innovation and creativity is supported by
a substantial body of literature. For example, research on civil
servants in China found that entrepreneurial leaders improve
the innovative behavior of subordinates (Miao et al. 2018), and
research on more than 100 municipal departments in Greece
found that entrepreneurial leaders positively influence explor-
atory innovation (Kousina et  al.  2024). Moreover, studies on
other types of organizations show that entrepreneurial lead-
ers inspire/improve both employee and team creativity (Cai
et al. 2019; Bagheri et al. 2022). Innovation and creativity are
based on the following three measurements (EL5, EL6, EL7): my
immediate supervisor encourages me to come up with new or bet-
ter ways of doing things, my SES manager encourages innovation
and creativity, and people are recognized for coming up with new
and innovative ways of working.
Finally, entrepreneurial leaders inspire passion among subordi-
nates, and encourage some level of risk-­taking (Renko et al. 2015;
Gupta et al. 2004). This means that employees are inspired to
give their best at work every day, and that failure during the cre-
ative and innovative process does not lead to any sort of recrim-
ination (Renko et al. 2015; Vivona and Lewis 2023). Instead, the
entrepreneurial leader recognizes and supports the notion that
failure is a part of innovation. Risk-­taking (EL8) is measured
with the survey question my agency recognizes and supports the
notion that failure is a part of innovation, whereas passion (EL9)
is measured with my agency really inspires me to do my best work
every day.
To examine the disaggregated perspective of EL, we separated
the EL measurement scale adapted for this study into two main
dimensions (Renko et  al.  2015). The first dimension includes
factors that capture leadership behaviors that, despite being in-
tegral to EL, are not unique to it (comprising EL2, EL3, EL4,
EL9). These items, also referred to as the leadership dimension,
overlap with dimensions that are also central to other leadership
styles, such as transformational, servant, and adaptive leader-
ship. For instance, encouraging regular review and improve-
ment of work practices is a core part of EL, but it also aligns with
the emphasis of transformational leaders to strive for high stan-
dards and continuous improvements (Vogel and Masal  2015).
Furthermore, while providing constructive feedback is part of
EL, it also aligns with the adaptive leadership trait that empha-
sizes the development of individual and organizational capaci-
ties to respond to changing demands (Murphy et al. 2017). The
second dimension includes instead factors that are established
in previous literature as unique to entrepreneurship (Renko
et al. 2015), and is also referred to as the entrepreneurial dimen-
sion in this study (comprising the remaining items, comprising
EL1, EL5, EL6, EL7, EL8).
The other variables examined in this study include well-­being
promotion, to assess how leaders promote the well-­being of their
followers, and inclusion promotion to assess how well entrepre-
neurial leaders promote an inclusive workplace culture. Finally,
we examine organizational effectiveness with a question asking
employees to rate their agency's success in meeting its goals and
objectives. All questions measuring these variables are included
in Table S2.
The Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-­
SEM) was used for data analysis. According to Hair et al. (2019),
PLS-­SEM is a preferable analytical method when the purpose of
the study is to predict a theoretical framework, explore theoret-
ical extensions of established theories, and involve the use of
secondary data. Because this study attempts to predict the re-
lationship between EL and other factors, explore the extension
of the established theory of EL to well-­being and inclusion, and
use secondary survey data (i.e., data that were not specifically
designed by the authors to examine the latent constructs), it was
decided that PLS-­SEM was an ideal choice. For data analysis,
EL is the exogenous latent variable, meaning that it is the tar-
get construct that explains other constructs. However, inclusion
promotion (coded as IP) and well-­being promotion (coded as
WP), and organizational effectiveness (coded as EFF) are de-
pendent or endogenous latent variables. All analyzes were con-
ducted using the R program for statistical computing (R Core
Team  2017), and subsequently the SmartPLS software (ver-
sion 4).
4   |   Results
4.1   |   Results of the Unified Perspective on EL
The results of the descriptive and model fit analysis are pre-
sented in supplementary text 1 in Supporting Information. EL8
was excluded from the analysis of the unified perspective on
EL because the outer loading was below the 0.60 limit recom-
mended by scholars (Hair et al. 2019). Moreover, the results of
the PLS-­SEM analysis are presented in Table 2 and Figure 1.
The results show that EL significantly increases the effective-
ness of PSOs by a factor of 0.343 (p < 0.001) within a 97.5% con-
fidence interval of (0.333; 0.354). This finding supports our first
hypothesis. Furthermore, we also found that entrepreneurial
leaders are very likely to promote the well-­being of public sector
employees by a factor of 0.684 (p < 0.001), and they significantly
promote inclusion in PSOs (= 0.859; p < 0.001). These final two
results support our hypothesis. Furthermore, the results of the
indirect effects show that EL has a positive indirect impact on
organizational effectiveness through well-­being promotion,
however it has a negative indirect effect on organizational effec-
tiveness through inclusion promotion.
In addition, the R-­squared (R2) analysis to examine how well
EL explains the variance in organizational effectiveness was
approximately 14%. This implies that the variation in organi-
zational effectiveness that can be explained by EL is approxi-
mately 14%. While this R2 value might initially appear low, it is
consistent with other similar studies on leadership in PSOs. For
instance, the research by Demircioglu and Chowdhury (2021)
on leadership and PSE reported an R2 of about 16%, while
Vivona's  (2024) research on EL reported an R2 of about 19%.
Meanwhile, the influence of EL on well-­being promotion has
an R2 of 0.469, whereas, for inclusion promotion, the R2 is 0.737.
This means that in the public sector agencies examined in this
research, EL explains approximately 47% of the variation in

5   |   Discussion
This study aimed to examine the influence of EL on the orga-
nizational effectiveness of PSOs and to identify to what extent
the promotion of well-­being and inclusion in PSOs mediates this
relationship. To provide a nuanced analysis of EL, the research
examined two separate perspectives on EL: (1) the unified EL
perspective, and (2) the disaggregated EL perspective comprised
of an entrepreneurial dimension and a leadership dimension.
The results of the unified EL perspective show that entrepre-
neurial leaders promote inclusion and well-­being, and make
PSOs more effective. However, the mediation analysis shows
that while well-­being promotion positively mediates the influ-
ence of EL on organizational effectiveness, inclusion promo-
tion does not. This implies that while well-­being promotion is
a complementary mediator of the influence of EL on organiza-
tional effectiveness, inclusion promotion is a competitive one
(Hair et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2010). Consequently, additional
studies that go beyond the three indicators of inclusion promo-
tion examined in this research are needed to further explore
the mediating relationship between inclusion promotion and
EL. For instance, future studies can consider the mediating
roles of specific inclusion outcomes, such as discrimination,
workplace harassment, and bullying, or the influence of other
DEI initiatives.
These results provide significant implications for the theory and
practice of management and public administration. First, it ex-
tends the growing consensus on the positive influence of public
sector entrepreneurs, and entrepreneurial leaders in particular,

While previous studies have found that leadership styles, such
as transformational, supportive, and servant leadership, can
influence the well-­being of followers (Montano et  al.  2017;
Lundqvist et al. 2022; Lundqvist and Wallo 2023), this study
empirically shows that entrepreneurial leaders do so too. In the
context of PSOs, where strict rules, democratic accountability
and interaction-­specific barriers limit the behavior of leaders
(Cinar et al. 2019), we found that entrepreneurial leaders can
still improve the well-­being of employees. This finding enriches
the nascent body of knowledge on the influence of leadership
on well-­being in PSOs (Lundqvist et al. 2022). Therefore, at a
time of immense stress, burnout, poor mental health, and high
turnover rates among employees in PSOs, especially those in-
volved in social work (Ravalier et al. 2023; Strandell 2020), EL
can be considered as one of the many possible solutions.
In addition, although an increasing body of literature suggests
that individuals who seek entrepreneurship might be doing so
to seek power and domination over others (Brownell et al. 2021;
Olumekor et al. 2023), this study joins other recent research in
demonstrating that this behavior might be more specific to pri-
vate sector entrepreneurs (Stavrou and Kafa  2024). We found
that public sector entrepreneurial leaders promote inclusive
workplace culture, involve followers in workplace activities,
and welcome ideas from all members of staff even when they
clash with theirs. This finding supports those of Stavrou and
Kafa (2024), who, after examining several leadership styles in
schools with large numbers of students with special education
needs, found that entrepreneurial leaders were the most likely
people to improve the inclusion outcomes of students with spe-
cial education needs. This result implies that the type of people
who become public social workers and school principals might
be different from the people who become tech entrepreneurs or
hedge fund founders, and this difference might influence their
entrepreneurial character.
Finally, the results of the disaggregated perspective on EL show
that both the entrepreneurial and leadership dimensions of EL
distinctly lead to effective outcomes for PSOs, including the
promotion of better well-­being and inclusion. This supports the
findings of previous public administration studies showing that
several styles of leadership can produce positive outcomes for
PSOs (Backhaus and Vogel 2022). Therefore, while there may be
more than one approach to improving the functioning of PSOs,
there are also important nuances in the direction and strength
of these improvements. For example, our results show that the
entrepreneurial dimension might be especially important for the
promotion of well-­being, whereas good leadership attributes can
more strongly foster inclusive working environments.
6   |   Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Research
Directions
This research examined the relationship between EL, well-­
being, and inclusion in PSOs. This research found that entre-
preneurial leaders make PSOs more effective and they promote
well-­being and inclusion. In addition, the promotion of work-
place well-­being increases the influence that entrepreneurial
leaders have on organizational effectiveness.
This study has several notable limitations. First, the inclusion
examined in this research is specific to the promotion of an
inclusive workplace culture where employees participate in
workplace activities, and their ideas are welcomed and valued,
regardless of their sociodemographic background. This study
does not examine DEI, social justice, organizational justice, or
the representation of minority demographic groups. Our re-
sults should be carefully interpreted within this limit. Future
studies can consider the interplay between EL and the afore-
mentioned areas of inclusion. For instance, future studies can
examine if entrepreneurial leaders are more or less likely to
appoint women to leading positions, or if they provide special
support for people with disabilities, or people from other mi-
nority groups.
Furthermore, future studies should consider developing an
EL scale that is specific to PSOs. A wider assessment scale to
measure PSE among both leaders and followers will also pro-
vide great benefits for advancing research on the topic. It can
also help to more clearly distinguish the differences between
entrepreneurial-­specific factors and the more generic leadership
factors. In addition, organizational effectiveness was measured
by a self-­reported assessment of public sector employees to the
following survey question: rate your agency's success in meeting
its goals and objectives. While this might not be a very objec-
tive measure, the APS ensures the confidentiality and privacy
of respondents and nobody has access to individual responses
or could identify individuals. Thus, employees have no reason
to provide misleading information about organizational effec-
tiveness as well as for other variables. Additionally, this mea-
sure of organizational effectiveness has been used in previous
similar studies using the APS survey (Vivona 2024). Likewise,
other studies use similar items to measure organizational effec-
tiveness in public organizations in other contexts (e.g., Pandey
et  al.  2007; Lan and Rainey  1992; Molnar and Rogers  1976).
Notwithstanding, we recommend that future studies test the
model used in this study against objective measures of organiza-
tional effectiveness in PSOs.
Finally, the results of the disaggregated perspective on EL
show significant differences between the entrepreneurial and
leadership dimensions. Therefore, we join the growing call to
establish clearer differences between various leadership styles,
such as EL, servant leadership, adaptive leadership, and trans-
formational leadership (Anderson and Sun 2017; Ravet-­Brown
et al. 2024), and their impact on different individual and organi-
zational outcomes.

Biographies
Michael Olumekor is a lecturer at Ural Federal University. His re-
search focuses on the effective functioning of public sector organizations
through inclusive innovation, digitalization, and entrepreneurship. His
research has been published in journals like Public Administration,
Public Policy and Administration, and Public Administration Review. In
popular press, his works have been featured or cited by leading news
outlets in the Spanish and English language media.

Emre Cinar is an associate professor at the University of Portsmouth.
His research examines the types and process dynamics of public sector
innovation, particularly how these are shaped by national contexts. In
parallel, he also studies public entrepreneurship, exploring how en-
trepreneurial approaches can transform public services. His work has
been published in leading journals, including Research Policy, Public
Administration Review, Public Management Review, Governance, Small
Business Economics, and the Journal of Technology Transfer.
Roberto Vivona is a postdoctoral researcher at the University of St.
Gallen. His research examines leadership, innovation, and entrepreneur-
ship in public organizations, with a focus on entrepreneurial leadership,
bureaucratic entrepreneurship, and the impact of emerging technologies
such as artificial intelligence on public sector leadership and organiza-
tional change. His work has appeared in journals like Research Policy,
Small Business Economics, Public Management Review, Policy and Society,
and the Review of Public Personnel Administration.
Mehmet Akif Demircioglu is an associate professor at the School of
Public Policy and Administration at Carleton University in Canada and
is affiliated with the National University of Singapore, the University
of St. Gallen, Yonsei University, Indiana University, and Arizona State
University. Most of his research focuses on innovation and entrepre-
neurship in the public sector. His first co-­authored book, Public Sector
Innovation (with David Audretsch), was published by Cambridge
University Press in 2024.

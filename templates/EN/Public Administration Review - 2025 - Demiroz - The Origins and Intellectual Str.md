ABSTRACT
Despite the recent interest in the deep state topic, the concept remains elusive. This research utilizes bibliometric metadata from
64 articles published between 2009 and 2024 to identify the intellectual structure and origins of the concept. The findings in-
dicate that discussions on the deep state are fragmented across disciplines such as political science, public administration, law,
international relations, and history, intersecting with themes like democratization, civilian-­military relations, and informal net-
works, yet the concept has not formed into a standalone idea. Interpretations of the concept differ regionally and temporally: in
the Middle East, it is associated with civilian-­military dynamics; in Thailand, with interactions among the monarchy, judiciary,
and officials; in the United States, historically, the focus has been the military-­industrial complex, which shifted to the admin-
istrative state post-­Trump. These variations highlight the need for a comprehensive theoretical framework, for which public
administration could provide essential theoretical and methodological support.
1   |   Introduction
The deep state is an intriguing concept for scholars of politics
and administration. Broadly, it refers to shadowy networks of
bureaucrats that operate independently of elected officials and
pursue their separate agendas. The term originated in Turkey
in the 1990s and arrived on the US political scene during the
Trump Administration (Gingeras  2019). Trump attributed
many of the challenges he faced as president, particularly al-
legations of ties between his campaign and Russia, to this so-­
called deep state. The far-­right website Breitbart, formerly led
by Steve Bannon, claimed that the “deep state” was behind the
unstoppable leaks and the ensuing chaos in Trump's cabinet
(Abramson 2017; Rucker et al. 2017). Trump and his inner cir-
cle claimed that Obama loyalists were orchestrating the orga-
nized resistance against Trump's policies, including tapping his
phones. Several Republican legislators, such as representatives
Steve King of Iowa, Thomas Massie of Kentucky, and Devin
Nunes of California, echoed these views. The American public
also shared similar views to some extent. A 2018 Monmouth
University poll found that 74% of respondents believed that
“a group of unelected government and military officials se-
cretly manipulate or direct policy” in Washington, though
63% were unfamiliar with the term “deep state” (Monmouth
University 2018).
The deep state rhetoric advanced by the Trump Administration,
Republican legislators, and conservative circles largely tar-
geted the administrative state. During Trump's first term, this
rhetoric served as a pretext to undermine bureaucratic au-
tonomy, with one of its most notable efforts being Executive
Order 13957, creating Schedule F in the Excepted Service (The
White House 2020). This order aimed to reclassify career civil
servants as political appointees, thus placing federal agencies
more directly under presidential control. In the second Trump
administration, the targeting of public servants became more
overt, particularly through the operations of the Department
of Government Efficiency (DOGE) and the policies and guide-
lines developed by the Office of Personnel Management. In re-
sponse to these efforts, scholars sought to examine and, when

There are two main reasons for this intellectual predicament.
First, the deep state is a relatively new term, and much of the
interest in it has been reactive rather than systematic, especially
in American politics. That is because, especially after the 2016
elections, the American government was caught between two
opposing forces: conservative politics and the right-­wing popu-
lism embodied by Donald Trump, which sought to weaken the
administrative state, and a progressive agenda advocating for
environmental regulation, a green economy, and expanded civil
rights. While this tension attracted considerable scholarly atten-
tion, much of the focus has been on the Trump Administration's
attacks on the administrative state, leading to a somewhat nar-
row scope of analysis.
Second, the meaning of the “deep state” varies significantly de-
pending on the country in which it is used, in contrast to its recent
use in US politics. In Turkey, for example, the deep state refers
to the ideological role played by the Turkish Armed Forces and
the broader national security establishment, which have influ-
enced the country's trajectory since the 19th century (Taş 2014;
Kaya 2009; Gingeras 2010, 2011; Söyler 2013). In countries like
Thailand, Sudan, Egypt, Pakistan, and Burkina Faso, the term is
associated with elite networks, patronage systems, and informal
institutions that exert power behind the scenes (Bartlett 2020;
Söyler  2013; Pronchev and Mikhailov  2021; Nehring  2022;
Oldenburg 2017; Haavik et al. 2022).
Additionally, in the United States, there is an older but less prom-
inent body of scholarship focused on the military-­industrial
complex and national security bureaucracy, which aligns more
closely with global interpretations of the deep state than with
recent discussions around the administrative state (Good 2018,
2022; Scott  2014a, 2014b, 2014c). Finally, the proliferation of
conspiracy theories during the first Trump presidency and the
COVID-­19 pandemic (e.g., QAnon, 5G, Pizza Gate) has further
muddied the term's meaning. The literature on deep-­state con-
spiracy theories spans a wide range of academic disciplines,
including communication and media (Thelwall et  al.  2021;
Tuters and Willaert  2022; Al-­Rawi et  al.  2022), religion
(O'Donnell 2020), linguistics (Procházka and Blommaert 2021),
and computational social sciences (Havey 2020), contributing to
the concept's ongoing ambiguity.
As a result, navigating the deep state literature is challenging,
making it difficult to discern how various discussions on the
topic are organized and interconnected, if at all. Without a clear
understanding of the intellectual traditions shaping this litera-
ture, those studying the deep state may miss important nuances
or misinterpret its meaning. To address these gaps, this article
identifies the intellectual traditions shaping the deep state liter-
ature and maps the structure of scholarly efforts examining it.
The findings from this analysis will not only shed light on the
concept and its intellectual boundaries but also offer valuable
guidance for future research.
The remainder of the article is organized as follows: The next
section offers a brief review of the deep state literature, explain-
ing how the term has been conceptualized in different contexts.
Afterward, the methodology section outlines the approach used
to identify and analyze the relevant literature. The findings sec-
tion then presents the results of the study, followed by a discus-
sion of their implications for the deep state literature. Finally,
the conclusion summarizes the study, provides directions for
future research, and acknowledges its limitations.
2   |   Literature Review
The term “deep state” originated in Turkish politics during the
country's struggle against the Kurdish insurgency led by the
PKK, a designated terrorist group, in the 1990s (Özkök 1997).
It primarily described the national security apparatus—includ-
ing the military, police, gendarmerie, and intelligence agen-
cies—and their covert affiliates like paramilitary groups, which
wielded substantial influence over national politics. This period
was marked by widespread human rights violations by security
forces, including extrajudicial killings, kidnappings, extortion,
torture, and forced depopulation of villages (Human Rights
Watch 1996a, 1996b, 1997), culminating in a military coup in
1997. This section examines the origins of the deep state concept
in Turkish politics and its subsequent adoption by scholars an-
alyzing other countries. Understanding its roots in Turkey pro-
vides a broader context for interpreting the concept's application
in other settings, particularly, in Egypt and Pakistan, where sim-
ilar political dynamics have shaped its usage. The development
of the deep state in the US context will be discussed separately,
as its use in American political experience differs significantly
from its application in other countries.

The concept of a “state within the state” in Turkey is traceable to

Committee also frequently used paramilitary groups and armed
civilians linked to the CUP to eliminate real or perceived secu-

the nascent Turkish Republic (Kaya 2009; Hanioğlu 1995, 2017).
The traditions of military coups, political conspiracies, and the
use of covert networks for national security are often associated
with this legacy (Demiroz and Kapucu  2012; Gingeras  2010,
2011; Kaya 2009).
2.2   |   State—Organized Crime Relations
and Military Tutelage
The intricate relationship between the state and the crimi-
nal underworld has been prevalent throughout Turkish his-
tory, with opium trafficking holding a major role in this nexus
(Gingeras 2011). However, arguably, the most significant covert
network was the Tactical Mobilization Committee (Seferberlik
Taktik Kurulu), established under the Turkish Armed Forces'
Special Warfare Department (ÖHD), as part of NATO's clan-
destine Operation Gladio. Initiated in 1956 by Italian intelli-
gence and the CIA, Gladio was a network of covert stay-­behind
units responsible for preparing for a potential Soviet invasion
and collecting intelligence (Nuti  2007). Right-­wing political
figures, organized crime, paramilitary groups, bankers, and
religious institutions were recruited and active in this network
(Williams  2018). Similar networks were established in other
European countries, including Turkey, and “Gladio” became a
blanket term for all stay-­behind operations across Europe. These
networks have been accused of engaging in false-­flag opera-
tions and terrorist activities, known as the strategy of tension,
aimed at preventing leftist movements from coming to power
(Ganser 2004, 2005, 2006).
Former Italian magistrate Felice Casson, who uncovered the ex-
istence of Gladio while investigating the 1972 Peteano bombing,
noted that the stay-­behind forces in Turkey had two branches: a
military branch called “Counter Guerrilla” and a civilian branch
known as “Ergenekon” (Ünver 2009).
The Counter Guerilla branch is associated with coordinating
state-­organized crime ties, which were partially exposed in
1996. A luxury Mercedes carrying an organized crime leader
sought by Interpol under a red notice, the deputy director of the
Istanbul Police Department, and a pro-­government Kurdish pol-
itician crashed into a truck near the Susurluk region in Turkey.
The mob boss and the deputy police chief were killed, while
the politician survived. Guns and silencers were found in the
vehicle's trunk. The accident, dubbed the Susurluk Scandal, led
to significant public uproar and legal investigations. However,
both elected leaders and senior bureaucrats stood by the ac-
cused individuals, Prime Minister Tansu Çiller calling “those
who shot and got shot at for the state” honorable (Çiller 1996;
Milliyet 1996).
The Ergenekon branch was investigated in the Ergenekon
Trial that started in 2008, which began following the discovery
of 27 military-­grade hand grenades in an Istanbul residence.
The case concluded in 2013, implicating various high-­profile
figures, such as retired generals, politicians, and journal-
ists. Both the Susurluk and Ergenekon cases were deeply
politicized. Susurluk trials ended without implicating any
significant names, while the Ergenekon trial was overturned
in 2016 under the pretext that it was a conspiracy against
the national army (Director of National Intelligence, Open
Source Center 2010; Demiroz and Kapucu 2012; Ünver 2009;
Taş 2014; Park 2008; Habertürk 2016).
The second theme in deep state discussions in Turkey is shaped
by the military's role in politics, which culminated in coups
d'état in 1960, 1971, 1980, and 1997. The military has desig-
nated itself as the protector of the regime's values, particularly
nationalism and secularism, positioning itself in a tutelary role
for politics (Söyler 2013, 2015). This tutelary role creates a dual-
ity in the state, with the military holding the prerogative state
role while the other institutions constitute the normal state
(Fraenkel 1941). The prerogative state determines when to de-
clare emergency situations and to create exceptions to laws. If
the military considers that the regime is under threat by real or
perceived enemies (e.g., subversive leftist movements, Kurdish
insurgents, Islamist parties), it can intervene in politics. The le-
gitimacy of such interventions cannot be ensured without the
help of formal (e.g., the Constitutional Court, business establish-
ments) and informal institutions (paramilitary groups) linked to
the military (Söyler 2013, 2015; Cizre 2008; 2008; Buğra 2021;
İnsel and Bayramoğlu 2004).
2.3   |   Expansion of the Deep State Discussions:
Informal Institutions and Patronage Networks
Deep state discussions in other countries overlap with Turkish
and American experiences while preserving their unique histor-
ical and political nuances. Egypt and Pakistan cases possess con-
siderable similarities to the Turkish deep state (Momani 2013).
In these countries, military and intelligence services have a
presence in politics with both formal and informal institutions
(Söyler  2013; Armbrust  2017; Norton  2013). They shape the
direction of the country by facilitating or sabotaging political
developments (Khosrokhavar  2014; Faris  2013; Atawna and
Othman 2015; Oldenburg 2017; Behuria and Shukla 2024).
Thailand has a different experience with the deep state.
Mérieau (2016) and McCargo (2021) describe the deep state of
Thailand as an amalgamation of formal and informal institu-
tions that form a “network monarchy.” Mérieau (2016) describes
a system where the monarchy and judiciary collaborate to main-
tain a preferred sociopolitical order, using their positions and
charismatic legitimacy to exert significant influence and pre-
vent democratization. Judges, acting as informal representatives

Burkina Faso, and Egypt, where informal institutions also play

In Sudan and Burkina Faso, informal institutions significantly
overshadow formal political structures, influencing gover-
nance through deeply embedded networks of patronage and
corruption. Bartlett  (2020) describes Sudan's political system
as a kleptocracy dominated by a network of power brokers—
intelligence agencies and media—that manipulate the truth
and maintain power through a system of patronage known as
“wasta.” This network distributes economic and political ad-
vantages to maintain the status quo, supported by military and
security services that preserve a facade of stability. Similarly, in
Burkina Faso, Haavik et al. (2022) characterize the governance
structure as an “autocratic clique” or “big man deep state,”
where formal and informal networks collaborate to exploit re-
sources and retain power. This system operates through clien-
telism, where military and business elites support the regime in
exchange for economic benefits and political protection, creat-
ing a semi-­formal governance structure that resists substantial
change and perpetuates the control of elites over the state and
its resources.
2.4   |   The Deep State Discussions in
America: Between the Security State
and the Administrative State
The deep state discussions in the US parallel the experiences
of other countries, but they are nuanced and deeply em-
bedded in America's history and ideological divides. Early
warnings about the influence and collusion of the military-­
industrial complex with business interests on American
foreign and domestic policy were voiced by figures such as
Major General Smedley Butler (1935) and President Dwight D.
Eisenhower (1961). Subsequent inquiries into the roles of pow-
erful, unaccountable officials took increasingly ideological
directions (Rohde  2021). Liberal-­leaning scholars and jour-
nalists draw attention to the political intrigues and conspir-
acies in which the US military and intelligence services were
involved, particularly, the Kennedy Assassination, Watergate
Scandal, heroin trade, mass surveillance, military invasions,
and coups in other countries (Scott  2003, 2008, 2013, 2017;
Good 2018, 2022; Glennon 2015; Rohde 2021; Ambinder and
Grady 2013; Priest and Arkin 2011).
Peter Dale Scott, former Canadian diplomat turned academic,
is one of the pioneers in the study of “deep politics”—a term
used to describe covert political processes that operate out-
side the law (Scott  2003). Since the 1970s, Scott has exten-
sively explored significant events such as the Vietnam War,
the Kennedy assassination, and the 9/11 attacks. His research
highlights the US's clandestine involvement in the drug trade,
oil business, and international terrorism (Scott  2003, 2008,
2013, 2017).
Scott's research uncovers recurring patterns in major incidents
that have shaped US security and foreign policy and exam-
ines their broader implications. For instance, he draws paral-
lels between the 9/11 attacks and the Tonkin Gulf Incident of
1964, suggesting that both were used by elements within the
deep state to precipitate major military responses that were
already desired and planned. Scott notes these incidents facil-
itated significant, open-­ended Congressional empowerments
to the White House (Scott  2013). Moreover, he argues that
deep politics can be driven by the agenda of the presidents or
bureaucrats, in some cases against the will of the elected lead-
ers, and are products of the dysfunctional and criminal under-
pinnings of the American political establishment (Scott 2003,
2008, 2013).
More recent research, in conjunction with Scott's scholarship,
updated the coverage of deep state politics. Scholars such as
Glennon (2015) and later Good (2018, 2022) have delved into
the concept of the US national security bureaucracy, which they
describe using terms like the “Trumanite network” and “dou-
ble government.” This terminology highlights how the national
security apparatus, expanded during the Truman administra-
tion, operates in parallel to the visible government, managing
substantial governmental affairs while maintaining a façade
of democratic governance. Good (2018, 2022) expands on this
by proposing a “tripartite state” model, comprising the demo-
cratic state, the security state, and the deep state. He argues
that the deep state functions as a supranational force collabo-
rating with other state elements to extend American global he-
gemony, heavily influenced by the transnational capitalist class
and global economic institutions. This structure, according to
Good (2018, 2022), allows the national security bureaucracy to
operate with minimal accountability, undermining democratic
processes and placing it in a privileged position over other gov-
ernment institutions.
Current debates on the deep state, however, are at odds with
scholarship focusing on the national security bureaucracy.
Bozeman et  al.  (2024) note that conservative politicians and
right-­wing pundits appropriated the deep state term to target
the federal bureaucracy. The attacks on the administrative state
typically center on two interconnected themes: (i) the politics
of deference, often discussed in the context of the Chevron
deference doctrine (Elinson and Gould  2022; Peterson and
Napolio  2022) and (ii) the unitary executive (Kitrosser  2018;
Ernst 2018; Feinstein and Hemel 2019; Skowronek et al. 2021;
Michaels  2017, 2018; Calabresi and Yoo  2008; Crouch
et al. 2020), which pertain to issues of bureaucratic account-
ability and resistance.
Regulatory authority has long been a contentious issue in
American politics, particularly, highlighted by the Chevron
deference doctrine, a pivotal principle in administrative law
established in 1984 by the landmark case Chevron U.S.A., Inc.
v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. This doctrine directs
courts to defer to an agency's reasonable interpretation of an
ambiguous statute it administers, effectively allowing legisla-
tive delegation of interpretive powers to administrative agencies

The unitary executive theory asserts that the President should

executive power resides with the President, suggesting a hierar-
chical and direct control over the administrative domain. Critics,
however, argue that the executive branch is subject to both ex-

and the unitary executive model would erode accountability

to an imperial presidency (Skowronek et al. 2021; Prakash 2015;

3   |   Method
This article employs bibliometric research tools to inves-
tigate the intellectual structure of the deep state concept.
Bibliometric research tools leverage author and journal ci-
tations, along with other metadata, to assess the impact and
efficacy of research institutions, academic journals, and indi-
vidual scholars. Citations act as “frozen footprints on the land-
scape of scholarly achievement,” as noted by Cronin (1984, 25),
summarizing the scholarly influence and network by reveal-
ing connections between authors, topics, methodologies, and
schools of thought. Zhao and Strotmann (2015) outline several
applications of citation analysis, including evaluating schol-
arly contributions, mapping research fields to understand
their intellectual structures, tracking the flow of knowledge
and diffusion of ideas, studying the use of scholarly literature,
and aiding in the organization and retrieval of information.
These tools and methods provide a comprehensive view of the
dynamics within academic research.
3.1   |   Eligibility Criteria
A comprehensive search was conducted in the Web of Science,
encompassing all its subdatabases such as SCI, SSCI, and ESCI,
focusing exclusively on academic articles published in English
on the topic of the “deep state.” The search was broad, cover-
ing all years due to the recent emergence of the topic. The term
“deep state” was specifically searched for in the titles, abstracts,
keywords, or main text of the articles. To ensure a focused study,
related terms like “dual state,” “double government,” “tripartite
state,” “invisible government,” “shadow government,” “gladio,”
“dark networks,” and “parapolitics” were intentionally excluded.
The inclusion criteria were limited to peer-­reviewed scholarly
articles, excluding books, book reviews, editorials, and nonaca-
demic publications.
3.2   |   Study Selection
The initial search for academic articles on the “deep state”
yielded 337 results, which were then carefully screened for du-
plicates and relevance. The screening process involved a thor-
ough review of the journals, article titles, keywords, abstracts,
and main text. Articles from unrelated fields such as engineer-
ing, psychiatry, and materials science were excluded. The focus
was narrowed to articles that substantively addressed the “deep
state” in contexts relevant to politics, administration, policy,
law, national and international security, international relations,
area studies, conspiracy theories, or similar subjects. Further
refinement excluded articles that mentioned the term “deep
state” only superficially, for example, those referencing it just
once without deeper discussion or context. After a meticulous
assessment, only 64 articles were deemed relevant and retained
for this study's investigation into the concept of the “deep state.”
3.3   |   Analytical Tools
A two-­step methodology was employed in the review of the
articles. First, the bibliometric data were analyzed using the
Bibliometrix package in R (Aria and Cuccurullo 2017). This
bibliometric analysis was instrumental in identifying intel-
lectual and structural patterns within the dataset, such as
conceptual maps, intellectual frameworks, and historical
developments.
In the second step of the analysis, the selected articles were
thoroughly reviewed and systematically coded in an Excel
spreadsheet. This spreadsheet consists of two main components
designed to organize and facilitate data analysis.
The first component includes 10 columns, each dedicated to
different types of information. Four columns capture general
article details, such as author name, year of publication, jour-
nal, and article title. One column is dedicated to country con-
text (e.g., Turkey, Egypt, the United States). The remaining five
columns extract specific content from the articles, including the
primary topic discussed, key quotes, two tags for easy categori-
zation, and the page number where each quote appears.
The second component of the spreadsheet classifies additional
contextual information. It identifies each article's country focus
and the academic discipline of the journal, whether in law, po-
litical science, or area studies. It also categorizes the specific
thematic focus of each article, such as conspiracy theories, the
administrative state, or democratization (see Appendix A).
Throughout this process, key concepts and definitions related
to the deep state, as well as relevant academic disciplines, coun-
try contexts, and broader themes, were systematically extracted
from the dataset and supplemented with external sources. To
accurately code journal fields, each journal's website was re-
viewed, and definitions from their “About this Journal” pages
were examined. Additionally, supplementary literature was in-
corporated to enhance the depth and contextual understanding
of the deep state and its manifestations across different coun-
tries. This included reviewing other research published by some

3.4   |   Missing Data
The preliminary analysis of the metadata revealed some discrep-
ancies in data completeness across various columns. Specifically,
there were missing values in cited references (3.12%), abstracts
(12.5%), author keywords (45%), and Keywords Plus—these are
generated by the Bibliometrix algorithm and were missing in
48.44% of cases. The completeness of data for the cited references
column is deemed good, and the completeness for the abstracts
column is acceptable. However, the high incidence of missing
data in author keywords and Keywords Plus is considered poor.
Due to these limitations, the conceptual and thematic analysis
was conducted solely based on the available article abstracts, as
they provided the most reliable and comprehensive information
among the elements reviewed. No missing values were identified
in other metadata columns, namely in authors, affiliation, corre-
sponding author, document type, journal, publication year, title,
and total citation.
4   |   Findings
The findings of this study are organized into three main sec-
tions. The first section presents the general results of the bib-
liometric analysis conducted on the metadata. This analysis
identifies trends related to authorship, publication patterns, and
other key information embedded within the metadata as well
as additional information about journal fields, article focus, and
countries examined. The second section explores the intellec-
tual origins and structure of the current deep-­state discussions
by analyzing the cocitation networks. The third section analyzes
the conceptual themes in the deep state literature and their his-
torical evolution between 2009 and 2024.
4.1   |   General Results
The search on the Web of Science identified 64 articles authored
by 90 scholars, published across 60 journals. Of these articles,
over 67% (43 out of 64) were single-­authored. The publications
in the dataset span the years 2009 to 2024. Interest in the “deep
state” topic remained relatively low until 2021. Between 2009
and 2020, the number of publications per year varied between
one and seven. However, there was a significant surge in interest
in 2021, with nine publications reaching a peak of 12 publica-
tions in 2022. This was followed by a sharp decline to just two
publications in 2023, before an increase to seven publications in
2024 (see Table 1 and Figure 1).
Fluctuations in publication numbers can be linked to significant
global events that have shaped interest in the deep state concept.
Around 2013, Egypt and the broader Middle East underwent
profound political transformations, boosting scholarly atten-
tion to the deep state concept. Interest in the concept resurged
following Donald Trump's presidency in 2017, particularly, as
discussions surrounding his allegations of a deep state gained
traction. The COVID-­19 pandemic further fueled this surge in
scholarship. As conspiracy theories regarding the origin of the
virus, vaccines, and QAnon spread rapidly, researchers began
examining their impact on public health, governance, and polit-
ical discourse in 2020 and onward. It is important to note that
these major events often lead to an increase in academic publica-
tions with some delay, primarily due to the lengthy peer-­review

4.2   |   Author and Journal Information
Interest in the deep state has been sporadic among both authors
and journals. Peter Dale Scott emerges as the most prolific author
with three publications, followed by Feinstein, Gingeras, and
Michaels, each with two publications. All other authors have con-
tributed only a single article on the deep state. A similar pattern
is observed among journals. The Asia-­Pacific Journal—Japan
Focus, which published all three of Scott's articles in 2014, leads
in the number of publications. Insight Turkey and Presidential
Studies Quarterly each published two articles. All other journals
have published only one article on the deep state (see Table 2).
The authors in the dataset represent 21 countries, reflecting a
significant diversity in their geographic affiliations, although

7   |   Discussion
Scholars and elected officials discussing bureaucratic responsi-
bility and accountability in a democratic government share legiti-
mate concerns (Stewart 1985). However, the deep state discussions
go beyond general concerns over bureaucratic responsibility and
scrutinize the existence of informal networks or institutionalized
behavior within the civilian and military bureaucracies working
toward their own agenda or in collusion with other actors, such
as financial interest groups. As evidenced in the literature, the
tone and focus of these discussions shift based on political devel-
opments (e.g., the Trump presidency), major world events (e.g., the
COVID-­19 pandemic), and one's ideological position. Historically,
much of this literature in the U.S. and elsewhere is concerned
with the military-­industrial complex, intelligence services, infor-
mal networks, and hegemony, while the attention shifted toward
the administrative state and proliferation of conspiracy theories
with Trump's presidency and onward. Accordingly, these findings
have three primary implications for scholars and political leaders
who are interested in the deep state or bureaucratic responsibility
generally.
First, researchers focusing on the deep state must recognize its
literature as diverse and composed of disconnected intellectual
clusters originating from various academic disciplines, includ-
ing political science, journalism, intelligence studies, and his-
tory. This fragmentation suggests that the deep state is often
discussed in conjunction with other academic subjects rather
than as a standalone concept. As such, it is frequently consid-
ered alongside themes like civilian-­military relations, democra-
tization, informal institutions, global hegemony, and conspiracy
theories, indicating that it has yet to mature into an independent
academic concept.

Second, the deep state's definitions and conceptualizations vary
significantly across different historical and national contexts.
Despite 15 years of contemporary interest, a universal definition
remains elusive. In regions like Turkey, Egypt, and Pakistan, the
term often relates to civilian-­military relations and democrati-
zation processes. In contrast, in Thailand, it pertains to interac-
tions among the monarchy, judiciary, and elected officials. Before
Trump's candidacy, US discussions predominantly focused on the
military-­industrial complex and US global hegemony, with lim-
ited academic interest. Post-­Trump, the narrative has expanded
to include the administrative state, significantly increasing schol-
arly publications on the subject. Scholars should acknowledge
this diversity in definitions and consider it in their research.
Third, given the diversity and underlying themes connecting
different conceptions of the deep state—such as democracy, bu-
reaucratic capacity, accountability, transparency, and the rule
of law—a more detailed exploration is necessary. This article
advocates for the development of a coherent theoretical frame-
work that accommodates various contextual factors and is appli-
cable across different political or historical circumstances. The
public administration discipline provides valuable theoretical
and methodological tools for such endeavors, potentially enrich-
ing the conceptualization of the deep state.
A foundational step in theorizing the deep state is situating it
within the broader framework of state-­society relations. The
nature of this relationship is shaped by formal and informal
institutions as well as the political dynamics within specific
contexts. For instance, in Sudan, highly extractive institutions
(Acemoglu and Robinson 2012) have fostered a patronage sys-
tem known as wasta, sustained by powerful social networks
and informal institutions. In contrast, Turkey's institutions
exhibit partially extractive characteristics, leading to a frag-
ile democratic system periodically interrupted by military
interventions.

The United States, by contrast, has historically been char-
acterized by inclusive political and economic institutions,
where democratic processes, a free press, and active civic
engagement serve as mechanisms to “shackle” the leviathan
(Acemoglu and Robinson 2019). However, in an increasingly
polarized political environment, the deep state narrative has
been weaponized by Trump and his supporters to undermine
democratic and administrative institutions. National security
concerns and emergency declarations have been exploited as
pretexts for expanding executive power, while federal pro-
grams designed to protect consumers and promote public wel-
fare have been systematically dismantled. Simultaneously, the
erosion of regulatory oversight has benefited narrow interest
groups. These developments hold significant implications for
public administration, particularly in relation to theories of
public values, democratic governance, bureaucratic responsi-
bility, and social justice.
Examining the state-­society nexus from the deep state stand-
point requires a deeper engagement with the extant literature.
Findings from the bibliometric analysis identify multiple con-
vergence points that, while preliminary, offer promising ave-
nues for future research. The first convergence point is the role
of informal institutions, which were mentioned in various con-
texts. Informal institutions influence political developments and
shape the deep state discussions in countries such as Turkey,
Egypt, Sudan, and Thailand. The second convergence point is
the role of networks, which were discussed implicitly and ex-
plicitly in multiple countries. For example, the CUP functioned
as a secret political network that left a lasting impact on Turkish
politics and became the origin of the deep state discussions.
Similarly, the political system in Thailand was dubbed “network
monarchy,” highlighting the power of networks in the country's
political system. The third convergence point is the relationship
between democracy and national security. This relationship has
been discussed in several countries, though the level of demo-
cratic development of a country determines how the deep state
is viewed in the literature. For example, in Turkey, the deep state
is discussed in the context of civilian-­military relations as well
as the relationship between the national security apparatus and
informal organizations capable of exerting social and political
power (e.g., organized crime). These institutional settings con-
tribute to the country's ebbs and flows in democratization.
When informal institutions and networks are analyzed within
the context of national security and democracy, they reveal the
outline of an informal governance system. The scholarship on
the deep state suggests that such a governance system does not
aim to deliver services but rather to maintain political control by
constraining the public within acceptable boundaries. This con-
trol is often exercised at the expense of democratic institutions
through a combination of legitimate and illegitimate uses of
violence, ideology, economic power, and administrative mech-
anisms (Mann 2012), with national security frequently serving
as a justification.
These dynamics can be examined through various theoretical
frameworks. For example, Kaya (2009) analyzes the deep state
in Turkey using Olson's (1984) distributional coalitions theory.
In Turkey's political system, the national security apparatus,
especially the military establishment, has historically been the
most influential actor. Olson's framework, which examines how
dominant coalitions shape economic and political outcomes,
offers valuable insights for developing a broader theoretical
framework.
Scholars studying the deep state may benefit from engaging
with conceptual and methodological tools from governance
studies, network theory, and cybernetics. Governance and cy-
bernetics concepts derive from the same Greek word, meaning
steersman (Wiener 2013), emphasizing control, communication,
and direction. In the context of the deep state, these concepts
demonstrate how societal control is exerted through a network
of institutional and individual actors. Although the cybernetics
thinking faded from academic discussions in the 1970s, the core
ideas of communication, cognition, and control continue to in-
form public administration literature under different theoretical
frameworks (Comfort 2007, 2019). These perspectives can offer
valuable insights into how informal governance structures oper-
ate for political and societal control in various countries.
Network theory and methodology also provide promising ave-
nues for enriching theoretical and methodological discussions
on the deep state, given the central role of networks in the exist-
ing literature. By applying network analysis, scholars can better
understand the relationships, power dynamics, and mecha-
nisms through which informal institutions exert influence, fur-
ther balancing theoretical discussions on the deep state.
The deep state is not merely a public administration concern
but intersects with broader theoretical debates across multiple
disciplines. Tilly's (2005a, 2005b, 2007) work on trust networks
and democratization provides a useful lens for understand-
ing the social mechanisms driving democratization and de-­
democratization processes in different country contexts and
their relationship to deep state discussions. Additionally, a meta-­
analysis of methodologies and theoretical frameworks in the lit-
erature could serve as a critical foundation for future research,
offering deeper insights into the evolution of the concept and its
application across different contexts.
Future research should also engage in comparative analyses
of related concepts, such as Gladio, “shadow government” and
“deep politics.” Also, exploring themes such as bureaucratic
responsibilities, the administrative roots of populism, and the
interplay between bureaucracy and democratic backsliding
offers fertile grounds for theoretical debates on the deep state.
Emerging scholarship that defends the administrative state
further contributes, albeit indirectly, to the development of this
concept.
8   |   Conclusion
This article outlines the results of a bibliometric analysis exam-
ining 64 articles on the deep state concept published between
2009 and 2024. It reveals that the concept was largely overlooked
in the United States until the presidency of Donald Trump. Prior
to this, the focus of deep state literature was primarily on coun-
tries outside the US Trump's presidency not only heightened in-
terest within the United States but also shifted the focus from
the national security establishment to the administrative state.

While concerns about bureaucratic overreach and resistance are
valid, it is crucial to consider both domestic and international
political contexts when evaluating the deep state criticisms ema-
nating from Donald Trump and conservative circles. The global
rise of populism and instances of democratic backsliding led
many scholars to consider the criticisms of the administrative
state as an attack on democratic institutions and have provoked
a scholarly rebuttal.
Despite this heightened interest in the deep state concept fol-
lowing the Trump presidency, the co-­citation network shows
that the literature consists of disconnected clusters of research
examining the concept in conjunction with other academic sub-
jects. This suggests that the concept has not yet congealed into
a coherent field. Nevertheless, the deep state literature, while
still nascent, is evolving and becoming increasingly entwined
with core issues of public administration, such as bureaucratic
responsibility and accountability.
Recognizing the diversity of the current literature, this article
advocates for an interdisciplinary approach to deepen and refine
the deep state concept. Insights from research on governance,
democratization, and populism could enrich discussions on ex-
ecutive power and the administrative state. Similarly, those re-
searching the military-­industrial complex might find valuable
insights from studies on informal political and bureaucratic
networks. This cross-­pollination of ideas could contribute to the
rich tapestry of the literature and advance the development of
the concept.
Finally, despite its nebulous nature, the deep state discussions
offer valuable insights into why accountability mechanisms fail
to work, how to respond to ethical dilemmas about bureaucratic
autonomy and political control, and how to manage trade-­offs
between security and individual liberties.
This study is not without limits. The search for keywords is re-
stricted to “deep state” to ensure the analytical coherence of the
results and establish the boundaries of the study. This choice
inevitably leaves some studies outside the analyses. A second
limitation is that this article focuses mainly on the article meta-
data and presents limited insights from findings from these
articles, limiting its scope. This is an outcome of the fact that
deep conceptual and theoretical discussions can be handled in
voluminous texts and require separate and distinct intellectual
efforts.

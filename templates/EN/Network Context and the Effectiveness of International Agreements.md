Network Context and the Effectiveness of International Agreements
BRANDON J KINNE

Why do some international agreements yield more cooperation than others? I argue that the network context of agreements
conditions their effectiveness. I focus on bilateral defense cooperation agreements (DCAs), which promote defense activities
like joint military exercises, peacekeeping, arms trade, and the sharing of classified information. Because DCAs emphasize
ongoing cooperative actions, they offer an ideal setting to assess treaty effectiveness. The analysis shows that when agreements
are embedded in transitive “friend of friend” relations, characterized by extensive ties to common third parties, they generate
higher levels of cooperation. I argue that this network effect is the result of policy convergence. When states share ties with
common third parties, their own policies become more closely aligned, and this alignment in turn reduces the costs and
increases the benefits of cooperative actions. The theory and findings developed here apply to a wide array of cooperative
interactions across multiple issue areas. The effectiveness of international agreements depends on network context.

Introduction
As Cold War tensions waned in the mid-1990s, the United
States formalized defense relations with emerging democ-
racies in Eastern Europe. These partnerships involved bilat-
eral agreements promoting a wide range of defense-related
activities, including scientific and technical cooperation,
military research, security of classified information, training,
and officer exchanges. Yet, high-resolution event data, col-
lected from regional news reports and similar sources, show
that the substantive impact of formal defense ties varied
widely ( Boschee et al. 2015 ). For some countries—Ukraine,
Poland, and the Czech Republic—these agreements pro-
duced dozens of unique instances of defense cooperation
every year. For others—Hungary, Moldova, and Lithuania—
they appear to have had little effect.

This article examines the influence of network context
on the effectiveness of bilateral agreements. Though of-
ten studied as discrete events, agreements do not exist
in a vacuum. Rather, they comprise a larger network of
relationships ( Hollway and Koskinen 2016 ; Warren 2016 ;
Beardsley 2022 ). Some agreements are deeply embedded in
webs of interdependent ties, while others are relatively iso-
lated. I argue that an agreement’s effectiveness or efficacy—
that is, whether it yields substantive cooperative behaviors—
depends on network context. While international relations
(IR) scholars have studied the role of network structure in
various conflict-cooperation scenarios (e.g., Dorussen and
Ward 2008 ; Kinne 2013 ; Chyzh 2016 ; Lupu and Greenhill
2017 ; Duque 2018 ; Beardsley et al. 2020 ; Lee 2023 ), schol-
ars have not yet considered whether the network context of
agreements affects the ability of those agreements to achieve
their intended goals. To the best of my knowledge, this is the
first study to theorize and empirically examine the impact of
networks on treaty effectiveness.
Research on effectiveness typically focuses on high-profile
multilateral agreements, such as those on human rights or
arms control (e.g., Fuhrmann and Lupu 2016 ; Creamer and
Simmons 2019 ). Studies of bilateral agreements, by con-
trast, have largely focused on economic areas like trade
and investment (e.g., Neumayer and Spess 2005 ; Baier and
Bergstrand 2007 ; Dür, Baccini, and Elsig 2014 ). I analyze
bilateral defense cooperation agreements (DCAs). These
agreements have proliferated widely as states have shifted
their defense and security postures toward a more diverse
range of interstate and nontraditional threats. By 2010,
there were nearly 2,000 active DCAs, with new agreements
signed at a rate of 100–150 per year. DCAs are now the dom-
inant institutional avenue through which states “pursue co-
operation in defense, military, and security issues” ( Kinne
2020 , 730).
The characteristics of DCAs are particularly advantageous
in assessing treaty efficacy. DCAs promote routine forms of
defense cooperation—joint military exercises (JMEs), arms
trade, research and development, personnel exchanges,
sharing of classified information, etc.—thus creating an im-
mediate expectation for signatories to engage in substantive
defense activities. At the same time, DCAs are strictly bilat-
eral, lack strong enforcement mechanisms, and involve no
intergovernmental organizations—features that cleanly sep-
arate efficacy from the influence of institutionalized punish-
ments, multilateral politics, formal organizations, and simi-
lar confounders.
I argue that although governments sign DCAs with the in-
tention of achieving joint security gains, the quantity of co-
operation generated by any given DCA depends on network
context, where network context is defined in terms of third-
party or “triadic” ties ( Burger and Buskens 2009 ). Ceteris
paribus, when DCA partners share defense ties with com-
mon third-party states, their respective bilateral agreements
generate more cooperation. Bilateral ties interact with net-
work context. I further argue that this network effect is the
result of policy convergence ( Bennett 1991 ; Drezner 2005 ;
Greenhill 2010 ). Third-party defense partnerships bring
states’ defense policies into closer alignment, and this align-
ment in turn reduces the costs and increases the benefits of
cooperative actions. I develop a typology of network-driven
policy convergence centered on four types of third-party in-
fluence: (i) active facilitation , where a third party deliberately
attempts to align the policies of its partners; (ii) opportunis-
tic alignment , where states sign agreements in order to align
their policies with disconnected potential partners; (iii)
coincidental alignment , where states’ policies come into closer
alignment simply by virtue of alignment with a common
third party; and (iv) lock-in effects , which involve increased
costs for severing ties with one group of partners and/or
creating ties with a new group. Importantly, this typology ap-
plies to a wide range of bilateral issue areas, beyond defense
cooperation.
I test the argument using a broad measure of bilateral de-
fense and security cooperation derived from global event
data ( Boschee et al. 2015 ). The empirical model accounts
for numerous sources of confounding, including treaty se-
lection effects and statistical dependencies in outcome vari-
ables. Across multiple specifications, I find that, absent
third-party ties, bilateral DCAs have little to no effect. As
third-party ties accumulate, DCAs generate increasing lev-
els of defense cooperation. Using placebo-like tests to as-
sess bias, I find that, consistent with the logic of policy
convergence, the conditional effect of network context is
strongest for those pairs of states that have not otherwise
had an opportunity to align their policies. In the online ap-
pendix, I examine specific areas of defense cooperation—
JMEs, peacekeeping missions, arms trade, and mutual third-
party support in conflicts—and find results similar to those
for the more general measure. I further extend the anal-
ysis to wholly different forms of cooperation—preferential
trade agreements (PTAs) and bilateral investment treaties
(BITs)—and find that network context also determines co-
operative outcomes in those issue areas. Overall, the find-
ings point to a general conclusion: the effectiveness of inter-
national agreements depends on network context.
The Study of Treaty Effectiveness
Though often conflated with compliance, treaty effective-
ness is a distinct phenomenon ( Martin 2013 ; Meyer 2014 ).
Compliance is a legal concept that refers to whether state
behavior conforms to an agreement’s formal requirements
( Simmons 2010 ). By contrast, effectiveness is an empirical
concept focused on whether an agreement changes a state’s
behavior “from what it would have been in the absence of
the law” ( Meyer 2014 , 94). Martin ( 2013 , 591) argues that
if we wish to assess the causal impact of institutions, social
scientists must shift focus from compliance to effectiveness.
Research on effectiveness is fractured. Scholars have
examined the effects of specific multilateral agreements and
treaty-based organizations, such as the Convention Against
Torture ( Creamer and Simmons 2019 ), the Nuclear Non-
proliferation Treaty ( Fuhrmann and Lupu 2016 ), and the
International Criminal Court ( Jo and Simmons 2016 ). Yet,
multilateral treaties comprise only a small percentage of
formal international agreements. And as states increasingly
work to circumvent the cooperation problems that plague
multilateralism ( Thompson and Verdier 2014 ), bilateral
strategies have displaced multilateral approaches. In the
1950s, governments signed, on average, ten bilateral agree-
ments for every one multilateral agreement. By the 1980s,

The literature also lacks consensus on why efficacy varies.
Scholars often draw attention to implementing legislation,
regime type, and other domestic influences (e.g., Simmons
2010 ; Von Stein 2016 ). Others focus on institutional design
and treaty depth (e.g., Dür, Baccini, and Elsig 2014 ; Mitchell
and Zawahri 2015 ). While these factors certainly matter,
this paper brings attention to an influence that is relevant
across multiple issue areas but has been largely ignored: net-
work context. International relations are, in fact, network
relations ( Hafner-Burton, Kahler, and Montgomery 2009 ).
Scholars have examined the network aspects of trade, diplo-
macy, military alliances, international organizations, and
militarized conflict, among others (e.g., Ward, Siverson, and
Cao 2007 ; Dorussen and Ward 2008 ; Maoz 2010 ; Cranmer,
Desmarais, and Kirkland 2012 ; Kinne 2013 ; Chyzh 2016 ;
Duque 2018 ). Yet, the possibility that the effectiveness of in-
ternational agreements depends on the network structure
of those agreements has not been considered.
To develop a network theory of treaty effectiveness, I fo-
cus on bilateral DCAs. These agreements first emerged in
the 1980s and now number in the thousands ( Kinne 2018 ).
While military alliances and defense pacts focus on contin-
gencies surrounding mutual defense, armed conflict, and
deterrence ( Morrow 2000 ; Leeds et al. 2002 ), DCAs in-
stead promote practical defense cooperative activities, such
as high-level policy coordination, JMEs, training and edu-
cation, peacekeeping operations, defense-related research
and development, weapons procurement and arms trans-
fers, and security of classified information ( Kinne 2020 ,
733).
The characteristics of DCAs are particularly useful in
drawing inferences about network context and treaty effi-
cacy. First, DCAs are “proactive” agreements that encourage
routine, substantive interaction among signatories. Assess-
ing efficacy is a matter of measuring outcomes across rele-
vant issue-areas. 2 Second, DCAs do not specify enforcement
mechanisms, delegate authority to international organiza-
tions, or otherwise involve elements of “hard law.” These
characteristics hold constant such potential confounders as
treaty design and enforcement. They are also shared by bi-
lateral agreements in other issue areas, which improves the
theory’s generalizability. Third, DCAs are strictly bilateral,
which means they can be analyzed as standard unipartite
networks. Network analysis of multilateral agreements, by
contrast, requires more complicated approaches, such as bi-
partite or affiliation network models ( Latapy, Magnien, and
Del Vecchio 2008 ; Hollway and Koskinen 2016 ).
Finally, as noted above, DCAs have practical importance.
Alliances remain crucial to international security, but their
numbers have changed little since the early 1990s. The DCA
network, by contrast, continues to densify and evolve. Fur-
ther, DCAs address not just interstate security concerns but
also terrorism, trafficking, organized crime, and the other
nontraditional threats that define contemporary global se-
curity. Former US Secretary of Defense Ash Carter de-
scribed DCAs as part of a “principled and inclusive security

A Network Theory of Treaty Effectiveness
International cooperation involves a cost-benefit trade-off
( Morrow 1994 ). For DCAs, the benefits of cooperation com-
prise a basket of joint products, some of which primarily ac-
crue to individual states and others of which resemble club
or public goods ( Cornes and Sandler 1984 ). These bene-
fits include professionalization and modernization of mil-
itaries; collaboration on regional security issues like mar-
itime piracy and state failure; and responsiveness to global
security concerns like transnational terrorism and nuclear
weapons ( Kinne 2018 , 801). More generally, states simply
wish to improve their security positions with regard to the
myriad threats that define contemporary global security.
Achieving these gains requires not just formal treaty com-
mitments but concrete cooperative activities.
The costs of defense cooperation take a variety of forms.
Because policy coordination defines cooperation as such
( Keohane 1984 , Chapter 4), I focus here on the costs of
aligning one’s defense policies with those of one’s partners.
Given that such costs afflict cooperation generally and are
not limited to defense cooperation ( Milner 1997 , 46), this
focus ensures the theory’s relevance to multiple issue areas.
Changes in defense policy, in particular, are vulnerable to
costs emanating from domestic political priorities ( Lindsay
1987 ), defense budgets and economic growth ( Smith 1995 ),
technological innovations ( Bellais and Guichard 2006 ), and
political conflict ( Biddle and Friedman 2008 ).
As with many contemporary treaties, DCAs are frame-
work agreements ( Matz-Lück 2014 ). They enable bilateral
cooperation by providing a legal framework for signatories
to subsequently deploy protocols, implementing arrange-
ments, agency directives, domestic legislation, and other
policy actions to initiate defense activities ( Kinne 2020 ,
732). Given this straightforward cooperative process, the key
question is why some DCAs increase cooperation more than
others. States enter into treaties at varying degrees of pol-
icy distance ( Drezner 2005 ). Highly divergent policies ne-
cessitate deeper, costlier shifts ( Morrow 2000 , 69–70), while
convergent policies require less adjustment and thus fewer
costs. Governments often cannot fully foresee the costs of
cooperation, such that policy distance is uncertain, and
those costs may shift unexpectedly in response to domes-
tic political dynamics and other influences ( Svolik 2006 ). If
the costs of policy adjustment are too great, states will re-
tain their status-quo policies and refrain from cooperation
( Drezner 2005 , 846). Further, because bilateral agreements
facilitate detection and reciprocity ( Thompson and Verdier
2014 ), if one state avoids cooperation due to perceived high
costs, others may reciprocate in kind, diminishing the pro-
duction of joint security goods ( Sandler and Hartley 2001 ).
As Pannier and Schmitt ( 2014 , 271) observe, “policy conver-
gence is a prerequisite to effective cooperation.”
Building on this logic, I argue that certain network con-
texts facilitate policy convergence , defined here as an increased

Characteristics of Defense Policy
Defense policy involves strategic considerations about which
threats to prioritize and how to address those threats ( Posen
and Ross 1996 ), as well as technical considerations about in-
teroperability, logistics and supply, and standards for sharing
classified material, among others. Policy divergence at the
strategic level poses a fundamental barrier to defense coop-
eration. In recent decades, grand strategies have vacillated
between, on the one hand, a focus on interstate threats and
great-power competition ( Mearsheimer 2001 ; Porter 2019 )
and, on the other hand, a prioritization of nontraditional
threats like civil war, failed states, and transnational terror-
ism ( Ikenberry 2001 ; Suri 2009 ). If states disagree on which
threats to prioritize, they will find little common ground for
substantive cooperation.
Divergence in technical policy also limits cooperation.
For example, interoperability—or the ability of systems to
provide services to or accept services from other systems
( Moon, Fewell, and Reynolds 2008 , 5)—is crucial not only
for peacekeeping, joint exercises, counterterrorism, and
other live operations, but also for more routine activities like
arms transfers, resupply of spare parts, and officer training.
Participation in live operations requires coordination in lo-
gistics & supply—or processes for “moving, supplying, and
maintaining military forces” ( Thorpe 1986 , xi)—ranging
from food, water, and health services to fuel, weapons, am-
munition, and spare parts ( Van Creveld 2004 ). Defense
partners must further standardize protocols for the secure
transfer, disclosure, storage, and destruction of classified in-
formation ( Reveron 2006 ). Discrepancies in technical stan-
dards increase the costs of the substantive activities that
DCAs are meant to promote, while convergence facilitates
cooperation.
Transitivity and Network Context
From a network perspective, states are actors or “nodes” in
a global network, where formal agreements comprise the
ties or “edges” of that network ( Maoz 2010 ). Interactions be-
tween nodes, though separable, are not independent. Edges
influence one another. This interdependence draws atten-
tion to DCA partners’ local network context, as illustrated in
figure 1 . In some cases, DCA partners have few if any extra-
dyadic connections. In other cases, those partners share
DCA ties with common k third-party states, or “friends of
friends.”
Such friend-of-friend arrangements, known as triangles or
transitive triads, are an essential network structure. Because
triads enable social arrangements that are impossible with
only two actors—mediation, brokerage, and coalitions—
they are the building blocks of networks ( Simmel 1950 ).
Triads are also ingredients of more complex network fea-
tures, such as hierarchy and clustering ( Newman 2006 ). Tri-
ads thus offer fundamental insights into the effects of struc-
ture on behavior. While more complex ways of defining net-

Figure 2 illustrates the shifting topology of the global DCA
network using hive plots ( Krzywinski et al. 2012 ). Three in-
sights are worth noting. (i) The network has substantially
densified over time. (ii) Bilateral DCA ties are increasingly
embedded in transitive triads, as shown by the colored edges
of the hive plots. Further, yearly clustering coefficients (bot-

in the network as a whole has steadily, if unevenly, increased
( Watts and Strogatz 1998 ). At the same time, transitivity
still varies across edges; some DCAs involve numerous third-

work has become less centralized, or less dominated by a
small number of influential nodes, as shown by annual cen-

that were not well connected to one another. Decreased
centralization reflects both greater connectivity among the
“spokes” themselves and a potential shift away from hierar-
chical, great-power politics ( Mearsheimer 2001 ; Lake 2009 ;
Maoz 2012 ).
Network Context, Policy Convergence, and Defense Cooperation
I argue that the third-party ties of transitive triads pull states’
defense policies into closer alignment, thus reducing the
costs and increasing the benefits of defense cooperation. Bi-
lateral DCAs interact with network context. Ceteris paribus,
a bilateral DCA’s effectiveness increases as the number of
transitive triads shared by those DCA partners increases.

Third, in coincidental alignment the defense policies of
states converge simply by virtue of coordination with a com-
mon third party, absent facilitation or opportunism. This
mechanism reflects a phenomenon widely observed in social
networks wherein transitive triads promote belief similarity
or “homophily” among actors ( Asikainen et al. 2020 ). When
two actors align their policies with a common third party,
they indirectly align those same policies between them-
selves. For example, the transition in the Asia-Pacific region
from a hub-and-spoke system—dominated by bilateral re-
lations with the United States—to a “web of security rela-
tions” was enabled by Asia-Pacific governments’ shared his-
tory of cooperation with the United States ( Blair and Hanley
2001 , 15). While coincidental alignment is particularly ob-
vious in concrete areas like arms trade—e.g., states that
import weapons from the same third party can more eas-
ily exchange materials in secondary markets and collabo-
rate in trainings, exercises, and live operations—it poten-
tially affects all varieties of defense policy. Australia and
Japan’s respective third-party ties facilitated direct activities
like arms procurement, joint ballistic missile defense, intel-
ligence sharing, and force complementarity ( Wilkins 2015 ,
102–3).
Fourth, transitive triads generate lock-in effects , where “ties
formed with one actor place constraints on ties with oth-
ers” ( Gulati, Nohria, and Zaheer 2000 , 210). These con-
straints arise from resource limitations or, in the case of
DCAs, the costs of policy adjustments. When a state aligns
its policies with a given set of partners, the opportunity costs
of aligning with different partners increase. For example,
arms-exporting countries push for monopolistic relations
in part to restrict arms importers’ ability to cooperate with
outsiders ( Kinsella 1998 ). In 2019, US officials expressed
concern over India’s potential purchase of S-400 missiles
from Russia, arguing that a switch to Russian technology
would endanger interoperability with US systems. 6 Lock-in
effects also deepen cooperation among existing partners.
When the US authorized deployment of the P-8 Poseidon
aircraft to Singapore, Secretary Carter argued that the move
would promote interoperability and coordination between
Singapore and regional militaries. 7 Lock-in effects are not

Through these four avenues, third-party ties reduce the
policy distance between states and lower the costs of en-
gaging in cooperative actions. Transitive triads thus gener-
ate network efficiencies ( Jackson and Wolinsky 1996 ; Kinne
and Kang 2023 ). The utility of a given i j tie is not solely a
function of the tie itself but varies according to the broader
network context in which that tie is embedded. Ceteris
paribus, by lowering the costs of cooperation, ties embed-
ded within transitive triads generate more utility than stan-
dalone ties. This increase in utility improves the cost-benefit
trade-off of cooperation and lowers the odds of defection.
Further, because the utility of network effects increases with
the number of participants ( Katz and Shapiro 1994 ), as a
given i j dyad’s third-party ties increase in number, so do
the prospects for substantive cooperation. This logic yields
a simple hypothesis:
H1
The mor e thir d-party DCA ties a given pair of DCA
partners have in common, the greater the level of bilateral
defense cooperation between them .
This emphasis on policy convergence contrasts with the-
ories of cooperation that focus on information ( Simmons
2010 , 287), which argue that institutions alleviate bargain-
ing problems in part by facilitating information flows (e.g.,
Boehmer, Gartzke, and Nordstrom 2004 ). In networks, indi-
rect ties allow states to signal their cooperative intentions
credibly, thus reducing uncertainty ( Dorussen and Ward
2008 ; Kinne 2013 ; Lupu and Greenhill 2017 ). Extrapolating
from this logic, third-party ties may shed light on a potential
partner’s trustworthiness, and this information, in turn, may
improve prospects for direct cooperation.
I favor the policy-convergence mechanism over the infor-
mation mechanism for two reasons. First, informational ar-
guments assume that cooperation is impeded primarily by
concerns over cheating, as in one-shot prisoner’s dilemma.
Yet, few scenarios in treaty politics involve such starkly ex-
ploitative incentives ( Wagner 1983 ; Sandler 2008 ). As with
most self-enforcing agreements, the primary risk associated
with DCAs is not exploitation but the possibility that one’s
partners simply will not follow through on their commit-
ments ( Oye 1985 ; Morrow 1994 ). This risk, in turn, is largely
a function of costs, such as the cost of policy alignment. Sec-
ond, insofar as cheating concerns exist in defense coopera-
tion, they are most apparent when states initially commit to a
formal agreement. Trust is a “necessary condition” for DCA
signature ( Kinne 2018 , 810). The act of signing an agree-
ment is itself an indication of cooperative intent and con-
veys a minimum level of trustworthiness ( Kydd 2005 ). Put
differently, if mistrust between two countries is sufficiently
high as to imperil subsequent cooperation, those countries
are unlikely to sign a DCA in the first place. I thus anticipate
that, once an agreement materializes, issues of trustworthi-
ness and information are less relevant than issues of policy
alignment. The online appendix includes placebo tests con-
ducted under an assumed information mechanism, which
finds little support.

The main source of confounding with DCAs is nonran-
dom selection into agreements—a problem that is well
known in the study of treaty compliance (e.g., Simmons
2010 ; Fuhrmann and Lupu 2016 ). States strategically choose
which treaties to join and which to ignore. Thus, the factors
that influence DCA membership may be correlated with out-
comes of interest. Because we observe the full population
of observations, not a truncated sample, Heckman mod-
els and similar sample-selection techniques are inappropri-
ate; rather, nonrandom selection is an omitted variable bias
problem ( Lupu 2013 ; Vance and Ritter 2014 ).
The model accounts for three distinct sources of nonran-
dom selection: covariate, network-covariate, and network-
outcome. Covariate-based confounding involves standard
dyadic and monadic influences, which mainly affect bilat-
eral DCA formation (i.e., DCA Bilateral i j ). Following Kinne
(2018) , I control for known determinants of DCAs that may
be related to bilateral defense cooperation, including log-
transformed trade flows ( Barbieri and Keshk 2016 ), differ-
ence in ideal points in UN General Assembly voting ( Bailey,
Strezhnev, and Voeten 2017 ), and formal military alliances
( Leeds 2005 ). I also control for i and j’s respective levels of
democracy ( Boix, Miller, and Rosato 2012 ), per-capita gross
domestic product ( Feenstra, Inklaar, and Timmer 2015 ) and
military capabilities ( Singer 1987 ). 10 These covariates reflect
states’ willingness and incentives to engage in bilateral de-
fense cooperation.
Network-covariate confounding involves higher-order
and multilateral effects of standard covariates. Third-party
ties in trade, alliances, foreign-policy preferences, or other
areas may influence a country’s propensity to form DCAs.
For example, i and j may be members of the same multi-
lateral defense pact, or they may be common adversaries of
a rising power (e.g., China). If these higher-order relations
lead i and j both to sign a DCA and increase their bilat-
eral defense cooperation, an analysis that excludes higher-
order relations will suffer from omitted variable bias. This
confounding, where the right-hand-side terms exercise in-
fluence beyond standard dyadic or monadic constructions,
is an important but rarely modeled aspect of networks. I es-
timate higher-order relations in covariates using a multiplex
community detection algorithm, applied to all of the above
covariates ( Mucha et al. 2010 ). 11 The resulting term, de-
noted ω i j , indicates pairs of countries with similar patterns
of ties. Because this term considers every country’s relations
with every other country across multiple substantive dimen-
sions, it controls for a wide variety of arbitrarily complex

in the main specification, and they are consistent with pol-
icy convergence as the primary mechanism behind network
context.
Conclusion
Bilateral agreements offer a unique opportunity to exam-
ine the structural impact of global networks. Although IR
scholars frequently study such agreements as discrete phe-
nomena, driven solely by monadic or dyadic attributes, the
results here show that the larger network context of these
agreements plays an important role. This finding has practi-
cal significance. Countries that wish to increase cooperative
activities may find that, in a sparse network context, bilateral
agreements have little effect, and governments with seem-
ingly disparate interests may achieve beneficial outcomes if
third-party ties bring their policies into closer alignment.
Although I have developed and tested the argument with
regard to defense cooperation, the logic is highly gener-
alizable. The theory especially applies to policy-rich issues
like the environment and climate change ( Hollway and
Koskinen 2016 ), global public health ( Taylor 2004 ), cross-
border taxation ( Barthel and Neumayer 2012 ), and transna-
tional terrorism ( Sandler 2003 ), among many others. At
present, empirical research into these and similar issue areas
is limited only by data availability. Nonetheless, the analysis
points to the broad conclusion that network context plays
a central role in treaty efficacy. Additional analyses in the
online appendix show, for example, that third-party con-
nections increase the effectiveness of BITs and PTAs. In-
progress work explores in greater detail those areas of bi-
lateral cooperation that lie outside of defense and security
issues, with a particular focus on how the logic of network-
driven policy convergence can be adjusted and extended to
apply generically to all varieties of cooperation.

Potential sources of variation across treaties are also wor-
thy of exploration. I find that network context matters for
multiple subtypes of DCAs, but DCAs tend to be similar in
structure; in other issue areas, bilateral agreements may be
more varied. If those variations allow for less overlap in the
substantive content of agreements, then the convergent ef-
fect of third-party ties may not materialize. On the other
hand, scholars have recently shown that states have a ten-
dency to “copy and paste” the text of new treaties from prior
agreements ( Manger and Peinhardt 2017 ). According to the
logic of network-driven policy convergence, such a practice
may have unanticipated effects beyond bilateral outcomes,
potentially spilling over into relations among partners.
Multilateralism offers yet another area of future ex-
ploration. The exclusively bilateral nature of DCAs eases
the burden on theory development and empirical analy-
sis. But in some issue areas, states prefer multilateral ar-
rangements ( Thompson and Verdier 2014 ). Examining the
network context of multilateral agreements poses unique
challenges. In most multilateral agreements, every node is
“connected to”—i.e., comembers with—every other node,
which precludes variation across nodes. The regime com-
plex literature offers one potential way forward ( Raustiala
and Victor 2004 ; Keohane and Victor 2011 ). By exam-
ining how multiple layers of institutions intersect with
varying state memberships, analysts may be able to de-
rive insights about the strength of network ties, the po-
sitions of specific nodes, and the structure of the over-
all network (e.g., see Hollway 2021 ). Advancements in the
study of bipartite and multiplex networks offer promising
methodological approaches to such an inquiry ( Snijders,
Lomi, and Torló 2013 ). Given the central role that pol-
icy plays in regime complexes, the same policy-convergence
mechanisms that promote cooperation in bilateral networks
should, in principle, also promote cooperation multilater-
ally.

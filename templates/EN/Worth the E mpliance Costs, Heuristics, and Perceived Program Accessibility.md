Abstract
Administrative burdens can deter individuals from engaging with government programs before they ever encounter formal
application requirements. Drawing on the administrative burden framework and theories of heuristic decision-making, this
study examines how prospective applicants form early judgments about program accessibility when presented with varying
levels of compliance costs. Using three survey experiments centered on a fictional grocery benefit (N = 2,407, N = 965) and a
one-time federal tax rebate (N = 1,004), we assess how documentation requirements and effort cues shape perceptions of eligi­
bility, willingness to apply, and perceived accessibility. We find that greater documentation requirements or mismatched time
cues lowered perceived eligibility and willingness to apply. Yet asking respondents to pause and estimate the effort reversed
those effects, but only for individuals who already possessed the documentation or could form a concrete time estimate
about the effort. These findings highlight the role of expected burden and heuristic judgments in shaping pre-application de­
cisions, extending administrative burden research beyond realized experiences to the earliest stages of program engagement.
Key words administrative burden, accessibility, heuristics, compliance costs, survey experiment
Introduction
Administrative burdens can present substantial barriers to ac­
cessing government assistance, often discouraging eligible indi­
viduals from completing the steps required to receive benefits.1
Across many social programs, take-up rates remain well below el­
igibility, including in core safety-net programs such as Temporary
Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) and the Supplemental
Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). This persistent gap un­
derscores a central problem: administrative burdens can deter
otherwise eligible individuals from engaging with programs de­
signed to provide support (Bhargava and Manoli 2015; Halling and
Baekgaard 2024; Herd and Moynihan 2019).
Of the three primary forms of administrative burden—learning,
compliance, and psychological costs—compliance costs at the ap­
plication stage may be especially consequential. The time, finan­
cial, and cognitive demands of applying can shape early judgments
about whether a program is “worth the effort” (Christensen et al.
2020; Currie 2004). Yet these early judgments are not necessarily
grounded in a detailed understanding of the requirements. Instead,
people lean on heuristic shortcuts that translate a documentation
list or a time cue into an immediate sense of expected burden.

trative burdens affect program take-up, well-being, and trust in

factor shaping early judgments about whether to engage with a

Administrative burdens and access
Administrative burden refers to “individuals' experience of policy
implementation as onerous” when interacting with public servic­
es (Herd and Moynihan 2019: 22). These experiences arise from
practical and procedural features of program rules and day-to-day
implementation, such as documentation requirements, complex
forms, or unclear eligibility criteria (Burden et al. 2012; Herd and
Moynihan 2019). These hurdles often stem from intentional policy
design decisions and arise during the implementation process to
create friction when the public experiences or attempts to access
government services. Importantly, administrative burdens need
not be directly encountered to shape behavior; individuals may
form expectations about the time, effort, or frustration involved
in engaging with a program based on available cues and prior
knowledge. Burdens can have serious consequences: not only
do they deter individuals from accessing essential services but
also weaken the effectiveness of policies meant to provide public
support (Bhargava and Manoli 2015; Halling and Baekgaard 2024;
Heinrich 2016).
To better understand these impacts, Herd and Moynihan (2019)
break administrative burden into three distinct components:
learning, compliance, and psychological costs. Learning costs
arise when individuals need to acquire new knowledge to under­
stand their eligibility or the benefits offered by a program, such
as knowing application deadlines or which documents to submit.
Compliance costs are the logistical actions required to complete
the process, from gathering documentation to attending required
appointments or paying fees. Psychological costs include the
emotional toll experienced by applicants, such as stress, stig­
ma, and feelings of dehumanization. These types of costs often
intersect, particularly for individuals who may already lack the
resources to navigate these complex requirements (Christensen
et al. 2020; Mullainathan and Shafir 2013b).
Over the last decade, scholars and practitioners have turned
their attention to understanding the impact of administra­
tive burdens on both the public and government programs
(Bhargava and Manoli 2015; Daigneault and Macé 2020; Fox,
Feng, and Reynolds 2023; Yates et  al. 2022). One of the most
critical impacts is the reduction in program take-up rates, where
burdens such as complex application procedures and extensive
documentation requirements deter otherwise eligible individu­
als from enrolling in benefits (Herd et al. 2013; Hock et al. 2021).
Studies have also shown that these burdens disproportionately
affect marginalized populations and those with fewer resourc­
es, including low-income individuals, the elderly, and those
with limited education, leading to under-utilization of essential
services like healthcare, food assistance, and welfare programs
(Heinrich 2016).
Administrative burdens also exacerbate social and economic
inequalities by imposing greater hurdles on those who are least
equipped to navigate complex bureaucratic processes (Heinrich
2018; Kyle and Frakt 2021; Olsen, Kyhse-Andersen, and Moynihan
2022). For instance, Christensen et al. (2020) highlight that individ­
uals with lower levels of human capital experience higher learning
and compliance costs, which impede their ability to access ben­
efits and support. This unequal distribution of burdens contrib­
utes to a cycle of disadvantages, where vulnerable groups face
compounded difficulties in improving their socioeconomic status
(Herd and Moynihan 2019). Moreover, the anticipation of burdens
can deter individuals from even attempting to engage with gov­
ernment services due to expected stress or stigma (Baekgaard
and Madsen 2024).
More recent research examines the psychological consequenc­
es of administrative burdens for individuals' well-being and en­
gagement with public institutions. Psychological costs, including
stress, shame, and loss of autonomy, can undermine mental
health and trust in government when administrative processes
are experienced as personal affronts rather than neutral bureau­
cratic procedures (Herd and Moynihan 2019; Soss 1999, 2000).
These emotional costs may also discourage future interactions
with government programs and contribute to reduced civic en­
gagement, particularly when burdens erode individuals' sense of
autonomy (Madsen and Mikkelsen 2022).
Much research on administrative burden examines what hap­
pens after applicants enter the application process, even though
the decision to engage often occurs earlier. Anticipated or ex­
pected burden, meaning what people expect the paperwork and
documentation process, learning curve, or emotional toll to be
(Baekgaard and Madsen 2024), can shape decisions to apply and
attitudes toward a program prior to direct interaction. If indi­
viduals anticipate long waits, confusing paperwork, or stressful
interactions, they may never initiate the process. While existing
research documents how compliance costs affect participation,
there is more limited understanding of how perceptions of those
costs, particularly expected time and effort, shape willingness to
engage at the outset. This study contributes to that literature by
examining the heuristic processes individuals use when assessing
administrative requirements and how these mental shortcuts in­
fluence attitudes toward program participation.

Disparities in program take-up
Administrative burdens are widely understood to be associated
with whether individuals complete an application or never ini­
tiate one. One common way scholars assess this relationship is
by examining take-up, defined as the share of eligible individuals
or households who participate in and receive program benefits.
Take-up captures the gap between eligibility and enrollment and
is often used as an indicator of how effectively a program reaches
its intended population. Persistently low take-up can limit policy
impact and leave eligible individuals without access to essential
resources (Aizer 2007; Currie 2004; Herd and Moynihan 2019; Ko
and Moffitt 2022; Linos and Riesch 2020).
Estimating take-up is challenging, as many programs lack
standardized data on the eligible population (Bryant and Huang
2023) and, in some cases, low enrollment reflects supply con­
straints rather than applicant choices. For example, housing
vouchers and other capped or wait-listed programs where fund­
ing covers only a share of those eligible. Even so, substantial gaps
persist across US assistance programs. In 2019, only twenty-three
of every 100 eligible families received benefits from Temporary
Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) (Pavetti and Safawi 2021).
Government estimates indicate that roughly one-third of those
eligible for Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and the Special
Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and
Children (WIC) do not receive benefits, while take-up for energy,
housing, and childcare assistance remains below 22 percent (Falk
et al. 2015). Gaps also persist in other prominent programs like
the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP)2 and the
Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)3, with each showing a take-up
shortfall of approximately 18–21 percent. These patterns indicate
that many eligible individuals fail to access essential government
support.
Yet, take-up gaps are unevenly distributed across programs and
sociodemographic groups (Brodkin and Majmundar 2010; Gibson
2003; Kuka and Stuart 2021). Research shows that individuals
with the lowest incomes and greatest need often face lower par­
ticipation rates and greater difficulty navigating application com­
plexity than expected (Bhargava and Manoli 2015; Tempelman
and Houkes-Hommes 2016). Disparities in take-up are also as­
sociated with race and ethnicity, education, family structure,
gender, and age (Aizer 2003; Bargain, Immervoll, and Viitamäki
2012; Blumenthal, Erard, and Ho 2005; Blundell, Fry, and Walker
1988; Kuka and Stuart 2021). Prior contact with welfare programs
tends to increase participation, while concerns related to citizen­
ship status or documentation requirements deter take-up among
some groups (Aizer and Currie 2004; Alsan and Yang 2024; Currie
and Gruber 1996; Kaushal and Kaestner 2005).
Variation in take-up underscores that non-enrollment among
eligible individuals is a multifaceted phenomenon with no single
cause or solution. Prior research has approached this problem
from multiple angles, with mixed results. Studies of targeting
and outreach show that program materials using destigmatiz­
ing language or simplified messaging can increase take-up for

Existing explanations of take-up patterns generally emphasize
a common set of factors (Currie 2004; Janssens and Van Mechelen
2022; Kleven and Kopczuk 2011; Riphahn 2001). Benefit size and
duration matter, with larger or longer-term benefits associated
with higher participation, particularly among individuals with
sustained need (Anderson and Meyer 1997; Bargain, Immervoll,
and Viitamäki 2012; Blank and Ruggles 1996). Access to informa­
tion and prior program contact also shape enrollment, as famili­
arity with social assistance can lower informational barriers (Aizer
and Currie 2004; Currie and Gruber 1996; Saarela 2004). Stigma,
whether arising from social judgment or legal concerns, further
depresses take-up (Manchester and Mumford 2010; Moffitt 1983;
Riphahn 2001). Finally, transaction costs interact with these fac­
tors, as higher compliance demands are more tolerable when per­
ceived benefits are substantial, but can deter participation when
access feels costly or uncertain (Currie 2000, 2004).
While existing research points to many contributing factors, it
rarely isolates how people’s expectations about those costs can
guide the decision to apply. To better understand this process, the
next section breaks compliance costs into multiple components
and examines how higher demands can affect enrollment. We
then outline a heuristic model that traces how people turn those
expected costs into quick judgments about whether to apply and
how they rate a program’s value.
Expected compliance costs and
enrollment
Access to benefits rarely involves a single type of burden. Learning,
compliance, and psychological costs often stack up in one encoun­
ter with the government (Herd and Moynihan 2019). For example,
meeting with a caseworker may involve learning costs if individu­
als are unaware of the documentation required, compliance costs
in arranging transportation or childcare, and psychological costs
from the stress or perceived stigma associated with seeking aid.
When combined, these burdens can amplify one another: the
stress of potential judgment (a psychological cost) may deter ap­
plicants from seeking support for compliance requirements, while
a lack of information (a learning cost) can increase the logistical
and emotional demands of complying with documentation re­
quirements (Christensen et al. 2020; Lipsky 2010).
As discussed, compliance costs are the tangible actions re­
quired to secure benefits. Unlike learning and psychological costs,
which center on acquiring knowledge or managing emotional
burdens, compliance costs cover what potential applicants or cur­
rent program participants must do. These costs can arise at any
point in the application process and are likely to influence pro­
gram take-up by creating barriers that deter eligible individuals
from enrolling.
Compliance costs encompass several dimensions, each pre­
senting unique challenges; among these are time, financial,
cognitive, opportunity, and coordination costs. All of these are
forms of compliance costs that we break down to isolate where

Third, cognitive compliance costs are the mental efforts need­
ed to navigate bureaucratic requirements, such as understanding
technical language, interpreting eligibility rules, and ensuring
accuracy in applications (Burden et al. 2012). Although cognitive
costs overlap somewhat with learning and psychological costs,
they are distinct in that they arise within the steps of the appli­

accuracy and fulfill program requirements. Fourth, opportunity
costs reflect the sacrifices individuals make to meet these compli­
ance demands, such as rearranging work schedules or organizing
childcare. Finally, coordination costs arise when applicants must
interact with multiple agencies or institutions to secure necessary
documents, manage conflicting deadlines, or continually explain
their needs across departments (Herd and Moynihan 2019). These
coordination efforts increase both logistical and cognitive bur­
dens, as applicants attempt to navigate the interconnected bu­
reaucracies involved.
While each type of compliance cost presents challenges, they
collectively create barriers to program take-up. Compliance de­
mands, the state-imposed requirements to access benefits, are
often justified as necessary for verifying eligibility or ensuring
program integrity (Baekgaard et al. 2021). However, they impose
a burden that discourages eligible individuals from completing
applications, especially those with limited resources or complex
life circumstances (Christensen et al. 2020).
Multiple recent studies demonstrate that compliance costs can
have a considerable effect on program participation. Most litera­
ture on compliance costs focuses on evaluating existing program
rules, such as eligibility requirements, documentation demands,
and mandatory appointments, to determine their impact on en­
rollment (Fox, Feng, and Reynolds 2023). Although analyzing pro­
gram rules offers insights into administrative burden, it often fails
to capture how individuals experience, interpret, or anticipate the
demands of navigating these requirements. Scholars like Currie
(2004) argue that focusing solely on program rules overlooks oth­
er critical factors influencing take-up, such as the applicants' own
understanding of program demands. Her theoretical contribution
of take-up transaction costs, or the weighing citizens engage in
when deciding to apply for a program, operates similarly to our
approach to understanding the public’s decisions through cog­
nitive shortcuts when evaluating compliance costs. The study of
compliance costs, therefore, offers a unique perspective on how
logistical and cognitive barriers affect initial take-up decisions,
shifting focus from the rules themselves to personal experiences
navigating requirements.
Based on existing research, we expect that higher compliance
costs will lower willingness to apply by adding logistical and
cognitive hurdles. Time and financial demands impose direct
­obstacles, while the mental effort needed to navigate bureaucrat­
ic language or complex eligibility rules can overwhelm applicants
(Burden et  al. 2012; Herd and Moynihan 2019). Crucially, these
costs do not need to be directly experienced to have an effect.
When people expect long waits, multiple appointments, or con­
fusing forms, the prospect of burden alone can make the applica­
tion seem not worth the effort. Expected costs therefore reduce
both the likelihood of applying and perceptions of a program’s
accessibility and value. As such, we expect the following:
Compliance Costs Hypothesis: Increasing expected compli­
ance costs decreases program take-up and perceptions of
program accessibility.
Heuristics, administrative burden,
and program take-up
When confronted with complex or overwhelming processes, the
public often relies on heuristics or cognitive shortcuts that simplify
decision-making by focusing on immediate, visible cues rather than
deeper assessments of the process (Battaglio et al. 2018; Gigerenzer
and Gaissmaier 2011; Jones 2003; Tversky and Kahneman 1974).
For many government programs, which require individuals to un­
derstand dense rules, gather documentation, and navigate tech­
nical requirements, heuristics provide a way for people to make
faster judgments. Although heuristics help individuals streamline
choices, they can lead to oversimplified decisions and, ultimately,
to misjudgments about program accessibility (Simon 1957). Faced
with information overload or limited time, people frequently sub­
stitute complex judgments with simpler evaluations based on more
accessible cues, such as the apparent length or perceived difficulty
of the process (Ariely 2010; Kumlin 2004; Soss 2000).
Time investment is a particularly influential factor in heuristic-
driven judgments. Research indicates that individuals are quick
to judge the effort required to complete a task based on initial im­
pressions of time, rather than an objective assessment of each re­
quirement. When people encounter a long list of documentation
requirements or detailed instructions, they may quickly conclude
that the process will demand significant time. This perception can
serve as a heuristic cue, leading individuals to dismiss the pro­
gram as “not worth it” without fully considering potential bene­
fits (Ariely 2010; Moynihan and Herd 2010). Here, the perception
of the time burden people expects to face becomes a stand-in for
actual effort, discouraging further engagement and reducing pro­
gram take-up.
Cognitive overload further compounds this effect. When indi­
viduals are required to process extensive information or numer­
ous steps, their cognitive capacity can become strained, leading
them to avoid in-depth evaluations and instead rely on simpler,
heuristic-based judgments (Heinrich 2016). For instance, see­
ing multiple form pages or complex language can signal an “in­
trusive” administrative experience (Kumlin 2004), prompting a
shortcut response to avoid the task even if the end result would
ultimately benefit a potential applicant. Rather than carefully
weighing the costs and benefits of applying, applicants may only
consider visible cues of difficulty, like form length or the number
of steps, which creates a self-reinforcing cycle where complex re­
quirements deter those who need support the most.

These snap judgments are forward-looking. Applicants im­
agine the effort that lies ahead, weigh it against expected
benefits, and decide whether starting the application is worth­
while. In other words, expectation of burden—formed through
heuristics—becomes an early gatekeeper to take-up.
This heuristic approach to assessing administrative burden
aligns closely with findings in the policy feedback literature,
which examines how experiences with government red tape and
bureaucratic hurdles shape individuals' perceptions of govern­
ment programs and influence future behavior (Moynihan and
Herd 2010; Moynihan and Soss 2014; Soss 2000). Direct encoun­
ters with bureaucratic processes, such as completing lengthy
forms or navigating complex eligibility requirements, often leave
lasting impressions on individuals' attitudes toward government
assistance, affecting both their immediate willingness to engage
and their long-term views on government efficacy. However,
these effects are not limited to firsthand experiences; indirect
encounters—such as hearing about the difficulties others face or
recalling prior challenges in accessing unrelated services—can
also inform individuals' perceptions of administrative burden. In
this way, even a single application process can reinforce negative
beliefs, leading citizens to expect similar hurdles in other pro­
grams and ultimately shaping their future interactions with gov­
ernment services.
Perceptions of administrative requirements as intrusive or
overly demanding can, therefore, lead to disengagement, not only
reducing individuals' likelihood of applying but also diminishing
their general trust in government responsiveness. This sense of
alienation can foster a cycle of withdrawal, where individuals
avoid engaging with available programs due to expected burdens
(Herd and Moynihan 2019; Soss 2000). This withdrawal weakens
the reach of social programs, reducing their ability to effectively
serve the eligible population, creating a self-reinforcing feedback
loop: the more burdensome a process appears, the more indi­
viduals opt out, limiting a program’s success and perpetuating
negative attitudes toward government involvement. In this way,
both direct and indirect experiences with bureaucratic complexity
contribute to broader disengagement from public services, high­
lighting the role of administrative burdens in shaping public per­
ceptions and ultimately affecting social program efficacy.
In short, heuristics allow people to bypass what they perceive
as mentally taxing or potentially time-consuming tasks by focus­
ing on immediate, surface-level cues, such as application length
or procedural complexity. While these cognitive shortcuts help
individuals quickly judge the accessibility of a process, they can
lead to misjudgments that deter individuals from applying for val­
uable assistance. When the application process appears lengthy
or complex, people may interpret these visible cues as indicators
of greater time or effort than is actually required, prompting them
to conclude that the program is “not worth it.” This reliance on ini­
tial impressions can prevent individuals from fully considering the
benefits of participation, resulting in lower take-up rates driven by
perceived difficulty rather than actual barriers.
Most applicants do not calculate every step of an application.
They scan the paperwork, translate visible cues into a quick ex­
pectation of time and hassle, and act on that forecast. When those
surface cues signal high costs, the forecast can overshoot reality,
leading people to conclude the program is “not worth it.” Because
the decision rests on a mental shortcut, it can be nudged. Asking
applicants to pause and make an explicit estimate of the time
involved should temper the initial heuristic, raise willingness to
apply, and improve perceptions of the program’s accessibility. As
such, we propose the following hypothesis:
Burden Heuristic Hypothesis: Encouraging individuals to
reflect on the time required for an application will reduce
reliance on initial impressions and increase take-up while
improving perceptions of program accessibility.
Analytic approach
We fielded three survey experiments to examine how heuristics
about compliance costs shape willingness to apply for govern­
ment programs and perceptions of accessibility.4 The first exper­
iment (N = 2,407) introduced a fictional grocery support program
in which we varied documentation requirements while holding el­
igibility constant and randomized whether respondents estimat­
ed the time needed to gather paperwork, allowing us to observe
both unprompted heuristics and the effects of reflection. The sec­
ond experiment (N = 965) informed a new sample of respondents
about expected documentation time using values drawn from the
first study (no time cue, 30 min, or 2 h), testing the impact of ex­
plicit effort information. The third experiment (N = 1,004) shifted
to a tax rebate program and prompted respondents to estimate
the time required to gather different types of documentation, as­
sessing whether these effects generalize across policy contexts.
Together, the experiments capture natural heuristic formation,
responses to supplied information, and generalizability across
programs.
Participants in all three experiments first completed a series of
pre-treatment questions, then read a vignette describing the fic­
tional government program, and finally answered the outcome
measures on take-up and perceived accessibility. The complete
survey instruments and additional details are available in the
Supplementary. We fielded each study to a representative sample
of adults in the United States drawn from Lucid-Theorem, an on­
line survey aggregation platform.5 Lucid’s respondent pools close­
ly align with national US benchmarks, making it a reliable source
for experimental research (Coppock and McClellan 2019). Because
online opt-in panels can suffer from inattentive and low-quality
responses (Ternovski and Orr 2022), we followed best practices
and implemented a series of safeguards. First, we administered a
series of pre-treatment screening questions to ensure inattentive
respondents never completed the survey. Second, we adminis­
tered a manipulation check that required respondents to identify
the program they read about.6 Finally, we conducted a series of

Survey experiment II
While the first experiment suggests heavier documentation re­
quirements amplify perceived burdens, it also suggests that
prompting respondents to reflect on expected effort can, in some
cases, temper these effects. However, that design leaves open
how explicit information about effort, rather than self-generated
estimates, shapes evaluations of compliance costs and willing­
ness to apply. To address this, we recruited a new sample (n = 965)
and reused the extensive documentation vignette from the first
experiment as the baseline. Two treatment conditions added
explicit information about the time early applicants reportedly
spent completing the application: 30 min (one standard deviation
below the first experiment median) or 2 h (the median). In each
condition, respondents were told that early applicants spent an
average of the stated time completing the application, including
the documentation process. Documentation requirements were
otherwise identical across conditions.
Following the vignette, respondents answered the same set of
questions from the first experiment assessing perceived eligibili­
ty, willingness to apply, and views about the application process.
Only after these questions did we ask respondents to estimate
the time required to gather the documentation. This sequencing
prevents respondents' own time estimates from influencing their
initial evaluations, while still allowing comparisons with the first
experiment.
Results: survey experiment II
Figure 5 presents the findings from the second experiment, where
we explicitly anchor the time burden. Panel (a) shows the differ­
ences in perceived eligibility and program support relative to the
baseline, while Panel (b) depicts willingness to apply, perceived
helpfulness, sense applying is worth the time, and interest in
learning more about the program.
The results show that respondents in the 30-min condition ex­
hibited a significantly lower willingness to apply by 0.25 (0.15 SD)
points on the scale and reduced interest in learning more com­
pared to the baseline condition by 0.31 (0.2 SD), but perceptions
of the other outcomes are unchanged. Telling respondents, the
paperwork takes 2 h yields estimate that are statistically indistin­
guishable from the baseline on every outcome.
These initial results echo the first experiment by suggesting
that explicit effort cues are associated with shifts in attitudes but
not in a straightforward “less time, more uptake” fashion. Framing
the task as taking 30 min reduces willingness to apply and learn
more, which may reflect either a low-benefit heuristic, where a
brief application signals limited program value, or skepticism
toward an implausibly low estimate given the stated documenta­
tion requirements. This reaction is consistent with findings from
Keiser and Miller (2020) and Nicholson-Crotty, Miller, and Keiser
(2021), who show that public support varies with how burdens
are framed. Because the design cannot adjudicate between these
mechanisms, we offer a cautious interpretation of this finding:
optimistic or poorly calibrated effort cues could dampen pro­
gram appeal.
Figure  5 shows that explicit time cues can shift average atti­
tudes but cannot reveal what happens when individuals' own
expectations diverge from those cues. To better understand the
impact of that mismatch, we regress each outcome on the gap be­
tween a respondent’s personal estimate and the vignette’s stated
time. We restrict the analysis to respondents in the 30-min and
2-h conditions who supplied an estimate (shown in fig. 6), then
calculate the gap by subtracting the vignette value (0.5 or 2 h)
from their own figure. On average, respondents expected the pa­
perwork to take 1.2 h longer than advertised, allowing us to test
whether larger gaps undermine perceived accessibility and will­
ingness to participate.

Survey experiment III
The first two studies showed that heavier paperwork and mis­
matched effort cues lower willingness to apply for a generous
grocery benefit. To see whether the same quick, effort-based
­heuristics operate in a different setting, we fielded a third experi­
ment built around a modest, one-time federal tax rebate.

and presented everyone with a vignette describing a $250 rebate
available to anyone who has filed a federal tax return in the past
five years (Table 3). Claiming the rebate requires three pieces of

would take to locate and upload each document. Unlike the first
experiment, we asked participants to reflect on each piece of doc­

“I’m not sure.”13

making documentation effort salient still dampens interest when
the payoff is modest, one-time, and almost universally available.

sign cannot determine whether this drop stems from higher per­
ceived burden, expected ineligibility, or both, the reflection helps
differentiate people who can readily act on the benefit from those
who cannot.
Discussion and Conclusion
Understanding program take-up and accessibility is central to
effective program implementation, particularly for programs in­
tended to provide support to vulnerable populations. Building on
the administrative burden framework, this study examines how
people form their earliest judgments about whether applying for
a program is worthwhile. Across three survey experiments, the
evidence points to the role of expected burden: what applicants
believe a process will demand, rather than what it ultimately de­
mands, shapes willingness to engage before anyone confronts
actual paperwork.
Across the three studies, a consistent pattern emerges. When
documentation requirements increase, perceived eligibility de­
clines and program appeal weakens. When compliance demands
remain fixed, but the stated time appears implausibly short,
willingness to apply also falls, although we cannot distinguish
whether this reflects skepticism about the estimate or an infer­
ence that a quick application signals low program value. Most
importantly, the effects of explicitly reflecting on the expected
documentation process are concentrated among respondents
who already possess the required documents and can translate
those requirements into a concrete time estimate. For these indi­
viduals, pausing to reflect is associated with higher willingness to
apply and more favorable assessments of helpfulness, worth, and
interest. By contrast, respondents who lack at least some docu­
mentation or who cannot form a concrete estimate respond less
positively to the same prompt. This pattern appears across both
program contexts studied: a recurring state-administered grocery
benefit and a one-time federal tax rebate.
Taken together, these findings extend the administrative bur­
den framework by showing that applicants form judgments
about program accessibility prior to direct interaction, relying
on expectations about effort rather than realized experience.
The results suggest that early “worth the effort” assessments
are malleable and responsive to how expected burden is framed,
particularly among individuals who are positioned to act on that
information. By focusing on anticipatory burdens, this study
draws attention to a population often overlooked in empirical
research: individuals who opt out before initiating an applica­
tion. This perspective complements existing work that primari­
ly observes applicants already willing to engage and highlights
how cognitive shortcuts may shape participation decisions at the

Our conclusions, however, come with important caveats and
point to several avenues for future research. First, the experi­
ments measure stated intentions rather than observed behavior
and rely on an online panel in which real stakes are absent. Future
research could incorporate tasks with genuine time costs or eval­

to expected burden. Second, while this study focuses on initial
application decisions, compliance costs may also shape contin­

participation (Baekgaard and Madsen 2024). Longitudinal designs
following applicants over time could clarify whether early expe­

participation. Finally, research could explore whether targeted
psychological or informational interventions, such as personal­

tic judgments about compliance costs and influence take-up.

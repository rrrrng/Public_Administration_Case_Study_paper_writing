Abstract
The US bureaucracy routinely issues major public policy decisions that affect Americans’ lives. Government agency leaders make those decisions
based on a subjective understanding of their agency’s available policy discretion. Over time, discretion has become a prominent theoretical con­
struct in the bureaucratic politics and public administration literature, but it is rarely measured directly. In this article, we create a new measure
of agency policy discretion. We draw on research suggesting that discretion is derived from the bureaucracy’s key political principals: the elected
executive, legislators, and interest groups. We use data from the American State Administrators Project and trigonometry to calculate the dis­
cretion area scores for 8,955 state agencies between 1978 and 2018. We then evaluate the measure through a series of construct validation
assessments. The article offers an innovative and generalizable way to operationalize discretion that will advance future scholarship in organiza­
tional behavior, public administration, and bureaucratic decision-making.
Key words: agency policy discretion, agency leaders, survey research, multiple principals.
Introduction
Few political bodies have as much policy impact as the US
bureaucracy. Government agencies regularly establish state
and national legal requirements for topics as weighty as water
quality, unmanned aircrafts, derivatives trading, and health
pandemics. Yet, to make major policy decisions in these areas
and others, leaders of government agencies must first iden­
tify whether they have the discretion to make a major policy
change, and, if so, how much. Across time, agency policy dis­
cretion has developed into one of the most important the­
oretical constructs in the study of bureaucratic politics and
public administration. Scholars have not, however, created a
comprehensive way to operationalize it. We aim to remedy
this information gap by developing a new measure, which
will usher in a wave of pathbreaking research on bureaucratic
discretion.
We define agency policy discretion as the perceived
area within which the bureaucracy’s key external polit­
ical principals—the elected executive, legislators, and in­
terest groups—lack influence over an agency’s major public
policy decisions.1 This definition builds on the existing liter­
ature suggesting that agency policy discretion is connected
to the formal and informal influence that the bureaucracy’s
principals hold over important agency decision-making
(e.g., Hall and Miller 2008; Huber 2007; Meier 1980;
Rourke 1984; Shipan 2004; Wood and Waterman 1994).
Although political principals do at times attempt to restrict
an agency’s decision-making authority, they also routinely
provide agencies with the latitude to make policy decisions.
Legislators, for instance, can affect bureaucratic discretion by
passing statutes that delegate rulemaking authority to public
agencies (Golden 1998; Kerwin and Furlong 2018; Rinfret
2021; Rosenbloom 2015; West 1995). Elected executives,
such as presidents and governors, can issue executive or­
ders to shift some policymaking power to the bureaucracy
(Ferguson and Bowling 2008; Kennedy 2015). And, interest
groups often increase agency discretion by encouraging and
enabling bureaucratic action (Carpenter 2002; Yackee 2015).
Many researchers theorize that the struggle for power
between the bureaucracy’s multiple principals can create
space for agencies to make their own policy choices (e.g.,
McCubbins, Noll, and Weingast 1989; Moe 1984; Synder and
Weingast 2000; Tsebelis 2002). Indeed, scholars have long
theorized about a discretionary space—sometimes referred to
as a discretionary region, area, or zone—within which leaders
can make major policy decisions on behalf of their organiza­
tions without the fear of being overturned or sanctioned (see
broadly, Barnard 1938; Hammond and Knott 1996; Huber
2007; Shipan 2004; Simon 1947; Volden 2002). Based on this
general logic, our article’s main contribution is to create a
new measure for operationalizing a public bureaucracy’s dis­
cretionary space.
The measure has three advantages. First, it taps an agency
leader’s subjective understanding of his/her agency’s policy
discretion, which we expect to be informed by perceptions of
political principal influence. In doing so, we argue for the im­
portance of subjective-based measures of discretion and follow
past research that concludes the perceptions of bureaucrats
are critical to capturing external influence (Clinton, Lewis,
and Selin 2013), as well as existing work that demonstrates
a bureaucrat’s perceptions shape his/her behavior (Vinopal
2020). Second, our measure captures the push-and-pull for
influence across the bureaucracy’s key political principals that
has been much theorized about but never quantified for a large

We use data from the American State Administrators

discretion and describe how we create discretion scores.
Next, we validate the measure through a series of correla­

each of our evaluations. We then explain how this measure
provides scholars with an opportunity to answer new research
questions related to a variety of academic fields. Finally, we
conclude with a discussion of the importance of the measure
and direct scholars to where it can be downloaded.
Conceptualizing Agency Policy Discretion
The tension between political accountability and agency dis­
cretion is a central conflict in the bureaucratic politics and
public administration literature. As Wilson (1989) explains,
no agency leader can realize complete discretion for their
agency because representative democracies are complex and
necessitate political accountability. Within the US’s separa­
tion of powers system, accountability is further complicated
by the fact that the bureaucracy’s multiple political principals
oversee agency policymaking and often jockey for influence.
The existing literature provides ample evidence that
legislators, elected executives, and interest groups hold in­
dividual and shared powers that may be used to affect the
policy discretion afforded to agencies. Legislators can limit
discretion in several ways, including holding oversight
hearings (Aberbach 2002), setting agency budgets (Calvert,
McCubbins, and Weingast 1989), and using riders to
strengthen statutory constraints (MacDonald 2010). Elected
executives constrain an agency’s discretion because they often
have the power to appoint agency leaders, with and without
legislative approval (Lewis 2008; Wood and Waterman 1994).
In addition, executives can increase their influence over
the bureaucracy by centralizing decision-making processes
(Seifter 2017; West 1995) and issuing executive orders in an
attempt to control an agency’s policy agenda (Ferguson and
Bowling 2008; Kennedy 2015). Finally, interest groups can
play an important role in minimizing discretion by working in
concert with legislators to police agency policy actions (Hall
and Miller 2008; McCubbins, Noll, and Weingast 1989) and
by raising procedural and substantive concerns during the
rulemaking process (Kerwin and Furlong 2018; Potter 2019;
Rinfret 2021; Rosenbloom 2015; Yackee 2015).
However, while an agency’s decision-making is constrained
by its political principals, an agency can also derive policy dis­
cretion from the competition for influence across its multiple
principals (Moe 1984; Palus and Yackee 2022; Tsebelis 2002).
In fact, Hammond and Knott (1996) argue that it is often
disagreement among principals that increases an agency’s
ability to pursue its own policy goals. Put differently, when
the bureaucracy’s political principals compete for influence
over agency policymaking, it provides agencies with more
­decision-making latitude (Calvert, McCubbins, and Weingast
1989; McCubbins, Noll, and Weingast 1989; Miller and
Whitford 2016; Snyder and Weingast 2000). Formal theorists
often represent this “gridlock region” on a unidimensional
plane, where the bureaucracy’s multiple principals cannot
come together to overrule or otherwise sanction agency action
(e.g., Shipan 2004; Volden 2002). As a result, public agency
leaders “constantly concern themselves” with whether their
agency—and its policy choices—remain within the predicted
area inside of which they can make decisions without external
rebuke from their political principals (Huber 2007, 24). We
trace this general line of thinking to Barnard’s (1938) zone
of acceptance and Simon’s (1947) zone of indifference. Both
constructs rely on the idea that authority relationships be­
tween a manager and an employee create a theoretical space
within which an agent’s actions remain unquestioned but, be­
yond which, the principal intervenes to constrain the agent’s
behavior.
Public agency leaders can employ various strategies to
maintain or even increase the size of their discretionary
zones. Tsebelis (2002) writes that bureaucrats interpret laws
according to their interests and are careful to avoid being
overruled by their political principals. Similarly, agencies may
tactically augment their discretionary powers by relying on
their technical expertise (Carpenter 2010; Rourke 1984),
strategically ignoring procedural requirements (Hamilton
1996; Potter 2019), capitalizing on unclear goals (Wilson
1989), reimagining previously delegated authority (Selin
2021), and exploiting the ways in which political principals’
influence on agency decision-making can vary by policy do­
main (e.g., Dillon et. al 2018; Rinfret 2021; Rourke 1984). As
a result, the literature puts forward a variety of reasons why
at times agencies have the ability to expand the scope of their
policymaking discretion.
Why Perceptions?
Although there is general agreement that public agency policy
discretion is largely predicated on the extent to which an
agency can act independently from the influence of its po­
litical principals, scholars have yet to develop a measure
that systematically quantifies discretion from the agency’s
point of view. This gap in the literature deserves consider­
able attention—perhaps most importantly because a leader’s

understanding of her/his agency’s discretionary space provides
the foundation from which he/she makes and directs the
agency’s policy decisions. Therefore, perceptions of discretion
are a valuable and important means for conceptualizing and
operationalizing agency policy discretion. After all, a defining
feature of modern public agencies is that their leaders are con­
stantly pressed to make decisions, particularly formal policy
decisions (Kaufman 1981). A natural consequence of this
reality is that agency leaders “must first” perceive when po­
litical principals are exerting pressure and, after recognizing
that pressure, “react to it” (Furlong 1998, 63).
In making the argument that a perception-based approach is
an appropriate method for capturing agency policy discretion,
we are able to build on past research implying that perceptions
shape the conditions under which agency leaders operate (see
broadly, Bertelli 2016; Bertelli et al. 2015) and extend upon
work arguing that public officials make decisions based on
the discretion they believe is available to them (Carpenter
and Golden 1997; Vinopal 2020). Using a survey-based tech­
nique to measure a theoretical construct like policy discretion
allows us to capture a “comprehensive overview of influence”
(Gormley 1982, 299). As we discussed previously, there are nu­
merous tools that political principals might use for influencing
agency action, including submitting comments during agency
rulemaking, issuing executive orders, requiring legislative
hearings, and writing restrictive statutes. By measuring an
agency’s discretionary zone from the perspective of the agency
leader, we not only account for the complexity and variation
of the different factors that contribute to agency-level policy
discretion, but we also capture less observable factors that are
either unknown or difficult to measure directly.
Lastly, we give special attention to the role that statutes play
in shaping our measure because previous research uses the
text of statutes to understand the policy discretion afforded
to agencies via legislative delegation, which provides the legal
foundation for most agency policy action (e.g., Epstein and
O’Halloran 1999; Huber and Shipan 2002). These scholars
convincingly argue that discretion is informed by the statutes
governing an agency’s work. Here, we see two advantages for
a perception-based approach that yields a different (and com­
plimentary) measure of policy discretion by extending upon
statute-based measures. First, an agency’s statutory environ­
ment is one of many critical variables that a leader weighs
when evaluating the agency’s available policy discretion.
Consistent with our argument above, numerous push-and-
pull factors constrain and enable discretion, and our subjec­
tive measure allows the agency leader to contemplate and
then ultimately judge these forces. Second, agency leaders can
interpret the same statute in divergent ways, which engenders
different levels of discretion (Keiser and Soss 1998).2 As a re­
sult, our perception-based measure is able to account for the
ways that discretion varies depending on how leaders inter­
pret and apply statutes to their agency’s policy decisions.
Quantifying Discretion
To operationalize agency policy discretion, we use data col­
lected from ASAP. ASAP is a decades-long survey of state
agency leaders with surveys implemented in 1978, 1984, 1988,
1994, 1998, 2004, 2008, and 2018.3 To carry out the survey,
administrators used the Council of State Governments’ yearly
State Directory to identify every state agency leader across all
agency types in the given year. ASAP responses were solicited
primarily by mail,4 with an average response rate of 37%
across the survey waves.5 The ASAP survey probes agency
leaders about their attitudes and experiences while working in
their agency (Yackee and Yackee 2021). We focus on agency
leaders because they are responsible for the policymaking ac­
tivities in their agencies (Boushey and McGrath 2017) and be­
cause they consistently scan the larger political environment
to ensure they are making policy decisions that are within
their discretionary powers (Huber 2007).
We exploit an ASAP question bank designed to assess the
sources of influence on important agency decision-making.
Specifically, agency leaders were asked, “In making agency
decisions it is usually possible to identify and weigh several
major sources of influence. Among these are (1) governor,
(2) legislators, (3) clientele groups. Please indicate below (by
circling) the degree of influence each source has on decisions
your agency makes in the following decision areas.” The
survey asks about several areas, but we focus on responses
to the “major policy changes” decision area. The response
options were: none (0), slight (1), moderate (2), and high (3).6
Because we are interested in capturing bureaucratic dis­
cretion rather than bureaucratic constraint, we flip the scales
of each survey response so that low influence from a polit­
ical principal translates into high agency discretion. This ap­
proach is consistent with previous scholarship that equates
low levels of influence from an agency’s political principals
with greater policy discretion (e.g., Palus and Yackee 2016,
2022). As a result, we are left with three discretion variables
that range from 0 to 3, with 0 representing the lowest level of
discretion and 3 indicating the highest.7 We use these values
to determine an agency’s policy discretionary zone by plotting
each as a point estimate and using trigonometry to calculate
the area of the resulting triangle. This ultimately yields 8,955

pret larger Discretion Scores more generally? Such zones
may be caused by a number of scenarios, and we put for­
ward three here. First, large zones may come about because
a key political principal wants to influence the bureaucracy’s
policymaking but that principal’s efforts are countered by
another principal. Under this scenario, disagreement among
political principals regarding a preferred policy choice leads
to inaction and ultimately results in larger discretionary
zones for the bureaucracy. Second, large zones might occur
when an agency is working in an area or policy domain that
is not particularly important to its political principals. If an
agency’s principals do not exert formal or informal influ­
ence, this would yield large discretionary zones. Third, po­
litical principals may want to exert influence but may not be

Assessing Discretion Scores
We perform a two-part validation assessment to ensure that
discretion scores reflect what we purport to measure (i.e., our
empirical strategy appropriately captures the underlying theo­
retical concept of agency policy discretion). We focus on con­
struct validity, which is judged based on whether the variable
corresponds to established expectations for how it should
operate (Volden and Wiseman 2014). First, we compare our
measure to three question banks in the ASAP survey to assess
the agency’s statutory environment. Using correlation anal­
ysis, we verify that discretion scores are related to statutory
constraints in the expected directions. Second, we conduct a
series of hypothesis tests to ensure that our perception-based
measure correctly predicts discretion-related relationships
that are well-established in the literature.
To begin, we examine the relationship between statutes
and Discretion Scores. To do so, we exploit three ASAP
survey question banks that ask each agency leader to pro­
vide an assessment of the agency’s statutory environment.
These questions distinguish introduced legislation from
passed/enacted legislation that affects an agency, and, impor­
tantly, allow us to leverage how discretion varies depending
on where statutes originate. Our expectation is that as the
proportion of introduced legislation (or passed/enacted legis­
lation) that originates in the agency increases, agency policy
discretion will also increase because agencies are unlikely to
advocate for statutes that limit their discretion. In contrast,
as the proportion of introduced legislation (or passed/enacted
legislation) that originates with the legislature, governor’s of­
fice, or interest groups increases, agency policy discretion will
decrease because each of these actors often desire more influ­
ence over agency policymaking.9

view, the findings corroborate our expectations and are an im­
portant first step in demonstrating the validity of Discretion
Scores. Table 1 provides the results attached to an ASAP
question where agency leaders were asked, “Of all the legis­
lation affecting your agency considered by the legislature in
the past four years: what proportion introduced in the legisla­
ture originates in: ___ % your agency; ___ % legislature; ___
% governor’s office.” Table 2 displays the results for a sim­
ilar question that asks about passed/enacted legislation. Both
survey banks were asked across multiple ASAP waves. Table 3
focuses on a different question bank where agency leaders were
asked, “To the extent that (a) interest groups and (b) national
officials are sources of ideas for legislation affecting your
agency, about what proportion of passed/enacted legislation in

As expected, Tables 1 and 2 display negative correlation
coefficients for the governor’s office (−0.115 and −0.111) and
the legislature (−0.068 and −0.082) for both the proposed
and enacted legislation variables. In Table 3, we also find a
negative correlation between discretion scores and interest
groups (−0.074).11 These results imply that lower levels of
policy discretion occur when the bureaucracy’s political
principals are responsible for larger proportions of legislation
affecting the agency. Furthermore, we find a positive correla­
tion between Discretion Scores and both variables capturing
legislation that originates within the agency, with coefficients
of 0.117 for proposed legislation and 0.122 for enacted leg­
islation. Taken together, these findings support the expecta­
tion that policy discretion is lower when an agency’s political
principals—rather than the agency itself—play a key role in
determining the content of statutes that govern agency ac­
tion. Therefore, the correlations between Discretion Scores
and each statutory variable lend credibility to the construct
validity of our measure.
To further evaluate Discretion Scores, we supplement our
first validity check with a series of hypothesis tests. If our
measure is capturing agency policy discretion, then we ought
to find statistically significant and directionally appropriate
support for the relationship between Discretion Scores and
several variables that existing literature demonstrates are re­
lated to bureaucratic discretion. Put differently, quantitative
evidence of each hypothesis will provide additional support
for the conclusion that we are properly measuring agency
policy discretion.

Hypothesis 3. Scholars have long suggested that agency
policymaking is captured (Croley 2008; McCubbins, Noll,
and Weingast 1989; Rex 2018; Scheffler 2020). Capture is
defined as when an agency assigns greater weight in their
­decision-making to a narrow set of private interests than
to the broader public interest (Noll 1971). Bernstein (1955)
theorizes that capture occurs when the dominant interest
groups in a policy area take control of a public agency’s
policymaking objectives. Across time, the occurrence of cap­
ture has been debated (Carpenter and Moss 2014; Yackee
2022), but the idea that the policy decisions of some govern­
ment agencies are more prone to interest group influence than
others has remained. Barkow (2010, 15), for instance, suggests
that executive branch agencies are more susceptible to cap­
ture than other agency types because they are more likely to
experience “lopsided pressure” from interest group lobbying
that can be “one-sided and intense.” Research also finds that
interest group influence is prominent in agencies whose policy
areas are more specialized, such as education (Rourke 1984)
and the environment (Dillon et. al 2018; Magat, Krupnick,
and Harrington 1986; Rinfret 2021). Given that the literature
suggests agencies with a defined and narrower mandate are
at an increased risk for interest group influence, the breadth
of an agency’s functional role in government will impact its
policy discretion.
H3) Agencies focused on specialized policy areas will have
less policy discretion than agencies whose tasks cross mul­
tiple issue areas.
Research Design
To test these hypotheses, our primary information source is
the ASAP survey, which we describe above. Additionally, we
include several other data sources to complement the 1978 to
2018 ASAP survey waves, which we explain below. For each
of the models, Discretion Scores is the dependent variable.
The independent variables we use to operationalize
hypotheses 1 and 2 are collected in the ASAP survey and
focus on each agency leader’s method of appointment. The
appointment method variable allows us to analyze the ex­
isting argument in the literature that the more removed the
agency leader’s appointment is from their elected principals,
the more discretion that agency will hold. The baseline for
our analysis (i.e., the excluded category) is a leader who is
appointed by the Governor with Legislative Consent because
it is the appointment type for which agency leaders should
report the lowest levels of discretion. We then include the full
complement of six binary indicators capturing other appoint­
ment methods in the models. These are: Governor without
Legislative Consent, Board with Governor’s Consent, Board
without Governor’s Consent, Appointment by Department
Head, Popularly Elected, and Other Appointment Process.13
We expect each variable to be signed in the positive direc­
tion, meaning these agencies will have more discretion than
the baseline category.
Specifically, our expectation for hypothesis 1 is that
agencies with Popularly Elected leaders will hold the most
policy discretion relative to the baseline. For hypothesis 2,
we expect the relationship between the appointment process
and discretion to increase in magnitude as the appointment
method becomes further removed from an agency’s political
principals. We anticipate that Governor without Legislative
Consent and Board with Governor’s Consent will have small,
positive coefficients and will operate most similarly to the
baseline. The coefficient for Appointment by Department
Head should be somewhat larger because the appointment
of these leaders is one-step removed from gubernatorial in­
fluence. Board without Governor’s Consent and Other
Appointment Process are anticipated to return larger positive
coefficients, as these are leaders whose appointment methods
are even further removed from the governor and legislators.
Finally, as previously stated, Popularly Elected leaders will
yield the largest, positive coefficient.
We assess hypothesis 3 by including six indicator variables
that tap an agency’s functional role in state government. Cho
and Wright (2004) use the State Directory to assemble the
ASAP data into a classification system for state agencies by
the agency’s task environment. We present these categories in
Table 4 and also provide examples of agencies that fall within
each policy area grouping. The baseline category for the
models is Major Agencies. Agencies in this category, such as the
Treasurer and Secretary of State, often hold broader mandates
and their tasks frequently cross policy areas and topics.14 We
expect agencies in the categories of Human Resources, Natural
Resources and Transportation, Economic Development and
Regulation, and Criminal Justice to be negatively signed and
statistically significant because these agencies often experience
intense lobbying. We have less clear theoretical expectations
for agencies in the Staff and Other Agency Type groupings.
Each of the six variables is a binary indicator, meaning every
agency receives a score of 1 for their corresponding functional
category, and scores of zero otherwise.
Beyond the independent variables that speak directly to our
hypotheses, we also control for several potential confounders
that may impact an agency’s policy discretion. At the agency
level, we create an Agency Size variable, which measures each
agency’s budget in 2018 dollars.15 Smaller agencies that require
fewer resources tend to experience increased autonomy from
political principals (Miller and Whitford 2016) and should
therefore report higher levels of discretion. We create six
equally sized bins, where agencies with budgets that fall within
the smallest bin are coded as a 1, agencies with budgets that
fall within the second smallest bin are coded as a 2, and so on.
Our second agency-level variable is Revenue Diversification,
which is coded as a 1 for agencies that receive federal funding
and a 0 for those that do not. Agencies with access to this

and match that average to the appropriate survey year. State
Policy Liberalism ranges from −50 to 50, with negative num­

the possibility that the partisanship of a state’s elected officials

government’s partisan make-up. Using data from the Council
of State Governments’ Book of the States, we create two bi­
nary variables. Unified Republican receives a score of 1 if the
governor and a majority of both houses of the state legislature
are associated with the Republican Party, and a zero otherwise.
Similarly, Unified Democrat is coded as a 1 in states when the
governor and both houses of the state legislature are majority-
Democrat. Divided Government is the excluded category.
Results
The descriptive statistics for the variables in our models
are reported in Table 5 and the results of our validation
tests are presented in Table 6.19 Model A presents the main
results using state fixed effects, and Model B is a replication
of Model A using multilevel modeling with leaders nested in
states. Model B serves as a robustness check and returns very
similar results. Both models include survey-year fixed effects.
To further ensure that our results are robust, we conducted
several auxiliary models. First, we iteratively added control
variables into the Model A specification, such as whether the
state had a Republican governor or a Republican majority
in the legislature. We also included the agency’s number of
employees, the respondent’s age, the respondent’s experience
working in state government, and the respondent’s experi­
ence working in the agency before becoming its leader. Even
with these additional controls, the key conclusions drawn
from the model are unchanged. Second, we conducted a sen­
sitivity analysis where we substituted Discretion Scores with
an additive scale that combines each agency’s responses to the
three survey questions measuring the discretion they receive
from the governor, legislators, and interest groups. The model
returned substantially similar conclusions.
The findings in Table 6 confirm each of our hypotheses,
which ultimately yields strong evidence that we are appropri­
ately quantifying agency policy discretion. We uncover sup­
port for hypotheses 1 and 2 across both model specifications.
As discussed above, the literature suggests that agency policy
discretion should increase as an agency leader’s appoint­
ment process becomes further removed from his/her political
principals. The Popular Election coefficient of 0.729 is statis­
tically significant and positively signed in Model A, indicating
that agencies whose leaders are directly elected by citizens
have a discretionary zone that is 0.729 larger than agencies
that are headed by a gubernatorially appointed leader with
legislative approval. Substantively, this is a notable shift given
that the average Discretion Score across all agencies included
in the model is 1.230. As predicted, Popular Election has the
largest coefficient when looking across the appointment indi­
cator variables.
Neither Board with Governor’s Consent nor Governor
without Legislative Consent is statistically significant, but, as
anticipated, both are positively signed and small in magnitude.

provides additional evidence in support of the construct va­
lidity of Discretion Scores. The existing literature suggests that
agencies in more specialized policy domains may experience
greater interest group lobbying and, in turn, higher levels of
influence from organized interests. Given our research design,
this should translate into Major Agencies being afforded more
discretion while agencies with narrower mandates reporting
less discretion. Our results confirm this expectation. In fact,
all six of our agency-type variables are statistically significant
and negatively signed. For example, Natural Resources and
Transportation have a coefficient of −1.139 and Criminal
Justice agencies have scores that are 0.883 smaller than Major
Agencies.

Second, Discretion Scores can be aggregated to the state-

our full dataset. Overall, the map shows that a handful of
states in the South, such as South Carolina and Alabama,
tend to have higher levels of agency policy discretion, while
states in the West, including Oregon and California, report
lower levels. In fact, South Carolina’s score is roughly 51%
larger than the mean Discretion Score across all 50 states,
and Alabama’s score is approximately 68% larger than the
national average. We find that Minnesota has the lowest av­

suggest that policy discretion in Alabama across all survey

these notable differences, future research by interest group
scholars can analyze variation in lobbying registration

agency discretion, while state politics and policymaking
scholars can study how agency discretion interacts with leg­

and gubernatorial time in office can affect a state’s policy
discretion.

functional role in state government to explore substantive
differences in discretion across or within specific policy areas.

such as by criminal justice or by human resources agencies—
scholars of different policy domains can use these data to
study substantive patterns in their fields. For example, scholars
of environmental politics can investigate over time changes in
agency policy discretion and can correlate these changes with
state-level fluctuations in public opinion on environmental
policies. Similarly, scholars of social welfare agencies can ana­
lyze how agency-level discretion affects the attitudes and work
choices of front-line employees, including the flexibility of
street-level bureaucrats in responding to client needs.
Conclusion
The primary contribution of this article is a new measure of
public agency policy discretion. While discretion has been at
the forefront of the bureaucratic politics and public admin­
istration literature for decades, few studies have developed a
way to systematically measure it. Perhaps more importantly,
previous research has not quantified discretion in the way
that scholars frequently theorize about it: as a region, area,
space, or zone inside of which agencies can act without inter­
ference from their political principals. As a result, until now,
there has been a severe disconnect between theories of agency
discretion and assessments of those theories.

We anticipate the sharing of these scores will result in a

an important tool for public administration scholars as well

future scholars answer new questions and expand our knowl­
edge of the public bureaucracy in order to improve our under­
standing of how and why administrative agencies operate in
the ways that they do. Toward that end, in the previous section
of the article, we articulated a number of research questions
that are ripe for answers with this new variable. However, it
is worth reemphasizing one of the key benefits to Discretion
Scores here: their flexibility. Because we capture discretion by
agency, future work can use these scores at the “individual
level.” Yet, Discretion Scores can also be transformed in useful
ways, including aggregating them by agency type, by state, or
even by time period. For instance, when we analyzed discre­
tion scores by state, we revealed previously unidentified state-
to-state differences. Now that we know this variation exists,
additional theorizing and data analysis are needed to explain
not only why this is the case, but also the political and policy
consequences of these differences.
Future work is also needed to advance our normative under­
standing of public agency policy discretion. While this article
aims to quantify discretion—and, in doing so, to jumpstart
empirical scholarship on the bureaucracy’s discretionary
powers—we are agonistic as to whether low or high discre­
tion is “good,” as well as “good” for whom. Research into
the normative aspects of this topic will shine light on what
the right level of public agency discretion ought to be and the
implications that this holds for democratic governance in the
American states. Happily, the measure created here will in­
form and advance that important and enduring debate.

Appendix
A1. ASAP Survey-Additional Details
This article uses human subjects research from eight ASAP
surveys. Each wave of the survey was completed separately.
In all cases, the survey participants were public officials
who participated voluntarily. The surveys were sent to each
official’s work address and queried the officials about their
work duties, experiences, and impressions of state govern­
ment. The 1978 to 2004 surveys were implemented by mail,
while the 2008 and 2018 surveys were implemented by mail
and online. The researchers’ names, institutions, and contact
information were clearly communicated, as was the general
purpose of the research, the benefits of the research, and an
explanation of what participation entailed. For all survey
waves, respondents were identified as state public officials
through the Council of State Governments’ yearly State
Directory. Scholarly access to the ASAP data requires a signed
data transfer agreement.
The 1978 to 2008 survey waves did not provide payment
for the completion of the survey, which was appropriate be­
cause the respondents were not seeking survey participation in
return for fair wage compensation. The 1978 to 2008 survey
data were provided to the article’s authors via a signed data
transfer agreement from an established university, which
allows for the transfer of de-identified human subject data. The
2018 survey used an advanced letter that, like previous survey
waves, stressed that participation was voluntary and included
relevant information about what participation entailed. The
advance letter included a $2.00 bill with an envelope to re­
turn the token incentive, if so desired. The $2.00 bill was not
compensation because each potential respondent could keep
or return the token incentive regardless of whether he or she
completed the survey. The 2018 survey was deemed exempt re­
search by the University of Wisconsin-Madison’s Institutional
Review Board. It was accessed directly by the authors.

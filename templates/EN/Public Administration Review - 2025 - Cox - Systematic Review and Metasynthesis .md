ABSTRACT
This study investigates how dissent and guerrilla government research shape theory and practice in public administration. The
research takes a metasynthetic approach to a traditional systematic review by incorporating ethnographic methods to synthesize
the state of the dissent and guerrilla government research. The findings suggest that dissent plays a critical role in the public
sector, such as promoting transparency and accountability and fostering a learning culture through feedback. However, the sit-
uation and the context surrounding rationales for transgressive behaviors matter. The study finds that dissent and disobedience
may be useful tools for dealing with organizational failures and dysfunctions, but these methods should only be used when nec-
essary. Practitioners should be aware of the range of consequences that arise from actions relating to dissent and disobedience.
Several gaps are identified and demonstrate a clear need to continue research on dissent and transgressive leadership in public
administration, leadership, and public policy.
1   |   Introduction
Public institutions are under attack—critical public services and
agencies are being dismantled under the virtue of government
efficiency, while immigrants and marginalized communities
are sustaining policy attacks that endanger their basic rights and
protections to live and work in the United States. In the same
vein, recent executive orders have diminished constitutional
protections. Using executive authority, President Trump has or-
dered widespread unemployment among career public servants,
initiated mass deportation of immigrants and international stu-
dents, and imposed punitive measures to suppress dissent within
organizations. Destructive leadership is on the rise, and certain
publics are convinced these efforts hold virtue. Yet, managers and
public employees alike are being forced to make difficult deci-
sions that threaten the promise and future of their organizations.
These issues add complexity to the roles of career public servants
and responsible managers when they must decide whether to
implement harmful policies or not. The current state of public
administration necessitates critical discussions surrounding the
approaches and tools for effective decision-­making, specifically in
contexts of organizational dysfunction and failure. For example,
should managers knowingly implement harmful public policies?
Are public servants adequately prepared to effectively manage or-
ganizational failure, destructive leadership, and harmful policies
in organizations? These issues warrant a clear need to understand
what tools, strategies, and approaches will be effective. As these
are unprecedented issues, this study will look to unprecedented
solutions—namely, how the literature on dissent and guerrilla
government can bridge these gaps.

purpose and relevance to this research. First, dissent pertains
to employee dissatisfaction with specific actions undertaken
by public organizations (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020). Cox (2024)
further defines dissent as institutional feedback and informa-
tion voiced in opposition to organizational actions, rules, pro-
cedures, or practices. Conventional literature and philosophies
in public management often view dissent as a problem of un-
ethical or insubordinate behavior despite critical advancements
that identify conflict as essential to institutional learning and
progress (Aldrich 1972; Nelson 1989; O'Leary  2010; O'Leary
and Bingham 2007; Uhl-­Bien 2021; Balfour 2007; McGregor and
Eugene 2007; Reed 2023; Sharp 2006; Warm 2010). Guerrilla
government, on the other hand, is frequently used interchange-
ably with dissent. Guerrilla government, however, refers to the
irregular activities that occur in government (O'Leary  2006,
2014, 2020; Hollibaugh. Jr et al. 2020). While dissent can be
understood as the vocalized (or written) expression of dis-
agreement, guerrilla government can be understood as agentic,
transgressive recourse for ignored dissent (Cox 2024).
But why do concepts like dissent and guerrilla government
matter for public administration research and practice? Today,
citizens are witnessing a dramatic consolidation and abuse of
power by the federal executive branch that push the bound-
aries of law and ethics. To date, President Trump has issued
an executive order directing agencies to make substantial
personnel reductions in the federal workforce. While most
public service personnel can be legally dismissed for reasons
relating to misconduct or poor performance, federal agencies
must comply when implementing efforts to promote structural
changes, such as by implementing reductions in force (RIF)
efforts. Executive a RIF, however, requires legal justification,
and often, employees are provided adequate notice to find al-
ternative employment. In response, the Trump administration
signed an executive order on the first day in office to recate-
gorize career public servants as “Schedule P” and “Schedule
C” classes, which allows the President to fire public servants
at -­will (Queen and Wiessner 2025). To date, the White House
reported that 200,000 federal workers have been laid off since
implementing the Department of Government Efficiency
(DOGE) and more than 75,000 federal workers agreed to a
deferred retirement (buyout) plan (Reuters 2025). Additional
issues concerning the shift towards executive authoritarian-
ism, including the implementation of selective laws, ignoring
court orders, and using executive power to override the Civil
Service Reform Act, as well as taking control of independent
regulatory agencies and dismantling federal agencies such as
the IRS, NIH, AID, and Department of Education (Kunzelman
and Tucker 2023; Roberts 2021).
Government guerrillas are career public servants who disobey
the wishes of their superiors, and the state of guerrilla govern-
ment is growing as administrative efforts endure to stifle and
punish dissent. Examples of this include President Trump's use
of executive power to block Harvard University from receiving
billions of federal funds for defying administrative demands to
control Harvard curricula, staff, and policies (Mannweiler 2025;
Gross 2025). The President has also threatened to arrest and de-
port a college student and Palestinian activist for protesting at
Columbia University, claiming the students and pro -­Palestinian
protests are terrorist acts that should be found, arrested, and
deported (Offenhartz and Marcelo  2025). Amid the concerns
facing higher education, the Trump administration argued to
aggressively revoke Chinese student visas and intensify visa ap-
plication processes for those with “connections to the Chinese
Communist Party or studying in critical fields” (Knox  2025).
So what? The topic of dissent is classic, but could not be more
important to the history and future of public administration as
it is today. Government is under attack—not simply from out-
side, but also from within. We are witnessing growing threats to
basic constitutional protections relating to speech, equal career
opportunities, and legal protections for career public servants to
carry out work. It is critical today, more than ever, that we have
a foundation for protecting ethical dissent in organizations. In
response, this study forms in the need to comprehensively ex-
amine how dissent and guerrilla government inform public ad-
ministration theory and practice.
2   |   Methodology
To systematically examine how dissent and guerrilla govern-
ment influence public administration theory and practice,
this study adopts a systematic review protocol informed by
PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews
and Meta -­Analyses) standards for structured and transparent
research practices and meta-­synthesis methodology. To main-
tain accountability and transparency of the protocol, the re-
search was pre-­registered in the Research Registry database,
adhering to the ENTREQ (enhancing transparency in report-
ing the synthesis of qualitative research) guidelines. This
procedure ensures the rigor of systematic reviews, aiming to
uncover how published studies on dissent and guerrilla gov-
ernment inform public administration theories and practices
(Stewart et  al. 2012; Tong et  al. 2012). Effective search and
scoping methods are crucial to the completeness of the sys-
tematic review. The research extends to multiple databases,
including ABI/INFORM, ERIC, and Scopus, to perform an
exhaustive search. The search employs narrow and broad
Boolean search strategies, and the review comprehensively
scopes for literature, including peer-­reviewed articles, books,
theses, and other published works. The search was limited
to English language materials but included diverse digital

2.1   |   Screening and Selection
The screening and selection process began with compiling all
potentially relevant citations, organizing them into a unique
Zotero subcollection, and categorizing them by the source

materials were systematically flagged and removed. An ini-

resulting in 93 eligible for retrieval. The records were then as-

studies in the final review. Figure 1 demonstrates the specific
inclusion and exclusion criteria used in various stages of the
screening process. PRISMA was used to ensure a structured
and methodological assessment of the literature, and critical

When identifying methods for critical appraisals for system-
atic reviews informed by qualitative research, Cochrane argues
that no single agreed-­upon method exists for reviews that take
a meta-­synthesis approach, as overly rigid adherence could lead
to skewed or contextually inadequate findings (Hannes 2011;
Hannes et al. 2010). When selecting the critical appraisal tool,
the instrument needed sufficient flexibility to adapt to the nu-
ances of our research question while ensuring the findings were
comprehensive and contextually relevant (Long et  al. 2020).
Consequently, this study incorporates Cochrane's recommenda-
tions to evaluate (1) methodological quality and rigor, (2) appro-
priateness of data collection and analysis methods, (3) credibility
of findings, (4) contextual relevance, and (5) contributions to the-
ory and practice. We selected the 10-­item Critical Appraisal Skills
Programme (CASP) checklist over the Joanna Briggs Institute
(JBI)'s Qualitative Assessment and Review Instrument, as CASP
offers a broader assessment of the considerations necessary for
this study (see Table 1). See Appendix A and C in Data S1, for a
detailed summary of the screening and appraisal process.
2.2   |   Metasynthesis
A synthesis approach requires an iterative process as informed by
ethnographic and grounded theory methodologies (Seers 2015;
Chrastina 2018; Campbell et al. 2011; Sattar et al. 2021). Like
a standard systematic review, the metasynthesis involves a
systematic process for collecting, analyzing, and interpreting
findings from multiple studies. However, metasyntheses focus
on providing contextually rich understandings of phenomena
under study. This integration is achieved using reciprocal and
refutational synthesis. Reciprocal synthesis involves identifying
and combining concepts across different studies through shared
meanings, while refutational synthesis critically examines con-
tradictions, differences, or discrepancies among the studies and
the identified concepts (Noblit and Hare 1988; Lefebvre et al.
2019). Though complex and time-­consuming, these techniques
aim to produce contextually rich information of the data (see
Appendix A in Data S1).

Research in public administration offers a counterpoint. In cit-

Moreover, Cox (2024) argues that conflict is not only central to
dissent, but that dissent itself is a form of constructive conflict
necessary for a healthy and deliberative democracy. Without dis-
sent, he posits that innovation and progress could be stifled or
would make way for poor policy or leadership to arise.
In this study, it is assumed that conflict underpins the motiva-
tions for dissent and ethical forms of disobedience. The forms
of conflict, as informed by O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020 do vary. To
simplify the findings, various dynamics seemingly arouse con-
flicts that lead to dissent, such as foundations relating to value
conflicts, ethical dilemmas, rule-­based conflicts, leadership/su-
perior directive conflicts, policy conflicts, and organizational/
bureaucratic conflicts. These conflicts seem to interplay and
arise through various dynamics and interactions, such as intra-
personal dynamics (i.e., tensions between the self and the role),
interpersonal dynamics (i.e., individual -­level interactions),
cultural dynamics (i.e., identity v. organizational), professional
dynamics (i.e., ethics v. law), organizational dynamics (i.e., pol-
icy v. values), leadership dynamics (i.e., strategy v. culture), and
other external dynamics (i.e., partisan politics v. organizational
policy and culture).
4.2   |   Forms of Dissent
While there are various strategies for dissent described in
the literature, forms include formal dissent, ethical dissent,
internal dissent, and external dissent. Formal forms of dis-
sent, as described in the literature, concern the direct-­line
disagreement that occurs through effective communication
channels. Disagreement, in this form, is voiced directly to su-
periors or in a manner that is ascribed to the conventions of
the organization (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020). Informal forms
of dissent would be the irregular activities that occur in the
organization, often relating to the more popular cases such
as whistleblowing, bureaucratic resistance, rule-­bending
and rule-­breaking behaviors, and guerrilla government (see
“Strategies” and Figure 4). The ethics of dissent is, as afore-
mentioned, more challenging to determine as both a norma-
tive question and a practical endeavor, as ethical values vary
considerably from individual to individual and organization to
organization (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020). As aforementioned,
Figure 3 depicting Waldo's 12 obligations offers insight into
various ethical dimensions that inform the actions dissenters
and guerrillas take in addition to the actions managers take
in response to conflict. Determining whether or not dissent is
ethical depends largely upon what the organization (and re-
spectively, the public) values. If organizational and public val-
ues conflict, an understanding of the purpose of dissent may
help to inform the ethical considerations for dissent in use.
For example, if organizations see a rise in dissent and disobe-
dient behaviors in response to an organizational policy or de-
cision as opposed to public value agreements (Bozeman 2018;

Finally, internal and external forms of dissent arise from les-

be expressed in a downward direction (i.e., from superior to sub-

4.4   |   Strategies
The strategies for dissent that appear in the literature range con-
siderably and appear to be driven by the contexts that shape the
conflict scenaio. For example, in a state where the organization
is effectively operating and dissent is appropriately managed,
scholars argue that formal strategies are preferred (Cox 2024;
O'Leary  2006, 2014, 2020; Borry  2017). Formal strategies are
discussed at length in the preceding paragraphs and draw upon
Hirschman's  (1970) Exit, Voice, Loyalty (and Neglect) model.
Exit strategies are fairly straightforward, with emphasis on leav-
ing the institution, department, and agency altogether. Voicing
strategies entail the communication processes involved with
relaying information verbally or in writing. Neglecting strat-
egies are considered both formal and informal disobedience,
such as shirking and deliberate inaction in organizations. Other
forms recognized in the literature include guerrilla government,
which may involve more informal and irregular activities in
organizations, such as whistleblowing, leaking, sabotage, and
other activities involving disobedience (O'Leary  2006, 2014,
2020; Borry 2017; Cox 2024; Miceli and Near 1985; Near and
Miceli 1995).
4.5   |   Informing Theories
From the literature, there are quite a few theories that au-
thors have used to examine dissent and guerrilla government.
In public administration research, guerrilla government
relates to theories of bureaucratic politics, organizations,
management, and ethics—see Figure 5 (O'Leary 2006, 2009,
2014,  2020; Cox 2024). Dissent is generally connected with
bureaucratic control theory (Golden  1992), red tape and
rule-­bending (Borry 2015; Brockmann 2017), and public val-
ues (Baehler et al. 2014; de Graaf 2011; Van Wart 2007; and
Edyvane  2020). Dissent usually involves Hirschman's Exit,
Voice, Loyalty (and Neglect) model (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020;
and Van de Walle and Vogelaar 2010), but also extends to other
theoretical concepts such as policy entrepreneur theory (Lavee
and Cohen 2019), street-­level bureaucracy (Brockmann 2017;
Gofen 2014; Lavee and Cohen 2019), systems theory (Catlaw
and Jordan 2009; Van de Walle and Vogelaar 2010), networks
(O'Leary and Bingham  2007; Jos  2016), collaborative public
management (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2017a, 2017b, 2020), ­ethics
(O'Leary  2006, 2014, 2020), bricolage (Van de Walle  2014),
and whistleblowing (de Graaf  2010; Kang  2022; Meyer and
Ouverson 2015; Near and Miceli 1995; Miceli and Near 1985).
Other social science disciplines have used various theories to un-
derstand dissent in organizations, such as unplanned behavior
theory and employee action (business), employee resistance and
three-­elephant theory of feeling, decision-­making, and sensem-
aking (communications), psychoanalytic and decision-­making
(psychology), and civil disobedience, incivility, political organiz-
ing, and feminist theory (political science). Figure 6 visualizes the
theories used by the field of study. While each theory may help
to understand and contextualize dissent and guerrilla govern-
ment in research, it is unclear which theory best informs dissent
research.

on what specific factors or conditions give rise to the rationales
for transgressive behaviors. For example, what actions or internal
“checks” might prevent destructive behaviors from occurring in
the future? Further research will help to fill these gaps.
6   |   Implications and Recommendations for
Practice
Several critical insights can be drawn from the findings of
our metasynthesis. This section aims to provide insights and
actionable steps for effectively addressing dissent and guerrilla
government, in addition to important considerations for public
managers and leaders in fostering a proactive, ethical workplace
environment that values communication and the constructive
integration of dissent in organizations and policy. Implications
will center around organizational leadership, practices, and pol-
icies, specifically.
From the review, authors are consistent in arguing that eth-
ical dissent can lead to productive conflict in organizations—
especially when challenging harmful or outdated policies,
processes, or practices (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020; Borry 2017;
DeHart-­Davis 2007; Morrison 2006; Cox 2024; Menzel Donald
and White Harvey 2015). While studies have argued that ethi-
cal dissent can lead to productive conflict in organizations, our
metasynthesis reveals that the effectiveness of dissent varies
greatly depending on a range of factors, including intrapersonal,
interpersonal, cultural, professional, organizational, leadership,
and external dynamics. After synthesizing the data, we find that
these various dynamics may also influence the type and strategy
of dissent used. Understanding the conflict dynamic may help
to inform practitioners on the form and strategy of dissent most
effective for navigating the conflict scenario.
Moreover, public managers and organizations would histor-
ically observe conflict and dissent as organizational “prob-
lems” that threaten bureaucratic authority, stability, and
productivity (Pondy 1967; Holzer 2022). The findings of the
metasynthesis challenge these assumptions and posit that
this might not be the case. Particularly, if different conflict
scenarios necessitate new or innovative solutions. In the con-
text of destructive leadership, for example, following execu-
tive orders or implementing a destructive policy could present
larger threats to the future, stability, and productivity of the
organization and its public. Our findings suggest that practi-
tioners should see dissent as information and tools for advanc-
ing organizational learning and improving decision-­making

processes in organizations. One suggestion from the litera-
ture is to cultivate a culture of learning that accepts dissent
as feedback for improvement (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020). This
would involve structural changes to shaping and reinforcing
organizational rules and values, including the creation of reg-
ular dissent channels and professional networks to ensure ef-
fective learning (Cox 2024; O'Leary 2010; Van de Walle 2014;
O'Leary and Bingham 2007; Dunlop et al. 2020; Garofalo and
Geuras  2015). It may also be useful to develop professional
services like workshops and consulting efforts that can better
prepare public managers to deal with organizational dysfunc-
tions and failures.
Moreover, the findings of the metasynthesis suggest that pun-
ishing employees for disobedience may not deter guerrilla
behaviors. Specifically, we find that public managers should
continue to provide flexibility and respond in a manner that
does not suppress employee dissent (Hollibaugh. Jr et al. 2020;
de Dreu et al. 2000; Sementelli 2020; O'Leary 2010; Cox 2024).
This is particularly important as fear of punishment or guilt
may already deter employees from speaking up and out (de
Graaf  2010; Getha-­Taylor  2009; Lavee and Cohen  2019),
but especially in organizations with strict procedural ad-
herence (Van de Walle 2014; Jos 2016; Kassing 2011, 2002).
Additionally, public administrators can act as change agents
in organizations when they engage in dissent and transgres-
sive leadership, particularly in response to poor policy or
leadership failures (deHaven Smith  2006; Cox 2024). By ac-
knowledging employee concerns and incorporating their
feedback into decision-­making processes, managers can en-
courage greater employee engagement and performance to re-
duce resistant behaviors (O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020). However,
we argue that practitioners should consider the range of con-
sequences from our findings prior to engaging in dissent.
Our findings also suggest that a choice of using which form and
what strategy is also influenced by conflict dynamics and the
state of the organization, leadership, and policies at play. In the
case of ineffective policy, Edyvane (2020) discusses recent at-
tempts by UK legislators to legislate “civility.” The legislation
under study specifically aims to criminalize public behaviors
considered to be disruptive or annoying, such as dissent. He
argues that when policy aims to govern civility, incivility is an
inevitable response. Similarly, when bureaucrats feel that a pol-
icy limits their ability to act with professional expertise, they
may choose to diverge from it (Gofen 2014). Furthermore, the
greater the perceived threat of harm, the more likely public ser-
vants are to resort to dissent and guerrilla tactics (Hollibaugh.
Jr et  al.  2020; Kassing  2011, 2002; Newswander 2015; Park
2009). This is particularly the case at the street-­level (Meyer
and Ouverson 2015). The findings from our study suggest that
managers should be aware that when policies threaten employee
expertise and well-­being, resistance or dissent may be inevita-
ble. Additionally, managers may find themselves engaging in
dissent, whether they want to or not.
A major takeaway from this study is that public servants may
need to seek alternative avenues and ‘workarounds’ to ensure
organizational learning and effectiveness. Given the capacity of
dissent to promote organizational learning and progress, there
is a dire need to continue fostering feedback in organizations
(O'Leary 2006, 2014, 2020; Cox 2024). This is crucial to ensure
the public sector's responsiveness to evolving challenges and
issues. Moreover, encouraging dissent and feedback should not
be limited to traditional channels. From our findings, other
strategies to encourage dissent include organizing professional
networks or services that can shape or inform bureaucratic
behaviors. Nonprofits, for example, may provide additional
resources and funding opportunities than organizations with
stronger political influence and control (Fyall 2016). These al-
ternative avenues for support may be useful in situations where
policy harms or leadership failure becomes an organizational
standard. Contexts, for example, where “political appointees
treat bureaucrats with overt hostility and distrust, dismantle
programs that enjoy broad public support, or behave in viola-
tion of the law” (Moynihan and Roberts 2010, 577–578).
The synthesis finds that there are many consequences to dissent
that practitioners should consider prior to engaging in dissent
(see Figure 7). Foremost, dissent appears to bring various posi-
tive, negative, and neutral consequences. Negative consequences
include being demoted or relocated, censured by professional
networks and organizations, in addition to other long-­term re-
percussions such as legal prosecution or employment termina-
tion (O'Leary  2006, 2014, 2020; de Graaf  2010; Roberts 2015;
Van de Walle 2014; Borry 2017; Lyons 2016). Moreover, some
scholars argue that dissent may, in fact, undermine effective
organizational leadership and stability, especially in contexts
wherein organizations are functional and effective in learning
(Kassing 2011; McGregor and Eugene 2007). Neutral outcomes,
on the other hand, may include consequences that are neither
wholly positive nor negative. If, for example, dissenting and
transgressive strategies are ineffective in achieving a desirable
result, consequences may include only temporary changes to
leadership behaviors, policy, and trust. When engaging in dis-
sent, it will be important that transgressors weigh the potential
risks and benefits carefully before deciding on a course of action,
especially if certain strategies may be more effective in certain
contexts than others. If used effectively, there may be consid-
erable benefits to dissent and ethical disobedience, including
immediate and long-­term change and organizational learning.
Public employees should decide whether certain disobedient
strategies will be effective and necessary for dealing with par-
ticular organizational issues and failures while balancing their
potential unforeseen consequences.
In grounding these arguments, we understand that guerrilla
government is on the rise—from outside and within. The find-
ings suggest that alternative tools and strategies may be effec-
tive in certain conflict scenarios; but especially for practitioners
aiming to safeguard the future of their organizations.

The present study explored how research on dissent and guer-
rilla government informs the theoretical and practical consid-
erations in public administration research, theory, and practice.
The present study takes a meta-­synthetic approach to traditional
systematic review by incorporating ethnographic methods to in-
ductively synthesize the findings (see Appendix A in Data S1).
To date, this is the first time a systematic review has explicitly

explored dissent and guerrilla government research. The major
findings of the review reflect the major consequences, forms,
theories, strategies, foci, and dynamics of dissent research.
Concerning contemporary issues in public administration, dis-
sent and disobedience may serve as promising tools for effec-
tively responding to organizational failures and dysfunctions.
For example, where there is a shift in public values from dem-
ocratic to autocratic (Lotta et al. 2024) or where neutral com-
petence fails to respond to sustained attacks against democracy
(Portillo et al. 2020; Bozeman et al. 2024; Boyte 2005; Brewer
and Selden 1998). From these findings, we suggest the need for
a paradigmatic shift among managers to seriously consider the
role of productive conflict and dissent as tools for organizational
learning and progress.
Additionally, the study highlights the necessity for more re-
search on the various forms, consequences, dynamics, and
strategies for theory development on dissent and transgressive
leadership. This will be particularly useful in prescribing solu-
tions and prevention strategies for future and long-­term adverse
outcomes that arise from, for example, destructive leadership
and ineffective policies in organizations. Fields of public man-
agement, leadership, and public policy will substantially benefit
from more research and theory building on dissent. Strategies
for dissent, such as informal and formal strategies and the con-
sequences, depend on a clear understanding of the context in
use. Additional research unpacking this context may help to
define the ethical parameters for which methods may be most
effective, and which may not. As consequences for dissent may
range from negative to positive (as well as from formal to infor-
mal), further context may help to clarify the reasons for and con-
sequences of dissent in use. Despite potential risks associated
with dissent and transgressive behaviors, there is potential for
the use of disobedient behaviors in addressing ethical dilemmas
and dysfunctions in organizations.
This article argues for the importance of dissent not as a prob-
lem, but as a tool for organizational learning and progress. The
findings suggest that public managers and organizations should
continue working to foster a culture of learning that accepts
dissent as feedback for improvement to ensure responsive and
responsible organizations. Alternative means may be neces-
sary to uphold the public interest, and public administrators
are encouraged to seek their professional networks and outside
resources to leverage opportunities and resources as necessary
until the public sector can effectively serve the public and its
future interests. From the literature, punishing employees for
disobedience does not deter guerrilla behaviors, and these are
likely to persist so long as the public sector remains resistant
to learning and dissent. Hence, managers should maneuver in
a manner that will not suppress critical dissent. As a long-­term
solution, managers should continue to acknowledge employee
concerns and incorporate their feedback into decision-­making
processes to encourage greater engagement and performance.
Contrary to traditional assumptions of the public manager, bu-
reaucrats can be change agents—especially when they leverage
dissent and transgressive leadership strategies to effectively
respond to institutional failures. In certain cases, like destruc-
tive leadership or ineffective policy, disobedient behaviors will
come at a cost, but may also help to ensure long-­term learning.
Various consequences may arise from dissent (see Figure 6), but
are bureaucrats willing to do what it takes to protect the future
of our democratic institutions?

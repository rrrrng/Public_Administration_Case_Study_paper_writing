ABSTRACT
Although the results of paradigmatic change are a common focus of the literature, significantly less attention has been paid to the
process through which public administration reform takes place. In particular, the role of meso-­level induced changes has only
recently started to receive some attention, and not much is yet known about how collaborative systems at the managerial level
affect pathways of administrative reform. Here we compare the two most significant periods of reform in New Zealand in the
past 50 years. The 1988–1989 reforms were based on simple (arguably simplistic) assumptions, amenable to rapid and politically
led top-­down reform. In contrast, the 2012–2020 reforms conceived of public administration as a complex social system suited
to incremental managerially induced change that was mainly driven by the bureaucratic elite. Our article shows that collective
leadership may result in extensive paradigmatic changes through endogenous feedback and meso-­level induced processes.
1   |   Executive Summary
The way that public agencies are organized and managed needs
to change—either because no institution is perfect and therefore
can always improve, or because the operating context, needs,
expectations, and technologies have changed. Countless docu-
ments advocate for one destination or another, but fewer look at
the question of how to get there—how to change from the cur-
rent state to the desired state. After all, a hypothetical perfect
administrative design is of little use if you cannot get there. We
refer to the alternate pathways of getting from the current state
to the desired state as “reform processes.”
In this article, we look at different periods of reform within
New Zealand that each employed different reform processes.
Despite being a small and remote country, New Zealand is of in-
ternational interest to civil servants, policy-­makers, and public
administration scholars both because of its sudden and radical
reforms in the 1980s and its gradual reforms in the period since.
It is notoriously difficult to establish which jurisdiction is “best”
but New Zealand's civil service is generally regarded as high
performing.
The first period that we look at is 1988–1989. Following a fis-
cal crisis, an ideological Treasury advised the Government to
implement radical reform. Reforms were theoretically pure,
drawing from specific branches of economic theory. Only a
handful of people were involved, several senior officials from
central agencies and their respective ministers. Legislation was
the main mechanism for introducing change—the State Sector
Act 1988 and the Public Finance Act 1989—with little prior
consultation with the agencies affected by the changes. Reform
was very much something “done to” civil servants and public
agencies by politicians acting on the advice of the Treasury. This
idea of reform being “done to” an unwilling or change-­resistant
bureaucracy is commonly seen around the world.

The dramatic jolts of 1988 and 1989 were followed by a decade
or more of agencies working to implement the new rules. There
was surprisingly little tinkering over this period, and no obvi-
ous mechanism for improvement. One commentator noted that
New Zealand's theoretical purity in the 1980s may have made it
more difficult to contemplate adaptations in the 1990s. A series
of small changes occurred in the 2000s, most notably in 2004,
but until 2011 there was considerable evidence that the radical
reforms of 1988 and 1989 had “stuck,” in both positive and neg-
ative senses of the word.
The second period we look at is 2012–2020, although it is not
yet clear that this period of reform has ended. In the aftermath
of the global financial crisis of 2008, New Zealand again faced
a worsening fiscal outlook. They challenged civil servants with
the task of becoming more effective. The ensuing reforms in-
volved much more leadership by the permanent politically neu-
tral civil service. The administrative heads of agencies (“chief
executives”) formed a team that collectively led a reform pro-
gram that persisted through multiple elections, changes in the
prime minister, and changes of government. These reforms had
many “parents”—rather than theoretically pure, it was a brico-
lage of different ideas. Reform was led by a group in which every
chief executive was replaced at least once, new members were
inculcated into the values and assumptions of the group, and the
basic ideas stayed the same. Reforms were implemented grad-
ually, first experimenting on a small scale, then progressively
scaling, formalizing the solutions that were effective, and even-
tually codifying practice with the Public Service Act 2020.
From this country study, we draw several inferences that may
be of use to civil servants and politicians alike. The first is that
reform does not need to come from politicians, and it is possible
for civil servants to reform themselves. Politicians can challenge
civil servants to take responsibility for changing the way that
public agencies are organized and managed to become more ef-
fective. Civil servants can earn the freedom to undergo self-­reform
by being so relentless in their efforts to improve that politicians
feel they do not need to introduce more change from above. Civil
servants can become “reform entrepreneurs,” experimenting and
innovating with change. These alternate leadership roles, between
external political leadership and internal civil servant leadership,
are continually and tacitly negotiated. We consider both as alter-
nate pathways with different advantages and disadvantages.
A second key insight is that reform does not need to start with a
“bang.” The sequence does not need to begin with announcing
big changes and then scrambling to implement them. Large-­
scale change can happen through small incremental changes in
the same direction over a long period of time. In fact, for reasons
we articulate below, it is possible that externally imposed reform
needs to be sudden because it is difficult to sustain, and inter-
nally driven change needs to be incremental to maintain broad
support. Legislation can occur at different phases and serve dif-
ferent purposes—it can create change, or it can be used later to
codify emergent practices.
A third insight is that reforms do not need to be theoretically pure
or follow a single big idea. Theoretically pure reforms usually allow
for only a few people to take the lead and feel a sense of responsibil-
ity for the change. Anything with many “parents” is likely to be a
messy combination of different ideas that can achieve an imperfect
and dynamic consensus across this wider group. Broad ownership
may allow reform periods to outlast individual leaders—so long
as the entire leadership group is not replaced at once, new leaders
learn and adopt the reform program as they seek social member-
ship within the group. Additionally, hybrid reforms may be more
adaptable as different ideas assume greater salience in response to
policy feedback and changing the context.
A key downside of incremental reform of this type is that it re-
quires persistence. Many reforms, particularly those imposed
from outside the civil service, “burn out” after only a couple of
years. This may be because it requires enormous effort and the
expenditure of political capital to continually impose change on
a resistant bureaucracy, or because narrowly owned change de-
pends on key individuals who may move on. A permanent civil
service may feel that it can wait out the impermanence of indi-
vidual ministers. If the reform “window” is thus constrained,
politicians may be tempted to push as much change as possible
before the window shuts. We, therefore, contrast two different
viable approaches—theoretical purity, narrow external own-
ership, sudden, with a short duration, or hybrid and adaptive,
with broad internal ownership, incremental, but persisting over
a long period of time to create large change.
One final consideration is that each alternative pathway may
create its own path dependence and feedback loop (see Figure 2).
Externally imposed reform disempowers civil servants and may
teach passivity. Internally driven reform builds the capacity to
lead reform but also the expectations from civil servants that
they will be involved in any future change.

2   |   Introduction
Although the results of paradigmatic change are a common
focus of the literature on ideational policy change, significantly
less attention has been paid to the mechanisms and processes
through which it takes place (Chackerian and Mavima  2001;
Torres 2004). The literature is largely consensual on the need to
further develop ideational mechanisms to understand how ex-
actly ideas influence policy change (Allain and Madariaga 2020;
Campbell  2002; Metha  2010). Among these mechanisms, bri-
colage—“the recombination of old and new ideas by policy
entrepreneurs” (Allain and Madariaga  2020)—has started
to receive some attention but its theorization and empirical
demonstration remains incipient (Carstensen  2011). Studying
pathways and observing the mechanisms of ideational change is
important because public administration reform does not hap-
pen automatically. Instead, the literature is filled with cases of
theoretically sound reforms that have failed to be implemented
(Wilenski 1986; Caiden 1999; Sarker 2004).
Here we use the most significant periods of reform in New
Zealand (“NZ”) in the past 50 years to illustrate two distinct and
contrasting approaches, showing the emergence of what we con-
sider a new trend in administrative reform: sudden top-­down
change driven through decree; and gradual meso-­level induced
(bureaucracy driven) change that is subsequently codified.
We contribute to the literature on ideational policy change by
contrasting the mechanisms and processes of a traditional par-
adigmatic shift (as originally presented by Hall (1993)) during
the 1988–1989 reforms, to a gradual and bricolage process that
resulted in the 2020 Public Service Act reform. In particular,
we emphasize the new constitutive and political role of senior
bureaucrat managers (agency “chief executives”) following the
enactment of collective leadership institutions and processes.
We contribute to the literature on “agency at the managerial in-
terface” by going beyond traditional theoretical debates on top-­
down/bottom-­up changes, as well as by adding to the evolving
debates on the relationship between politics and administration
(Cloutier et al. 2016; Compton and Meier 2017). Finally, we also
reflect on the political and practical consequences of bricolage
in policy changes, contributing to another under-­researched
field and, potentially, to the design of more successful adminis-
trative reforms by practitioners (Sewerin et al. 2022; Allain and
Madariaga 2020).
This managerial meso-­level takes on additional importance
in a New Public Management (NPM) and post-­NPM context.
Traditional permanent secretaries might have been assumed
to be joined-­at-­the-­hip with the minister they were supporting.
NPM introduced a more delegated public service bargain (Hood
2002) and greater managerial autonomy for chief executives
(Hood  1991). Nowhere was this truer than in NZ, where un-
equaled managerialism was compounded by the physical sepa-
ration of ministers in the “beehive” executive wing of Parliament
from chief executives and the departments they lead (Scott and
Hughes 2025). This raises the question of how chief executives
use this increased autonomy to influence institutional reform.
We argue that paradigmatic changes through processes that
have been previously theorized by Hall (1993) no longer apply
to the current societal/historic context in NZ, which does not
mean to imply that governments became unable to achieve
extensive paradigmatic transformations. Our analysis points,
instead, to a tendency of paradigmatic changes that are driven
by endogenous feedback and managerial negotiation processes
(mainly driven by the bureaucratic elite as opposed to by minis-
ters). We contend, moreover, that paradigmatic changes as neat
replacement of old paradigms by new ones are likely to become
less common in our complex societies, giving place to “brico-
lage” and “interparadigmatic hybridity,” meaning the creative
combination of “ideas and tools proffered by different public
governance paradigms” (Carstensen et al. 2023).
The reforms implemented in NZ from 1988 to 1989 were based
on simple (arguably simplistic, see Gregory 2000) assumptions,
and therefore were suitable for top-­down, ministerially driven
reform. Rapid top-­down reforms were seen as necessary to re-
spond to a fiscal crisis (Henderson and McIntyre  1990), and
inversely, the crisis was seen as an opportunity to implement
radical reforms previously dreamed up by the NZ Treasury
(Vitalis and Butler 2019). This approach may have been effective
for addressing the low-­hanging fruit of a low-­performing bu-
reaucracy, but it struggled to produce further incremental gains
in a complex environment.
In contrast, the reforms implemented between 2012 and 2020,
followed the introduction of collaborative and network-­based ar-
rangements that led to negotiated and manager-­driven changes.
The NZ public service was already seen as relatively high-­
performing; as a senior public servant noted, this was not the
time to “throw the baby out with the bathwater” (interview). As
shown in this article, these reforms were trialed through non-­
legislative means and refined over several years, incorporating
ideas from heterogeneous sources and theoretical inspirations.
While non-­legislative solutions were more flexible, they were
also seen as providing less stability over time, and so the most
successful practices were eventually codified. The limitation of
this approach is that it is not suited to situations where there is
a contextual (political, fiscal) need for sudden drastic change.
Various authors have argued that the era of simplistic re-
form is over, and instead we need bricoleurs (Freeman  2007;
Fuglsang 2010; Van de Walle 2014; Bugge and Bloch 2016). This
article underscores this view, suggesting that bricolage reform
that is implemented incrementally and then subsequently cod-
ified as and when needed, as seen in NZ 2012–2020, tends to
be effective and as transformative as radical change. Structural,
cultural, and environmental drivers mean that reforms are
increasingly more complex and hybrid. As Christensen and
Lægreid (2011) argue: “rather than looking at hybrid forms as
some kind of an illness that needs to be cured, we should regard
it as a systemic feature that may have advantages of flexibility.”
The goal of this article is, thus, three-­fold. First, we will explain
why the traditional understanding of the process through which

1988 and the Public Service Act 2020.
3   |   Paradigms, Pathways, Causes, and
Consequences
There are multiple approaches to describing the processes and
pathways of public administration reform (and stasis). The
importance of “path dependency” and “increasing returns” in
delaying or impeding institutional change has been frequently
addressed in the literature. According to this view, change
would necessarily be incremental, moving in small, and usu-
ally slow steps (Lindblom 1959; Pierson 2000). Complementing
that view, some have also drawn attention to the fact that the
adding up of small and incremental changes over time can
result in radical alterations of the status quo (Streeck and
Thelen  2005). Others would not deny the influence of path
dependence, but would stress “punctuated equilibrium” tra-
jectories, when “focusing events” draw attention to a certain
area and trigger dramatic change that is followed and pre-
ceded by policy stability (Baumgartner and Jones 1993). The
importance of emerging and declining levels of enthusiasm
in addressing difficult issues is also highlighted by Downs'
“issue attention cycle,” according to which a stage of “alarmed
discovery and euphoric enthusiasm” around an issue will be
followed by the realization of costs and a “gradual decline of
interest” (Downs  1972). Kingdon  (1995) also contributed to
this debate in depicting policy change as inherently depen-
dent on the opening of “windows of opportunity,” a rather un-
predictable situation in which problem perceptions, political
contexts, and pre-­designed policy solutions converge and can
eventually lead to policy change.
The importance of ideas and institutions in administrative
change has also been broadly recognized in the past decades,
but not much consensus has been reached on the processes or
pathways of “ideas-­driven” administrative reforms (Hall 1993;
Hajer  1995; Blyth  1997, 2011; Fischer  2003; Carstensen  2011;
Hogan and Howlett  2015). A crucial problem identified with
this literature is the lack of specification of causal mecha-
nisms, which have been said to emerge from a rather narrow
focus on actors, institutions, or discourses (Campbell 2002). In
this context, the concept of “policy paradigms” is paramount.
Commonly defined as an “interpretive framework” or a “frame-
work of ideas and standards that specify not only the goals of
policy and the kinds of instruments that can be used to attain
them, but also the very nature of the problems they are meant to
be addressing”; paradigmatic changes have been used to explain
some of the most dramatic policy changes in history (e.g., the
movement from Keynesianism to monetarism in the 1970–1989
British experience) (Hall 1993, 279).
Most institutionalists (Hall 1993; Hall and Taylor 1996; Capoccia
and Kelemen 2007; Schmidt 2008) would describe paradigmatic
changes as “crisis driven,” “episodic,” and “radical” shifts. It fol-
lows from theory that administrative reforms that emerge from
paradigmatic change are likely to be comprehensive, internally
coherent, and represent a clear rupture with the past. These
changes are described as driven by factors that are exogenous to
the established institutions, emerging from new “political judg-
ments” that win due to the positional advantages of particular
factions. They are, thus, politically driven due to their associ-
ation with the political judgment of those who face complexity
and conflicting opinions and “decide whom to regard as author-
itative” (Hall 1993, 280).
The necessarily radical process of change described by Hall
has, however, been met with criticism. Later studies on policy
dynamics have begun to point to the potentially gradual and
endogenous nature of paradigmatic change as a more com-
mon and perhaps even more desirable pathway of change. The
identification and description of a “bottom-­up pathway” point
to paradigmatic shifts that emerge from the incremental evo-
lution of ideas (Lindblom 1959), emphasizing dynamic endog-
enous drivers such as feedback effects and balance of power
shifts, and presenting policy paradigms as much less stable
and internally coherent than previously assumed (Mahoney
and Thelen 2009; Carstensen 2011; Hogan and Howlett 2015).
More emphasis is also warranted on the increasingly import-
ant role of managers in policy reform. Situated as intermedi-
aries between political elites and “street-­level bureaucrats”
(Lipsky 1980), those sitting at this managerial interface are
perceived to have gained more political centrality as constitu-
tive agents of democratic systems (Compton and Meier 2017),
but very few studies have addressed the mechanisms through
which they engage in and promote policy change (Cloutier
et al. 2016).
One of the pillars of this alternative theorization is the idea that
first-­ and second-­order changes can result in third-­order para-
digmatic change without external shocks or broader societal cri-
sis (Sewerin et al. 2022).1 This perspective adds, moreover, that
ignoring aspects such as the “directionality of change” and its
“tempo” impedes analysts to observe the cumulative and highly
transformative aspects of policy change over long periods. Thus,
this argument suggests that if slow changes are moving in one
single direction for a relatively long period of time, they may
add up resulting in big paradigmatic changes (Cashore and
Howlett 2007, 538).
Contributors to this view also emphasize the importance of
processes such as “layering,” “hybridity,” and “bricolage.”
According to these models of change, a “sharp line between
institutional stability and institutional change” cannot be
drawn (Streeck and Thelen  2005). Incremental processes of
change would instead be widespread and lead to “major his-
torical discontinuities” through, for example, “minor addi-
tions and repairs” and “layering” (Streeck and Thelen 2005;

Kuhnian theories of paradigmatic change (Carstensen 2011;
Hogan and Howlett 2015; Mahoney and Thelen 2009; Streeck
and Thelen 2005). This would not be in opposition to the per-
ception of continuity as a crucial trait of paradigms or institu-

shocks are required for their change (Hall and Taylor 1996;
Pierson  2000). This view is similarly acknowledged by dis-
cursive institutionalists who, despite still focusing on crisis
as motivators of change, accept that policy discourses display
“evolutionary changes” (Schmidt 2002, 224).
Our investigation of NZ history of administrative reforms ex-
emplifies and contributes to the understanding of the mecha-
nisms of gradual paradigmatic change, to the importance of
managerial agency, while also challenging the conventional
wisdom around the overall coherence of its potential results
(Howlett and Ramesh  2002; Lindner  2003). We argue that
due to the impact of collaborative tools and institutions, the
NZ government might no longer be adopting purist forms of
public governance paradigms, as has already been demon-
strated by previous work (Scott et  al.  2022; Christensen
and Lægreid 2011), and has instead tended toward paradig-
matic hybridity and bricolage or the articulation of dissimi-
lar elements belonging to different governance paradigms
(Carstensen et al. 2023). In this article, we propose, therefore,
to discuss and exemplify two evolving streams of the liter-
ature on policy change, the first relates to new pathways of
paradigmatic reforms and the nature of its endogenous mech-
anisms, and the second concerns the results or consequences
of these new processes.
In our case description, we will, first, explain why the tradi-
tional understanding of the process through which paradig-
matic shifts take place may not apply to recent administrative
reforms in NZ, despite the large magnitude of the resulting
changes and remarkable ideational shifts. Second, we claim
that layering, hybridity, and bricolage are more likely results
of these gradual and managerial-­induced change processes.
We achieve these goals via historical process tracing. We
compare two large reform periods in NZ administration: the
NPM reforms implemented through the State Sector Act 1988
and Public Finance Act 1989, and the more recent reforms
that resulted in the Public Service Act in 2020. The NPM re-
forms that were implemented in NZ in the late 80s provide a
relatively clear example of administrative reforms emerging
from a classical process of paradigmatic shift. As described in
section 4.1, the process through which they took place would
broadly resonate with the description epitomized by Hall as
third-­order changes. The reform trajectory characterizing
the enactment of the recent Public Service Act in 2020, on
the other hand, despite resulting in paradigmatic shifts (Scott
et al. 2022), does not confirm the trajectory of Hall's theory. It
would resonate instead with descriptions of bricolage paradig-
matic shifts. We do, therefore, rely on the analysis of endoge-
nous institutional drivers as well as directionality of changes
in NZ administrative reforms to claim that despite not follow-
ing the classic pathway, the 2020 NZ reforms did result in par-
adigmatic change. We also oppose Hall and add to more recent
critical voices in that the accumulation of anomalies from one
paradigm will necessarily result in a rearrangement of “the
organization and standard operating procedures of the policy
process so as to institutionalize a new paradigm.” We argue
that the anomalies might be responded to via a process of ad
hoc, incremental, and rather inelegant set of changes that re-
sult in “interparadigmatic hybridity.” Given the increasing
importance of collective leadership and collaborative gover-
nance mechanisms in NZ public sector, we observe the crucial
importance assumed by managers as “bricoleurs” or reform
agents in this process, shedding new light to the endogenous
mechanisms of gradual paradigmatic reform and, thus, con-
tributing to theory development and practitioners' awareness.
4   |   Methodology
This article uses historical process tracing (Collier 2011) to study
two reform periods within a single jurisdiction: 1988–1989 and
2012–2020 in NZ. Historical process tracing is based on detailed
historical description and the identification of “critical events”
that may allow for causal inferences. We approach this using
a comparative rather than within-­case method. Despite study-
ing a single jurisdiction, the period of stability that separates
both reform periods allows them to be treated as separate cases
(Lange 2012). This falls within the tradition of “policy feedback
research” and looks at endogenous factors or the impacts of
“real-­world effects” of adopted policies on subsequent changes
through historical process tracing. In other words, by looking
at official documents, conducting semi-­structured interviews,
and acting as participant observers in the NZ government,
we take a closer empirical look at “post-­adoption feedback” as
both “self-­reinforcing” and “self-­undermining” trends of pol-
icy reform (Jacobs and Weaver  2015; Skogstad  2017; Béland
and Schlager 2019). We look, for example, at the justifications
given for policy reforms, their methods of implementation, and
their consequences according to local commentators and partic-
ipants. We highlight three areas of contrast: theoretical purity
versus eclecticism and pluralism, the socialization of reform
concepts and direct influence by managers, and the role of legis-
lation in supporting reform.
NZ is a jurisdiction of high international interest since its rapid
introduction of administrative reforms associated with NPM in
the period 1988–1989 (Kettl 2000). Several other features make
it an ideal candidate for study: it has a unitary system with
most services delivered by the central government, no states
or provinces, and a small role for local government (Pollitt and
Bouckaert  2011); and its administrative features are broadly
described in a single piece of foundational legislation that has
been repealed and replaced three times and describes four dis-
tinct, relatively stable periods of administrative settings (Scott
et al. 2022). NZ's “public service” was established by the passage

We limit our analysis in this article to the two most recent

none of the architects of these earlier reforms are still alive. In
contrast, the passage of the State Sector Act 1988 and Public
Service Act 2020 has been documented extensively. The study
relies on various types of qualitative evidence to trace the his-
tory and critical events associated with each reform: government
documents, semi-­structured interviews, participant observer
notes, and academic accounts.
The 1988–1989 period is documented extensively by historian
Henderson and McIntyre (1990), including interviews with key
figures. The leading public servant from that period recorded his
reflections in a 2001 book (Scott 2001), and there have been nu-
merous academic studies (e.g., Boston et al. 1996; Schick 1996;
Gregory 2000, 2006). Key facts were verified with help from the
Parliamentary library. The 2012–2020 period is documented
by a combination of government documents, including all ar-
ticles in which public administration reforms were debated by
Cabinet (Coleman  2012a, 2012b, 2012c, 2012d, 2012e, 2012f,
2012g, 2012h, 2012i, 2012j, 2012k, 2012l, 2013; Bennett  2016;

and replacement with the Public Service Act 2020. In addition,
semi-­structured interviews were conducted with six other offi-
cials who contributed to these reforms.
5   |   Findings: Two Reform Periods
NZ is a jurisdiction of high international interest due to its his-
tory of significant, theory-­informed reform (Schick 1996) and
due to an internal drive for continuous improvement (Donadelli
and Lodge 2019). We contend for the purpose of this article that
there are two significant periods of public administration re-
form in the past 50 years and that both resulted in paradigmatic
change: the sudden reforms of 1988–1989 and the incremental
bottom-­up changes over the period 2012–2020, although we ac-
knowledge that it is too soon to conclude that this most recent
reform period has ended. Our core argument is that these re-
forms represent contrasting reform mechanisms, and we add to
existing theorizations by pointing to possible causes and con-
sequences. The first is akin to the traditional process of policy
paradigm changes described by Hall (1993). The second follows
a bricolage process that results in interparadigmatic hybridity.
5.1   |   Sudden Change: 1988–1989
The period from 1962 to 1988 is generally described as central-
ized, bureaucratic, and unresponsive (see, e.g., Gorringe 1987;
Henderson and McIntyre  1990; Dunleavy and Hood  1994;
Boston et al. 1996; Schick 1996; Kaul 1997; Scott 2001), although
others argue that such descriptions are exaggerated or carica-
tured (Gregory 2000).
Shortly after each national election, NZ public service depart-
ments supply new and returning ministers with a document
known as the Briefing to the Incoming Minister (“BIM”). The
1984 election saw the center-­left Labor party return to govern-
ment after 12 years in opposition. Following this election, the
Treasury supplied a BIM advocating for radical economic and
institutional reforms (Treasury  1984, see also characteriza-
tion in Schick 1996). The government took forward two major
changes in the short term: the State-­Owned Enterprises Act 1986
changed the legal structure of “commercial” activities owned by
the crown, and machinery of government changes on 1 April
1987 that separated several departments into separate agencies
responsible for policy and operations (Boston et al. 1996). A third
input, a 1986 report “Pay fixing in the state sector” recommended
changes to employment arrangements that would influence sub-
sequent legislative change (Henderson and McIntyre 1990).
The 1987 election saw the Labor party re-­elected. The Treasury
BIM (1987) again advocated for significant change. The election
was held on 15 August 1987. In October, “a group of four con-
cerned ministers and some of their senior officials held several
long meetings” to discuss radical reform (Scott 2001, 1). On 8
December, just 2 months later, the State Sector Bill was intro-
duced into Parliament (Parliamentary Hansard 1987).
This legislation passed into law as the State Sector Act follow-
ing royal assent on 30 March 1988. By 26 July 1989, the entire
system for the management of public accounts was significantly
altered by the assent of the Public Finance Act. In little more
than a year, NZ had shifted from a traditional Westminster-­style
bureaucracy, to “the most extensive and intellectually coher-
ent” (Lodge and Gill 2011, 142) implementation of what would
become aligned with the global NPM movement (Hood 1991;
Osborne and Gaebler  1992). Kettl  (2000, 446) argued that no
jurisdiction had gone “farther or faster” in implementing NPM
reforms.
The details of the changes are explored at length elsewhere
(e.g., Boston et al. 1996). In brief, NZ implemented a range of
reforms that were informed by organizational theory and new
institutional economic theory drawn from analogous practices
in the private sector (Schick 1996). This saw ministers re-­cast as
“purchasers” of goods and services from departments through
quasi-­contractual “purchase agreements,” with agency “chief
executives” given unprecedented autonomy in how to manage
the organizations they controlled to deliver those goods and ser-
vices (Ayto 2011).
Whether these changes were effective in their stated aims or
in improving the overall performance of the NZ public service
is difficult to ascertain. Some commentators claim significant
benefits to efficiency, effectiveness, and political responsive-
ness (Scott 2001), whereas others focus on potential challenges
(Gregory 2000). What is not disputed is that these changes rep-
resented a radical and sudden departure from the status quo
(Henderson and McIntyre 1990) and were predominantly driven
by new “political judgments” that win due to the positional ad-
vantages of particular factions (Hall 1993, 280).

5.2   |   Stable Period: 1990–2011
The period from 1990 until 2011 saw relatively less change,
despite three further periods of economic contraction in 1991,
1998, and 2009 (Hall and McDermott 2016). That is not to say
that this period did not experience turbulence, but instead
that the design of the public administration model for NZ
changed significantly more through the passage of the 1988
and 1989 legislation than in the subsequent 21 years (Scott and
Donadelli 2024). By “significantly more” we mean that changes
were not implemented on a high abstraction level (i.e., goals and
instrumental logic) characteristic of paradigmatic changes, but
initially occurred on “measures” and “operationalization levels”
(Sewerin et al. 2022). For example:
•	 the development of Strategic Results Areas in 1994, what
Pallot (1998, 1) described as the “collective strategy phase”;
•	 the Managing for Outcomes phase beginning in 2002
(Baehler 2003; Cook 2004; Ryan 2006);
•	 the Crown Entities Act 2004, which made changes to the
way that arms-­length bodies were governed and managed
(Gill 2009).
The characterization of the 1988–1989 period as sudden and
dramatic, and of the 1990–2011 period as incremental without
altering the underlying logic, was shared by many observers.
Gregory  (2006, 153) observed that “the original reforms were
theoretically informed, comprehensive in scope, and authorized
quickly by the opportunistic dominance of the political execu-
tive. By comparison, the latest changes are pragmatic, incremen-
tal, and much more slowly put together” (Gregory 2006, 153).
The 1990s saw what Scott (2003, 91) described as the “imple-
mentation” phase and what Ryan (2003, p39) described as “pro-
gressive collective learning, in contrast to the rapid and radical
reforms of the first stage.”
Lodge and Gill (2011, 160) reviewed changes in this period and
found “considerable evidence to suggest an entrenchment of
ideas that were initially formalized in the 1980s.” Schick (1996,
3) contended that the coherence of the NZ model would make
it difficult to make improvements, as doing so would “strip the
system of its magnificent conceptual architecture.” Two studies
argued that changes in the 1990s and 2000s were incremental,
pragmatic, or politically driven (Chapman and Duncan  2007;
Duncan and Chapman  2010), and that these changes had re-
sulted not in the creation of a “new New Zealand model” but
instead a muddling that lacked “the theoretical coherence and
market-­led focus” that it once had (Duncan and Chapman 2010,
1). Ultimately, Lodge and Gill  (2011, 141) concluded that “far
from a new era of administrative reforms,” the idea of any new
model of public administration in NZ emerging between 1990
and 2011 was a “myth.”
5.3   |   Persistent Incrementalism: 2012–2020
In 2011, the NZ government was facing a worsening fiscal out-
look in the aftermath of the global financial crisis. Other coun-
tries had implemented significant austerity programs, but the
NZ government looked for alternatives, including improving
the fiscal outlook by improving public services to achieve
better outcomes and therefore reduce future demand on gov-
ernment resources (Scott and Boyd 2017). As then-­Finance-­
Minister Sir William English (English 2015, see also Scott and
Boyd 2022) put it, “what works for the community works for
the government's books.” The government commissioned an
advisory group of government, community, and business lead-
ers to report on what would make public services “better.” The
Better Public Services Advisory Group Report (State Services
Commission  2011a) was submitted to the government in
November 2011 and informed a relatively consistent direction
of travel for the next 9 years.
While the reforms of 1988–1989 were closely associated with
the Fourth Labour Government, the reform period 2012–2020
spanned several elections and a change in government from the
right-­leaning Fifth National Government (2008–2017) to the
left-­leaning Sixth Labour Government (2017–2023). On 7 August
2020, the State Sector Act of 1988 was repealed and replaced
with the Public Service Act 2020, in the first major rewrite of
NZ's foundational public administration legislation in over
30 years. An earlier article (Scott et al. 2022) conducted a textual
analysis of the Public Service Act and associated Cabinet papers
(Hipkins 2019a, 2019b, 2019c, 2019d, 2019e, 2019f, 2019g) and
concluded that the Act represented a significant change to the
status quo and was much more representative of “post-­NPM”
ideas than those of NPM. Two other studies associated the 2012–
2020 reform periods with “behavioral” approaches to public ad-
ministration (Scott and Hughes 2023, 2025). The Cabinet papers
use the words “codify” or “codification” 24 times, reinforcing
that while the Act is a substantial change from its 1988 predeces-
sor, it was not a change in direction from contemporary practice,
instead simply confirming and legislating for previously non-­
legislative solutions.
Interviews with senior public servants involved in the legisla-
tion confirmed that the Public Service Act 2020 was intended to
be consistent with a direction of travel established by the Better
Public Services Advisory Group in 2011, and tested and refined
between 2012 and 2019. They also clarified the important role of
chief executives' collaboration in supporting government prior-
ities, which started as informal “meetings” evolving toward the
institutionalization or codification of what is currently known
as the “Public Service Leadership Team”—a group tasked with
leading public sector reform in a cohesive and “joined-­up” way
(Scott and Hughes 2025). When asked about how they would
rate the continuity of ideas of the 2020 reforms in a Likert scale
(1 being complete agreement and 5 being complete disagreement
with the statement) all the interviewees completely agreed that
“there was considerable continuity in ideas.” As exemplified by
one of the interviewees: “particularly the paper I was involved
in, was all built upon the changes that were brought about in
2011–2012, some of those things were strengthened such as the
Public Service Leadership Team; they already existed, but it
was not in legislation. Another example is the functional chief
executive, one could be the chief executive of a function of an
agency, that was an extension of the functional lead role that
was brought in in 2012” (Interviewee 3, 30 August 2021).
This seems to be a good representation of “bottom-­up” par-
adigmatic changes (meaning that the final codification or

to be more sustainable for two reasons. First, the fact that
it is “bottom-­up” makes it “fool proof” as it has been imple-

larger shares of the Public Service Leadership Team and soci-

The passage of the Public Service Act in 2020 involved signifi-
cantly more engagement with lower-­level public servants and
with citizens than did the State Sector Act 1988 (Henderson
and McIntyre 1990; Scott and Merton 2023; see also the public
consultation document State Services Commission 2018), it was
still a heavily hierarchical process with chief executives occupy-
ing a privileged position. In this case study, the involvement of
the leaders of all 34 public service departments was particularly
different from the “top-­down” reform of the 1980s, driven by a
few ministers and leaders of the three central agencies. A truly
bottom-­up reform, crowd-­sourced from an even broader group,
would have provided an even stronger contrast; however, this
influence deserves attention as an indication of an evolving role
for managers.
Describing and understanding this process may provide a
palpable example for policymakers to promote future para-
digmatic changes in policy, which may lead to transformative
societal change in times when they are most needed. There
are numerous examples of consistent direction of travel,
which would point to the importance of endogenous drivers
and support the “cumulative effect” hypothesis by Sewerin
et al. (2022). These include: collective leadership, collabora-
tive governance, functional leadership, spirit of service, and
stewardship.
5.3.1   |   Collective Leadership
When the State Sector Act was enacted in 1988, chief executives
were individually responsible for delivering the goods and ser-
vices purchased by a minister. An amendment passed in 2013
made chief executives also responsible for supporting the “col-
lective interests of government” (State Sector Act 1988, s32).
Beginning in 2014, chief executives began to meet regularly
together and took upon themselves the moniker “State Sector
Leadership Team” (now “Public Service Leadership Team”)
and the responsibility to act as “collective stewards of the pub-
lic service” (Scott and Macaulay  2020). In 2020, the Public
Service Leadership Team was given legal status in the Public
Service Act. The change from individual responsibility to col-
lective meetings and a responsibility for supporting the collec-
tive interests of government are clear examples of change in the
operationalization level of specific mechanisms of control as de-
scribed by Sewerin et al. (2022, second order change in a policy
tool intended at coordination). For example, rather than each de-
partment developing and pursuing their own programs for im-
proving diversity and inclusion of the public service workforce,
the Public Service Leadership Team formed a subcommittee
(“Papa Pounamu”) that was responsible for developing a diver-
sity and inclusion plan for the entire public service; chief execu-
tives, through unanimous assent at a Public Service Leadership
Team meeting, then agreed to implement this plan (Hughes and
Scott 2021). The introduction of Public Service Leadership Team
into law in 2020 represents the higher abstraction level change
(third-­order change) that emerged from the operational change
in a bottom-­up pathway (from instrumental change first to par-
adigmatic change).
5.3.2   |   Collaborative Governance
The Better Public Services Advisory Group also gave consid-
eration to how small groups of chief executives could be held
collectively responsible for specific cross-­cutting tasks (“spe-
cific purpose boards,” State Services Commission  2011d).
Several “boards” were trialed between 2012 and 2019 (Scott and
Bardach 2019; Scott and Merton 2021; Scott and Boyd 2023), be-
fore “Interdepartmental Executive Boards” were given formal
legislative status as (Public Service Act 2020, s25). For example,
the Climate Change Chief Executives Board is an interdepart-
mental board responsible for coordinating the implementation
of NZ's emissions reduction plan; this board includes chief exec-
utive from eight departments providing consolidated advice to a
single minister.
5.3.3   |   Functional Leadership
Following the passage of the State Sector Act 1988, NZ chief ex-
ecutives had unprecedented autonomy in how they configured
the departments they led. This resulted in substantial innova-
tion, with each department implementing different systems
and processes for the delivery of back-­office functions (Jensen
et al. 2014). In 2012, Cabinet appointed three “functional leads”
with responsibility for improving capability and interoperabil-
ity between departments in the management of procurement,
property, and information and communications technology.
Over time, additional functional leaders were added for data
stewardship, health and safety, and service transformation, and
the powers and responsibilities of functional leads were further
modified. The Public Service Act 2020 formalized this model
(as “System Leads”), providing legislative legitimacy. This is
another example of an operational, programmatic change that
was later codified (second-­order change endogenously motivat-
ing third-­order change). It also speaks to the importance of au-
tonomy and experimentation by chief executives, who had the
freedom to try different models and collectively evolved toward
more interoperability.

5.3.4   |   Spirit of Service
The State Sector Act 1988 was influenced by public choice the-
ory and assumed that public servants acted out of rational self-­
maximization (Schick 1996). Nonetheless, a symbolic provision
was retained in the Act that suggested that one of the purposes of
the legislation was to create a state sector “imbued with a spirit
of service to the community” (State Sector Act 1988, s2). From
2014 onwards, NZ public administration has emphasized public
service (intrinsic) motivation, as themes in its conferences and
awards (Scott and Hughes 2023). The Public Service Act 2020
(s13) formalized the responsibility for chief executives to “pre-
serve, protect, and nurture the spirit of service to the community
that public service employees bring to their work.” A spirit of ser-
vice was intended as a central and unifying pillar of public ser-
vice identity (Hipkins 2019b) strengthening the constitutive role
of bureaucracy in the democratic system. For example, a spirit
of service to the community was promoted through an awards
program, and through reflective (“self-­persuasive,” Perry 2020)
activities at forums of senior managers (Scott and Hughes 2025).
5.3.5   |   Stewardship
The reforms of 1988–1989 assumed that ministers had “pur-
chase” and “ownership” interests over departments (Ayto 2011),
but over time ministers have recognized the difficulties they
face in discharging their ownership interests effectively and
acting as good stewards of public institutions (Upton 2001).
Successive reforms have gradually increased the extent to which
chief executives are expected to act as “stewards” (custodians
with a proactive duty of care (Scott and Hughes  2025)) over
the departments they lead and the assets and capabilities they
control. In 2013, an amendment to the State Sector Act 1988
(s32) required chief executives to support ministers to be good
stewards. In the Public Service Act 2020 (s12), stewardship ob-
ligations on chief executives were further strengthened with an
independent duty to promote stewardship. These (third-­order)
symbols in turn influence practice; in one example, the govern-
ment regulatory community of practice (“G-­Reg,” see Scott and
Merton 2022) used the stewardship obligations in the 2013 legis-
lative amendment to justify the proactive review of regulations
that may be out of date (Scott and Merton 2023). Once again, the
agency of managers was a crucial driving force behind subse-
quent reforms.
While these reforms were each intended to improve the per-
formance of the public service, they undoubtedly influenced
both the way reforms could be implemented and the way re-
forms must be implemented. The Public Service Leadership
Team, exemplifying collective leadership and with a mandate
for stewardship, was an endogenous driver of the change pro-
cess and integral in sustaining the reform. While there was in-
dividual turnover within the group (as chief executives finished
their terms and were replaced), these changes happened grad-
ually and with sufficient continuity of membership to sustain
the norms, values, and assumptions of the group and inculcate
new members (Scott and Macaulay 2020). As this group took
on an identity as the stewards of the public service, it arguably
made exogenous reform more difficult; the expectation be-
came that changes would be socialized, amended, agreed, and
implemented by the Public Service Leadership Team as a collec-
tive. This potentially represents a third virtuous feedback loop
as an addition to the bottom-­up paradigmatic change model de-
scribed by Sewerin et al. (2022), discussed further below. For
example, in 2019 the Public Service Commissioner and head of
the public service raised the possibility of centralizing employer
decision rights, but this was not supported by other members
of the Public Service Leadership Team, and the suggestion was
withdrawn (Scott and Hughes 2025).
6   |   Discussion: Contrasting Two Periods of
Reforms
The 1988–1989 and 2012–2020 reform periods exemplify con-
trasting approaches to the reform process. As previously men-
tioned, we highlight three areas of contrast: theoretical purity
versus eclecticism and pluralism, the socialization of reform
concepts and direct influence by managers, and the role of leg-
islation in supporting reform. The 1988–1989 reforms allowed
chief executives to have more freedom and “constitute” or shape
the system via experimentation and collaboration; this was in-
fluential in the nature or pathway of subsequent reforms. We
can represent these different approaches to reform conceptually
in Figure 1.
The 1988–1989 reforms were characterized as theoretically pure
and demonstrating magnificent conceptual architecture, draw-
ing from organizational theory and new institutional economics
(Schick 1996). Works by Treasury officials Scott and Gorringe,
in particular, explicitly acknowledge that the reforms were
a theory-­based and politically led exercise to translate previ-
ously untested ideas from New Institutional Economics to the
public sector (Gorringe  1987; Scott and Gorringe  1989; Scott
et al. 1990, 1997; Scott 2001). In contrast, the Public Service Act
extends some ideas from New Institutional Economics but also
draws from a range of social and psychological theories (Scott
and Macaulay 2020; Scott et al. 2022). Rather than consistently
applying theory across the public service, it contingently draws
from different fields, traditions, and practical experiments to
solve different problems in different contexts (Scott et al. 2022).
While ideas implemented during the 1980s were previously only
theoretical, the Public Service Act 2020 reflected ideas that had
been broadly socialized and tested, including with the chief ex-
ecutives of all 34 public service departments over several years.
Its theoretical eclecticism reflects the many contributors oper-
ating a “bricolage” reform process, in contrast to the few key
contributors to the 1988 and 1989 reforms.
In 1988 (State Sector Act) and 1989 (Public Finance Act), rad-
ical legislative change preceded an implementation phase; in
2020 (Public Service Act), legislation codified changes that had
been underway for several years. This represents a fundamen-
tally different approach to legislation, and to the prevailing
assumptions about the behaviors and motivations of public ser-
vants. The 1988–1989 reforms were influenced by public choice
theory (Boston et  al.  1996), in which assumptions of rational
self-­maximizing behavior were imported from economic fields
and applied to public officials (Buchanan and Tollison  1984).
Hence the 1988 and 1989 legislation were intended to be largely

As noted in the findings, we speculate that there may be a rela-
tionship between the aims of the reform and the method of im-
plementation. The recent reforms were described as containing
elements consistent with the collaborative focus of post-­NPM
and the normative focus of behavioral public administration. It
is perhaps unsurprising that the method of implementation was
also collaborative and normative. Further study is required to
explore whether the converse can also be true—whether collab-
orative and normative reforms can be implemented by sudden
top-­down decree and whether rules-­based reform can be imple-
mented with meso-­level managerial bricolage.
From this country study, we draw several practical implications
that may be of use to civil servants and politicians alike. The
first is that reform does not need to come from politicians, and
it is possible for civil servants to reform themselves. Politicians
can challenge civil servants to take responsibility for changing
the way that public agencies are organized and managed to be-
come more effective. A second key insight is that reform does
not need to start with a “bang.” The sequence does not need to
begin with announcing big changes and then scrambling to im-
plement them. Large-­scale change can happen through small
incremental changes in the same direction over a long period of
time. A third insight is that reforms do not need to be theoreti-
cally pure or follow a single big idea. Theoretically, pure reforms
usually allow for only a few people to take the lead and feel a
sense of responsibility for the change. Anything with many
“parents” is likely to be a messy combination of different ideas
that can achieve an imperfect and dynamic consensus across
this wider group. Broad ownership may allow reform periods
to outlast individual leaders—so long as the entire leadership
group is not replaced at once, new leaders learn, and adopt the
reform program as they seek social membership within the
group. Additionally, hybrid reforms may be more adaptable as
different ideas assume greater salience in response to policy
feedback and changing context.
Finally, The New Zealand case points to the new role of civil
servants as designers of more negotiated reforms. By emphasiz-
ing civil servants' agency in determining the directions of ad-
ministrative change, this article intends to promote awareness
of the crucial and constitutive role of civil servants in our demo-
cratic contexts, as well as the potential impacts of engaging and
contributing to what may feel like constant government reform
processes.
7   |   Conclusion
We contend that this gradual transformation process closely ex-
emplifies recent theorizations of paradigmatic policy change as
“bottom-­up” (via low-­level instrumental policy design changes
toward codification) processes, mostly induced by managers
(Sewerin et  al.  2022). We provide an original contribution to
assessing the practical impact of collaborative, “joined-­up” or
“governance” based trends on administrative reform pathways,
and add to the literature on ideational policy change by showing
how new ideas, in particular by managers, may influence re-
form. We also underscore previous studies (i.e., Scott et al. 2022)
by pointing to the theoretical hybridity of recent results as op-
posed to the theoretical purity of previous reforms, as well as to
the impact of negotiation and experimentation as endogenous
drivers of change (Allain and Madariaga 2020). These findings
contribute to the most recent literature debates on gradual and
endogenously driven pathways of paradigmatic change. It ex-
pands on this literature by looking at NZ public service history
and practically demonstrating the nature and operation of en-
dogenous drivers of change. It points to the importance of es-
tablishing leadership collaborative governance as a factor that
institutionalized negotiation and, over time, acted as an incen-
tive for hybridized paradigmatic changes. This is potentially
a third virtuous feedback loop that informs the dynamics of
bricolage change (see Figure 2). While previous reforms were
closely associated with political leadership, collective public
service leadership and an ethos of stewardship have allowed
gradual reform processes to be sustained despite a change of
government. Despite being more time-­consuming, the current
direction of travel of NZ administrative reforms is potentially
more sustainable and adapted to the production of effective and
socially transformative policies in our increasingly complex so-
ciety (Carstensen et al. 2023). While we can claim that the pe-
riod of continuous active reform from 2012 to 2020 was of longer
duration than the “blitzkrieg” approach (Gregory 2000) of 1988–
1989, it is too early to tell if the resulting public administration
arrangements will prove more sustainable.
In this article, we speculate that these feedback loops make en-
dogenous reform more likely than exogenously driven reform.
The practices of building a reform leadership group create the
expectation (both within the Public Service Leadership Team
and without) of involvement by chief executives in future re-
form. As with any speculation about the future, the proof will
only be available over the decades to come. In the interim, fur-
ther research is required to explore whether NZ's recent reform
processes are an outlier or represent a new trend in adminis-
trative reform, and whether the context and culture of NZ are
uniquely suited or whether such processes are replicable. If en-
dogenously driven reform is desirable, then the conditions and
enablers of success are still to be fully described.

Abstract
We study the impact of policy venue specialization, the deliberate creation of domain-specific, expertise-intensive ­institutions,
on environmental policy compliance. Leveraging a recent institutional reform in China, where municipal-level environmen­
tal courts were introduced to strengthen China’s environmental governance, we use a multi-period difference-in-differences
approach to assess their influence on environmental misconduct among a key group of policy targets: local businesses. Our
findings indicate that these specialized venues significantly deter environmental misconduct, with stronger effects observed
among larger firms and those in less-polluting industries. Mechanism analyses further show that increases in firms’ innova­
tion efforts, rather than enhanced government attention to environmental issues, help explain these observed compliance
improvements, clarifying the causal pathways through which venue specialization enhances policy compliance. By linking in­
stitutional design directly to compliance outcomes, we move beyond the conventional process-oriented focus of policy venue
research and demonstrate how new, specialized institutions affect governance outcomes. Additionally, our study offers new
insights into the distinctive institutional capacities and policy impacts of judicial venues, complementing prior research that
has primarily examined executive, legislative, or hybrid collaborative policy venues.
Key words institutional design, policy venue specialization, DID, environmental misconduct, polycentric environmental governance
Introduction
Research in public policy and administration consistently
emphasizes the importance of the institutional mechanisms
through which policies are formulated and enforced in shaping
policy effectiveness (Siddiki et al. 2022). Among these mecha­
nisms, policy venues, defined here as rule-governed spaces
where policy decisions are deliberated, made, and/or imple­
mented (Baumgartner and Jones 1991; Berardo and Lubell
2016), play a critical role (Pralle 2003).1 Typical policy venues
include, for instance, administrative agencies, legislative bod­
ies, and judicial institutions at various levels of the government
as well as international organizations, advocacy networks, and
collaborative policy forums (Jenkins-Smith et al. 2014; Ley and
Weber 2015). These venues differ in their capacity, logistics and
routines, and receptivity to different policy beliefs and alterna­
tives (Baumgartner and Jones 1991; Ley and Weber 2015). Given

This study focuses on policy venue specialization, defined
as the deliberate introduction of new policy venues structured
around the consolidation of expertise and decision-making au­
thority within specific institutions dedicated to particular pol­

on policy compliance. In doing so, we leverage a recent case of
venue specialization in China, where environmental courts have
been established to address the country’s growing environmen­
tal issues and analyze how this specialization influences the
deterrence of business environmental misconduct. Employing
municipal-firm-level data and a multi-period difference-in-differ­

and placebo tests, we find that the establishment of environmen­
tal courts significantly curbs business environmental misconduct.
Additional heterogeneity analyses reveal that this deterrent effect
is especially pronounced for larger firms and for those operating
in industries with lower pollution levels. Furthermore, our mech­
anism analyses explore two possible pathways underpinning
these outcomes: enhanced governmental environmental policy
attention and increased corporate green innovation efforts. While
evidence for heightened governmental attention as a mechanism
is limited, our analysis supports increased corporate innovation
efforts as a pathway enhancing compliance. Together, these find­
ings underscore that policy venue specialization can meaning­

design targeted at strengthening policy effectiveness.
Our research advances the public policy and administration lit­
erature in two important ways while offering valuable practical in­
sights. First, unlike much prior research focusing primarily on the
processes through which actors engage with and navigate venue
complexity, we establish direct empirical links between venue spe­
cialization and measurable compliance outcomes. By connecting
venue specialization to policy compliance and exploring the un­
derlying mechanisms, we answer recent scholarly calls for more
rigorous, evidence-based policy evaluation and causal inference.
Furthermore, our findings reveal that organizational character­
istics such as firm size and industry type condition policy targets’
responsiveness to specialized venues, thus enriching our under­
standing of when and how venue specialization most effectively
enhances governance outcomes. Second, while existing literature
has extensively explored legislative, executive, and collaborative

Weimer 2011; Makse 2021; Olivier and Berardo 2022), judicial ven­
ues have received relatively limited attention within polycentric
governance systems. Our study addresses this oversight by explor­
ing the distinctive institutional capacities and impacts of judicial
venues, which helps broaden our understanding of different types
of policy venues and their impacts on policy effectiveness.

Pertinent literature and research
gaps
The polycentric governance literature emphasizes that policy
venues are inherently polycentric, with multiple and functionally
overlapping venues operating concurrently to enable coopera­
tion and facilitate policy learning among diverse actors navigat­
ing boundary-spanning collective-action issues (e.g., Lubell and
Robbins 2022; Morrison et al. 2023; Weible et al. 2020). Prior re­
search has focused on both the rationales behind, and impacts
of, venue complexity and multiplicity. Regarding the rationales,
research suggests that the institutional design of many political
systems, characterized by horizontal and/or vertical separation
of policy authority, permits venue complexity and multiplicity
(Leland et al. 2021). Relatedly, research indicates that the exist­
ence of multiple policy venues is driven by either choice or neces­
sity. Here, choice refers to the deliberate balance of power among
multiple venues, while necessity arises from a policymaker’s
limited responsibility, capacity, and/or control over the policy­
making environment (Cairney, Heikkila, and Wood 2019). As for
the impact, prior research has concentrated on their implications
for policy change and the policymaking environment, exploring
key questions such as how shifts and transitions between ven­
ues ­contribute to policy change (see, e.g., Meijerink 2008), how
interdependencies among coexisting venues influence govern­
ance formation and configuration (see, e.g., Arias-Yurisch et  al.
2024), and how interactions among multiple venues impact
agenda-setting and policy instrument priorities (see, e.g., Lemke,
Trein, and Varone 2023).
More recently, scholars have examined the deliberate in­
troduction of specialized policy venues designed to address
complex challenges or accommodate particular actor de­
mands (Lubell and Robbins 2022; Olivier and Berardo 2022).
Such specialization typically entails the consolidation of
­expertise and/or decision-making authority within specific
­institutions (Mullin 2008) and is increasingly salient in contexts
involving socio-technical transitions and multidisciplinary co­
ordination (Breunig and Klüser 2025; Mavrikou, Zahariadis, and
Karokis-Mavrikos 2022). Existing research suggests two mecha­
nisms for the introduction of specialized venues. The first draws
from the institutional change literature, which highlights the es­
tablishment of alternative policy venues in a more permanent
manner, often catalyzed by top-down approaches (e.g., bureau­
cratic restructuring; Carey, Buick, and Malbon 2018; Stacey and
Rittberger 2003; Van der Heijden 2015). This form of specialization
is shaped by both endogenous factors such as knowledge, atten­
tion, and internal coordination, and exogenous factors includ­
ing shifts in social norms, attitudes, and beliefs, with theoretical
roots in historical institutionalism and ideational institutionalism,
respectively (Galik, Ba, and Bobbitt 2023; Greif and Laitin 2004;
Koning 2016). The second mechanism, informed by the policy
entrepreneurship literature, emphasizes venue specialization as

Building on these insights, existing research has likewise ex­
plored the implications of introducing specialized venues, such
as their influence on structural and power dynamics within
polycentric systems (Heikkila, Villamayor-Tomas, and Garrick
2018; Morrison et  al. 2023; Van der Heijden 2015), their im­
pact on policy responsiveness and decision-making processes
(Mullin 2008), and how policy entrepreneurs strategically use
new venues to drive policy changes (Mavrikou, Zahariadis, and
Karokis-Mavrikos 2022). Despite substantial progress, empirical
research linking the introduction of specialized policy venues to
policy implementation and compliance remains limited. Policy
implementation entails translating policy goals and objectives
into actionable measures through instruments such as rules, reg­
ulations, and initiatives (deLeon and deLeon 2002), whereas poli­
cy compliance refers to the extent to which policy targets such as
individuals and organizations conform to these rules, regulations,
and initiatives (Ege et al. 2024). There are strong reasons to hy­
pothesize the impact of the introduction of specialized venues on
policy implementation and compliance. First, specialized venues
are typically established to consolidate expertise, resources, and
decision-making authority around specific policy challenges and/
or actor demands (Breunig and Klüser 2025; Mullin 2008). This
consolidation facilitates clearly articulated policy goals, tailored
instruments, and consistent enforcement, which are conducive to
enhancing policy implementation compliance and effectiveness
(Howlett 2019). Moreover, by leveraging domain-specific knowl­
edge and/or concentrated policy attention, specialized venues
may also enhance the perceived legitimacy and credibility of
regulatory actions among policy targets (Siddiki and Curley 2022;
Tosun and Treib 2018). Second, specialized policy venues often
attract actors with shared interests and/or aligned beliefs relevant
to the focal policy domain (Olivier and Berardo 2022), which could
reinforce and institutionalize particular policy orientations and
normative preferences, leading to clearer policy signals and more
consistent compliance expectations for policy targets (Breunig
and Klüser 2025). Consequently, this enhanced clarity improves
policy targets’ understanding of compliance requirements and
increases their perceived likelihood of enforcement, which is par­
ticularly important when enforcement agencies hold substantial
discretionary powers (Evans 2020; Young and Tanner 2023).
As for policy venues, existing research has predominantly fo­
cused on legislative and executive agencies as well as hybrid
collaborative arrangements such as policy forums, where diverse
stakeholders, both governmental and non-governmental, inter­
act to address shared policy interests (Ba, Berrett, and Coupet
2025; Mewhirter, McLaughlin, and Calfano 2024). Despite valu­
able contributions, judicial venues remain understudied in the
polycentric governance literature. Judicial venues, broadly refer­
ring to institutions within a political system’s judiciary, such as
courts or specialized tribunals, differ significantly from legislative
and executive venues with distinct institutional characteristics
and capacities. Specifically, judicial venues primarily rely on legal
interpretation, established precedents, and statutory frameworks
(Beckett and Koenig 2005), and they typically operate reactively
in response to issues raised by litigants, thus limiting their ability
to shape policy agendas proactively (Bali and Halpin 2021; Lane
and Black 2017). In contrast, legislative and executive venues em­
phasize policy deliberation, stakeholder negotiation, and policy
compromise, facilitating proactive agenda-setting and swift re­
sponsiveness to emerging policy challenges (Birkland 2007; West
and Raso 2013). Along this line, participation in judicial venues
tends to be confined to directly involved stakeholders and legal
professionals, whereas legislative and executive venues generally
encourage broader public participation and mobilization (Fung
2015; Hoppe 2011). Moreover, the legitimacy of judicial venues
derives largely from their perceived impartiality, procedural fair­
ness, and adherence to constitutional principles, whereas exec­
utive and legislative venues derive legitimacy primarily through
electoral accountability and responsiveness to public preferences
(Rosenbloom, Kravchuk, and Clerkin 2022).
Compared to hybrid collaborative arrangements such as poli­
cy forums, judicial venues differ primarily in their formal author­
ity and more clearly defined linkages to policy outcomes. Policy
forums, which are structured around combinations of informal
norms and formal rules, often vary widely, encompassing work­
shops, conferences, meetings, steering committees, and working
groups (Fried, Hamilton, and Berardo 2023). They typically feature
voluntary and fluid participation, resulting in relatively ambigu­
ous lines of authority (Ansell and Gash 2018). This ambiguity aris­
es from their collaborative nature, which necessitates balancing
­influence between state and non-state actors as well as between
incumbent stakeholders and emerging interests (Purdy 2012; Scott
and Thomas 2017). Furthermore, policy forums generally ­maintain
less direct ties to specific policy results, as they are primarily
process-oriented, serving advisory and/or consultative func­
tions, or emphasizing stakeholder exchange and mutual learning
(Olivier and Berardo 2022). Additionally, they are also influenced
by interactions with other forums operating within the same poli­
cy domain (McLaughlin 2023). In contrast, judicial venues possess
the authority to issue binding decisions and interpretations, which
can influence policy implementation and compliance through en­
forceable rulings and established precedents (Kapiszewski and
Taylor 2013). Additionally, judicial venues serve as vital accounta­
bility mechanisms by ensuring government actions align with con­
stitutional and statutory obligations, thus reinforcing checks and
balances within polycentric governance systems (Barkow 2016).
The stability and permanence provided by judicial precedents
further offer policy actors clear, predictable signals (Lawrance
2021), distinguishing judicial venues from the relatively more tran­
sient and flexible processes typical of policy forums. Given these
­distinctive attributes, judicial venues represent a critical yet un­
derexplored form of policy venues in the polycentric governance
scholarship, meriting further scholarly exploration to understand
their role in shaping governance outcomes.
In brief, the literature points to three gaps. First, outcomes
of venue specialization, especially implementation and com­
pliance, remain underexamined despite growing demands for
evidence-based policymaking and feedback-driven policy im­
provement (Christensen 2021; Mettler and Sorelle 2018). Second,
judicial venues remain underrepresented within polycentric
governance research. Third, much of the existing work relies on
case studies and correlational designs, underscoring the val­
ue of stronger causal identification (Capano 2011; Newig et  al.
2018). In response, this study assesses the impact of introducing

Theoretical guidance and
hypotheses
Policy venue specialization refers to the deliberate introduction of
new policy venues that consolidate expertise and decision-making
authority within institutions dedicated to a particular policy do­
main (Mullin 2008).2 In environmental governance, for example,
specialization may involve creating dedicated agencies, commit­
tees, or judicial bodies focused on environmental challenges.
Beyond developing issue expertise and enhancing the legitima­
cy, effectiveness, and efficiency of policymaking (Turnhout et al.
2015), the literature identifies three additional non-mutually
exclusive motivations that help further explain this approach
(Hamm, Hedlund, and Post 2011). First, from an informational
perspective, venue specialization enhances the utilization of in­
formation in policymaking, which helps stakeholders navigate
uncertainty (Workman, Jones, and Jochim 2009). Second, the
distributive perspective argues that venue specialization aims to
accommodate the diverse policy preferences and beliefs of stake­
holders, ensuring a targeted allocation of benefits among qual­
ified participants within specific venues (Tosun and Treib 2018).
Third, and relatedly, the partisan perspective argues venue spe­
cialization functions as an agent to fulfill the policy objectives of
partisanized members, such as securing the median policy posi­
tion of their political party (Lewallen, Park, and Theriault 2024).
We use these perspectives to motivate competing expectations
about whether specialization improves compliance (Hypotheses
1a–1b). Our mechanism hypotheses (H2–H3), however, are de­
veloped primarily from the informational perspective, as it yields
clearer, observable implications for policy signals, attention, and
policy targets’ adaptive responses in our empirical setting.
From an informational perspective, specialized policy venues
are often better equipped to address domain-specific problems
because they concentrate expertise and resources. Such expertise
can enhance perceived legitimacy by signaling competence and
credibility (Turnhout et  al. 2015), and perceived legitimacy has
been shown to facilitate compliance intentions and willing coop­
eration (Tyler and Jackson 2014). For instance, empirical studies
in areas such as climate policy and public health suggest that citi­
zens are more likely to comply with regulations that are informed
by expert knowledge (e.g., Angelou et al. 2024; Muradova, Walker,
and Colli 2020). Moreover, in contexts where trust in government
is declining (Toshkov, Carroll, and Yesilkagit 2022), technocratic or
expertise-driven governance may be especially valued and may
help mitigate distrust in ways that support compliance (Bertsou
and Caramani 2022; Stauffer, Miller, and Keiser 2023). Along this
line, specialized policy venues are also more likely to have target­
ed resources (e.g., dedicated budgets and sustained attention;
Bruff 1991), which can translate into tailored policy tools that en­
hance the effectiveness of policy implementation (Wu, Ramesh,

In addition to estimating the overall impact of policy venue spe­
cialization on environmental policy compliance, our study likewise
examines the mechanisms through which this impact materializ­
es. Consistent with an informational perspective, we focus on two
pathways: (1) shifts in government policy attention and enforce­
ment salience, and (2) policy targets’ innovation responses under
clearer regulatory expectations. Together, these mechanisms
capture both the perspective of policymakers and the strategic re­
sponses of policy targets. Drawing on research on environmental
regulation and enforcement (Winter and May 2001; Yee, Tang, and
Lo 2016), we argue that specialization influences compliance by in­
creasing policy targets’ perceived costs of (non)-compliance. These
perceived costs are primarily shaped by two factors: the likelihood
that violations will be detected and sanctioned and the severity
of consequences when violations occur (Heyes 2000; Li and Ba
2025; May 2005). In this context, heightened policy attention can
increase (or credibly signal increases in) enforcement probability
and/or sanction severity, while innovation captures policy targets’

efforts to manage compliance costs through operational and tech­
nological adjustment. Importantly, we acknowledge that these
two mechanisms are likely interrelated: greater policy attention
may precede and even drive policy targets’ innovation responses
(e.g., Gao, Zhang, and Jiang 2025). However, given the scope of our
study, we examine attention and innovation separately for analyt­
ical clarity, while noting that future research could explore their
interactions and sequencing more fully.
Policy attention, typically conceptualized from a resource-based
perspective, refers to the weight an issue holds in policy
decision-making (Jones and Baumgartner 2005). Recognizing
policymakers’ limited information processing capacities and the
constant influx of information (Workman, Jones, and Jochim
2009), policy attention is necessarily selective and unevenly dis­
tributed across issue areas (Jones and Baumgartner 2005; Kang
and Silveira 2021). A growing body of research underscores the
influence of policy attention on environmental policy implemen­
tation and compliance, showing that increased attention posi­
tively impacts practices such as carbon emissions reduction (Liu
et al. 2023) and climate adaptation (Biesbroek et al. 2022). One
possible explanation is that, to better navigate uncertainties, es­
pecially in societies characterized by low transparency in policy
decision-making (Fowler 2021; Yee, Tang, and Lo 2016), policy
targets often interpret increased policy attention as a credible sig­
nal of potential regulatory interventions or shifts in enforcement
priorities (Dutt and Joseph 2019; Engau and Hoffmann 2011).
Heightened policy attention thus incentivizes policy targets to
adjust their environmental behavior due to increased compliance
pressures and/or the prospect of accessing government resources
(Ba, Berrett, and Coupet 2025).
As discussed earlier, policy venue specialization concentrates
expertise and decision-making authority within institutions
­dedicated to a focal domain (Mullin 2008). This consolidation can
institutionalize sustained policy attention to the focal issue (e.g.,
environmental protection), which in turn may strengthen com­
pliance incentives. Research on issue salience and “unbundling”
suggests that general-purpose venues often prioritize issues
based on immediate visibility and competition among agenda
items (Landa and Le Bihan 2018; Mullin 2008). By unbundling, we
mean separating a focal issue (e.g., environmental regulation)
from the broader agenda of general-purpose venues so it faces
less competition for time, expertise, and organizational attention,
which sustains clearer and more stable policy signals even when
public salience fluctuates. Specialized venues represent one in­
stitutional form of unbundling and can maintain more consistent
attention and credible enforcement signals, stabilizing expecta­
tions among policy targets and encouraging compliance-oriented
behavior. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:
Hypothesis 2: Policy venue specialization affects environ­
mental policy compliance through increased government
policy attention.
In addition to the heightened attention following policy venue
specialization, policy targets’ innovation efforts represent anoth­
er key mechanism linking specialization to compliance. Drawing
on the policy innovation literature, innovation by policy targets
can be defined as the development and implementation of new
or more advanced practices, technologies, or operational mod­
els that can help policy targets more effectively meet policy and
societal demands (Berry and Berry 2014; Blind 2012; Yi, Berry,
and Chen 2018). Such innovation can be considered as a form
of beyond-compliance effort, characterized by policy targets’
intentional behavioral adjustments to exceed the requirements
of existing policies. Here, beyond compliance differs from over­
compliance, where policy targets deliver more than what policies
mandate, often due to technological or operational indivisibil­
ities. For example, firms adopting uniform technologies across
facilities that face varying regulations may inadvertently result
in overcompliance at certain locations, not as a result of inten­
tional behavioral adjustments (Prakash 2001). In the context of
environmental policy, beyond-compliance efforts can be motivat­
ed intrinsically, such as by the policy targets’ own sense of envi­
ronmental and societal responsibility, or extrinsically, such as in
pursuit of market entry, economic benefits, political favoritism, or
regulatory advantages (Du 2015).
As aforementioned, policy venue specialization implies
­increased compliance pressure, and compliance, whether it in­
volves providing policy-mandated environmental public goods
or internalizing environmental externalities based on regula­
tions, incurs costs (Winter and May 2001). To offset the potentially
heightened compliance costs induced by policy venue specializa­
tion, it is reasonable to expect that policy targets may engage in
innovation efforts that enhance their environmental performance
(Cohen and Tubb 2018; Lim and Prakash 2014; Wagner and Fain
2018). Additionally, specialized venues typically provide clearer
and more targeted regulatory adjustments that help reduce regu­
latory uncertainty. Such clarity enables policy targets to align their
innovation strategies with specific environmental goals, making
it easier to plan and invest in advanced environmental technolo­
gies or sustainable practices (Clausen et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2023).
Finally, policy targets operating under specialized oversight may
also pursue innovation to strengthen their ­competitive posi­
tion (Iraldo et al. 2011), which further reinforces innovation as a
­strategic response to venue specialization and a key mechanism
linking specialization to policy compliance. In this case, we hy­
pothesize that:
Hypothesis 3: Policy venue specialization affects environ­
mental policy compliance through policy targets’ increased
innovation efforts.
Empirical context and research
design
Empirical context
We focus on the establishment of environmental courts in China to
contextualize our empirical analysis. As the world’s largest emitter
and a leading manufacturing hub, China provides an important
context for studying environmental governance. Environmental
governance in China is structured as a government-led polycen­
tric system, in which authority and responsibilities are distribut­
ed vertically from central to local governments and horizontally
across multiple departmental actors (Galik, Ba, and Bobbitt 2023).
While China’s authoritarian governance regime enables strong
central influence, this influence primarily takes the form of setting
overarching goals (e.g., the five-year plans; Xu 2020). In practice,
subnational environmental governance often operates in a more

decentralized way, involving multiple, overlapping policy venues
(Lo 2015). Consistent with our bounded use of “self-organization,”
centrally defined objectives are translated into locally tailored
instruments and enforcement practices, generating cross-local
variation. Local governments also learn from and emulate one
another’s environmental initiatives, producing horizontal learn­
ing and peer effects alongside vertical oversight (Yi et al. 2018).
Prior to the introduction of specialized environmental courts,
cases of environmental misconduct were addressed through
fragmented channels, including local administrative bodies (e.g.,
Environmental Protection Bureaus (EPBs)), general civil or ad­
ministrative courts, or informal negotiation and mediation. Under
this structure, local EPBs, while responsible for enforcement, of­
ten lacked sufficient coercive authority, and general courts faced
challenges in technical expertise and prioritization of environ­
mental cases (Wang and Xia 2023).
Recognizing these limitations, China introduced specialized
environmental courts in 2007 as a top-down institutional reform
initiated by the Supreme People’s Court. These courts were intro­
duced as new divisions within the existing court system, adding
judicial specialization without broader institutional restructuring.
By December 2021, a total of 2,149 environmental courts had been
established across the country. These courts handle three primary
types of cases: environmental pollution disputes, resource own­
ership and development disputes, and natural resource protec­
tion and utilization cases (Qi et al. 2023). Cases reach these courts
via several channels: (1) civil lawsuits filed by private individuals
or communities; (2) public interest litigation brought by eligible
non-governmental organizations (NGOs) or by government pros­
ecutors (the People’s Procuratorates); and (3) cases arising from
government investigations and administrative enforcement ac­
tions (Wang and Xia 2023). Although the courts were introduced via
top-down judicial reform, their operation is activated through the
actions of these multiple actors and the linkages among various
venues, creating inter-venue adaptation and mutual adjustment
in implementation and compliance. By concentrating environ­
mental adjudication and standardizing judicial practices, environ­
mental courts increase the legal and financial costs of misconduct
for polluting entities and enhance the consistency and procedural
clarity of environmental trials in China (Zhang, Yu, and Kong 2019).
From an institutional perspective, specialized environmental
courts represent the addition of a new type of judicial venues
within an already complex governance process. These courts
possess both trial and enforcement authority, significantly ad­
dressing the enforcement limitations of traditional environmen­
tal agencies. For instance, they can take coercive actions such as
confiscating property, freezing assets, and enforcing corrective
measures. Another key feature is their cross-regional jurisdiction,
which enables them to operate relatively independently of local
administrative pressures. This autonomy reduces the risk of collu­
sion between local governments and polluting entities that might
otherwise prioritize economic growth over environmental protec­
tion. By holding governmental bodies and agencies accountable
for negligence or misconduct through administrative lawsuits and
judicial directives, as well as by raising non-compliance costs for
regulated entities (Winter and May 2001; Yee, Tang, and Lo 2016),
the environmental courts have enhanced China’s environmen­
tal governance effectiveness (Wang 2010; Zhang, Yu, and Kong
2019). The establishment of these courts thus serves as a valu­
able ­example of venue specialization in environmental policy,
providing useful context for our empirical analysis. Importantly,
“specialization” here refers to courts dedicated to the broad range
of environmental cases, rather than issue-specific, compartmen­
talized venues focusing exclusively on areas such as water or
­energy. This comprehensive approach consolidates technical
expertise and judicial efficiency across diverse environmental is­
sues, whereas further specialization into narrower domains may
introduce trade-offs by fragmenting expertise, complicating coor­
dination, and reducing resource efficiency.
Research design
Data sources
We constructed our dataset from Chinese listed firms on the
A-share market between 2007 and 2022, leveraging three primary
sources. The China Stock Market & Accounting Research (CSMAR)
database provides essential firm-level information and corporate
financial data (see https://global.csmar.com), while data on corpo­
rate environmental misconduct were obtained from the CCERTM
database maintained by Sinofin Financial Information and the
China Center for Economic Research at Peking University (see
https://www.ccerdata.cn). We also manually collected information
on the ­establishment of environmental courts by consulting the
websites of intermediate people’s courts in each prefecture-level
city and reviewing related news reports. After merging these da­
tasets, we applied winsorization at the 1 percent and 99 percent
levels to ­continuous variables and used a gross domestic product
deflator to adjust monetary variables. Additionally, we excluded
listed firms designated as ST (“special treatment”) or *ST, which
indicate serious operational or financial abnormalities that can ob­
scure firms’ underlying prospects. Because companies with these
designations are also more likely to face delisting under China
Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) rules, retaining them
could confound our analyses. We also excluded financial firms due
to the distinct nature of their production functions compared to
non-financial firms. The final dataset consists of 3,792 firms.
Here, our choice of cases of corporate environmental
­misconduct to operationalize policy compliance is driven by
two considerations. First, these cases provide a conservative yet
meaningful indicator of corporate environmental policy compli­
ance. Specifically, these cases, drawn from the CCERTM database
that compiles regulatory enforcement records from multiple gov­
ernment agencies in China, capture verified instances where firms
have violated environmental laws and regulations. Examples in­
clude exceeding pollutant discharge limits, failing to cooperate
with inspections, neglecting to operate required pollution control
equipment, discharging hazardous substances without authori­
zation, and failing to fulfill other legal obligations. As such, they
serve as a lower-bound estimate of compliance, highlighting clear
failures to meet baseline legal or regulatory standards. In China,
where regulatory and market incentives for proactive compliance
remain limited, and comprehensive data on voluntary compliance
are largely unavailable (Liu et al. 2015), this approach is especially
practical. That said, we acknowledge the potential concern that
the creation of specialized environmental courts could lead to
more legal filings without reflecting actual behavioral change.
However, our dependent variable captures verified violations not
just filings, so changes in this measure are likely to reflect genuine
shifts in corporate compliance behavior, rather than fluctuations

Results
Baseline regression
Using equation (1), we estimate the impact of environmental
court establishment on corporate environmental misconduct,
with the results presented in Table 2. In Column (1), the coeffi­
cient for Court is −0.0944, which is significantly negative at the 5
percent level. In Column (2), after introducing control variables,
the coefficient remains negative at −0.0852. This indicates that
the establishment of environmental courts leads to an average
reduction of 0.0852 in the number of environmental misconduct
cases among firms in the treatment group compared to those in
the control group, after accounting for differences in pre-policy
trends. These findings provide strong evidence for our hypothesis
1a that policy venue specialization has a positive impact on en­
vironmental policy compliance. Specifically, the establishment of
environmental courts in China plays an effective role in deterring
corporate environmental misconduct.
Robustness tests
To assess robustness, we conduct several additional checks
(reported in Tables A1 and A2 and Figures A1 and A2 in
the Supplementary Appendix). First, an event-study spec­
ification shows that the lead coefficients one to two years
­prior to court establishment are statistically indistinguish­
able from zero, ­supporting the parallel trends assumption,
while post-treatment estimates are negative. Second, we re-
estimate the baseline model using a propensity score–matched
sample (nearest-neighbor matching with a 0.001 caliper); the
estimated court effect remains negative and comparable in
magnitude, and balance tests indicate no systematic differences
between matched treatment and control groups. Third, a per­
mutation placebo test with 500 random treatment assignments
yields coefficients centered around zero and distinct from the
baseline estimate. Finally, because misconduct is a zero-inflated
count outcome, we estimate a zero-inflated negative binomial
model as an alternative specification and again obtain a nega­
tive effect of court establishment. Together, these checks help
reinforce the robustness of our main results.
Heterogeneity analyses
Firm scale
Large-scale enterprises often benefit from more abundant re­
sources, resource slack, and economies of scale (Philipp and
Raithel 2018). Beyond our main results, we therefore examine
whether the establishment of environmental courts affects cor­
porate environmental misconduct differently depending on firm
size. We categorize firms as large or small based on the median
corporate total assets within each industry and year, and then
re-estimate equation (1) for both groups. As shown in Columns
(1) and (2) of Table 3, the coefficient for Court is –0.1534 for large
firms, significantly negative at the 10 percent level, while the co­
efficient for small firms is not significant. These findings suggest
that environmental courts primarily deter environmental miscon­
duct among larger enterprises. This might be because larger en­
terprises receive greater public and governmental scrutiny than
their smaller counterparts (Marquis and Bird 2018). Additionally,
in China, large enterprises are often state-owned and/or main­
tain closer ties to government entities (Koppell 2007). As a result,
these firms may be more responsive to policy changes and the ef­
fects of venue specialization.

Discussions and conclusion
Research in public policy and administration has long empha­
sized the role of institutional mechanisms, particularly policy
venues, in shaping policy effectiveness. As policy problems be­
come increasingly complex and knowledge intensive, venue spe­
cialization, which consolidates expertise and authority within
domain-specific institutions, has become as a promising design
strategy. Yet much of the existing literature has focused on the
policy process (e.g., structural and power dynamics in polycentric
systems, responsiveness and decision-making, and entrepreneur­
ial venue creation) rather than directly testing how specialization
affects compliance and outcomes (Heikkila, Villamayor-Tomas,
and Garrick 2018; Mavrikou, Zahariadis, and Karokis-Mavrikos
2022; Morrison et al. 2023; Mullin 2008). Our study contributes to
filling this gap by empirically assessing the impact of venue spe­
cialization on environmental compliance, focusing specifically on
China’s introduction of environmental courts designed to enhance
environmental governance. Using municipal-firm-level data and a
multi-period DID approach, we find that the establishment of en­
vironmental courts significantly reduces corporate environmen­
tal misconduct. Moreover, this deterrent effect is stronger among
larger firms and those operating in lower-pollution industries.
Furthermore, our analysis explores two potential mechanisms
underlying these outcomes: enhanced governmental policy at­
tention and increased firm innovation efforts. While our results
offer limited support for the role of governmental attention in im­
proving compliance, they robustly indicate that enhanced green
innovation efforts by firms constitute a key mechanism driving
increased policy compliance. Together, our findings demonstrate
that policy venue specialization can effectively enhance policy
compliance, offering valuable implications for institutional de­
sign in public policy and administration research.
Our research makes two main theoretical contributions with
practical implications. First, by linking venue specialization
with measurable compliance outcomes, we extend the primar­
ily process-focused policy venue literature to address critical
questions of policy effectiveness. This advancement responds
to recent calls for greater attention to policy effectiveness and
outcomes (Capano and Howlett 2021; Newig et al. 2018) and to
a broader emphasis on policy evaluations grounded in causal in­
ference (Dague and Lahey 2019; Ferraro, Sanchirico, and Smith
2019). Moreover, our analysis of governmental attention and
firm innovation as mechanisms deepens theoretical understand­
ing of how specialized venues translate into improved policy
outcomes in the context of our study, offering greater clarity on
causal pathways that future research can further test and refine
(Keele, Tingley, and Yamamoto 2015). Second, while existing lit­
erature has made significant progress in understanding legisla­
tive, executive, and hybrid collaborative policy venues (including
policy forums), judicial venues remain underexplored. Judicial
venues differ significantly from legislative and executive venues
due to their reliance on legal interpretation, precedent-based
decision-making, and more reactive operational nature in shap­
ing policy agendas (Bali and Halpin 2021; Lane and Black 2017).
In contrast to hybrid collaborative arrangements such as policy
forums, judicial venues also stand out due to their formal author­
ity structures and clearly delineated linkages to policy outcomes
(Kapiszewski and Taylor 2013). By examining judicial venues
within a polycentric governance framework, our study provides
novel insights into their distinctive capabilities and impacts, thus
enriching the broader scholarly discussions on effective institu­
tional design and governance outcomes.
Beyond these contributions, our findings suggest several prac­
tical implications. For policymakers, our findings suggest that es­
tablishing expertise-driven, domain-specific venues significantly
enhances policy compliance and deters undesirable behaviors.
Such strategic institutional design can mitigate local political
pressures that might otherwise compromise environmental pro­
tection goals. Furthermore, our heterogeneity analyses highlight
that venue specialization’s effectiveness varies by firm size and
industry, underscoring the value of tailoring enforcement strate­
gies to organizational and sectoral conditions. Importantly, estab­
lishing expertise-driven institutions is not unique to the Chinese
context but is part of a broader trend reflecting responses to in­
creasingly knowledge-intensive and complex policy challenges
(Christensen 2021). Although recent political dynamics in some
contexts have weakened the authority of such expert institu­
tions; for example, the Trump administration’s limitations on the
power of professional experts within the US federal bureaucra­
cy; our analysis nonetheless demonstrates the efficacy of creat­
ing specialized, expertise-driven institutions. For policy targets,
the growing complexity and specificity of policy challenges may
lead to more frequent specialization in regulatory institutions. To
maintain competitiveness and manage potentially heightened
compliance costs, businesses must learn to navigate effectively
these niche regulatory environments. Finally, for policy advo­
cates and environmental groups, specialized venues may provide
a viable alternative when conventional institutional channels are
less effective, particularly in emerging areas where governance
arrangements remain underdeveloped.
Finally, several limitations point to directions for future research.
First, policy issues typically unfold within polycentric systems with
multiple overlapping venues. Although our design isolates the ef­
fect of environmental courts on firm compliance, we do not model
how courts interact with other venues (e.g., regulatory agencies or
collaborative arrangements). Future work could draw on institu­
tional navigation or an Ecology of Games perspective to examine
how venue interactions jointly shape implementation and compli­
ance (e.g., Lubell and Morrison 2021). Second, our study focuses
on environmental governance; comparative work across policy
domains would strengthen generalizability and refine theory on
when specialization is most effective. Third, our mechanism anal­
yses are exploratory and primarily informed by an informational
perspective, focusing on expertise- and uncertainty-related path­
ways. Because distributive and partisan motivations may also
shape venue specialization, future research could assess these
alternatives more directly; for example, through analyses of case
selection patterns, litigant composition, or shifts in enforcement
pressure across constituencies. Future work could also examine
how attention and innovation interact and sequence, and whether
additional channels (e.g., coercive authority or institutional inde­

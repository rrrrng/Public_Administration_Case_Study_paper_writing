Abstract
This article investigates how implementation collaborations between law enforcement and mental health organizations generate resilience to
sustain and adapt themselves in volatile environments. Data come from interviews with practitioners and reviews of media and planning, and
program documents in four distinct local settings. The study extends understanding of resilience in three ways. First, it examines practices to
build interorganizational resilience from different strands of literature—boundary practices that help diverse partners understand and work together
across differences, and high-reliability practices to sustain joint operations. Second, it identifies four challenges that volatility poses for implemen­
tation collaborations and shows that collaborations in different settings can combine the practices in distinct ways to address each challenge.
Local conditions, thus, may matter less for building resilience than how collaborations combine practices. Third, it uncovers a new reflexive
practice—collaborative assessment and adjustment—likely to be particularly useful to build resilience in volatile environments. Resilience amid
volatility, thus, may be less a state that collaboration achieve than an ongoing process that partners cultivate in different ways in response to
specific challenges. The article concludes with ideas for future research, including studying challenges of volatility and the practices that cultivate
interorganizational resilience in additional policy fields.
Key words: collaboration; resilience; police; mental health; volatility.
Much policy implementation today occurs through collabora­
tions between multiple organizations, often from different sectors
or policy fields. Implementation collaborations usually tackle
complex public problems that transcend the capabilities of a
single organization (Bryson, Crosby, and Stone 2006; Emerson
and Nabatchi 2015), such as homelessness, emergency response,
community development, or serving families with multiple needs.
Complex problems pose numerous implementation challenges
that can become supercharged when the policy environment
surrounding a collaboration becomes volatile, unstable, and
unpredictable. This article investigates how implementation col­
laborations sustain and adapt themselves amid volatility.
Policy implementation is complicated under ordinary cir­
cumstances (Sandfort and Moulton 2015), but recent increases
in the volatility of some policy fields have exacerbated the dif­
ficulties. Developments such as increases in climate-related
emergencies, disruptive political tactics, and misinformation
and disinformation on social media have increased instability
or unpredictability in implementation settings (Calo et al.
2021). Volatility can render policy guidance, service needs, or
stakeholder pressures more demanding, complex, or conflicted,
disrupting interorganizational relationships, resource flows,
communication channels, and other implementation operations
(Berthod and Sydow 2021).
To manage implementation amid volatility, collaborations
can cultivate resilience—the interorganizational capacity to
sustain or reconfigure operations in the face of severe disrup­
tions (Getha-Taylor 2019; Quick and Feldman 2014). While
resilience offers benefits amid volatility, research investigating
how to cultivate it is scarce, particularly in interorganizational
settings. This article explores how boundary practices to build
collaborative resilience can combine with high-reliability prac­
tices supporting operational resilience. It compares the uses of
the boundary and reliability practices by collaborations
between law enforcement and mental health organizations in
four communities that differ in multiple ways. Results indicate
that implementation collaborations in diverse local contexts
can combine boundary and reliability practices in distinctive
ways in response to specific challenges of volatility. Setting
characteristics, thus, may matter less for building collaborative
resilience than do the specific challenges a collaboration faces
and how it combines the practices. The data also reveal a reflex­
ive practice of collaborative assessment and adjustment in all
four sites, likely to be especially useful amid volatility.
Collaboration, volatility, and resilience in
policy implementation
Implementation collaborations “execute tasks and achieve
operational goals that often are determined externally” by leg­
islatures or other policy authorizers (Emerson and Nabatchi
2021: 402). Because they bring together organizations with
different missions and cultures, implementation collaborations
often encounter turbulence, manifesting in complex service
needs, disagreements or misunderstandings among collabora­
tive partners, or vague policy mandates (Bryson, Crosby, and

At times, policy, political, or community conditions may change
so suddenly and intensely that scholars classify the context as
“volatile”—qualitatively more unstable and unpredictable than
the turbulence collaborations typically face (Schoemaker, Heaton,
and Teece 2018). Problem severity and public needs may change
dramatically, as with the influx into communities of potent, inex­
pensive opiates such as fentanyl that exacerbated problems of
substance use, mental health, and homelessness (Frantz et al.
2023). Political debates, policy directions, and resource commit­
ments may shift fundamentally in response to such crises (Boin,
Hart, and McConnell 2009; Nohrstedt et al. 2018).
The unpredictability, disruptions, and contention character­
izing volatility create acute challenges for implementation col­
laborations. Interorganizational procedures, shared mental
models, political support, and coordination mechanisms can
lag or fail, leaving partners uncertain how to respond to emer­
gent situations. “In these settings, timely responses, shorter
planning cycles, dynamic resource allocation, and quicker
information processing are essential” (Renteria 2025: 8).
Alongside flux in service needs, policies, or resources, volatile
environments often feature skepticism about the capabilities,
desirability, and legitimacy of public institutions (Breunig and
Koski 2018; Lemmie, Quick, and Williams 2021). Skepticism
may derive from new frames or images introduced by social
movements or advocacy coalitions, or from institutional strug­
gles to perform effectively due to increasingly severe social
needs (Boin, Hart, and McConnell 2009). In the field of public
safety, for example, police brutality and the Black Lives Matter
protests arising in response diminished public perceptions of
safety, police trustworthiness, and police performance (Wright
et al. 2023). Under such circumstances, stakeholder pressures
or policy guidance may become more demanding or conflicted,
while working relationships between implementing organiza­
tions deteriorate or become more contentious (Crain 2003).
As challenges in a volatile policy field reinforce and com­
pound one another, collaborative operations and systems may
break down or become overwhelmed. When policy directions,
resource flows, institutional operations, and normative assump­
tions in a field come into question, “one cannot know in
advance what resources one will need, or even know in advance
what might be a resource” (Beunza and Stark 2003: 135).
Collaboration in volatile fields therefore can benefit from
resilience—a system’s capacity to sustain or reconfigure itself in
the face of disruptions and turmoil “perceived…to fall outside
the range of normal and expected disturbances” (Boin, Comfort,
and Demchak 2010: 9). Resilient collaborations maintain oper­
ations amid disruptions through redundant connections among
diverse actors (Berthod et al. 2017), reassembling and adapting
resources, relationships, and activities to reconstitute themselves
when breakdowns occur (Berthod and Sydow 2021).
Research finds that collaborations remain operationally resil­
ient using five high reliability practices: connecting diverse
actors, dynamic sensemaking, creative problem solving, and
operating parallel capabilities (Berthod and Sydow 2021;
Weick and Sutcliffe 2007). These reliability practices constitute
interactive routines that help maintain implementation during
disruptions and rebuild implementation capacity when disrup­
tions diminish it. Research also identifies three boundary prac­
tices to nurture relationships and collaborative resilience
among partners working across organizations and communities
(Quick and Feldman 2014): translating across differences,
aligning among differences, and decentering differences.
The reliability practices have applications in interorganiza­
tional settings (Berthod and Sydow 2021; Berthod et al. 2017),
but originate in studies of single organizations (Weick and Sut­
cliffe 2007). The boundary practices, meanwhile, come from
multiple case studies of collaboration in a single community
(Quick and Feldman 2014). This study extends the research
on resilience in multiple ways: it investigates uses of the bound­
ary practices for collaborative resilience in different communi­
ties and how collaborations combine them with the reliability
practices for operational resilience amid volatility. It addresses
two specific research questions:
1.	 Can interorganizational partners use the three boundary
practices and four reliability practices to cultivate resil­
ience in volatile implementation settings?
2.	 If so, how do they combine the various practices in
response to different challenges of volatility?
Research design and methods
Study context
In recent decades, professionals in public safety and behavioral
health have struggled to address the increase in street-level
challenges faced by people living with homelessness, mental
illness, or substance use disorders. Maintaining public safety
and serving this growing population amid escalating housing
costs and limited funding for public health and related services
has left local law enforcement and emergency responders work­
ing at or beyond the limits of their training and expertise.
In response, local governments have launched collaborations
between law enforcement and mental health organizations to
reduce violence and better serve people in crisis on the streets
or in the criminal justice system. Police departments from Los
Angeles to Miami and elsewhere have partnered with mental
health providers and, in some cases, emergency response
departments to create “co-response” programs. Teams of police
officers, licensed clinicians, and sometimes emergency medical
technicians (EMTs) join forces to respond to people in crisis.
In some cases, these teams respond to 911 calls after being
dispatched by call center staff trained to identify calls with
mental or behavioral health components—expanding the range
of collaborating organizations to include emergency dispatch.
Some county sheriff’s offices, meanwhile, have built partner­
ships with mental health organizations to help people in or
being released from jail gain access to behavioral health services
(Watson, Compton, and Pope 2019).
While these collaborations have considerable promise, law
enforcement and mental health organizations differ dramati­
cally in their core missions, cultures, and frontline tasks, and
hence in how line staff integrate and apply knowledge. The
capacity to combine different types of specialized expertise to
address changing situations as they unfold is essential to serve
people in crisis (Watson, Compton, and Pope 2019). Collabo­
ration between law enforcement and mental health profession­
als, thus, requires integrating knowledge across boundaries
between the two fields and navigating their distinct routines
and approaches to frontline work (Tell 2017).
Despite these inherent difficulties, collaborations between
law enforcement and mental health organizations have

as public safety and law enforcement have become increasingly

to police violence against Black people and other marginalized
communities, popularizing calls to defund or radically recon­

deepened the historically tense, fraught relations between com­

following the murder of George Floyd by Minneapolis police
in 2020 amid spikes in crime and the public health, social, and
economic disruptions accompanying the COVID-19 pandemic
(Shuman et al. 2022). All these factors combined to create
volatile—even socially explosive—dynamics in some commu­
nities (Stajkovic and Stajkovic 2025) while raising serious ques­

public institutions, including police and public health depart­
ments (Hartley et al. 2023).
Given this volatility, instances of interorganizational resil­
ience between law enforcement and mental health organiza­
tions represent extreme cases of resilient collaboration
(Seawright and Gerring 2008). That is, if today’s collaborations
between law enforcement and mental health organizations can
cultivate resilience, collaborations between partners with more
similar professional knowledge likely also can.
Site selection
To study practices to cultivate resilience among law
enforcement-mental health collaborations, I took an abductive
approach, tapping concepts related to collaborative resilience
and high-reliability networks to analyze original data collected
for this project. Abduction allows iteration between theory and
data, so each informs exploration and interpretation of the
other (Tavory and Timmermans 2014; Ashworth, McDermott,
and  Currie  2019). Investigating combinations of boundary
and reliability practices used to cultivate resilience benefited
from a process study (Van de Ven 2007) of how the practices
emerged and evolved over time. Because process studies benefit
from variation in multiple dimensions across cases (Van de Ven
2007: 195), I studied partnerships in communities differing in
various aspects of volatility and collaboration.
•	 To vary the severity, scale, and unpredictability of need
facing collaborations, I selected communities differing in
population size and density, median income, scale of
homelessness, and emergency department visits.
•	 To vary social unrest and policy preferences related to
policing and public health, I selected sites with different
political ideologies and levels of protest activity in 2020.
Recent research finds that both political ideology
(Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty, and Lee 2023) and
protest activity (Stajkovic and Stajkovic 2025) are asso­
ciated with differences in local criminal justice policies.
•	 I also chose sites with slightly different arrays of collab­
orative partners and durations of activity.

Consistent with process-study principles (Van de Ven 2007),
these selection criteria did not seek to test associations between
local conditions and collaborative resilience. That is, none of the
conditions in Table 1 represented a potential causal antecedent
in the study. Rather, I sought to document and understand com­
binations of practices in a sufficient variety of settings to generate
findings with plausible generalizability to elaborate theory about
the practices (Ashworth, McDermott and  Currie et al. 2019).
I finalized case selection using data on multiple collabora­
tions between law enforcement and mental health organiza­
tions from the US Department of Justice’s Bureau of Justice
Assistance and the Council of State Governments’ Justice Cen­
ter
(https://csgjusticecenter.org/projects/law-enforcemen
t-mental-health-learning-sites/, accessed February 3, 2025). I
sorted those collaborations on the variables described imme­
diately above to select the sample of four sites summarized in
Table 1. The sites all had at least some experience of collabo­
ration (their law enforcement and mental health organizations
had worked together between 4 and 15 years when the research
started), but I had no prior knowledge of whether or how they
had used the practices or cultivated resilience.
Data collection
Data came from semistructured interviews with public manag­
ers and others, along with local media coverage and planning
and evaluation documents. I conducted video interviews with
29 individuals specifically for this study, identified through
snowball sampling. Thirteen were analysts or policy advisors
in intermediary or research organizations with broad knowl­
edge of public safety, mental and behavioral health, and their
intersection; they mostly provided field-wide information and
helped focus my site selection. Sixteen interviewees were local
policy makers or implementation staff—approximately four in
each site—who provided detailed information about the ori­
gins, evolution, and practice of collaboration. In each site, I
interviewed at least one individual each from mental health,
law enforcement, emergency response (where relevant), and
elected or politically appointed office. This breadth of perspec­
tives ensured I spoke with core partner organizations and pub­
lic officials in each community. Other than the elected and
appointed officials, most interviewees were program supervi­
sors or unit directors with direct line-management responsibil­
ities; two served as senior coordinating staff for their local
collaborations. All interviewees had at least a decade (in many
cases, multiple decades) of experience in their fields; each of
the elected and appointed officials also had backgrounds in
either law enforcement or mental health. A complete list of
interviews organized by site and position is available by request.
Media sources included local newspapers, magazines,
recorded webinars, and television news identified through
internet searches using names of the collaborations, partner
organizations, and practitioners in the sites (specific sources
available by request). Media coverage varied with the supply
and nature of local journalism, so the number of articles or
videos I reviewed in each site ranged from five or six in the two
smaller sites to twenty-five or thirty in the larger sites.
The planning and evaluation materials I reviewed included
websites and reports dating to the founding of each collabora­
tion. For each community, I reviewed websites for local law
enforcement and mental health organizations, the collabora­
tion itself (if it had a website), and the local legislature and
chief executive. The websites contained information on collab­
oration activities, their origins and funding, committee delib­
erations, and in some cases the motivations and operations of

Data analysis
Abductive analysis of data proceeded in four phases, each
informed in different measure by concepts from research on
resilience. Three drew on primary data; the fourth on second­
ary data assembled from a review of recent research examining
collaboration between law enforcement and mental health
organizations.
First, I assembled and reviewed data sources from each site
(interview notes, media coverage, and documents) to identify
major challenges and disruptions confronting the emergence
and evolution of their collaborations. Scanning data from all
four cases for common themes revealed four types of challenges
in the sites: “increasingly severe needs,” “competing problem
frames,” “contention on teams,” and “barriers to service.”
Although they emerged directly from the data, these challenges
broadly reflected the study’s focus on volatile implementation
settings. Two challenges manifested volatility in the local com­
munity (Challenge 1: increased public safety and mental health
needs; Challenge 2: competing problem frames). Another cap­
tured internal volatility within the collaboration (Challenge 3:
contention within teams), and the fourth combined evolving
volatility in the community with collaborative capacity limita­
tions (Challenge 4: barriers to service). For each site, I recorded
subthemes coinciding with each type of challenge in the data
and relevant actions the collaborative partners took before, as,
or after each challenge emerged.
Second, I grouped data from the sites about each challenge
and compared the subthemes from all four sites related to each
challenge. That cross-site comparison revealed broad similar­
ities as well as a few potentially revealing differences in the
scope and scale of the challenges and partners’ actions across
the sites.
The third phase of analysis began with a return to theory to
develop codes for the reliability and boundary practices for
cultivating resilience using keywords in the literature’s defini­
tion of each practice. Using the codes in Table 2, I reviewed
data from each site related to each challenge to pinpoint
instances in which collaborative partners used each practice in
response to each type of challenge. I combined and sorted data
on all the sites’ uses of the reliability and boundary practices
by challenge, identifying patterns across the sites and probing
for cross-case commonalities and differences in their responses
to each challenge. In doing so, I noted anomalies or new
insights emerging from the data that suggested a need to refute,
revise, or extend understanding of the uses and combinations
of the reliability and boundary practices. I devised plausible
explanations for the new observations and sought support or
disconfirmation through further data analysis (Van de Ven
2007: 140). This iterative process helped me recognize and
incorporate tensions and surprises while coding the data, con­
sidering prior knowledge (van Hulst and Visser 2025). I sub­
sequently elaborated theoretical concepts in line with findings
from the coded, sorted data.
An important instance of iteration between theory and data
occurred as I reviewed notes from an interview with a law enforce­
ment official at one site. They described an ongoing process
through which their collaborative partners practiced and—often
tacitly, yet consistently—committed to respecting and exploring
each other’s different perspectives to learn together how to better
serve people in crisis. The interview description of this process
struck me as more organic and informal than a typical instance
of collaborative discovery and definition of ground rules, group
norms, and application principles (Emerson and Nabatchi 2015).
Reviewing interview data from multiple sites, I noted quotes cap­
turing analogous processes among partners at the managerial or
front-line levels in all four sites (see Table 5 and findings below).
I conjectured that these processes reflected a distinct practice
enabling joint learning and adaptation of the boundary and reli­
ability practices. After consulting theory on reflexive governance
and public administration and governance (Cunliffe and Jun 2005;
Sabel and Zeitlin 2012), I added reflexive practice (explained
below in the findings) to my framework of practices to cultivate
interorganizational resilience.
Fourth, I compared findings from my analysis of case data
to themes reported in recent studies of comparable police-mental
health collaborations in other communities in the United States
(Bailey et al. 2018; Watson, Compton, and Pope 2019). Themes
in these secondary studies, frequently reported as “barriers”
(or “constraints”) and “enabling factors,” largely corroborated
or at least did not contradict findings from my case sites.

Challenge 1: increased needs involving public
safety and mental health
In all four sites, collaboration between law enforcement and
mental health organizations emerged in response to significant
increases in the severity and visibility of human needs involving
mental health and public safety. In Denver and Houston,
increased needs crystallized following police shootings of peo­
ple with severe mental health issues, prompting conversations
among mental health providers, the city police departments,
and others about ways to prevent similar incidents in the future.
Wichita and Yavapai County saw steady growth in mental
health-public safety issues without a corresponding increase in
suitable responses. Public officials described encountering in
their daily work more and more people with severe, unserved
mental health needs. A law enforcement official in Yavapai
County recalled, “There were so many people incarcerated…
with acute mental health diagnoses or chronic substance use.
They ended up there because of [criminal] behavior, but it’s
really because of mental illness or substance use.” The visibility
of these incidents elevated local perceptions of pressing prob­
lems at the intersection of mental health and public safety.
Boundary practices
In the face of these problems, mental health and law enforce­
ment professionals in all four sites worked to decenter differ­
ences through joint discussions. Denver and Houston convened
task forces of mental health and law enforcement professionals
to assess the scope and scale of the issues and develop inter­
ventions, augmented by informal, ad hoc conversations about
how prospective partner organizations might work together.
The discussions helped line staff and policymakers in both fields
better understand the needs of people in crisis and consider
collaborative responses. A mental health manager in Houston
recalled, “Sometimes our outreach team would visit a home
after a call [to the mental health crisis line], realize there was
a safety issue, and call the police. A police lieutenant and folks
from the mental health center got together and discussed the
idea of co-response, and we got funding for a pilot project.”
The smaller sites (Wichita and Yavapai County) started more
informal interorganizational discussions about new programs
combining law enforcement and mental health after profes­
sionals in the two fields shared concerns and ideas. An emer­
gency services manager in Wichita learned about a co-response
program in another state and invited local mental health staff
to go see it together. In his words, “We visited and thought it
was the coolest thing ever, so we started telling everyone we
could.” A mental health supervisor in Yavapai County recalled:
“In 2016, we realized people with severe mental health chal­
lenges were getting arrested for things that were inappropriate
for arrest. So we started a mobile crisis team to offer more
frontline care to people in crisis.”
This decentering of differences across professional fields, in
turn, enabled partners in all sites to start aligning among those
differences as well. After their visit to another city’s co-response
program, for example, the partners in Wichita presented the idea

Challenge 2: competing problem frames
A second source of friction and disruption in all four sites,
albeit manifesting in different ways, was competing problem
frames. Consistent with increasing polarization of public opin­
ion around police violence and the growing prevalence and
visibility of mental health crises, views of the public and poli­
cymakers about law enforcement and mental health issues were
divided in the sites. Divisions manifested in attributions of
causality or blame for problems at the nexus of public safety
and mental health, and concerns from some advocates about
the dangers of involving armed police in clinical responses of
any type.
Divisions were more pronounced in interviews in Denver
and Yavapai County than in Houston and Wichita. Denver saw
ongoing, pointed public debates about whether mental health
staff should work with police. A program manager for the city’s
collaborative programs noted, “Some of our steering commit­
tee members don’t want [mental health] clinicians anywhere
near police.”
Disagreements about the sources and symptoms of problems
involving public safety and mental health in the other sites were
less visible and heated, but still divisive. Collaborative partners
in all four sites endured tense, negative work environments
during the height of the COVID pandemic and the social unrest
following George Floyd’s murder in 2020. Interviews indicated
these sorts of difficulties were especially pronounced in Denver
and Houston, which saw longer, more sustained protests
against police (see Table 1). A law enforcement supervisor in
Denver recalled, “I was working downtown in 2020. I’ve been
a police officer for years and seen some protests in my time,
but I’d never seen that sort of day-to-day rioting, with [nearby]
agencies called in to assist. We’re used to backing up other
jurisdictions, but here everyone was called in to help us.”
Boundary practices
Proponents of collaboration in all four sites addressed com­
peting problem frames and their symptoms by translating
across differences, intentionally and consistently using policy
language suited to predominant local preferences to describe
the collaborations. In Denver (the most politically liberal site),
interviews indicated that the public and elected officials alike
talked about co- and community-response as “public health
programs.” In Houston, in contrast, a mayor’s report referred
to collaborative programs between mental health and law
enforcement organizations as “public safety” interventions.
Interviewees in Wichita and Yavapai County, meanwhile,
reported that collaborative partners and elected officials alike
referred to their initiatives as “collaborations between law
enforcement and behavioral health.” An elected official in
Yavapai County elaborated, “When I’m in front of liberals,
I sell it as public health. When I talk to Republicans, I call it
public safety.”
Reliability practices

connecting by diverse actors in the four sites, which helped

assumptions:

my position…. Five years into talking regularly—even when

repeatedly is crucial.

Challenge 3: contention within collaborative teams
A third challenge and source of disruption that all four collab­
orations confronted was managing working relationships
between frontline law enforcement and mental health staff who
were unfamiliar and sometimes hostile to one another. Inter­
viewees in several sites described poor communication and even
disdain between mental health and law enforcement organiza­
tions prior to collaboration. A frontline supervisor in Wichita
remembered, “Things were completely siloed. Emergency ser­
vices, the police, the sheriff, mental health—we never talked to
each other unless something really went wrong.” The supervi­
sor added that when people with mental health issues were
picked up by emergency services or the police, “They’d just sit
in a hospital bed or a holding cell; it was miserable for everyone
involved.” Yavapai County interviewees recalled “…bitter his­
tory between law enforcement and behavioral health…. The
cops would say, ‘If you’d do your job, we wouldn’t need to
arrest people.’ Behavioral health [staff] would say, ‘You just
need to stop arresting people.’”
Even where staff from different organizations did not openly
oppose working together, managers described misgivings and
skepticism among staff about working across organizational
lines. A manager overseeing staff from multiple mental health
programs working with several law enforcement agencies in
Houston reported, “We’ve had prospective hires come into
interviews and talk negatively about police with a bunch of
officers sitting there. We’ve had co-response team members
who had difficulty getting along with officers they rode with.”
A mental health program manager in Denver described con­
cerns and skepticism from police officers and 911 dispatchers
about unarmed clinicians’ abilities to respond safely to people
in crisis: “Dispatchers would say, ‘It feels weird to send civilians
sometimes [to respond to 911 calls].’” A law enforcement offi­
cer echoed, “When we started the co-responder program, I
remember thinking, ‘This is not gonna go well.’ ….I was con­
cerned somebody from the force would say something stupid
at roll call that someone [from mental health] didn’t like.”
In addition to mutual hostility between partners, different
organizations’ frontline practices, record keeping, and other
procedures fostered confusion, disagreements, and inconsistency
between collaborative teammates in the sites (see also Bailey et
al. 2018). In Wichita, tensions emerged within co-response teams
around who should lead call responses and how the responses
should proceed. A program manager explained:
EMS [emergency medical services] assumes implied consent
[to receive services]. If there are no safety or medical concerns
and mental health is leading a response, though, they need to
do HIPAA [health care consent paperwork], which takes time.
The police department have their own criteria and proce­
dures; cops have very clear lines they can’t cross; they have
to be invited to enter someone’s home. Even two years into
the program, we were troubleshooting issues like this in team
meetings.
Managers of co-response programs also reported disputes
among collaborative partners about dress and equipment.
Co-responders in Denver refused to wear bulletproof vests to
avoid intimidating people in crisis or being perceived as armed
officers. A mental health supervisor whose staff preferred
t-shirts recalled,
After working with us for six months or so, the cops said,
‘This has nothing to do with community perceptions; it’s
about your safety.’ They worried people were going to fire
at us, but as crisis clinicians we’re used to going into all sorts
of places without body armor; we have a pretty good sense
when we’ll be safe and when we won’t.
A program manager in Wichita reported similar disputes
between partner organizations over whether clinical staff
would wear vests and uniforms identifying them as first
responders. Insignia on mental health responders’ cars was
another point of disagreement among Wichita’s partner orga­
nizations. (Bailey et al. 2018 provide similar evidence from
additional communities.)

Reflexive practice
Analysis of interview data related to Challenge 3 revealed pat­
terns of partner interaction in the sites that went beyond the
boundary and reliability practices in the literature and helped
teams navigate internal contention. In different ways, the man­
agerial and frontline partners in all four sites reflected on and
revised how they worked together. This practice appeared most
starkly in Yavapai County, where one official described a work­
ing agreement the partners developed organically over time (see
Table 5, bottom row).
As Table 5’s bottom row shows, interviews in the other sites
suggested the emergence of similar reciprocal principles that
were often tacit. Repeated interactions helped partners recog­
nize issues in their working relationships and evolve provisional
solutions. This pattern in the data evoked behaviors of reflexive
governance and administration (Cunliffe and Jun 2005; Sabel
and Zeitlin 2012), so I consider it preliminary evidence of a
new reflexive practice to cultivate collaborative resilience:
assessing and adjusting joint work.
Challenge 4: barriers to service
Barriers to service in the sites stemmed from expanding
demand, insufficient capacity, or administrative obstacles that
limited funding or otherwise constrained program delivery.
Demand expanded gradually in the sites once collaborative
programs were operating as word spread, and then suddenly
during the COVID-19 pandemic, as violent crime and mental
health problems spiked across the United States.
Insufficient capacity also plagued all four sites, in part
because of expanding demand, but also because they began
with pilot projects serving narrow populations, individual
neighborhoods, or limited hours. One program manager noted
wryly, “Crises don’t just happen between 10:00 AM and 6:00
PM, Monday through Friday. They happen at 2:00 AM
Saturday.” Even successful expansions of collaborative pro­
gramming highlighted insufficient service capacity. Staff to an
elected official in Houston noted, “We’re lucky to have one of
the top mental health organizations in the country….but we
recognize we need a lot more mental health care. We just don’t
have enough therapists, enough counselors, across the region.”
A collaboration staffer in Yavapai County reported, “Since we
started, we’ve built out services and reduced gaps and needs,
but what remains isn’t low-hanging fruit. Our next challenges
loom really large…things like the housing crisis.”
Administrative barriers, meanwhile, were particularly prev­
alent in Denver and Yavapai County, which relied on Medicaid
to fund many collaborative services. A partner in Yavapai
County lamented, “I can flag problems to other program direc­
tors, and they’ll say, ‘That’s silly; I’ll have staff fix it.’ But after
they talk with staff, they come back, ‘Well, we can’t fix it
because we don’t have authority, discretion, funding, or some­
thing else.’”
Boundary practices
Addressing these barriers required decentering differences to
build political support and administrative capacity for service
expansion, launching new programs, and troubleshooting. All
four sites addressed growing demand and insufficient capacity
by expanding the scale and in some cases the scope of their
collaborative programming. Expansions of scale capitalized on
the initial pilot projects for specific populations in single neigh­
borhoods to convince elected officials and the broader public
of the benefits of collaborative programs. Through program
evaluations, publicizing performance data, information cam­
paigns, and word of mouth, the pilots built shared understand­
ings of what collaboration between mental health and law
enforcement could accomplish, decentering differences of opin­
ion. Denver and Houston ultimately came to offer co-response
and community response citywide (and throughout Harris
County surrounding Houston); Wichita and Yavapai County
gradually expanded the scale of their initial service offerings
as well.

Court liaison. Our Sheriff also sees across systems; he's so
high up he can’t always change his staff’s behavior, but he
has a lot of direct authority—unlike our Medicaid partners
who are mostly bound by state program requirements.
Discussion
The findings above offer plausible (if preliminary) answers to
my research questions about how collaborative partners com­
bine boundary and reliability practices to cultivate resilience
amid challenges of volatility. A sweeping view of Table 3
reveals similar challenges and roughly parallel uses of distinct
combinations of boundary and reliability practices in all four
sites in response to each challenge.
These similarities in very different communities suggest a
tentative conclusion about how implementation collaborations
navigate volatility: variations in local settings may matter less
for the use of different practices to cultivate resilience than do
the challenges a collaboration faces. That is, the sites’ broadly
consistent combinations of different boundary and reliability
practices in response to each challenge (shown in Table 3) belie

Practices to cultivate resilience amid different
challenges of volatility
More specifically, the consistent appearance of the same four
challenges in the sites suggests that volatility in a policy field
manifests in specific external and internal pressures on an
implementation collaboration. Patterns and anomalies in Table
3 reveal ways collaborations can address each challenge using
different boundary and reliability practices individually and in
combination. This subsection examines these patterns and
anomalies, considering the sequencing and content of the
challenges.
The first challenge interviews reported in the sites was
increasingly severe needs—the sites’ initial motivation for
launching collaborations between law enforcement and mental
health organizations. With little experience working together
across organizational and disciplinary silos, the new organiza­
tional partners needed to navigate difficulties of knowledge
integration across boundaries (Tell 2017). Differences between
law enforcement and mental health professionals’ knowledge
about field-specific issues and tasks required building mutually
compatible understandings of the increasing needs and how to
design and deliver joint services. As the findings described,
interagency task forces and informal conversations enabled
decentering and aligning among differences to build collabo­
rative relationships in the sites that, in turn, enabled connect­
ing, sensemaking, and problem solving in the face of increasing
needs. In knowledge integration terms, decentering and align­
ing among partners’ differences in field-specific knowledge
enabled connecting, sensemaking, and problem-solving around
issues related to joint service-delivery tasks. Two proposi­
tions follow:
P1: To address increasing needs, organizations with limited
experience working together across boundaries are likely to
begin collaborating by decentering and aligning among
differences.
P2: When needs increase, decentering and aligning among
differences increase the likelihood of collaborating organi­
zations connecting, sensemaking, and problem solving.
In response to Challenge 2 (competing problem frames), all
the sites used the boundary practice of translating across dif­
ferences and the reliability practice of connecting diverse actors
to address the challenge of competing problem frames. Com­
peting frames reflect and reinforce divisions in society, so con­
necting and translating across differences may offer baseline
practices to address them when the external environment is
especially volatile and conflict-ridden. As research on conflict
resolution and collaborative governance finds, learning about
others’ experiences, interests, and understandings of important
issues are critical early steps to bridge social divisions (Emerson
and Nabatchi 2015). In the face of deep disagreements in soci­
ety, boundary or reliability practices other than translating
across differences and connecting may be ineffective or beyond
the scope of a collaboration to cultivate collaborative resilience
or implementation reliability, at least initially. These observa­
tions suggest:
P3: To address competing problem frames in volatile envi­
ronments, implementation collaborations are more likely to
engage in translating across differences than decentering or
aligning across differences.
P4: To address competing problem frames in volatile envi­
ronments, implementation collaborations are more likely to
engage in connecting than sensemaking, problem solving,
or operating in parallel.
After launching collaborations, the sites confronted a third
challenge: contention within collaborative teams. In response,
all four sites (with one partial exception—Wichita) combined
decentering and aligning among differences as boundary
practices with connecting, sensemaking, and problem solving
among the reliability practices. This mix of practices is the
same one the sites used to address increasing needs (Chal­
lenge 1). Two possibilities might explain the partners’ use of
multiple, similar practices to address contention within the
frontline teams, as well as those they used to address increas­
ing needs.
First, the partners may already have been familiar with
those boundary and reliability practices after having used
them to launch collaboration in response to increasing needs.
Put another way, building interorganizational capacity to
address one challenge enabled the collaborators to expand
and deepen their interorganizational capacity to address other
challenges. This explanation is consistent with the finding that
collaborations build joint capacity for action through devel­
opmental dynamics (Bardach 2001; Emerson and Nabatchi
2015). Hence:
P5: Collaborative partners’ use of certain boundary and
reliability practices to address the initial challenges they
face increases the likelihood they will use the same prac­
tices to address subsequent challenges.
The second possibility considers the sites’ additional use of
the reflexive practice of assessing and adjusting collaborative
relationships alongside the more familiar boundary and reli­
ability practices to address contention within teams. On this
interpretation, the partners may have seen contention within
frontline teams as a threat to collaborative implementation and
responded by experimenting and adapting practices that proved
helpful. One can imagine partners thinking, “We have to do
something about our frontline staff relationships or our entire
collaboration will struggle, but we aren’t sure exactly what.
We may as well try different things, see how it goes, and adjust
as we go.” The emergence of reflexive practice alongside var­
ious boundary and reliability practices, thus, suggests partners
experimented pragmatically to navigate the uncertainty accom­
panying volatility (Ansell and Boin 2019; Sabel and
Zeitlin 2012).
P6: Amid contention within frontline teams, collaborative
partners will experiment with many different reflexive,
boundary, and reliability practices.

Once collaborative programs were operating, Challenge 4
emerged—barriers to service. Sites responded with two distinct
combinations of practices. The first combination—connecting
around operations while decentering and aligning among
differences—was only evident in Wichita. The comparative
recency and small scope of Wichita’s collaborative programs
likely explain why it responded differently from the other sites
to barriers to service. (As Table 1 shows, Wichita’s collabora­
tion was relatively new and only operated a co-response pro­
gram at the time of study; the other sites were longer standing
and operated multiple collaborative programs.) While inter­
viewees in Wichita reported connecting to address barriers to
service capacity, evidence of joint sensemaking, problem solv­
ing, or operating in parallel was not yet apparent. The recent
start of collaboration perhaps left partners with insufficient
time or experience working together to begin sensemaking or
problem-solving to address barriers to service before data col­
lection began. Operating in parallel was simply not possible,
as Wichita only had one collaborative program at the time.
A second response to barriers to service appeared in the other
sites—decentering and aligning among differences combined
with sensemaking, problem solving, and operating in
parallel—with a partial exception in Denver. In these longer
standing sites, partners’ joint sensemaking and problem-solving
had already expanded the scale and scope of collaborative pro­
gramming, so more services were operating in parallel when
barriers to service emerged. Because of those parallel opera­
tions, perhaps connecting was unnecessary when barriers to
service arose; the partners could address the barriers by tapping
into past experiences, sensemaking, and solving problems
together. This interpretation is again consistent with the bene­
fits of developmental dynamics for building joint capacity for
action (Bardach 2001; Emerson and Nabatchi 2015).
P7: When barriers to service arise, more experience collab­
orating increases the likelihood of partners sensemaking and
problem solving to expand joint programs or create
new ones.
A partial exception to this second combination of practices
appeared in Denver, where a confluence of two challenges may
have restricted possibilities for aligning among differences. Spe­
cifically, competing problem frames (Challenge 2) were espe­
cially divisive when barriers to service emerged in Denver amid
protests against police in 2020. Aligning among differences
between different types of program staff (eg, unarmed commu­
nity responders and armed police co-responding with unarmed
clinicians) therefore may have been more difficult in Denver
than elsewhere. A local headline from 2022 about expanding
Denver’s community response program read, “As STAR
Expands, Community Members Feel Left Out of Planning”
(https://www.rmpbs.org/blogs/rocky-mountain-pbs/as-sta
r-expands-the-community-feels-left-out-of-planning-heres
-why-it-matters, accessed July 28, 2025). This example suggests
that even partners with experience cultivating operational reli­
ability and decentering differences may face obstacles to align­
ing among differences when the broader political environment
is highly volatile.
P8: When barriers to service arise, more divisive competing
problem frames decrease the likelihood of partners aligning
among differences.
Finally, the foregoing reflections and propositions suggest
two reasons why reflexive practice—assessing and adjusting
joint work—may hold distinctive potential to nurture collab­
orative resilience.
1.	 Underlying Proposition 6—that collaborations navigated
uncertainty and contention within teams (Challenge 3)
through pragmatic experimentation—is the possibility
that reflexive practice may be associated with the use of
boundary and reliability practices to build resilience. For
example, reflexive practice enabled some boundary prac­
tices in the sites (as with Yavapai County partners’ com­
mitment to “abandon…‘No’” in Table 5) as well as
reliability practices, including sensemaking and problem
solving (as when Wichita partners “hear each other’s
challenges and suggest ways to address them”).
2.	 Reflexive practice may catalyze and reinforce develop­
mental dynamics among collaborative partners that can
build their capacity for joint action over time (Bardach
2001; Emerson and Nabatchi 2015). Because assessing
and adjusting collaborative work amid uncertainty can
increase shared understandings of progress and how to
build on it (Ansell and Boin 2019; Sabel and Zeitlin
2012), partners’ joint capacity to use boundary and reli­
ability practices to cultivate resilience amid volatility may
also increase.
If reflexive practice supports various boundary and reliability
practices and developmental dynamics across organizational
lines, it may offer a distinctive, complementary way to adapt
collaborative relationships and implementation amid volatility.
The continuous reflection and adjustment it embodies further
suggests that resilience in volatile settings is not a state or qual­
ity a collaboration achieves, but rather an ongoing process
partners cultivate through multiple types of practices.
Conclusion
Research has repeatedly shown that resilience is important for
both ongoing operations and collaborative relationships in
volatile settings (Berthod et al. 2017; Weick and Sutcliffe 2007).
This study contributes to the theory and practice of collabora­
tive resilience in three ways. First, it identified four challenges
that collaborations face in volatile environments. Second, prop­
ositions distinguished ways implementation partners can com­
bine high reliability practices (Berthod and Sydow 2021) with
boundary practices (Quick and Feldman 2014) to build inter­
organizational resilience in response to each challenge. Third,
it uncovered a reflexive practice of collaborative assessment
and adjustment that may be especially useful to build interor­
ganizational resilience amid volatility.
While the study’s abductive design sought to elaborate theory
rather than draw definitive conclusions, collaborative partners
in four diverse sites used the boundary and reliability practices
in consistent combinations in response to different challenges.
This finding held across local contexts differing in multiple
ways, including community size, demographics, political ide­
ology, and indicators of volatility (including severity of needs
and social unrest). Local background conditions therefore may
affect collaborative efforts to build resilience less than the chal­
lenges partners face and how they combine different practices
in response.

The similar combinations of practices and collaborations
used to cultivate resilience in the various case settings are strik­
ing, particularly given recent volatility and stress facing law
enforcement and mental health organizations. While volatility
and stress likely affected local communities in different ways,
all four collaborations in the study used roughly similar com­
binations of different practices.
Future research could refine or refute this study’s findings by
studying more cases of collaboration between law enforcement
and mental health organizations. Scholars could investigate
more systematically the combinations of practices identified
here between specific types of challenges facing implementation
collaborations in volatile settings and the uses of the various
reflexive, boundary, and reliability practices. Of particular
interest is whether and how specific practices enable the use of
other practices. For example, scholars could explore whether
and how translating across or aligning among differences
enables joint sensemaking or problem solving. Research could
also investigate whether and through what mechanisms a rela­
tionship between reflexive practice and developmental dynam­
ics unfolds to build partners’ capacity for joint action.
Finally, research could study challenges of volatility and
practices to cultivate interorganizational resilience in other
policy fields. Political contention and pressing needs currently
abound in US public education, disaster relief, climate change,
and other fields. Research investigating whether findings from
this study apply in other volatile settings may prove valuable
for scholars and practitioners alike.

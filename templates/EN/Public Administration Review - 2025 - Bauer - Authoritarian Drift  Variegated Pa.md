ABSTRACT
This article develops a typology of illiberal rule by identifying four distinct models of administrative transformation: neoauthor-
itarian, neopatrimonial, neoreactionary, and neoclientelist. Drawing on cases including Viktor Orbán, Donald Trump, Javier
Milei, and Andrés Manuel López Obrador, it argues that illiberal leaders reshape bureaucracies through diverse strategies of
control, each rooted in specific conceptions of the state and particular modes of bureaucratic design. In light of the varying
illiberal trajectories, the article concludes by proposing differentiated reform strategies tailored to the specific threats posed by
each variant of illiberal rule, aiming to bolster bureaucratic resilience accordingly. The analysis calls for a reorientation of public
administration theory—from a focus on compliance to a normative conception of civil servants as stewards of democratic order.
1   |   Introduction1
The spread of illiberal rule has emerged as a serious threat to
the survival of democratic government (Zakaria  1997, 2002;
Plattner  2020a, 2020b; Nord et  al.  2025). Around the world,
democratically elected but authoritarian-­leaning political lead-
ers are subverting liberal norms and institutions from within,
undermining pluralism, the rule of law, and minority rights
even as they maintain the outward appearance of a democ-
racy (Levitsky and Ziblatt 2018; Lührmann and Lindberg 2019;
Diamond 2022).
Scholarship has clarified the role of illiberal ideology, exec-
utive aggrandizement, and legal erosion in illiberal trajecto-
ries (Bermeo  2016; Huq and Ginsburg 2018; Plattner  2020a;
Sajó 2019; Schäfer and Zürn 2023; Haggard and Kaufman 2021).
However, fewer works have systematically compared what hap-
pens inside the state apparatus under illiberal rule: how state
administrations are restructured, whether administrative au-
tonomy survives, and in what ways administrative organizations
are reshaped to serve illiberal ends (Green 2019; Rockman 2019;
Peters and Pierre  2019; Bauer and Becker  2020; Peters  2024;
Lotta et al. 2024; Alon-­Barkat et al. 2025; James 2025).
Illiberal regimes, as emerges from the literature, tend to regard
their state administrations either as a tool to consolidate their
political dominance or as an impediment to their ambitions,
which must therefore be neutralized, bypassed, or dismantled
(Bauer et al. 2021; Silva and deÁvila Gomide 2024; Hajnal and
Boda 2021). For example, in Hungary, Viktor Orbán centralized
authority and placed loyalists throughout the civil service to turn
the state apparatus into a vehicle of party control (Hajnal and
Csengődi  2014; Bíró-­Nagy  2017; Hajnal  2021). Donald Trump,
during his first term, by contrast, framed the bureaucracy as a
hostile “deep state” and sought to sabotage regulatory agencies
by installing mission-­skeptical leadership or reducing operational
budgets (Johnston 2018; Moynihan 2021). Javier Milei's model of
governance appears designed to render the traditional bureau-
cracy obsolete by dismantling state institutions and substitut-
ing public authority with privatized, market-­based mechanisms

Against this backdrop, the aim of this article is to unpack the
varying strategies pursued by illiberal rulers by examining how
they seek to restructure public administration systems, with the
goal of identifying the strategies driving the erosion of demo-
cratic bureaucracy.
This focus is important because public administrations are not
merely passive implementers of policy (Olsen 2006; Stivers 2008);
they are the organizational backbone of liberal democracy—tasked
with upholding legality, impartiality, and accountability across po-
litical cycles (Bertelli 2021; Bauer 2024c). When illiberal regimes
reconfigure administrative structures, they thus do more than just
shift organizational charts; they redefine the rules of democratic
governance itself, often weakening institutional constraints and
insulating executive power from scrutiny. By identifying the un-
derlying logics of these transformations, we stand a better chance
of grasping the political strategies of illiberal rulers to subvert for-
mal democratic institutions. Understanding these guiding prefer-
ences and patterns can clarify the conditions and contexts under
which specific illiberal strategies endure or falter, promising in-
sight into where democratic resilience might be reinforced within
the democratic administrative state to counter illiberal threats
(Plattner 2020b; Laruelle 2024).
The article proceeds as follows. It begins by disentangling pop-
ulism from illiberalism, arguing that the latter concept is better
positioned to provide an analytical lever to explain the observable
diverse institutional restructuring of democratic administration. It
then develops a typology grounded in two main dimensions—state
conception and administrative control—and illustrates the value
of such a typology by analyzing the public administration philos-
ophies of Vitor Orbán, Donald Trump, Javier Milei, and Andrés
Manuel López Obrador. These illustrations inform four ideal types
of illiberal rule: neoauthoritarian, neopatrimonial, neoreactionary,
and neoclientelist.2 The final sections link this typology of illiberal
rule to potential countermeasures that public sector reformers can
employ to enhance the resilience of public administration systems
against specific illiberal challenges.
2   |   Populism as a “Thin-­Centered Ideology”: Going
Beyond for Understanding Illiberal Administrative
Reform
Much of the recent scholarly effort in comparative public admin-
istration regarding questions of democratic bureaucracy under il-
liberal rule has conceived ongoing administrative transformations
through the lens of populism (Green 2019; Peters and Pierre 2019;
Bauer et al. 2021). This literature has grown in tandem with the
political success of populist parties, particularly those categorized
as authoritarian populists (Urbinati  2019; Mudde and Rovira
Kaltwasser 2017; Stoker 2021; James 2025). However, I argue that
the analytical value of populism theory for explaining patterns of
administrative reform is limited (Croissant and Tosun 2025).
This is so, because, as political theory emphasizes, populism
does not constitute a comprehensive political ideology on its own.
Instead, it functions as a meta-­level host ideology, which must be
combined with other ideological tropes to assume a concrete polit-
ical form (Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser 2017; Müller 2016). This
claim is backed by the most widely accepted definition of populism
advanced by the so-­called ideational approach, which character-
izes populism, in light of its incompleteness, as “a thin-­centered
ideology” (Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser 2017, 6). Accordingly,
populism's central topos is the idea that society is ultimately di-
vided into two homogeneous and antagonistic camps—“the pure
people” versus “the corrupt elite”—and that politics should there-
fore express the general will of the people (Mudde and Rovira
Kaltwasser 2017; Müller 2016).3 The ideological core of populism is
thus moral and relational. It pits a unified, virtuous people against
a corrupt political elite, often in a diffuse anti-­institutional and
anti-­pluralist fashion. While this antagonistic moral dualism is
electorally mobilizing, it offers no detailed programmatic blueprint
for governing complex administrative systems. As Mansbridge
and Macedo (2019) maintain, although the ideological core of pop-
ulism remains stable, its peripheral elements vary so widely that
attempts to generalize from them a coherent political agenda are
bound to fail (Urbinati 2019; Moffitt 2020; Bauer 2024a).
This conceptual meagerness of populism is particularly problem-
atic for public administration research. If populism's content varies
from left to right, from nationalist to libertarian, from neoliberal to
redistributive, and from technocratic to anti-­expert, then this vari-
ability raises serious doubts about whether populism theory alone
can account for predicting distinct patterns of administrative
transformation entertained by all of those ideological positions.
And indeed, there are only scarce concrete conceptual implica-
tions stretching from populism to questions of administrative
structure, the role conceptions of bureaucrats, or the design of
accountability mechanisms. Populism, understood as a thin-­
centered ideology, is inherently flexible in content and compat-
ible with a wide range of political and administrative reform
projects. As such, it therefore offers little explanatory lever for

However, the subset of “authoritarian populists” in govern-
ment also seems to implement its anti-­pluralism and anti-­
elitism differently when it comes to reforming their states and
public administrations (see the collection of case studies in
Bauer et al. 2021). It appears that even in authoritarian popu-

but, in some respects, fundamentally incompatible, which be-

of hierarchical state capture with Milei's vision of private-­sector
substitution. Hence, there must be more specific ideological
programs beyond anti-­pluralism and anti-­elitism that drive the
respective projects of state transformation of authoritarian pop-
ulists. But which are they? And how can they be systematically
distinguished?
Populism theory cannot answer these questions. It has no co-
herent program for administrative reform.4 It cannot accurately
predict what approach individual populist movements will take
in terms of their policy toward state institutions and institutional
change. Populism theory tells us why populists may frame bu-
reaucracies as illegitimate, but not how they will seek to change
them. Populism provides the language of contestation, but it is,
as I argue, illiberalism that supplies the logic of aspired institu-
tional change. If public administration scholarship is to provide
adequate conceptual tools for analyzing these developments, it
must decouple the two concepts.
I thus suggest conceiving illiberal rule as a vision of governance
that rejects the normative core of liberal democracy—individual
rights, institutional checks, and pluralism—while often preserv-
ing electoral procedures (Galston 2018; Laruelle 2024). Unlike
populism, which is perhaps better understood as a political style
(Kriesi and Pappas 2024; Rueda 2021), illiberalism is a substan-
tive ideological project grounded in a different political ontol-
ogy as well as an alternative to the liberal democratic model
(Waller 2024).
Philosophically, illiberalism draws on notions of collectivist
unity, organic national identity, and executive sovereignty, re-
jecting liberalism's commitment to moral universalism, legal
neutrality, and procedural constraint. It is deeply skeptical of in-
dividual autonomy and distrustful of intermediary institutions
(courts, parliaments, bureaucracies), which it sees as obstruct-
ing the will of an imagined unified majority or nation (Sajó
et  al.  2021). Illiberal thinkers like (Schmitt  2005) emphasize
decisionism over deliberation, arguing that sovereignty lies in
the capacity to act outside the law for the sake of the political
community's integrity. In practice, illiberal rule transforms the
administrative state into an extension of the executive will, le-
gitimized not by pluralist consent but by a claimed cultural or
moral homogeneity. While populism opens the door, illiberal-
ism frames the restructuring project that follows—durable, cen-
tralized, and normatively opposed to liberal constitutionalism
(Sajó 2019; Plattner 2020a; Waller 2023).
3   |   Varieties of Illiberal Rule: Case Evidence
If this line of thinking is correct, the divergent cases of Orbán,
Trump, Milei, and López Obrador present both a challenge and
an opportunity. By systematically distinguishing and theorizing
variants of illiberal rule across these cases—each pursuing dis-
tinct approaches to reshaping administrative structures and ex-
ercising bureaucratic control—we might be able to substantiate
the claim that illiberalism constitutes a more precise and analyt-
ically robust framework than populism for comparative public
administration research. Advancing this argument requires a
careful comparative analysis of empirical cases of illiberal rule,
giving particular attention to their underlying philosophies of
the state and strategies of administrative control. I contend that
this is best achieved through the development of a typology
capable of capturing the internal diversity and strategic logics
of illiberal rule as it manifests in the transformation of public
administration.5
A typology of illiberal administrative transformation—as any
typology in the social sciences—cannot be fully theorized ex
ante; its validity and utility hinge not on deductive coherence
alone but on its capacity to capture patterned variation grounded
in empirical reality (Collier et al. 2012). Typologies must emerge
from sustained engagement with case-­specific detail, allowing
conceptual categories to crystallize from within observed prac-
tices rather than being imposed from above. This approach re-
flects a methodological commitment to abductive reasoning (in
which theoretical constructs are refined iteratively through con-
frontation with real-­world complexity) and recognizes that the
significance of a typology lies in its explanatory traction when
applied by others across diverse empirical contexts (Tavory and
Timmermans 2014).
In this sense, typologies function less as definitive taxonomies
and more as heuristic instruments whose value is revealed
only in use (and thus by the scientific community itself). As
Sartori  (1970) cautioned in his classic critique of conceptual
stretching, premature generalization risks losing both analytical
precision and empirical relevance. Likewise, Collier et al. (2012)
argue that typologies serve as “productive fictions,” tools to or-
ganize comparative inquiry that must be empirically anchored
and progressively refined through application.
Following this reasoning, the next step is to turn to empirical
observations: examining carefully selected, concrete cases of
illiberal rule to observe how state bureaucracies are reconfig-
ured in practice, allowing the typological distinctions to emerge
inductively and iteratively from the logic and variation of these
transformations.
4   |   Orbán's Bureaucratic Capture
Viktor Orbán's consolidation of power in Hungary since 2010
represents the paradigmatic case of illiberal bureaucratic trans-
formation—because Orbán has held power for so long, he could
thus advance, if not complete, his agenda over various electoral
mandates (Hajnal and Boda 2021). Orbán did not aim to disman-
tle the administrative state but to reengineer it as a centralized
instrument of political control by his one-­party government.

His governance approach reflects a state-­directing philoso-
phy, where the state apparatus is utilized as such but must be
stripped of pluralism, autonomy, and professional norms (Bíró-­
Nagy 2017; Hajnal and Boda 2021).
Orbán's vision was able to build on Hungary's statist tradition,
rooted in both communist-­era administrative legacies and weak
liberal-­democratic safeguards. He reframed the bureaucracy as
a vessel for national renewal, subordinated to a majoritarian,
ethnonationalist conception of democracy advocated by his
FIDEZ party (Kovács and Hajnal 2017). The state is not illegit-
imate but must be insulated from internal dissent and external
accountability.
His strategy of administrative control thus focuses on central-
ized takeover. Structural reforms created, for example, new
“county” and “district government offices,” staffed by gov-
ernment appointees, displacing elected local governments.
Municipal funding was cut by nearly 80%, and ministerial au-
tonomy was curtailed via agency mergers and enhanced control
by his inner power center, the Prime Minister's Cabinet Office
(Hajnal 2015). New agencies, created by decree, bypassed par-
liamentary oversight and operated with reduced transparency.
Personnel policies further entrenched central executive control.
A 2010 law (later overturned without its substantial transforma-
tions being reversed) allowed for the dismissal of civil servants
without cause, followed by reforms that rewarded loyalty and
permitted intrusive surveillance (Hajnal and Csengődi  2014).
The result was a purge-­and-­replace model, politicization at all
ranks, eroding neutrality and fostering internal compliance.
Staffing decisions became a key mechanism for disciplining the
bureaucracy in line with regime objectives.
This capture extended to civil society oversight. Orbán's govern-
ment dismantled accountability mechanisms—weakening the
ombudsperson system, dissolving tripartite interest councils,
and withdrawing from international transparency frameworks.
NGOs critical of the regime were stigmatized or defunded, es-
pecially those with international affiliations. These moves
curtailed pluralist input into governance, rendering the bureau-
cracy more secluded and unaccountable.
In sum, Orbán's transformation of the Hungarian bureaucracy
constitutes the textbook case of illiberal state capture (Bauer
and Becker 2020; Meyer-­Sahling and Toth 2020). Through cen-
tralization, politicization, patronage, selective exclusion, and
not least control of the main media, the state is remade into a
vertically controlled apparatus of producing regime loyalty and
suppressing liberal norms. The goal is not efficiency, but insula-
tion from pluralism and democratic constraint. Hungary's case
demonstrates how public administration—when captured—can
be transformed into the backbone of a system of soft authoritar-
ianism (Bogaards 2018).
5   |   Donald Trump as “Owner” of the State
The USA under Donald Trump is now seen as the “fastest au-
tocratizing country in contemporary history that does not in-
volve a coup d'état” (Nord et  al.  2025, 1). Trump's approach
to governance embodies a mode of illiberal rule that sees the
administrative state not as a neutral framework, but as either
an ideological enemy or a tool for personal power and private
gain. His rhetoric casts bureaucrats as unelected obstacles to
the popular will—the “deep state” or “swamp”—delegitimiz-
ing the very idea of administrative autonomy (Moynihan 2022,
2025; Croissant and Tosun 2025; Bersch and Fukuyama 2023).
During his first presidency, Trump primarily sabotaged regula-
tory agencies—especially those concerned with environmental
and social protections—partly because he remained constrained
by “adults in the room” from the Republican establishment
(Moynihan 2021). By contrast, the first hundred days of his sec-
ond term reveal a radicalization of his governance style: Trump
now interprets his electoral comeback victory as granting him
personal ownership of the government and unrestricted author-
ity to restructure or exploit it for the benefit of himself and his
inner circle (Moynihan 2025).
Yet, Elon Musk's DOGE adventure aside,6 Trump's hostility to
the bureaucracy seems more tactical than ideological. He does
not aim to reduce the state so much as reshape it into a pure
instrument of personal loyalty. As Moynihan (2022) observes,
his goal is less deregulation than control: enforcement agencies
and watchdog bodies are politicized, not completely dismantled.
The state is tolerated insofar as it is subordinated and obedient
to the executive, or rather to the personal will of the president
(Kettl 2025).
Trump's administrative sabotage—slashing agency budgets,
leaving leadership posts vacant, and appointing officials as
agency heads hostile to the mission of their own organizations
(Moynihan and Roberts  2021a, 2021b; Guedes-­Neto  2024)—
thus followed no greater plan according to a coherent vision of
the state than dismantling regulation that he deemed bad for
(his) business (friends in the energy sector) or restricting his
room of maneuver.7 Schedule F, first introduced in 2020, which
constitutes the cornerstone of his civil service reform attempts,
is a good example. It reclassifies around 50,000 career officials
as at-­will employees, paving the way for ideological purges
(Moynihan 2025) and now implemented in a slightly different
form, as one of the first decrees signed by Trump after his sec-
ond inauguration in early 2025 (Beschel Jr. 2025; Kettl 2025).
Trump's strategy is thus not institutional—it is deeply person-
alist. Administrative appointments reward loyalty over compe-
tence (Moynihan 2025; Kucinskas 2025). Family members and
close allies occupy influential roles, blurring public authority
with private interest (Croissant and Tosun 2025). Moreover, the
office of the president of the United States is monetized: Trump
has extracted revenue through ventures like the Trump coin
cryptocurrency (“$TRUMP”), promoted a docuseries featuring
his wife, and demanded (and received) financial compensation
from media outlets. Administrative power thus becomes a chan-
nel for clan enrichment as much as control.
In sum, Trump's bureaucratic philosophy is neither reformist
nor technocratic. It is an illiberal model in which bureaucratic
legitimacy derives from its loyalty to the executive center; bu-
reaucracy is politicized and personalized while due process is
disregarded beyond legality. By framing political relationships
as transactions of personal loyalty rather than impersonal

6   |   The Chainsaw Doctrine of Javier Milei
Javier Milei's presidency represents a radical libertarian attempt
at state dismantling. Declaring war on what he calls the “para-
sitic caste”, Milei's “chainsaw plan” is not aimed at institutional
reform but at the ideological eradication of the state's presence
in social and economic life. Drawing on Austrian School eco-
nomics, particularly the anarcho-­capitalism of Rothbard and
the ordo-­liberalism of Eucken, his project envisions the state not
as a malfunctioning machine to be repaired, but as an illegit-
imate structure to be subtracted (Moch 2024; Kleinheyer and
Schnabl 2025; Heinisch et al. 2024).
Unlike Musk's vision of virtualized governance through tech
platform logic, Milei pursues a politics of radical administra-
tive cutback. Symbolic acts, such as tearing up the Ministry of
Culture live on television, were matched by the halving of na-
tional ministries, the dismissal of over 40,000 civil servants,
and the attempted liquidation of regulatory bodies, from the
National Institute of Cinema (INCAA) to the scientific research
agency CONICET (Rius-­Ulldemolins and Verdenelli  2025;
Debat 2024). Culture, labor, energy, and science were framed
not as public goods but as ideological battlegrounds corrupted
by “cultural Marxism”.
Institutionally, this transformation is orchestrated through
hyper-­presidentialist instruments. The newly created Ministry
of Deregulation and State Transformation, led by Federico
Sturzenegger, possesses the authority to annul legislation, re-
peal decrees, and bypass Congress to “rationalize” the state.
More than 350 laws have been slated for revocation, and over
3.5 regulations are reportedly eliminated per day (Kleinheyer
and Schnabl 2025; Knoell 2024). The strategy amounts to de-­
sovereignization by executive decree—less a reengineering of
governance than a controlled implosion.
Similar to techno-­populist models that emulate corporate mana-
gerialism (like Musk's DOGE), Milei's vision is not institutional
redesign but libertarian elimination:8 the abolition of the central
bank, dollarization, and legal frameworks enabling unmediated
currency competition (Milei 2020; Moch 2024). This approach
reduces the state to a residual mechanism whose administra-
tive capacities are either privatized, defunded, or delegitimized.
Bureaucracy is, in this model, not to be repurposed—but ren-
dered obsolete.9
Paradoxically, Milei's anti-­statist agenda consolidates power in
the executive. While railing against institutional elites, he has
marginalized Congress, suspended consultative procedures,
and centralized authority in the presidency. The result is a
dual movement involving both the dismantling of the admin-
istrative state and the aggrandizement of executive command.
Libertarian discourse thus converges with authoritarian prac-
tice, a model where deregulation masks democratic erosion
(Heinisch et al. 2024; Knoell 2024).
In sum, Milei's “chainsaw doctrine” outlines a distinct illiberal
variant of administrative transformation—neither capture nor
substitution, but annihilation. Whether this leads to economic
reanimation or institutional collapse remains uncertain. What
is already observable is the emergence of a governance model
in which public administration is subordinated to executive dis-
cretion, democratic deliberation is displaced by market-­centric
moral narratives, and the state is reconfigured through ideolog-
ically driven institutional retrenchment.
7   |   López Obrador: Power via Patronage
Andrés Manuel López Obrador, president of Mexico from 2018
to 2024, reconfigured bureaucratic and political structures in
order to centralize power and distribute benefits selectively,
thereby consolidating his political base. López Obrador's vision
of the state (following Orbán in this respect) was not to disman-
tle it but to use it. He reshaped it into a personalized tool for
political patronage via resource distribution and loyalist-­based
control (Galego and Nieto-­Morales 2024).
Similar to anti-­statists, he portrayed existing bureaucratic
institutions as corrupt and inefficient, but the answer was
not dismantling and replacement (like in Milei's vision) but
a transformation that would align the bureaucracy with his
power stabilizing objectives. This led to the weakening of au-
tonomous agencies and the centralization of authority within
the executive branch (Dussauge-­Laguna  2021). For instance,
he proposed the elimination of independent regulatory bodies,
arguing that their functions overlapped with those of federal
ministries and that their removal would lead to cost savings and
increased efficiency. López Obrador created then new structures
that bypassed traditional bureaucratic channels. The intro-
duction of “superdelegados” (super delegates) and “Servidores
de la Nación” (Servants of the Nation) illustrate this approach
(González-­Vázquez et al. 2024). These entities operated directly
under the executive branch, distributing social programs and
consolidating political support without the oversight of estab-
lished institutions (in those respects similar to what Fujimori
tried in Peru in the 1990s, see Bauer and Becker 2020).
Simultaneously, López Obrador expanded the role of the mili-
tary in civilian affairs (like Jair Bolsonaro did in Brazil, see also
Amorim Neto and Acácio  2025; Bauer et  al.  2024), assigning
them responsibilities ranging from infrastructure projects to
public security. The creation of the National Guard, a milita-
rized police force under military command, further blurred the
lines between civilian and military functions. This militariza-
tion strategy allowed for greater control over administrative
functions while circumventing traditional checks and balances
(Pansters and Serrano 2024).
In summary, López Obrador's governance approach transformed
Mexico's bureaucratic landscape into a system where formal

8   |   Four Modes of Illiberal Rule
Though not exhaustive, the preceding case discussions none-
theless suggest that illiberal regimes do not follow a singular
model of administrative transformation. Instead, they advance
divergent conceptions of state legitimacy and pursue distinct
strategies of bureaucratic control. To make sense of this varia-
tion, I propose a typology grounded in precisely these two core
dimensions: (1) the vision of the state—whether it is to be in-
strumentally retained or antagonistically rejected; and (2) the
mechanism of administrative control—whether such control is
pursued via internal capture or external substitution. Together,
these dimensions yield four ideal-­typical configurations of il-
liberal rule: neoauthoritarian, neopatrimonial, neoreactionary,
and neoclientelist.
8.1   |   Neoauthoritarian Rule (e.g., Orbán)
It is defined by internal capture and instrumental reten-
tion: the state is not discarded or bypassed but systemati-
cally reengineered as a vehicle for executive consolidation.10
Bureaucratic institutions are preserved in form but hollowed
out in function, subordinated through legal restructuring, po-
liticized appointments, and centralized oversight. Rather than
dismantling or substituting the administrative apparatus, this
model transforms it into a mechanism of ideological control,
filtering bureaucratic autonomy and pluralist accountability
through the lens of regime loyalty. Formal legality is main-
tained, but constitutional engineering, personnel purges, and
regulatory centralization ensure that institutional resistance
is neutralized. This approach is exemplified by Viktor Orbán's
Hungary, and echoed in Narendra Modi's India (Singh 2023),
Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's Turkey (Elçi  2024), and Benjamin
Netanyahu's efforts to restructure Israel's judiciary and public
administration to entrench executive authority under a legal-
istic façade (Shilon 2022).
8.2   |   Neopatrimonial Rule (e.g., Trump)
It emerges when the state is regarded as both antagonistic and
instrumental—a structure to be distrusted yet repurposed for
personalist control.11 Rather than dismantling the administra-
tive apparatus, this form of rule pursues internal capture: col-
onizing the bureaucracy through loyalty-­based appointments,
politicized oversight, and informal discretion. Procedural
neutrality and legal-­rational norms, in the Weberian sense,
are hollowed out and replaced by executive will, subordina-
tion, and informal enrichment (Bozeman et  al.  2024). The
legitimacy of state action stems not from institutional due
process (which is discarded) but from allegiance to the ruler
(Croissant and Tosun  2025). This strategy of bureaucratic
personalization is evident in the United States under Donald
Trump, where agencies were reoriented toward political loy-
alty, and in Italy under Silvio Berlusconi, where formal state
institutions were instrumentalized to advance private politi-
cal and economic interests (Newell 2010).
8.3   |   Neoreactionary Rule (e.g., Milei)
It embodies a radical rejection of the bureaucratic state, treat-
ing it as obsolete ballast to be bypassed and replaced through
external substitution.12 Traditional administrative structures
are dismissed as inefficient, politicized, and inherently sluggish,
giving way to privatized or executive-­centered alternatives.
Governance is reframed as a problem of market optimization,
elite control, and acceleration, often under the banner of dis-
ruption and epistemic superiority. This model fuses libertarian
suspicion of the state with post-­bureaucratic claims to compe-
tence, de-­institutionalizing authority in favor of digitally medi-
ated or corporate-­style governance. While exemplified by Milei's
anarcho-­libertarian ideology in Argentina, a subtype is exem-
plified by Musk's DOGE initiative, which is based on oligarchic
techno-­platform governance (Stanger  2025; Fukuyama  2025),
and in Nayib Bukele's algorithmic populism in El Salvador,
where state functions are increasingly outsourced to digital plat-
forms and executive discretion.
8.4   |   Neoclientelist Rule (e.g., López Obrador)
It is characterized by external substitution combined with in-
strumental retention: while the formal architecture of the state
is preserved, its administrative functions are increasingly dis-
placed into informal, parallel, or militarized structures.13 The
bureaucracy is maintained in appearance but bypassed in prac-
tice through networks of loyalty, targeted patronage, and clien-
telist redistribution. Rather than relying on impersonal rules or
rights-­based incorporation, state–citizen relations are reconfig-
ured around personal allegiance and discretionary benefit allo-
cation. This form of rule exploits the symbolic and redistributive
capacity of the state while hollowing out its procedural core. It
can be observed in Mexico under López Obrador, but similar
dynamics shape Bolsonaro's Brazil, Fujimori's Peru, and con-
temporary Serbia, where bureaucratic continuity conceals deep
politicization and patronage-­driven governance (Bauer and
Becker 2020; Peters and Pierre 2022; Menezes and Barbosa 2021;
Peci 2021; Radeljić and Đorđević 2020) (Table 1).
This typology suggests that illiberal rule is not monolithic but
can be systematically analyzed through distinct structural log-
ics. While all four types undermine core liberal-­democratic
norms—such as pluralism, legality, and accountability—they
do so via analytically distinguishable strategies of state trans-
formation. In practice, these ideal types rarely appear in pure
form; elements of different strategies can combine to produce
hybrid regimes. For example, during a period when Elon Musk
operated the DOGE “department,” one could observe a fusion
of Milei-­like neoreactionary tendencies with Trump-­style patri-
monial dynamics. The typology should therefore not be seen as
imposing rigid categories on empirical cases. Rather, it provides

9   |   Boosting Bureaucratic Resilience in an Era of
Democratic Backsliding: Strategies to Safeguard
Democratic Administration From Illiberal Drift
The four ideal types outlined above highlight diverse pathways
of illiberal administrative transformation. Each undermines
liberal-­democratic governance through a specific configuration
of state vision and bureaucratic control strategy. Understanding
these differential logics is essential not only for comparative
structural analysis, but also for identifying the institutional vul-
nerabilities that illiberal regimes aim to strategically exploit.
Against this backdrop, the central question becomes: What can
public managers and democratic reformers do to boost liberal
bureaucratic resilience in view of potential authoritarian drift?
Resisting illiberal transformation probably requires forward-­
looking institutional capacity building; it calls for an affirma-
tive commitment to a normative conception of public service,
one which combines ethical sensitivity, institutional guardian-
ship, and democratic anchorage (Sørensen and Torfing  2005;
Morlino 2005; Yesilkagit et al. 2024).
The answer to the various neo-­illiberal types of rule and re-
spective diverse threats to democratic bureaucracy might thus
be conceived as a neo-­liberal institutional counter strategy. The
challenge, then, is not simply to preserve bureaucratic auton-
omy, but to reassert a vision of public administration as a cor-
nerstone of systemic democratic integrity (Heath  2020; Bauer
et al. 2021). Our analysis shows that illiberal regimes do not pri-
marily pursue efficiency; their objective is to erode impartiality,
pluralism, and constitutional fidelity.14 The typology developed
above helps identify specific vulnerabilities and tailor responses,
enabling targeted counter-­strategies that enhance administra-
tive resilience in alignment with the logic of the particular il-
liberal variant.
Under neoauthoritarian rule, the state is retooled through le-
galistic means to serve regime consolidation. The challenge is
not overt dismantling but ideological control via formal mech-
anisms. Resilience requires the constitutional entrenchment
of horizontal accountability, like independent audit offices,
administrative courts, and ombuds institutions, which must be
protected from executive override by giving them legal and fi-
nancial autonomy.
Under neopatrimonial rule, the state is colonized from within
and repurposed for personal rule. The key vulnerability is polit-
icized staffing. Resilience needs institutional insulation, includ-
ing nonpartisan appointment procedures, fixed-­term mandates,
and external vetting panels. Just as important is the cultivation
of professional identity anchored in legal norms and constitu-
tional ethics, not partisan loyalty.
Under neoreactionary rule, bureaucracy is discarded and re-
placed by executive-­controlled, private, or algorithmic struc-
tures. These moves are likely to be cloaked in discourses of
innovation and efficiency. Resilience here depends on civic
anchoring: embedding public administration within broader
societal alliances that affirm its public value. Universities, pro-
fessional associations, and civil society actors can help legit-
imize public service expertise and expose the anti-­democratic
implications of technocratic substitution.
Under neoclientelist rule, the state is not dismantled but hol-
lowed out via informal, militarized, or patronage-­based deliv-
ery systems. Resilience here requires reasserting administrative
centrality in public goods provision, ensuring that new deliv-
ery structures are subject to oversight, transparency, and pro-
fessional standards. Competitive recruitment, protected career
paths, and legally defined service mandates are essential to
shield civil servants from being absorbed into loyalty networks
(Table 2).
Across all illiberal variants, peer deliberation about how to
navigate morally complex situations should be institutional-
ized—not only to counteract hierarchical or clientelist pres-
sure, but to cultivate spaces of ethical reflection. These allow
civil servants to share burdens, clarify public values, and mo-
bilize collective resistance appropriate to their specific insti-
tutional context (Arendt  1995; Kucinskas  2025). Resilience
also depends on strengthening the legal consciousness of
public servants. Illiberal regimes often thrive on legal ambi-
guity, selective enforcement, and the erosion of constitutional
norms through formalistic compliance. Democratic reformers
must counter this with clarity, consistency, and a normative
commitment to legality (Resh  2019; Koliba  2025). To this
end, rights-­based reasoning should be embedded in ongoing

10   |   Conclusion
This article has shown that illiberal rule is not a monolithic
project, but a cluster of divergent strategies aimed at reshaping
the state and its civil service. The various illiberal approaches
carry distinct implications for the design, function, and legiti-
macy of public administration. Against the prevailing tendency
to collapse such developments under the broad label of author-
itarian populism, I introduced a typology distinguishing four
ideal types: neoauthoritarian, neopatrimonial, neoreactionary,
and neoclientelist rule. These variants diverge not only in their
vision of the state, but also in the strategies they deploy to assert
administrative control.
This typology underscores that administrative transformation is
not just a side effect of illiberal rule; it is a strategic cornerstone
to advance authoritarian dominance. Each variant challenges
foundational principles of liberal-­democratic administration—
legality, impartiality, pluralist accountability—but through
distinct logics. Disaggregating these trajectories is essential
for crafting appropriate strategies of resistance and resilience
(Ingber 2018).
I therefore proposed a differentiated reform agenda along the
lines of institutionalizing peer deliberation, strengthening
rights-­based legal reasoning, reinforcing ethical frameworks,
and building support coalitions within and beyond the state.
Crucially, these reforms must be matched to the specific pathol-
ogies posed by each illiberal variant. Where anarcho-­liberalist
populist regimes erode public institutions through market sub-
stitution, reform must emphasize professional ethos and civic
value. Where neopatrimonial rulers politicize and personalize
appointments, the priority must be legal insulation and proce-
dural integrity.
These findings also carry conceptual implications for public
administration theory. While Weberian ideals of neutrality and
hierarchy remain foundational, they are insufficient in contexts
of systemic democratic erosion. I thus advocate a reconceptual-
ization of civil servants as ethical agents tasked with preserving

ABSTRACT
Attracting talent to public administration is a highly relevant issue of practical concern and scholarly debate. Building on unique
survey data of 424 individuals who switched jobs to join public administration, this study explores public employer attractive-
ness in a multi-­incentive setting, accounting for the experiences of a less explored population (i.e., job switchers) that may have
different preferences regarding public sector employment compared with the more commonly investigated student samples.
Combining fit theory and the expectancy-­disconfirmation model, this study reveals that intrinsic attributes and person-­job fit
significantly predict job switchers' perceptions of public employer attractiveness. In contrast, extrinsic attributes—both instru-
mental and symbolic—are less influential factors. Moreover, public service-­related attributes have an unexpected negative effect
for non-­sector switchers, suggesting a “belated” reality shock effect. These novel insights suggest public management should
adopt more nuanced recruitment and retention strategies in the face of labor shortages and the war for talent.
1   |   Introduction
Human capital is a critical resource for all types of organizations,
with their success and performance often relying on their ability
to attract competent employees (MacRae and Furnham 2014).
With a significant proportion of the current workforce ap-
proaching retirement age and given that around 30% of public
employees are currently aged 55 or older (OECD 2021), public
organizations face steep labor market competition in recruit-
ing the best and the brightest (Alshathry et al. 2017; Jakobsen
et al. 2023). Consequently, understanding what specific factors
determine public employer attractiveness is crucial for design-
ing and implementing effective Human Resource Management
(HRM) strategies for public organizations worldwide.
Employer attractiveness emerges from the interplay of multiple
incentives, attitudes, and motives. Since public organizations
differ from private sector organizations in their core values
and goals, they attract talent that is particularly responsive to
public sector-­specific signals, such as individuals with high
public service motivation (PSM) (Asseburg et  al.  2020; Perry
and Wise 1990; Ritz et al. 2023). Building on this assumption,
a substantial body of research has examined differences in
candidates' and employees' motivational dispositions and at-
titudes across sectors (Borst et al. 2020; Buelens and van den
Broeck 2007; Cordes and Vogel 2023; Van de Walle et al. 2015;
Winter and Thaler 2016), yielding mixed results (Baarspul and
Wilderom  2011) with reference to differences in work values
and intrinsic versus extrinsic reward preferences, among other
factors.
Furthermore, the group of job switchers—that is, employ-
ees who have recently moved to public administration—has
received limited scholarly attention, with only a few notable

Nevertheless, investigating the reasons behind job switching is
particularly relevant for two key reasons. First, it sheds light on
the growing trend of job hopping, which is especially prevalent
among younger employees but remains underexplored in public
personnel management literature (Gallup 2016; Frederiksen and
Hansen 2017). This phenomenon is gaining even more promi-
nence due to the ongoing transformation of civil service from
a traditional career-­based system to a position-­based1 system.
This shift creates new opportunities for experienced employees
to enter public administration from both within and outside the
public sector (Ritz and Weißmüller 2025). Second, focusing on
more experienced job switchers may help overcome the predom-
inant reliance in previous research on studying the perceptions
of students and newcomers, whose views are often shaped by
limited experience and stereotypical understanding of work in
public administration. Prior studies on job switching often im-
plicitly assume that job switching behavior is primarily driven
by differences in work values (de Graaf and van der Wal 2008)
and motivations (Frederiksen and Hansen 2017; Hansen 2014).
Although relevant, this assumption does not fully account
for the intricacies of job switching behavior, particularly re-
garding individuals who self-­select into public administration
(Holt  2018; Woo and Kim  2024). While individuals with di-
verse prior employment experiences and from different sectors
may be motivated by similar public service-­related preferences
(Tschirhart et al. 2008; Vandenabeele and Jager 2020), a cru-
cial factor often overlooked in previous research is the role of
expectations. Specifically, different groups of job switchers are
likely to hold distinct expectations regarding public sector em-
ployment due to their prior work experiences (Bourmault and
Anteby 2020; Sutton and Griffin 2004) and sectoral differences
(Rainey and Bozeman 2000; Rainey 1983).
To address this research gap, this study focuses on two distinct
populations within the broader category of job switchers, each
with potentially differing expectations: sector switchers, that is,
individuals who move from a private job to the public sector,
and non-­sector switchers, that is, individuals who switch jobs
within the public sector itself. By examining how various job
attributes (extrinsic, intrinsic, and public service-­related) affect
the perceived employer attractiveness among these two groups,
the study answers the following research question: To what ex-
tent do sector switchers differ from non-­sector switchers regarding
their evaluation of public employer attractiveness based on vari-
ous job attributes?
Combining person-­environment fit and the expectancy-­
disconfirmation model, this study investigates these relation-
ships within a multi-­incentive setting, in which various types
of employer attributes simultaneously influence individual atti-
tudes and behaviors (Linos 2018; Ripoll et al. 2025). The study
draws on original survey data from post-­entry civil service job
switchers in Switzerland, which is an ideal setting for two rea-
sons. First, the country faces a significant public sector labor
shortage (PWC 2023), making employer attractiveness a press-
ing issue. Second, its position-­based personnel system facilitates
cross-­sector job moves, offering insights applicable to similar
systems elsewhere (Ritz and Weißmüller 2025).
The study enhances research on both public employer attrac-
tiveness and job switching. It does so by explicitly considering
how job switchers' expectations influence their interpretation of
employer signals concerning various incentives. Furthermore,
the study's findings contribute to a more nuanced understand-
ing of the attributes that drive public employer attractiveness for
job switchers, specifically highlighting the potential unintended
negative effects of public service-­related attributes and the sig-
nificant role of intrinsic attributes.
The remainder of the study is structured as follows. First, the
theoretical foundations are discussed. Then, the exploratory sur-
vey design and the empirical findings are presented. The study
concludes with a discussion of the findings, future research, and
implications for practice.
2   |   Theory
2.1   |   Combining Person-­Environment Fit
and the Expectancy Disconfirmation Model
Before examining how job attributes shape job switchers' per-
ceptions of their current public sector employer, it is neces-
sary to outline the two theoretical frameworks that underpin
this study—person-­environment fit (P-­E fit) theory and the
expectancy-­disconfirmation model (EDM)—and to explain the
theoretical advantages of combining these two perspectives.
2.1.1   |   Person-­Environment Fit Theory
P-­E fit refers to the alignment between an individual's char-
acteristics and the contextual environment in which s/he op-
erates (van Vianen 2018). In a recent effort to systematize the

Wightman (2021) proposed a typology that categorizes different
approaches to understanding which factors determine P-­E fit
and how HRM can leverage these factors to stimulate attraction,
retention, motivation, and performance. They identify three dis-
tinct approaches to analyzing fit. The first is the environmental
approach, which focuses on the different levels—that is, organi-
zation, job, group, and the dyadic relationship between super-
visor and employee—at which fit can be assessed. The second
approach is functional and concerns supplementary fit (e.g., the
match between individual and organizational values) and com-
plementary fit (e.g., candidate skills complementing the orga-
nizational demand for skills). The third approach is a temporal
perspective that emphasizes how the nature of fit evolves across
HRM processes such as attraction, selection, and attrition.
Following these three approaches, this study expands extant re-
search by contributing to the exploration of P-­E fit in a multifac-
eted and integrative way. First, concerning the environmental
approach, it integrates both person-­organization (P-­O) fit and
person-­job (P-­J) fit—which pertain to the compatibility between
a person and the organization or job, respectively (Asseburg
et  al.  2018; Kristof-­Brown et  al.  2005)—to better understand
their relative influence within the broader fit theory frame-
work (Pedersen  2013). In doing so, this study challenges the
assumption common in prior research linking PSM with P-­O
fit (Wightman and Christensen 2024), namely that employers'
sector affiliation serves as a proxy for signaling organizational
values that, subsequently, attract personnel with similar value
dispositions. Instead, it adopts an organization-­level perspective
(Kristof-­Brown et  al.  2023). This approach allows for a more
holistic empirical analysis of how sector-­specific, organization-­
specific, and job-­specific factors determine P-­E fit in concert.
This holistic perspective also allows for the inclusion of both
sector switching and non-­sector switching candidates, who may
change their employer based on specific signals that a particular
public administration organization emits (Bright 2007), rather
than the sector as a whole.
Second, regarding the functional perspective of fit, the study
adopts the less-­well-­explored complementary approach to both
P-­O and P-­J fit. Concerning P-­O fit, this contribution aligns
with Linos (2018) by adopting a need-­supply fit perspective that
suggests that individuals assess the degree to which attributes
offered by a public employer may help fulfill their needs and
personal preferences. Regarding P-­J fit, the study incorporates
an appraisal of how central complementary demands–abilities
fit—that is, the perceived congruence between the job demands
and the skills, knowledge, and ability of the individual (Kristof-­
Brown  1996)—was for the decision to switch jobs (Ritz and
Thom 2019).
Third, in terms of the temporal dimension, the study focuses on
the attraction process of the attraction-­selection-­attrition (ASA)
model (Schneider et al. 1995), with employer attractiveness as
the dependent variable. The ASA model is central to under-
standing talent attraction and selection behaviors (Ripoll and
Ritz 2021) and explains the appeal of an organization based on
various factors that signal workplace desirability (Ehrhart and
Ziegert 2005), including both instrumental (e.g., compensation)
and symbolic elements (e.g., prestige) (Highhouse et al. 2003).
While P-­E fit theory centers on job switchers' subjective per-
ception of the match or mismatch between their individual
characteristics and the employer's features—aligning with an
interactionist perspective on employer attractiveness (Keppeler
and Papenfuß 2023)—it does not illuminate the cognitive pro-
cesses of job applicants. Specifically, it does not explain how
and why job switchers process the same public employer signals
differently, a lens more consistent with the environmental pro-
cessing meta-­theoretical perspective of employer attractiveness
(Ehrhart and Ziegert 2005). Given job switchers' varied social-
ization in different employment sectors, this study posits that a
key factor influencing how different types of switchers interpret
the same signals is tied to their preformed expectations about
public sector jobs, stemming from their specific prior work ex-
periences. To model precisely the mechanisms through which
these expectations shape job switchers' individual perceptions,
this study integrates the theoretical lens of the expectancy-­
disconfirmation model with fit theories.
2.1.2   |   Expectancy-­Disconfirmation Model
Expectations refer to beliefs about future outcomes (Olson
et  al.  1996). They shape individuals' attitudes and behaviors
when making employment decisions (Atlas and Wager  2013)
and guide individuals' interpretation of employment conditions
under uncertainty. In public HRM, the role of expectations in
job choices remains largely unexplored, although expectations
are argued to be likely to influence applicants' assessment of po-
tential employers (Bell et al. 2006).
Sector switchers and non-­sector switchers are likely to differ
in their expectations for two main reasons. First, they have
been socialized in different work environments. As a result,
their prior work experiences may serve as benchmarks when
assessing the potential outcomes of their decision to switch
jobs. Second, candidates may hold different pre-­existing beliefs
about public sector work. Sector switchers may have more heu-
ristic and stereotypical views of public sector jobs, while those
with prior public sector experience may have more accurate
perceptions. Consistent with prior research indicating that id-
iosyncratic perceptions of job attributes serve as a proxy for ap-
plicants' expectations (Su and Bozeman 2009a), theory on the
attractiveness of public sector employment may benefit from in-
corporating the insights of expectancy theories, and specifically
of the EDM. While prior public management research used this
framework to examine citizen satisfaction with public services
(van Ryzin 2006), its application can be broadened to highlight
the role of expectations as they relate to individual satisfaction
and judgment (James  2009). Translated to the context of job
switching, job attribute signals may be interpreted differently,
particularly in environments offering diverse incentives, and
may thus influence job switchers' satisfaction with their deci-
sion to change jobs in dissimilar ways for sector switchers and
non-­sector switchers, given their varying expectations toward
their new public employer.
According to EDM literature, expectations may influence indi-
vidual satisfaction through both direct and indirect pathways
(Mok et al. 2017). Among the various mechanisms outlined by
the EDM, two pathways are particularly pertinent to this study:

Concerning the direct effect, recent research indicates a

sectors, may still be navigating the operational dynamics of their
new organization and the broader implications of their new role.
This uncertainty may amplify the significance of the direct ef-

employment attributes and their overall satisfaction with their
current employer.
Shifting to the disconfirmation pathway, the EDM suggests that
fulfilling or surpassing expectations leads to greater satisfac-
tion, whereas unmet expectations result in diminished satisfac-
tion (Zhang et al. 2022).

2.2   |   Modeling the Role of Expectations
for Job-­Switchers' Public Employer Attractiveness
In the context of job switching, the direct effect of expectations
proposed by the EDM is rooted in the distinct pre-­formed ex-
pectations about job attributes shaped by job switchers' diverse
backgrounds. It specifically concerns how each type of job
switcher interprets signals about a particular job attribute based
on previous experiences with their previous (public or private)
employer. Conversely, the (dis)confirmation effect reflects the
degree to which these pre-­existing expectations are potentially
met or unmet by the actual job experience with the new public
employer.
Building on this rationale, the following section will present
the study's hypotheses based on the anticipated differences in
both the direct and (dis)confirmation effects between sector
and non-­sector switchers for a set of different job attribute types
(Neumann and Ritz 2015): (1) extrinsic attributes (e.g., salary,
job security), (2) intrinsic attributes (e.g., challenging work con-
tent), and (3) public service-­related attributes (e.g., opportunities
to contribute to the common good).
Figure 1 illustrates the theoretical integration of P-­E fit theory
and the mechanisms through which expectations can shape job
switchers' satisfaction according to the EDM: the direct effect
of expectations on satisfaction (A); the associations between ex-
pectations vs. actual experiences and satisfaction through the
(dis)confirmation effect (B), which results from the interplay
between expectations and actual experience, or “performance”
in EDM terms (C), and the direct association between actual ex-
perience/performance and satisfaction (D). Figure 1 highlights
the direct effect (A) and the disconfirmation effect (B) in bold
font, as these pathways are the focus of this study and inform
its hypotheses.
2.2.1   |   Extrinsic Employment Attributes
Extrinsic employment attributes refer to material factors or
outcomes associated with work. These can be categorized into
instrumental (e.g., income, job security, benefits) and sym-
bolic attributes, such as career prospects and prestige (Lyons
et  al.  2006). While monetary rewards and compensation are
HRM practices more closely associated with private-­sector em-
ployment (Feeney and Rainey 2010; Kalleberg et al. 2006; Knies
and Leisink 2018; Ng and Johnson 2020), public organizations
typically offer substantial pension plans, additional family and

Sabharwal et  al.  2019). Moreover, job security is a hallmark
of public employment (Clark and Postel-­Vinay 2008; Hur and
Perry 2016; Munnell and Fraenkel 2013), particularly in coun-
tries with a career-­based public personnel system based on
the “Rechtsstaat” tradition of public administration2 (Ritz and
Weißmüller 2025; Ritz et al. 2023).
Given established sectoral differences, the direct effect of ex-
pectations on perceived employer attractiveness may differ
between sector switchers and non-­switchers. Sector switchers
may interpret signals about instrumental extrinsic attributes
such as job security and benefits more favorably due to higher
expectations shaped by prior private sector experiences. In
contrast, non-­sector switchers, who are already familiar
with such public sector attributes, tend to have more neutral,
experience-­based expectations. As a result, sector switchers
may perceive these signals as more attractive, leading to a
stronger link between these attributes and employer attrac-
tiveness (Su and Bozeman 2009c).
Regarding the (dis)confirmation effect, both groups are likely
to find their expectations met. Public employers typically pro-
vide more transparency on extrinsic benefits such as job security
during recruitment, and sector switchers may indeed experience
improvements over previous private roles. Non-­sector switchers,
drawing on their previous public sector experience, are likely to
hold realistic expectations that align with their experiences in
their new job. Thus, both direct and (dis)confirmation effects
suggest the following:
Hypothesis H1a.  Signals about instrumental extrinsic em-
ployment attributes (IEEA) are more strongly associated with
public employer attractiveness for sector switchers compared with
non-­sector switchers.
Symbolic extrinsic incentives—such as prestige and career ad-
vancement—present a more complex picture. Public employers
are often seen as offering fewer career development opportu-
nities than the private sector (Houston 2000; Knies et al. 2022;
Ng et al. 2016), and public sector work is frequently associated
with negative stereotypes such as inefficiency or lack of am-
bition (Baldwin 1990; Bertram et al. 2022; Dinhof et al. 2024;
Weißmüller and Vogel 2021). These perceptions can reduce the
attractiveness of public sector jobs (Fowler and Birdsall 2020).
Hence, expectations about symbolic rewards likely differ be-
tween groups. Sector switchers, comparing their current ex-
periences with private sector norms like attractive career
development opportunities based on historically emphasized
“hands-­on” HRM practices (Knies et al. 2022), may expect less
prestige and fewer career opportunities in public sector jobs. In
contrast, non-­sector switchers, who are already familiar with
the public sector context, may approach such employer signals
with more neutral expectations.
Regarding the (dis)confirmation effect, also as far as it applies
to symbolic extrinsic job attributes, both groups may find their
expectations confirmed after entering public sector roles. Sector
switchers may encounter limited career advancement due to
resource limitations and a less favorable organizational image
compared to their prior private sector experience, aligning with
their lowered expectations. Non-­sector switchers, perceiving lit-
tle change from previous public roles, are likely to have their
neutral expectations validated. Thus, based on both the direct
and the (dis)confirmation effects, the following hypothesis can
be proposed:
Hypothesis H1b.  Signals about symbolic extrinsic employ-
ment attributes (SEEA) are less strongly associated with public
employer attractiveness for sector switchers compared with non-­
sector switchers.
2.2.2   |   Intrinsic Employment Attributes
Intrinsic employment attributes refer to desirable aspects of
the job itself, such as intellectually stimulating tasks that align
with candidates' pursuit of personal growth, self-­actualization,
autonomy, and fulfillment (Neumann and Ritz 2015). Because
these attributes are tied to the specific content of the job, its rel-
evance in shaping employer attractiveness may be independent
from an individual's prior work experience or familiarity with
the public sector for at least two reasons. First, these signals are
likely to be interpreted as indicators of complementary P-­J fit
and, thus, are more strongly related to the job's specific content
than to a sector-­specific effect (Christensen and Wright 2011).
Second, intrinsic job attributes may highlight the opportunity to
fulfill basic psychological needs for competence and autonomy,
which are associated with more autonomous forms of motiva-
tion (Deci and Ryan 2000), and resonate universally across indi-
viduals, irrespective of their previous employment sector. Thus,
it follows that:
Hypothesis H2.  There is no statistically significant differ-
ence in the relationship between signals about intrinsic employ-
ment attributes (IEA) and public employer attractiveness between
sector switchers and non-­sector switchers.
2.2.3   |   Public Service-­Related Employment Attributes
Public service-­related attributes reflect altruistic qualities like
contributing to society and are central to public sector work and
motivation (Ritz et al. 2020; Vogel and Satzger 2024; Weißmüller
et al. 2022). These align with Public Service Motivation (PSM),
defined as a mix of normative, affective, and rational motives
driving efforts to benefit society at large and the public interest
(Schott and Ritz 2018, 30). Public administrations offer roles that
support these values, attracting individuals committed to social
equity and service (Frank and Lewis 2004; Perry and Wise 1990;
Ritz et al. 2023). Such individuals often self-­select into public sec-
tor roles or are, over time, shaped by sector-­specific socialization
effects that reinforce PSM (Asseburg et al. 2018; Seidemann and
Weißmüller 2024; Wright and Christensen 2010). Consequently,
job attributes that fulfill PSM-­related motives are especially ap-
pealing to those candidates with strong public service values
(Ripoll et al. 2025).

ers and non-­switchers perceive the importance of fulfilling

Moynihan and Pandey  2007; Nicholson-­Crotty et  al.  2022;
Pedersen 2015). By prompting participants to reflect on pub-

public service motives, this study activates this identity and
assesses its role in shaping attitudes toward a new public em-
ployer after switching jobs.
Returning to the distinctions between sector switchers and non-­
sector switchers, individuals may choose a public sector em-
ployer driven by their public service motives, due to self-­selection
(in the case of sector switchers) or through a combination of
self-­selection and prior socialization (for non-­sector switchers).
However, these two populations may interpret signals priming
their public service identity through different lenses, shaped by
their prior experiences and expectations. Specifically, regarding
the direct effect of expectations, sector switchers may respond
more positively to employer signals that emphasize public ser-
vice, given the public sector's strong association with public
service motives and values like care, dedication, and social re-
sponsibility (Carpenter et al. 2012; Vandenabeele 2008). Sector
switchers are likely to expect their new environment to offer
greater opportunities to fulfill their public service identity com-
pared with their previous private employers. In contrast, non-­
sector switchers—who are already familiar with public sector
norms—may have more neutral expectations and experience
hardly any difference between their new and previous public
employers in terms of public service fulfillment.
Regarding the (dis)confirmation effect, both groups may see
their expectations affirmed after entering into their new public
sector roles. Sector switchers may find stronger alignment be-
tween their public service values and a public sector environ-
ment, confirming their expectations. Non-­sector switchers, with
more grounded and neutral expectations based on prior experi-
ences, are likewise likely to have their expectations met. Thus,
both direct effects and (dis)confirmation effects suggest the fol-
lowing hypothesis:
Hypothesis H3.  Signals about public service employment at-
tributes (PSEA) have a stronger association with sector switchers'
than non-­sector switchers' evaluation of the employer attractive-
ness of public administration.
3   |   Method and Data
Hypotheses were tested by raising original survey data in 2021
with individuals who had recently changed jobs to take up
employment in the Swiss public administration, including both
sector switchers and non-­sector switchers to conduct compara-
tive research.
The survey comprised a topical introduction, several batteries of
job and context-­related variables to capture fit and expectation-­
related employment attributes, as well as a questionnaire about
socio-­economic characteristics to assess the representative-
ness of the sample for the general working-­age population in
Switzerland. Representative sampling also increases the external
validity of the findings. As previously noted, Switzerland stands
out as an ideal setting for research into job switching. This is at-
tributable to its competitive labor market conditions and highly
professionalized civil service (Ritz and Weißmüller 2025), in ad-
dition to a prominent position-­based public personnel system.
In contrast to a career-­based system, this type of personnel sys-
tem exhibits greater flexibility (Ritz et al. 2025) and facilitates
exchange and mobility with the private sector in recruitment
(Fraenkel-­Haeberle 2025), which is conducive to job switching
across sectors.
3.1   |   Sampling Strategy
Despite the relevance of the Swiss case, its cultural diversity and
complex administrative structure pose a significant challenge
for conducting full-­scale survey research because regional regu-
latory differences in employment law stemming from cantonal
autonomy may affect data consistency. To mitigate this issue,
the sampling strategy focuses on raising data in two purpose-
fully selected organizations: the Swiss federal administration
and the cantonal administration of one canton that serves as a
microcosm of the average population and typical labor market
conditions in Switzerland: Solothurn. According to census data
(BfS 2021a), this canton is characteristic of many of Switzerland's
typical work and life conditions, particularly regarding some of
its important population characteristics, such as distributions
of age, gender, migration, urbanization, and rate of occupation
(BfS 2021b); see Supporting Information: Appendix A for com-
parative statistics. Narrowing the empirical scope to only one
canton that is a characteristic average, instead of all 26 cantons,
increases the precision and quality of the data as this purposeful
selection facilitates targeted sampling.
Data were raised in collaboration with the relevant public
personnel offices (i.e., the Federal Office of Personnel Affairs
(EPA) and the Human Resources Department of the Canton
of Solothurn), which granted unique access to the full target
population of n = 1263 job switchers in 2020. This population
includes all individuals who joined the public administration
workforce on the federal level and on the cantonal level in the
canton of Solothurn between 1 January and 31 December 2020
on a permanent contract. To maximize the response rate, the
online survey was distributed by sending individually person-
alized email invitations to all n = 1263 individuals.3 The survey
invitation was sent on 29 July 2021 with a reminder sent after
1 week to increase the response rate before the survey closed
on 12 August 2021. It was available in German and French, the
two most widely used administrative languages in Switzerland,
to motivate survey participation, which was strictly voluntary,
anonymous, and non-­incentivized.

3.2   |   Sample
Of the full population of n = 1263 that we contacted, 559 in-
dividuals started the survey and n = 495 completed at least
substantial parts of it (i.e., the dependent and independent
variables), which means that a response rate of 39.2% of the
target population was achieved. However, several partici-
pants were screened out for analytical rigor and to warrant
data quality. First, respondents who took longer than 60 min
to answer the survey were excluded (n = 30). Second, partici-
pants who opted out of providing the socio-­demographic in-
formation that was used to assess sample characteristics (such
as age, gender, or the level of government they work in) were
also excluded (additional 9 cases). Of this remaining sample
of n = 456, 59.9% (n = 273) of respondents had worked in the
private sector, 33.1% (n = 151) used to have other public sector
jobs, and 7.0% (n = 32) had worked in the nonprofit sector be-
fore switching jobs to join public administration. Given that
nonprofit work often entails value and goal hybridity (Lee and
Wilkins 2011; Ritz et al. 2023), these 32 individuals were pur-
posefully excluded from the analyses to strengthen the ana-
lytical precision by focusing specifically on private-­to-­public
sector switching.
This ultimately results in an analytical sample of n = 424 in-
dividuals who switched into public administration employ-
ment in 2020, either from the private or from the public sector.
Descriptive sample statistics are presented in Table  1. On av-
erage, these respondents took M = 16.2 (SD = 9.6) minutes to
complete the survey. Three hundred thirty-­six (79.3%) partici-
pants responded in German and n = 88 (20.7%) used French.
The analytical sample comprises n = 273 (64.4%) sector switch-
ers and n = 151 (35.6%) non-­sector switchers. Overall, 53.3% of
the sample identify as female; they are, on average, 39.6 years
old (SD = 9.8), 52.2% are married or in a long-­term relationship,
46.3% have childcare responsibilities, and are highly educated
with 56% holding a tertiary education degree. 13.0% have a lead-
ership position.
Compared with the Swiss federal public administration work-
force in 2021 (EPA 2022), the sample is on average younger
(39.6 vs. 45.7 years old); it comprises more women (53.0% vs.
44.4%) and more native German speakers (75.5% vs. 70.1%).
Given the Swiss Federal Personnel Office's strategic agenda
to increase the quota of women in office and attract younger
talent into public service (EPA 2022), the sampled data is par-
ticularly valuable because it is representative of the strategic
target population.
3.3   |   Operationalization
Given that there is no all-­encompassing battery of pre-­validated
scale items that captures the topical variety associated with
extrinsic, intrinsic, and public service-­related employment at-
tributes and employer satisfaction, a comprehensive set of sur-
vey items was developed based on prior research (Lievens and
Highhouse  2003; Perry  1996; Ritz and Waldner  2011; Su and
Bozeman  2009b). Regarding public service employment attri-
butes (PSEA), it is important to note that the employed measure
does not directly focus on the inherent pro-­social nature of the
job itself. Instead, it captures how respondents perceive specific
employment attributes as cues that activate their public service
identity.
Responses were obtained on a seven-­point Likert-­type scale
(min. = 1; max. = 7). Following best practices, exploratory
(EFA) and confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were em-
ployed to identify the proper factor structure per construct
deductively using reflective indicators (Jarvis et  al.  2003).
The measurement items of the dependent variable are pre-
sented in Table  2. To warrant high reliability and external
validity, these items were translated to fit the Swiss employ-
ment context and pretested for plausibility and completeness
with practitioners prior to fielding. Construct convergence
and distinctiveness were confirmed by conducting CFA and
SEM trait modeling for the scale measures (Hinkin  1998;
Podsakoff et al. 2003). The results are presented in Table 2,
reporting each item's factor loading for each of the four factors
(FLn), item uniqueness, and the Kaiser-­Meyer-­Olkin criterion
(KMO). The factor loadings indicate high data adequacy and
construct validity for each variable (see Table 2). The clusters
of the items' factor loadings show distinct patterns and indi-
cate that each factor—that is, each independent variable—is
a highly distinct construct. Furthermore, this procedure re-
veals that latent, unobserved variables are not an issue. This
procedure follows best practices by Williams et al. (2010) and
Podsakoff et al. (2003), and suggests high construct distinc-
tiveness, high scale reliability, and internal consistency (see
Supporting Information: Appendix B for more detail).
3.4   |   Dependent Variable: Employer Attractiveness
Employer attractiveness was measured using a three-­item short
version of the validated scale by Highhouse et al. (2003), with
slight adaptations to account for the fact that respondents had
already decided to join their public employer. This adapted mea-
sure serves as an indicator of satisfaction with participants' job
switch, as they were asked to assess the attractiveness of their
current employer compared with their expectations at the time
of the job switch and to reaffirm their decision to join the public
organization.
Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) reveals a solid single-­factor
scale (χ2 = 573.23, p < 0.000) with high reliability (Cronbach's
α = 0.855; average interitem correlation = 0.947). For rigor,
Table 3 provides further details on the EFA (i.e., each item's ro-
tated factor loadings, the KMO, uniqueness (U), inter-­item cor-
relations, and their item-­rest correlation (IRC)). The EFA reveals
that all three items are strongly and significantly correlated
with each other, display high factor loadings (all FL ≥ 0.748,
threshold: ≥ 0.30) and low uniqueness, and high communality
(IRC ≥ 0.693), all of which indicate high construct validity and
reliability.

3.6   |   Control Variables
Perceived employer attractiveness may vary with socio-­
demographic candidate characteristics but also due to circum-
stantial factors. The data collection period coincided with the
atypical labor market conditions of the Covid-­19 pandemic
in 2021. Consequently, to capture these effects, the survey
included a seven-­point item measuring the degree to which
the pandemic influenced respondents' switch into public ad-
ministration. Furthermore, a control for the level of public
administration job selected was also incorporated (binary
item; 1 = federal administration; 0 = cantonal administra-
tion). Moreover, two original Likert-­type scale measures—
person-­job fit (PJF; two-­item measure; χ2 = 369.91; p < 0.001;
Cronbach's α = 0.828) and push factors (PF; three-­item mea-
sure; χ2 = 213.30; p < 0.000; Cronbach's α = 0.660)—were
developed to clearly disentangle the effect of employment
attributes vis-­à-­vis these individual idiosyncratic factors (see
Supporting Information: Appendix B.5 for detail).

4.2   |   Hypotheses Testing
Hypotheses were tested by estimating seven linear (OLS) regres-
sion models on employer attractiveness (Table 5). Models I to
V rely on the pooled data. Model I is the direct-­effect baseline
model, and models II to V add interaction terms between the
employment attributes variables and a binary categorical indi-
cator for sector switching (0 = non-­sector switchers, 1 = sector
switchers). To test whether the revealed effects are robust and
meaningful despite the heterogeneity of the two subsamples,
additional split-­sample regression analyses were conducted
(models VI and VII). All estimated models are well-­specified
(F = 2.46–6.87, p < 0.010) and explain a substantial amount of
variance (adj. R2 = 0.095–0.204). Low variance inflation (mean
VIF = 1.44–1.66) indicates that multicollinearity was not an
issue despite the partial correlational association of some
variables.
Cross-­model hypotheses testing was conducted with the gen-
eralized Hausman specification test to compare the results of
models VI and VII for statistical significance. The coefficient
plot (Figure 2) further illustrates the results.
Theory suggested that instrumental extrinsic employment attri-
butes (IEEA) should have a stronger impact on public employer
attractiveness for sector switchers compared with non-­sector
switchers (H1a). This hypothesis finds conditional support.
Regression analysis reveals that IEEA is indeed predictive
for employer attractiveness for sector switchers (bVI = 0.144,
p = 0.037)—but not for non-­sector switchers (bVII = 0.033,
p = 0.801)—however, this is only revealed by split-­sample anal-
yses, which indicate that in order to reliably predict employer
attractiveness through IEEA signals, between-­sample hetero-
geneity (non-­significant Hausman test) must be considered.
The regression coefficient plot (Figure  2) further illustrates
the IEEA effect, which is a significant predictor only for sector
switchers.
Hypothesis H1b suggested that symbolic extrinsic employment
attributes (SEEA) would affect sector switchers' perception of
public employer attractiveness less strongly compared with non-­
sector switchers. H1b has to be rejected because although SEEA
significantly predicts sector switchers' (bVI = −0.189, p = 0.044)
perception of employment attractiveness but not non-­sector
switchers' (bVII = −0.033, p = 0.811), the data reveal a negative re-
lationship between SEEA and EA for sector switchers, whereas a
positive relationship was hypothesized based on prior research.
Regarding intrinsic employment attributes' (IEA) impact on
public employer attractiveness, research suggested that there
should be no statistically significant differences between sector
switchers and non-­sector switchers (H2). This hypothesis finds
support. There is no statistically significant interaction effect
(bIV = 0.009, p = 0.927) nor a statistically significant difference
regarding the direction of effect size of the estimated regression
coefficient in the split-­sample models. IEA is strongly and pos-
itively associated with public employer attractiveness for both
sector switchers (bVI = 0.359, p < 0.000) and non-­sector switchers
(bVII = 0.420, p < 0.001).

switchers (H3). While there is no meaningful direct effect of

5   |   Discussion and Implications
This study provides novel insights on public administrations'
employer attractiveness in multi-­incentive settings. Grounded
in the theoretical argument of how sector switchers and non-­
sector switchers interpret employer signals in light of their dif-
ferent prior expectations (direct effect) and how these interact
with their actual experience (disconfirmation effect), this study
yields four pivotal findings that advance theory.
First, signals about intrinsic employment attributes exhibit a
higher positive association with employer attractiveness for
both types of job switchers compared with other signals. Second,
job switchers from the private sector associate instrumental
and symbolic extrinsic employment attributes differently with
employer attractiveness (a positive and statistically significant
link), compared with candidates who already work in the public
sector (no statistically significant association). Third, the study
provides empirical support for a complementary approach to
person-­organization (P-­O) and person-­job (P-­J) fit. Fourth, the
association between public service-­related employment attri-
butes and employer attractiveness differs significantly between
sector switchers and new administrative personnel with prior
public sector work experience.
This study supports prior research accentuating the pivotal role
of intrinsic employment attributes for steering public sector at-
tractiveness (Asseburg et al. 2020; Linos 2018). By empirically
disentangling intrinsic from public service-­related employment
attributes, the study provides important novel evidence con-
firming the need to treat these two types of employment attri-
butes separately. This is a significant contribution given that
prior research tended to conflate the two, blurring intrinsic
with PSM motives (Van de Walle et al. 2015). The study's novel
empirical evidence suggests signaling strategies built upon in-
trinsic and public service-­related employment attributes may
lead to fundamentally different outcomes regarding candidates'
perception of employer attractiveness, particularly when aim-
ing to attract talent from outside the public sector. Based on
these results, relying mostly on intrinsic employment attributes
is the “safe choice” for HR practitioners seeking to attract talent
in general.
Additionally, this study advances the research by providing a
more nuanced perspective on the impact of extrinsic employer
attributes for between and within-­sector switchers. By consid-
ering that these two groups differ regarding their expectations,
this contribution highlights how prior work experiences affect
subsequent perceptions of employer attractiveness. In contrast
to prior research (Borst et al. 2020), its findings underscore that
instrumental extrinsic attributes—especially welfare state-­
oriented attributes as used in our study—are a more effective
tool for attracting sector switchers into public administration,
while they appear irrelevant when competing for job switchers
within public organizations.
Furthermore, symbolic extrinsic employment attributes may
act as a “double-­edged sword.” Their negative association with
employer attractiveness for sector switchers suggests that they
may backfire if highlighted in job advertisements, potentially
deterring candidates from the private sector while having no
impact on those already employed in other public sector organi-
zations. These findings reinforce the study's central theoretical
argument: the necessity of distinguishing between instrumental
and symbolic attributes, such as prestige, when seeking to un-
derstand the phenomenon of sector switching. This is because
sector switchers may internalize negative and stereotypical
views regarding the prestige of public sector roles compared
to their private sector roles (Fischer and Weißmüller  2024;
Pepermans and Peiffer 2024; Weißmüller 2022). Consequently,
these ingrained negative stereotypes shape how applicants with
prior private sector experience interpret signals about symbolic
extrinsic attributes from public employers, potentially leading
them to perceive these signals as unrealistic. However, besides
stereotypical views, genuine disparities rooted in the well-­
documented structural differences between these two sectors
exist and also shape expectations. Consequently, future research
is encouraged to build upon the insights of this study by disen-
tangling anti-­public sector bias from expectations grounded in
actual differences.
Moreover, this study addresses the role of intrinsic employment
attributes of P-­O fit while controlling P-­J fit. Although these two
variables may be conceptually related, their distinct and empir-
ically robust measurement reveals their idiosyncratic impact
on public employer attractiveness, and how they affect sector-­
switchers' vs. non-­sector switchers' perceptions differently. This
is a significant empirical insight and conceptual advancement
showing that the signaling effect of P-­O fit in the sense of intrinsic
employment attributes—related to specific needs such as auton-
omy—functions through a different cognitive pathway compared
with the more transactional, demand-­ability-­related P-­J fit con-
struct. Rather than being substitutes, these two fit perspectives
are complementary, as shown by the empirical evidence, and
can be used to design distinct recruitment messaging strategies
to more effectively attract both sector-­switchers and non-­sector
switchers alike. Furthermore, in line with suggestions by the
general management literature (Kim et al. 2020), future research
is encouraged to explore the distinction between complementary
demands-­ability and the needs-­supply characteristics embedded
in person-­job fit, with the latter relating to prosocial and public
service-­oriented attributes of the job. The current study is un-
able to disentangle these elements due to limited information
about the specific job types. However, its empirical insights open

Additionally, this study contributes to the discourse on the re-
lationship between employer attractiveness and public service-­
related employment attributes as a driving factor for sector
choice, specifically regarding the effects of PSM activation
(Pedersen  2015) and priming (Christensen and Wright  2018).
Its empirical evidence further substantiates PSM research in-
sights that suggest a positive association between PSM, public
service-­related attributes, and public employer attractiveness
(Ritz et  al.  2023, 2016). Interestingly, this positive correlation
only holds true empirically for individuals transitioning into
the public sector, not for those already familiar with it. For
the latter group, we observe a negative correlation, albeit only
a marginally significant one. This finding diverges from previ-
ous research on job switching, which suggests the importance
of public service-­related attributes for all types of job switchers
(Hansen 2014; Tschirhart et al. 2008). There are two potential
explanations for this result.

First, priming PSM for individuals with prior experience in the
public sector may actually diminish their inclination to perceive
a new public organization as a particularly attractive employer
because staying within their current employer provides a higher
chance to fulfill their public service-­related values due to pre-­
established socialization processes (Breaugh et al. 2022; Chen
et al. 2024). Second, the differences between sector switchers
and non-­sector switchers may be a matter of unmet expectations.
Contrary to one assumption of the present study, non-­sector
switchers may interpret signals from their public employer prim-
ing their PSM through the lens of high expectations rather than
neutral ones. The current study evaluates employees' perception
of employer attractiveness after having switched jobs into pub-
lic administration. Candidates may perceive the PSM-­related
signals as opportunities to switch to a job in another public or-
ganization that—through its unique characteristics in terms of
mission and public service values (Bright  2007)—may better
fulfill their public service identity. However, once embedded in
the reality of their new employer, these expectations may not
align with their actual experiences, negatively impacting their
retrospective perception of employer attractiveness. In other
words, a “belated” reality shock effect (Dean et al. 1984) may
have set in. This potential belated reality shock effect linked to
unmet expectations also offers insights into the level at which
the relationship between PSM and attitudes toward public sector
employment functions. More precisely, PSM socialization may
operate at a broader macro (sector) or narrower micro (job) level
rather than at the meso (organizational) level. As a result, the
PSM-­related expectations of individuals switching employers
within the same sector cannot be fully met since the new orga-
nizational environment may not foster their PSM in a way that
differs from their previous (public) employer. Future research is
encouraged to assess the applicability of the hypothesized “be-
lated” reality shock effect and explore it in greater depth. This
may involve testing these alternative explanations by, for exam-
ple, using a longitudinal approach and combining both quanti-
tative and qualitative methods.
Lastly, while primarily focused on public employer attractive-
ness among job switchers, this study also offers potential con-
tributions to the academic discourse on public sector retention.
Specifically, the analysis might also indirectly suggest the im-
portance of managing the differentiated reward expectations of
job switchers even after they join the public organization, poten-
tially enriching recent systematizations about turnover inten-
tion predictors (Hur and Abner 2024). Indeed, the psychological
contract between the public employer and distinct types of job
switchers may be characterized variously characterized because
of these pre-­existing differences in work experience and, conse-
quently, expectations.
Besides these important scholarly contributions, the study's find-
ings provide valuable insights for public sector HRM managers
and policymakers. Rather than adopting a “one-­size-­fits-­all” ap-
proach in the competitive talent landscape, public organizations
should target their recruitment efforts strategically to account
for the characteristics and (un)met expectations of their diverse
target populations, especially considering the higher propen-
sity of younger employees to switch organizations (AbouAssi
et al. 2021). For instance, when seeking to attract experienced
candidates with highly sought-­after skills from private or
non-­profit sectors, organizations may find higher success by em-
phasizing specific employment attributes, be they instrumental
extrinsic, intrinsic, public service-­related, or associated with
person-­job fit.
Moreover, they should be cautious when crafting HR strategies
to attract employees by signaling public-­service related employ-
ment attributes since non-­sector switchers, in particular, may
have specific expectations in this regard, which may be more
challenging to meet. In this vein, leveraging relational job de-
sign (Grant  2007) and providing ethical or transformational
leadership training (Jensen and Bro 2018) to better align the re-
ality of the work environment with expectations is crucial.
Public HR managers should also exercise caution when using
symbolic extrinsic attributes in their signaling, as these may at
best be ineffective for non-­sector switchers and, at worst, could
potentially discourage sector switchers. Consequently, they
should prioritize providing “realistic cues of fit” (Linos 2018, 68)
that align with what applicants find in their new public job. This
has potentially significant implications for developing success-
ful internal branding strategies (Leijerholt et al. 2022). Indeed,
by first acknowledging the expectations of different types of
employees reflecting their previous work and, subsequently
and subsequently—more importantly—by managing these ex-
pectations, avoiding any discrepancies between the communi-
cated employer image and the actual organizational practices
employed, public organizations could succeed in both attracting
and retaining talent.
6   |   Limitations and Conclusion
Like any empirical inquiry, this study acknowledges its limita-
tions. First, it relies on data from a single-­wave survey. Although
analyses revealed no discernible issues concerning construct
validity, future studies considering replicating this research are
encouraged to employ a two-­wave survey design to further re-
duce the chance of common source bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003).
Second, novel scale measures were developed and pre-­tested
due to the absence of pre-­validated scales for employment attri-
butes for the specific country context. While this is an important
methodological contribution in itself given that exploratory and
confirmatory factor analyses suggested that the measures per-
formed well in terms of both construct and discriminant valid-
ity, future replication studies are encouraged to test these scales'
reliability.
A third limitation lies in the measure used for public service-­
related attributes. While this approach is consistent with previ-
ous research on PSM priming, future studies could replicate this
analysis by explicitly focusing on pro-­societal job characteristics.
Fourth, this study is an initial effort to examine how expectations
influence public employer attractiveness. Although it aligns
with the expectancy-­disconfirmation model, it lacks direct mea-
sures of actual job performance or disconfirmation. As a result,
hypothesized differences between sector and non-­sector switch-
ers were only indirectly supported. Future research should ex-
tend the application of this model in public HRM, using clearer

measures to also assess the impact of exceeded—not just met or
unmet—expectations.
Furthermore, expectations of sector and non-­sector switchers may
converge over time due to continuous socialization within their
organizations. Future research could use longitudinal methods
to track how these evolving expectations affect employer attrac-
tiveness across time and tenure levels. Although respondents were
asked to recall perceptions from the time of switching, current
views may have influenced their answers. Multi-­wave surveys
could better capture changes in expectations.
Additionally, while the inclusion of a control for COVID-­19 sug-
gests the absence of a direct effect on job switchers' employer
attractiveness, the broader implications of this contextual factor
on employment decisions cannot be dismissed entirely. However,
given that its impact on the Swiss labor market was less pro-
nounced compared to other countries (Armingeon et al. 2021), this
contextual factor is unlikely to limit the validity of the findings.
Lastly, since one dataset comes from a single Swiss canton
and overrepresents the German-­speaking population, cultural
generalizability may be limited. However, because perceptions
of employer attractiveness are shaped by expectations tied to
sector-­specific socialization and structural differences consis-
tent across regions, the findings should remain valid across cul-
tural subgroups within each switcher group.
Despite these limitations, the study sheds light on key factors
influencing job switchers' perceptions of public employer at-
tractiveness. It underscores the importance of distinguishing
between switcher groups and suggests that sector-­specific ex-
periences shape expectations in meaningful ways. The study
also shows the value of integrating P-­E fit with the expectancy-­
disconfirmation model to better understand switching behavior.
In conclusion, the findings highlight the varying importance of
different employment attributes and reveal new, unexpected in-
sights into the appeal of intrinsic and public service–related fac-
tors for public employer attractiveness. This evidence not only
invites further scholarly inquiry but also offers practical guid-
ance for enhancing public sector recruitment strategies in times
of labor shortage.

Business, Economics and Social Sciences at the University of Bern and
is the Managing Director of the KPM Center for Public Management,
University of Bern (Switzerland). He researches and teaches, particularly,
on public service motivation, employer attractiveness, public sector lead-
ership, and the performance of individuals, teams, and organizations.

Abstract
Public officials are traditionally thought to be more risk-averse than private sector employees, yet consistent empirical evidence
is lacking. Prospect theory posits that individuals’ risk decision preferences are influenced by the framing of gains or losses, but
it does not distinguish whether gains and losses pertain to personal or public interests. This study incorporates the type of in­
terest with gain–loss framing to construct decision scenarios and compare risk preferences between public officials and private
sector employees. In Experiment 1 (Npublic officials = 897, Nprivate sector employees = 685), using a 3 (public interest vs. public–private mixed
interest vs. private interest) × 2 (gain frame vs. loss frame) design, public officials were found to exhibit greater risk aversion
than private sector employees when the loss frame involves the public interest, while no significant differences were observed
for a gain frame involving either the public interest or private interest. Applying blame avoidance theory, accountability is posi­
tioned here as a core factor to further explore why public officials are more risk-averse than private sector employees when fac­
ing public interest losses. In Experiment 2 (Npublic officials = 625, Nprivate sector employees = 630), using a 2 (reward vs. punishment-oriented
accountability) × 2 (process vs. outcome-oriented accountability) design in a public interest decision-making context, public
officials were found to be more risk-averse than private sector employees in the case of outcome-oriented punishment account­
ability. In the other three types of accountability conditions, public officials exhibited a similar risk preference to private sector
employees. These findings reveal the situational factors underlying public officials’ risk aversion, and offer practical insights for
designing accountability systems that effectively guide decision-making in the public sector.
Key words prospect theory, accountability, blame avoidance, risk decision, public–private differences

Introduction
Public officials are traditionally considered to be more risk-averse
than private sector employees in decision-making. This has
been explained by pointing out that risk-averse individuals
tend to self-select into public sector employment when choos­
ing jobs (Bellante and Link 1981; Chang 2024; Dong 2017), and
are subsequently socialized by their public sector training into

professional norms that make them even more risk averse in
their career. However, empirical studies on this issue have yield­
ed mixed conclusions. Some studies support the idea that pub­
lic officials are more risk-averse than private sector employees
(e.g., Buurman et al. 2012; Chang 2024), whereas others find no
significant difference, or even suggest that public officials may
be more risk-seeking than private sector employees in certain
decision-making contexts (Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty,
and Webeck 2019). These inconsistent findings may be due to the
different decision-making scenarios used in prior studies, sug­
gesting that there is a need to investigate the specific contexts in
which public officials’ risk aversion occurs and the underlying fac­
tors that drive it.
Prospect theory posits that individuals tend to avoid risks when
a choice is stated in terms of gains but will take risks when the same
choice is stated in terms of losses (Tversky and Kahneman 1981).
This gain–loss framing effect is associated with loss aversion,
whereby individuals assign greater psychological weight to losses
than to equivalent gains. This gain–loss frame bias, as a cognitive
and decision-making heuristic not only affects ordinary citizens
but is also known to influence the judgment and decision-making
of public officials as well (Bellé, Cantarelli, and Belardinelli 2018;
Fuenzalida, Van Ryzin, and Olsen 2021; Liu, Qin, and Zhang 2023).
Therefore, this study distinguishes decision-making contexts in
terms of the gain and loss frame to compare risk preferences be­
tween public officials and private sector employees.
In addition, regarding loss aversion in prospect theory, the is­
sue of “who experiences the loss” is also crucial. Unlike private
sector employees, who are accountable for their own personal
interests and organizational profits, public officials must not only
consider their personal interests but also serve the public interest.
Moreover, public officials have been shown to exhibit different risk
preferences when making decisions related to the public interest
versus private interests (Zhang et al. 2025). Furthermore, public of­
ficials are more likely to emphasize the avoidance of public inter­
est losses than actively pursue public interest gains (Weaver 1986).
Therefore, based on the gain–loss frame, this study further distin­
guishes between decision-making contexts that involve public and
private interests. In Experiment 1, the risk preference differences
between public and private sector employees are examined when
they are confronted with gain or loss frames in public interest and
private interest contexts. The results show that public officials are
significantly more risk-averse than private sector employees in the
context of public interest losses. However, in the contexts of pub­
lic interest gains and private interests, no significant differences in
risk preferences are observed between the two groups.
Blame avoidance provides a perspective for understanding
the findings of Experiment 1. Bureaucratic accountability means
that public officials must be answerable for their actions and de­
cisions. However, accountability and blame avoidance are two
sides of the same coin (Tu 2024). This implies that, in some con­
texts, accountability pressures can activate blame-avoidance
motives. This study focuses on two dimensions of accounta­
bility: the form of accountability (reward/punishment) and the
focus of accountability (process/outcome). Together, these di­
mensions constitute the key pathways through which account­
ability mechanisms influence behavior. Experiment 2 was, thus,
designed to investigate how reward and punishment-based ac­
countability, as well as process and outcome-oriented accounta­
bility, shape the differences in public officials’ and private sector
employees’ risk preference when facing public interest. The re­
sults reveal that public officials are significantly more risk-averse
than private sector employees, but only when outcome-oriented
accountability is combined with punishment-based accounta­
bility. These findings indicate that specific accountability struc­
tures shape public officials’ risk aversion in the context of public
interest loss.
In summary, motivated by the debate over whether public of­
ficials are more risk-averse than private-sector employees, this
study seeks to identify the specific decision contexts in which pub­
lic officials are more risk-averse than private sector employees and
to explore the potential factors driving it. The results show that
public officials are more risk-averse than private sector employ­
ees in decision contexts involving public-interest losses. Further
analysis indicates that outcome-oriented punishment accounta­
bility is an institutional driver for public officials’ greater risk aver­
sion in public-interest decision making. This study makes several
theoretical contributions. First, building on prospect theory’s in­
sight into gain–loss framing effects on risky decision-making, this
study incorporates decision interest type to test this theory’s ap­
plicability to public officials across decisions involving different
interests, and finds that public officials are particularly sensitive
to public-interest losses. Second, by integrating decision interest
type with gain–loss framing, we identify the specific decision con­
texts in which risk aversion emerges among public officials, provid­
ing a contextualized explanation for the debate on the differences
in risk aversion between public officials and private sector em­
ployees. Finally, we draw on blame avoidance theory to explain
public officials’ risk aversion in contexts involving public-interest
losses and find that specific accountability structures significantly
drive such risk aversion. Simultaneously, by incorporating blame
avoidance theory, we reveal public officials’ risk aversion under
an outcome-oriented punitive accountability, which supplements
the occurrence mechanism of loss aversion—prospect theory’s
core proposition—in the public sector.
Literature review and theoretical
hypotheses
Differences in the risk preferences
of public officials and private sector
employees under different decision-
making contexts
Risk aversion generally refers to a decision maker’s tendency to
reduce uncertainty in the decision-making process. In this study,
risk aversion is defined as the preference for assured outcomes
over risky options with the same expected value. A classic exam­
ple is the “Asian Disease Problem” (Tversky and Kahneman 1981),
in which participants choose between a certain option (“200 peo­
ple will be saved”) and a risky option (“a 1/3 probability that all
600 will be saved, and a 2/3 probability that none will be saved”).
The expectation values of both options are the same (i.e., saving
200 people). An individual’s choice of the certain option indi­
cates risk aversion, whereas choosing the risky option indicates
risk-seeking.

Although public officials are traditionally assumed to be more
risk-averse than private sector employees, empirical evidence
remains mixed. For instance, in behavioral experiments by
Buurman (2012), public sector employees exhibited greater risk
aversion than private sector workers. Similarly, Chang (2024) ana­
lyzed South Korean employees’ portfolio selection behaviors and
found that public sector employees are more risk-averse than
their private sector counterparts. By contrast, in a study using the
Asian Disease Problem, Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty, and
Webeck (2019) found that public managers were more likely than
private sector counterparts to choose the risky option under a loss
frame. These mixed results suggest that the traditional view that
public officials are more risk-averse than private-sector employ­
ees may hold only in specific contexts.
Gain and loss frame of the decision-making context.
Prospect theory posits that individuals’ risk preferences are in­
fluenced by the framing of outcomes, in that there is a tenden­
cy to avoid risk in gain frames and a tendency to seek risk in loss
frames (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). Prospect theory’s predic­
tions about how gain–loss framing shapes risk decision-making
have been widely applied across multiple fields. However, as re­
search has progressed, scholars have begun to argue that framing
effects are not universal cognitive biases but are instead complex
phenomena shaped by factors such as task domain, social con­
text, and decision-making goal (Ding, Li, and Qiao 2025; Wang
1996). Prospect theory was originally developed in individualized,
self-interest oriented experimental settings, and thus, its applica­
bility may be limited when applied to contexts in which decisions
involve collective interests and complex policy considerations.
For example, Baekgaard (2017) tested the applicability of pros­
pect theory in a public sector context and found that citizens were
more likely to support high-risk reforms under the gain frame
than under the loss frame. He concluded that the particularities
of the public sector should be taken into account when applying
insights from prospect theory to this sector. Public officials are
subject to strict oversight and accountability, and thus, the ques­
tion of whether their risk preferences align with the predictions of
prospect theory warrants further exploration.
Public and private interests in the decision-making
context.
Private interests and public interests are two typical types of
decision-making interests (Lewin 1991). The public sector relies on
oversight and accountability mechanisms to ensure that public of­
ficials are committed to serving the public interest (Bovens 2007).
By contrast, private-sector employees’ decisions are typically ori­
ented toward the pursuit of private interests. In decision-making
contexts involving the public interest, public officials are sensitive
to the loss of public interest. Actions that harm the public interest
can trigger punitive measures from the accountability system (Yao
and Qian 2025), which may result in disproportionate reputation­
al and career-related penalties, thereby undermining officials’
prospects for re-election or promotion (Weaver 1986). To avoid
potential blame, public-sector employees tend to adopt more
conservative and risk-averse decision strategies (Qin 2022; Zhang
et al. 2025). Private sector employees are not subject to public re­
sponsibility and accountability pressures, and thus, they may be
more likely to exhibit the risk-seeking tendency predicted by pros­
pect theory. However, in contexts involving public interest gains,
public officials are driven by credit-claiming motives (Leong and
Howlett 2017), and thus, they may demonstrate risk preferences
similar to those of private sector actors.
In decision-making contexts involving private interests, public
officials are not subjected to accountability pressure directly tied
to public responsibility. Self-interested motives are characterized
by automatic activation (Moore and Loewenstein 2004). Zamir and
Sulitzeanu-Kenan (2018) further note that even public-spirited
officials may be automatically and subconsciously influenced by
self-interest. Therefore, in contexts involving private interests, their
risk preferences should be similar to those of private sector employ­
ees and likewise follow the risk decision-making pattern predicted
by prospect theory. The following hypotheses are, thus, proposed:
Hypothesis 1(a): In public interest contexts, under the loss
frame, public officials are more risk-averse than private sector
employees, whereas no significant difference in risk prefer­
ence exists between the two groups under the gain frame.
Hypothesis 1(b): In private interest contexts, there is no signif­
icant difference in risk preference between the two groups, re­
gardless of whether the frame is one of gain or loss.

differences in the decision-making
of public officials and private sector

Accountability, blame avoidance, and risk aversion.
Although accountability has been extensively studied in public
administration research (Bovens, Schillemans, and Hart 2008;
Schillemans and Busuioc 2015), there is as yet no consensus on
its precise definition (Koppell 2005). Mulgan (2000) characterized
its core meaning as “being called to account for one’s actions.” To
address conceptual ambiguity and overgeneralization, Bovens
(2007) defined accountability as a relationship between actors
and forums, in which actors have an obligation to explain their
behavior, forums can raise questions and make evaluations, and
actors face potential consequences. Some scholars have argued
that accountability can promote desirable governance outcomes
such as greater transparency, stronger answerability, and reduced
misconduct (Dunn and Legge 2001; Mitchell et al. 1998). However,
bureaucratic accountability does not always yield beneficial ef­
fects; it can also evoke its evil twin, blame avoidance (Tu 2024).
Blame avoidance refers to strategies used to minimize, evade, or
transfer responsibility for avoidable harms or losses (Hood 2014).
Blame avoidance theory was developed to explain politicians’
motivations and strategies to avert blame. Weaver (1986) argues
that politicians are more strongly motivated to avoid blame for
unpopular actions than to claim credit for popular ones, a ten­
dency result from voters’ negativity bias. Hood (2011) extends
this perspective beyond politicians to public officeholders more
broadly—including managers, professionals, and street-level
bureaucrats—and treats blame avoidance as a central feature of
political and institutional life. To date, blame avoidance theory

has been widely applied to explain bureaucratic behavior (i.e.,
Nielsen and Baekgaard 2015; Nowlan 2026). Public officials op­
erate within accountability systems that are shaped by their po­
litical superiors, public opinion, and legal oversight. Within such
systems, the costs of failure far outweigh the rewards of success
(Bovens 2007; Weaver 1986). In political systems such as China’s,
where accountability structures are multi-layered and institution­
al pressures are strong (Liu, Jia, and Zhang 2025), blame avoid­
ance can become a pragmatic survival strategy for public officials
(Li, Ni, and Wang 2021). In such a context, risk-averse behavior is
often interpreted as a deliberate defensive mechanism aimed at
minimizing potential accountability risks by opting for safer choic­
es. Empirical research supports this view. For example, Pollmann,
Potters, and Trautmann (2014) found that the introduction of ac­
countability significantly increased agents’ risk aversion relative
to a no-accountability condition, regardless of whether it was im­
posed ex ante or ex post.
Reward-oriented and punishment-oriented
accountability.
Reward-oriented and punishment-oriented accountability are
two typical forms of accountability. Reward-oriented accounta­
bility emphasizes rewarding bureaucrats’ good behavior rather
than discouraging bad behavior (Tu 2024). Such reward-based
incentives can help build a more positive and appreciative at­
mosphere instead of the culture of fear and anxiety that can result
from the constant threat of punishment (Gürerk, Irlenbusch, and
Rockenbach 2009). Furthermore, Tu (2024) found that accountabil­
ity accompanied by strong positive reinforcement can reduce pub­
lic officials’ tendency toward blame avoidance. Meanwhile, private
sector employees typically operate in more performance-driven
environments that are highly responsive to external incentives.
Therefore, we predict that under reward-oriented accountability,
both public officials and private sector employees will exhibit a
relatively low risk aversion tendency.
Punishment-oriented accountability stresses negativity. It fo­
cuses on identifying faults, attributing individual culpability, and
imposing penalties (Li, Qin, and Koppenjan 2022; Schillemans
and Busuioc 2015). In contrast to the private sector, public organ­
izations operate within a highly regulated, proceduralized, and
multiply-supervised institutional environment (Bozeman, Reed,
and Scott 1992; Pandey and Kingsley 2000). When facing punitive
accountability, public officials are subjected not only to rigorous
internal scrutiny and legal procedures but also to potential media
scrutiny and public pressure that may expose them to significant
political risks. Studies have found that officials operating under a
punishment-oriented accountability logic tend to adopt gaming
strategies to prevent being blamed for potential failures (Ashforth
and Lee 1990; Tu 2024). Moreover, punishment-oriented account­
ability also discourages proactive behaviors such as innovation
and discretionary action (Howlett 2014). Although private sector
employees also face pressures from punishment-oriented ac­
countability, the accountability processes of the private sector are
more flexible, internalized, and open to negotiation. Therefore,
under conditions of punishment-oriented accountability, public
officials are more likely to experience greater punitive pressure,
and thus, more likely to adopt risk-averse decision-making behav­
iors. This leads to the second hypothesis:
Hypothesis 2: In public interest contexts, public officials
are more risk-averse than private-sector employees under
conditions of punishment-oriented accountability, where­
as this difference is not significant under conditions of
reward-oriented accountability.
Process-oriented and outcome-oriented
accountability.
Accountability mechanisms can be categorized as either
process-oriented or outcome-oriented, based on the criteria
used for evaluation. Under process-oriented accountability,
decision-makers are held accountable for the procedures they
used to arrive at a decision, regardless of the outcome (Bovens
et al. 2014; Siegel-Jacobs and Yates 1996). Process-oriented ac­
countability demands that actors prioritize compliance, trans­
parency, and procedural correctness, and thus, their discretion
is significantly constrained by institutional rules. In fact, process
accountability has been shown to reduce individuals’ risk-taking
behavior and their preference for risky strategies. For example,
Lefebvre and Vieider (2013) found that when executives were
told they would need to explain their decision-making process in
front of shareholders, their risk-taking behavior was significant­
ly reduced, and the quality of their decisions improved. Under
process-oriented accountability, both public officials and private
sector employees are held for their decision-making processes
and are required to follow certain procedures and norms. Thus,
their discretion is subject to the same constraints, and this may
decrease the differences in their risk preferences.
By contrast, outcome-oriented accountability focuses on the
outcomes of decisions, rather than the procedures used to reach
them (Siegel-Jacobs and Yates 1996). Under outcome-oriented
accountability, public officials are responsible for the final out­
comes of their decisions, and this increases their decision-making
pressure (Ossege 2012; Siegel-Jacobs and Yates 1996). This is be­
cause public sector decision-making and policy implementation
often involve substantial uncertainty (Andrews 2008). Outcomes
are shaped by multiple objectives, complex environments, and
interdependent implementation networks, and thus, actors have
only a limited ability to control the results of their decisions.
Meanwhile, incentive structures in public sector institutions are
often asymmetric. Rewards for success are often limited or uncer­
tain, whereas sanctions for mistakes or failures can be swift and
severe (Rainey 2009). In contrast, private sector institutions typi­
cally exhibit a higher tolerance for failure and often see it as a cat­
alyst for growth and innovation. Thus, under outcome-oriented
accountability, we predict that public officials will be more
risk-averse than private-sector employees. This leads to the third
hypothesis:
Hypothesis 3: In public-interest contexts, public officials are
more risk-averse than private-sector employees under condi­
tions of outcome-oriented accountability, whereas this differ­
ence is not significant under conditions of process-oriented
accountability.

Reward/punishment-oriented accountability in
combination with process/outcome-oriented
accountability.
These two types of accountabilities are closely interrelated. The
implementation of rewards and punishments must be based on
specific evaluative criteria: either compliance with procedures or
the achievement of outcomes. Combining these two dimensions
provides a more comprehensive understanding of how accounta­
bility structures shape the differences in risk preference between
public officials and private sector employees.
Under process-oriented reward and punishment account­
ability, the implementation of rewards and punishments de­
pends on whether decision-makers have complied with the
correct decision-making process. Research has shown that
process-oriented accountability decreases randomness and
bias in decision-making and improves consistency and accuracy
(Scholten et al. 2007; Siegel-Jacobs and Yates 1996). Under this ac­
countability mechanism, conforming to official decision-making
rules is a low-risk and low-effort coping strategy (Patil, Tetlock,
and Mellers 2017). Therefore, process-oriented reward and pun­
ishment accountability, by emphasizing the importance of pro­
cess norms, may lead to a convergence of the risk preferences of
public and private sector employees.
By contrast, outcome-oriented reward and punishment ac­
countability evaluates individuals based on the final outcomes
of their decisions, rewarding success and punishing failure.
Outcome-oriented reward accountability ties rewards to favora­
ble outcomes, and thus, reflects a logic of positive reinforcement.
Prior studies have shown that both public and private sector em­
ployees are motivated to pursue promotion, greater job security,
and higher pay satisfaction; in some contexts, public sector em­
ployees may even exhibit higher work engagement than their pri­
vate sector counterparts (Van Triest 2024). Therefore, both public
officials and private employees are motivated to engage in pro­
active behavior under outcome-oriented reward accountability,
and thus, there should be no significant difference in their risk
preferences. However, under outcome-oriented punishment ac­
countability, public officials may be more risk-averse than private
sector employees. The accountability structures in the public sec­
tor appear to be stringent (Mulgan 2000). Public officials typically
experience multiple layers of accountability pressure (Rabbi and
Sabharwal 2025). When their decisions go wrong, they not only
must deal with administrative accountability from their superiors
but may also face questioning from legislative bodies, judicial in­
vestigations, audit supervision, and even pressure from the media
and public opinion. Thus, blame avoidance tends to drive public
officials to adopt low-risk strategies to minimize their potential
accountability (Hood 2011; Zhang et al. 2025). This has been de­
scribed as a strategy of “better safe than sorry.” In contrast, the
range of activities for which private sector managers are held
publicly accountable is considerably narrower than that which
applies to public officials (Mulgan 2000). Private sector employees
are usually held accountable for failures when the consequenc­
es are related to company profits, and accountability tends to
focus on noninstitutional outcomes rather than formalized puni­
tive actions. Thus, public officials would be expected to be more
risk-averse than private sector employees under conditions of
outcome-oriented punishment accountability. The following hy­
pothesis is, thus, proposed:
Hypothesis 4(a): In public interest contexts, when accounta­
bility is process-oriented, there is no difference in risk prefer­
ence between public officials and private sector employees
when facing either rewards or punishments.
Hypothesis 4(b): When accountability is outcome-oriented,
public officials are more risk-averse than private sector employ­
ees when facing punishments, but the risk preferences of these
two groups do not significantly differ when facing rewards.
Methods
Experiment 1: design and procedure
Experiment 1 used a 3 (public interest vs. public–private mixed
interest vs. private interest) × 2 (gain frame vs. loss frame)
between-subjects design to test Hypothesis 1. The experimental
sample consisted of public officials and private-sector employ­
ees. Details on recruitment, sample characteristics, and repre­
sentativeness are reported in section Data Collection and Sample.
Participants completed a county-level government investment
decision task in which both the interest context and decision
frame were manipulated to examine their choices between cer­
tain options and risky ones. The experimental material was simi­
lar for both public officials and private sector employees. The only
difference was that the public officials were told that they worked
for the government of County A, whereas private sector employ­
ees were told that they were residents of County A.
In the public interest decision-making scenario, the partici­
pants evaluated a financial investment project that they were
told would affect only the residents of County B and would have
no direct impact on County A, where the participants themselves
were located. Therefore, the participants’ decisions were driven
solely by public interest rather than private considerations. In the
private interest decision-making scenario, private interests were
manipulated using a scenario in which participants made a pri­
vate financial investment decision. To more clearly determine the
effects of the public interest on decision-makers’ risk-based deci­
sion preferences, an additional control group was included and
provided with a context involving both public and private inter­
ests. In this context, the financial investment project is described
as influencing the income of local residents in County A and their
own job performance.
The gain–loss frame was manipulated by describing the in­
vestment project as being either profitable or loss-making.
Specifically, the gain frame described the project as currently op­
erating at a profit, while the loss frame described the project as
currently operating at a loss. (See Supplementary Appendices A1
and A2 for the complete experimental materials.)
Participants in both the public official and private sector em­
ployee groups were exposed to the same experimental procedure.
The materials were randomly assigned to the participants, who
had to read them for at least three minutes to ensure thorough
comprehension before proceeding to the subsequent questions.
They were then asked to choose between a certain option (option
A) and a risky option (option B), both of which had the same ex­
pectation value of utility. Manipulation checks were conducted
for both decision framing and interest context. Finally, the partic­

Experiment 2: design and procedure
Experiment 2 used a 2 (rewards vs. punishment-oriented ac­
countability) × 2 (process vs. outcome-oriented accountability)
between-subjects experimental design with both public officials
and private sector employees to examine how different types of
accountability influence differences in risk preferences between
the two groups in a public-interest decision context (Hypotheses
2, 3, and 4). To verify the results, a control group was included, to
whom neither reward/punishment nor process/outcome informa­
tion was provided. Participants were randomly assigned to one of
the five experimental conditions.
The experimental scenario was designed to capture a public in­
terest decision context and described a smart garage project that
requires cooperation between a government department and
a private sector company. The project involves some risks, and
the different experimental materials emphasized accountability
based on either process or outcome, as well as either reward or
punishment. The public officials and private sector employees
were given the same experimental materials (see Supplementary
Appendix A3 for details.)
The basic procedure of Experiment 2 was the same as in
Experiment 1, except for the measurement of the dependent var­
iable. In Experiment 2, risk preference was measured by asking
the participant how willing they were to take charge of the project
(from 0 = very reluctant to 10 = very willing.) A higher level of will­
ingness indicated a higher degree of risk seeking, while a lower
level of willingness indicated more risk avoidance. Demographic
information was collected, and then individual risk attitude, goal
orientation, public service motivation (PSM), and social respon­
sibility were measured as control variables (see Supplementary
Appendix D), as these variables have been shown to be closely re­
lated to risk preference in previous studies. Personal risk attitude
is a characteristic that influences self-selection: individuals who
are risk averse are more likely to enter the public service sector
(Bellante and Link 1981; Dong 2017). Individuals’ goal orienta­
tion affects their risk decision-making (Elliot and Church 1997).
Although studies on the relationship between PSM and risk pref­
erence have yielded mixed findings, prior research has suggested
that higher PSM is associated with more conservative choices in
the specific context of public interest decisions (Tepe and Prokop
2018). A high level of social responsibility can enhance individu­
als’ awareness of the responsibility entrusted to them and there­
by promote conservative risk-taking behavior (Bolton, Ockenfels,
and Stauf 2015; Charness and Jackson 2009). Moreover, public
officials are generally considered to have higher PSM and social
responsibility compared to private employees. Thus, these var­
iables were measured as control variables in Experiment 2. The
study protocols were approved by the Ethics Review Committee at
the authors’ university (ethics approval number CZLS2022041-A)
and pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.
org/10.17605/OSF.IO/4FVK2; see Supplementary Appendix E for
preregistration details).

Results of Experiment 1
The balance test showed that there were no significant differ­
ences in gender, age, education, household, political affiliation,
or monthly income among any of the six groups for either pub­
lic officials or private sector employees (see Supplementary
Appendices B1 and B2). This indicates that the randomization was
successful. Demographic variables were used for balance checks
and sample description and were not included as covariates in the
chi-square tests.
Table 3 presents the distribution of choices between the cer­
tain and risky options made by public officials and private sec­
tor employees across the different decision-making scenarios
involving varying types of interests and gain–loss frames, along
with the results from the chi-square test of statistical signifi­
cance. Based on these results, figure 2 presents the proportion
of public officials and private sector employees who selected
the risky option, under the different experimental conditions.
In the public interest condition, no significant difference was
found between the two groups under the gain frame. However,
under the loss frame, public officials were significantly less
likely than private sector employees to choose the risky option
(35.37% vs. 55.45%, χ2 = 10.815, P < .001), indicating a strong­
er tendency toward risk aversion when facing potential public
losses. A similar pattern appeared in the public–private interest
condition, in that the difference between the two groups was
significant under the loss frame (38.93% vs. 53.6%, χ2 = 6.139,
P = .013) but not under the gain frame. Therefore, Hypothesis
1(a) is supported.
Similarly, in the private interest condition, no significant dif­
ference between the public and private groups was found in the
gain frame. In the loss frame, public officials were less likely than
private sector employees to choose the risky option, but this dif­
ference did not reach statistical significance (51.08% vs. 62.39%,
χ2 = 3.150, P = .076). This suggests that when decisions involve
purely private interests, public officials and private sector employ­
ees exhibit similar risk preferences. Therefore, Hypothesis 1(b) is
supported.
In addition, note that in the public interest condition, the pro­
portion of public officials who chose the risky option was higher
under the gain frame (48.81%) than under the loss frame (35.37%).
This pattern of greater risk-taking under the gain frame rather
than the loss frame contrasts with the typical prediction drawn
from prospect theory.

Discussion
Drawing on prospect theory, this study addresses the mixed evi­
dence on risk aversion among public officials by comparing pub­
lic officials’ and private sector employees’ risk preferences across
gain–loss decision contexts involving public and private interests.
The results show that public officials are more risk-averse than
private sector employees under loss-frame conditions involving
public or public–private interests, but this is not the case when
the conditions involve in public interests in a gain frame, or when
they solely involve private interests. These findings can be ex­
plained using blame avoidance theory. Public officials tend to
make more risk-averse decisions under outcome-oriented pun­
ishment accountability to avoid the severe consequences of pub­
lic interest loss. These findings challenge the traditional view that
public officials are always more risk-averse than private employ­
ees, and show that their cautious behavior is instead strategic and
context-dependent.
This heightened risk aversion in public-loss scenarios may re­
flect public officials’ role as trustees of the public interest (Box
1992). Prior studies have shown that harm to the public interest
is an important trigger for holding public officials accountable
and imposing sanctions (Yao and Qian 2025). Thus, public offi­
cials may be more risk-averse when making decisions that involve
potential public interest losses. By contrast, public interest gains
are often regarded as an optional performance objective, and the
consequences of private-interest decisions are largely borne by
individuals; thus, neither of these contexts’ triggers risk-averse
behavior in public officials. In summary, consideration of the

The results of this study also show that public officials exhibit
stronger risk aversion under loss frames (35.37% chose the risky
option) than under gain frames (48.81% chose the risky option)
in scenarios involving public interests. This finding is inconsist­
ent with the prospect theory prediction that loss aversion leads
individuals to take greater risks under loss frames. One plau­
sible interpretation is that, when facing public interest losses,
the starting point of public officials’ decision-making logic is no
longer personal loss aversion, but their professional responsi­
bility as trustees who are entrusted to prevent damage to public
interests. Choosing low-risk options in such contexts may, thus,
serve as a strategy for professional survival. This finding answers
Baekgaard’s (2017) call to consider the unique characteristics of
the public sector when applying prospect theory in this context.
Therefore, although prospect theory offers a useful analytical
framework for analyzing risky choices, explaining public officials’
behavior requires that framing effects be contextualized within
public-sector institutions.
Why are public officials more risk-averse than private sector em­
ployees when potential public interest losses are involved? This
study investigates accountability as the underlying driving factor
that explains this difference in risk aversion, specifically by dis­
tinguishing between reward-oriented and punishment-oriented
accountability. The results show that public officials are more
risk-averse than private sector employees when accountability
is punishment-oriented, whereas no such difference is apparent
when accountability is reward-oriented. One possible reason
for this is that public sector organizations face more rigid con­
straints and have more complex regulations (Jung, Bozeman,
and Gaughan 2020), making sanctions more stringent. As a re­
sult, public officials may act more cautiously to limit punishment
risk under punishment-oriented accountability. By contrast,
reward-oriented accountability can reduce public officials’ ten­
dency toward blame avoidance through positive reinforcement
(Tu 2024), thereby encouraging some risk-taking in pursuit
of goals.

Theoretical contributions and practical
implications
This study makes several theoretical contributions. First, based
on prospect theory, it provides a contextualized explanation of
the long-standing debate over whether public officials are more
risk-averse than private sector employees. Comparison of risk
preferences under different gain–loss frames involving pub­
lic or private interests revealed that public officials are more
risk-averse than their private sector employees in the public in­
terest loss condition. This finding underscores the important
role of decision-making contexts in shaping the risk preferences
of public officials and private employees, and in particular the
importance of “who the decision is for.” Second, this study tests
prospect theory’s predictions using a sample of public officials. It
finds that, in gain–loss framing contexts involving public interests,
public officials’ risk preferences show a pattern opposite to the
predictions of prospect theory. This suggests that when prospect
theory is applied to public decision-making, contextual factors
such as professional roles, institutional constraints, and public
value orientations must be incorporated into the analysis if the
risk-taking behavior of public officials is to be explained. Third,
this study deepens our understanding of public officials’ risk
decision-making behavior from the perspective of accountability.
Focusing on two aspects of accountability—the form of account­
ability (reward-oriented vs. punishment-oriented) and its evalu­
ative focus (process-oriented vs. outcome-oriented)—this study
finds that public officials’ stronger risk aversion emerges when ac­
countability is oriented toward outcomes and punishment. This
suggests that public officials’ risk aversion is not a universal con­
sequence of accountability, but rather an institutional response
to specific accountability structures. This finding reveals the un­
derlying institutional logic of risk-averse behavior, deepens our
understanding of the “accountability–blame avoidance–risk aver­
sion” chain, and demonstrates that structural factors of accounta­
bility play a crucial role in shaping public officials’ risk preferences
and decision-making behavior.
These findings also have important implications for practice.
Public officials’ risk aversion is often seen as a barrier to innova­
tion and reform in the public sector. This study reveals that this
risk-averse behavior is largely a defensive response to institution­
al accountability pressures, and it is further exacerbated under
punishment-oriented and outcome-oriented accountability. In
contrast, accountability structures that focus on process and pos­
itive incentives can effectively mitigate this excessive risk aver­
sion and encourage more rational risk-taking in public officials.
These findings suggest that public organizations should be cau­
tious about results-only thinking and zero-tolerance accountabil­
ity mechanisms when strengthening accountability governance.
Public organizations can, thus, optimize accountability structures
by reducing their reliance on punitive outcome measures and
instead using process management and positive reinforcement.
Such changes may help build a more balanced incentive structure
and enhance organizational capacity for risk management and
innovation.
Limitations and future research
This study has a number of limitations that point to directions for
future research. First, although the experimental vignettes were
carefully designed, the use of such vignettes cannot fully capture
the complexity of real-world decision environments. In addition,
participants’ self-reported risk preferences may not perfectly corre­
spond to their actual risk-taking behavior (Champ et al. 2020; Tepe
and Prokop 2018), as their responses may have been influenced by
factors such as social desirability bias (Fisher 1993). Indeed, this
may be why the effect sizes observed in this study were generally
small to moderate. Thus, even when the results are statistically sig­
nificant, their substantive implications may be limited. Therefore,
the findings of this study should be interpreted cautiously when ex­
trapolated to real-world risk-related decisions. Future studies could
incorporate field evidence or behavioral observations (i.e., admin­
istrative records or observed decision outcomes) to strengthen the
external and ecological validity of the findings.
Second, prior research has shown that individuals’ risk-taking
preferences vary across task domains. For example, individu­
als tend to exhibit stronger risk-seeking tendencies when mak­
ing life-and-death decisions than when making monetary or
property-related decisions (Wang 1996). This domain sensitivity
suggests that the present findings may not generalize to all types
of policy or administrative decisions.

Third, in terms of sample representativeness, the sample
of public officials in this study consisted primarily of younger,
lower-ranking officials, and thus, largely reflects grassroots public
officials. Although China’s administrative system exhibits strong
structural homogeneity across hierarchical levels (Tu and Gong
2022), it is not clear to what extent our findings can be generalized to
higher-ranking or more senior officials. Future studies should incor­
porate a wider age and rank distribution and examine the applica­
bility of the findings in different national and institutional contexts.
Fourth, public officials and private employees may differ sys­
tematically in terms of individual characteristics other than sec­
toral affiliation. Although several individual-level variables were
controlled, and random assignment was used to mitigate con­
founding effects, the possibility of unobserved heterogeneity can­
not be entirely ruled out.
Finally, although this study focuses on two dimensions of ac­
countability (i.e., rewards versus punishment and process versus
outcome), it does not account for the source of accountability,
which may also influence risk preferences. For instance, hori­
zontal and vertical accountability may impose different behavio­
ral pressures on public officials, and thus, may lead to different
risk-taking patterns. Future research should further disaggregate
accountability sources to develop a more comprehensive under­
standing of how accountability systems shape the risk-related be­
havior of public officials.

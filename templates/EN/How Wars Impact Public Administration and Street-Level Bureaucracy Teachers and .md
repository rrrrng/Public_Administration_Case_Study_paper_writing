Abstract
The actions of street-level bureaucrats (SLBs)—such as teachers, healthcare workers, and police officers—during crises and emergencies have
received growing attention in public administration scholarship. Yet, a critical and underexplored dimension remains: the role of SLBs in context of
war and armed conflict, where threats to personal safety, political coercion, and institutional collapse converge. This article investigates how war—and
military occupation in particular—reshapes the everyday work, moral dilemmas, and response strategies of SLBs operating under extreme condi­
tions. We focus on the war in Ukraine—the most intense armed conflict on European soil since World War II. Within this context, we examine
education professionals, as schools and universities have become strategic targets of Russian occupation forces: cultural frontlines central to the
legitimization of territorial control and forced assimilation. Complex dilemmas emerge: should SLBs continue providing services to the population—thus
risking complicity with the occupiers and being branded as “traitors”—or should they refrain from any contact with them, at the risk of facing violent
punishment? Our analysis draws on qualitative data collected through fieldwork in six Ukrainian regions; semi-structured and in-depth interviews
(both online and face-to-face) with education professionals who experienced the Russian occupation; and qualitative document analysis of media
coverage and human rights reports. Following an abductive research design, we identify four structural features that distinguish war from other
crises: forced interaction with enemy state actors, institutional weaponization, loyalty dilemmas, and bureaucratic rupture under contested sover­
eignties. Building on these findings, we propose a fourfold typology of SLB response strategies—exit, accommodation, local defiance, and remote
adaptation—each grounded in distinct moral decisions. By bringing SLBs into the analysis of wartime governance, this article contributes to an
emerging research agenda on frontline bureaucracy under conditions of armed conflict—contexts that, alarmingly, are no longer exceptional.

Resumo
As ações dos burocratas de nível de rua (BNR) — como professores, profissionais de saúde e policiais–durante crises e emergências têm recebido
atenção crescente na literatura de Administração Pública. Contudo, um aspecto crítico e ainda pouco explorado permanece: o papel dos BNRs
em contextos de guerra e conflito armado, nos quais ameaças à segurança pessoal, coerção política e colapso institucional convergem. Este
artigo investiga como guerras–e, em particular, ocupações militares–transformam o trabalho cotidiano, os dilemas morais e as estratégias de
resposta dos BNRs em condições extremas. Abordamos a guerra na Ucrânia–o conflito armado mais intenso em solo europeu desde a Segunda
Guerra Mundial. Analisamos os profissionais da educação, uma vez que escolas e universidades se tornaram alvos estratégicos das forças de
ocupação russa: um verdadeiro front cultural, fundamental para a legitimação do controle territorial e a assimilação forçada da população. Nestes
contextos, novos dilemas complexos emergem: os BNRs devem continuar prestando serviços à população–correndo o risco de serem cúmplices
dos ocupantes e rotulados como “traidores” — ou devem evitar qualquer contato com os mesmos, arriscando-se a sofrer punições violentas?
Nossa análise baseia-se em dados qualitativos coletados por meio de trabalho de campo em seis regiões ucranianas; entrevistas semiestruturadas
e em profundidade (tanto online quanto presenciais) com profissionais da educação que vivenciaram a ocupação russa; e análise qualitativa de
documentos, incluindo reportagens e relatórios de direitos humanos. Seguindo um desenho de pesquisa abdutivo, identificamos quatro carac­
terísticas estruturais que distinguem a guerra de outras crises: a interação forçada com atores estatais inimigos, a instrumentalização institucional
para fins militares, os dilemas de lealdade e a ruptura burocrática sob soberanias contestadas. Com base nesses achados, propomos uma tipologia
de quatro estratégias de resposta dos BNRs–saída (exit), acomodação, resistência local e adaptação remota —, cada uma fundamentada em
distintas decisões morais. Ao incorporar os BNRs à análise da governança em meio a guerras, este artigo contribui para uma agenda emergente
de pesquisa sobre a burocracia de nível de rua em condições de conflito armado–contextos que, infelizmente, vêm ganhando amplitude.

Introduction
Street-level bureaucrats (SLBs) constitute the human face of the
state in everyday life, as they interact directly with citizens to
deliver public services (Smith 2012)—deciding who gets what,
how, and under what conditions (Lipsky 2010 [1980]). SLBs
are not passive implementers of top-down policy, but active
agents who mediate between formal rules and the lived needs
of the public, effectively functioning as policy-makers (Brodkin
2011). Their frontline position gives them significant discre­
tionary power to interpret and adapt abstract policies to local
conditions and community expectations (Maynard-Moody and
Musheno 2003).
Crises—such as natural disasters, armed conflicts, or
pandemics—intensify SLBs’ challenges (Gofen and Lotta 2021).
In such moments, SLBs move from being the everyday face of
public administration to the first line of government response
(Brodkin 2021). Their roles shift rapidly, often without suffi­
cient guidance or resources (Henderson 2014; Alcadipani et al.
2020). Crises disrupt the typical logic of service provision,
rendering established routines and professional expertise less
applicable (Møller 2021). This disorientation affects their cop­
ing mechanisms (Cox et al. 2021) and can ultimately under­
mine their authority and complicate their ability to act
decisively (Malandrino and Sager 2021).
Despite recent advances in understanding SLBs in crisis set­
tings, research on their role in the context of war, armed con­
flicts, and military occupation remains scarce. Much of the
existing literature on SLBs in crisis was developed in response
to the COVID-19 pandemic or democratic backsliding, empha­
sizing temporary disruptions within otherwise functioning
institutional environments. In contrast, wars and territorial
conflicts—including those involving military occupations or
territorial control by criminal organizations—introduce pro­
longed ruptures, contested sovereignty, violence, and
coercion—conditions that fragment authority, destabilize insti­
tutions, and pose acute personal and ethical risks for SLBs.
Against this backdrop, we ask: (1) How do war and territo­
rial conflict structurally affect SLBs’ work? and (2) What
response strategies do SLBs adopt to navigate and survive
within such volatile and dangerous contexts? To address these
questions, we examine an extreme case of violence and terri­
torial confrontation: Russia’s military aggression against
Ukraine, which unfolded in two phases—a localized invasion
in 2014 and a full-scale invasion in 2022—resulting in the
occupation of extensive Ukrainian territories.
Within this setting, we focus on education professionals, a
highly targeted category of SLBs. Russian occupying forces
systematically pressured teachers and school principals to
adopt the Russian curriculum as a tool of forced cultural assim­
ilation1 and to disseminate narratives portraying Ukrainians as
being “reunited” with their “true” homeland (Ferraro 2024;
Honchar 2022). As frontline community actors, teachers,
school principals, and university staff faced acute personal,
political, and ethical pressures that profoundly reshaped their
everyday work.
To investigate these dynamics, we employed an abductive
research design that integrated document analysis, fieldwork
in six Ukrainian regions, and semi-structured and in-depth
interviews with SLBs who experienced occupation. This com­
bination of methods allowed us to iteratively refine theoretical
insights and derive an empirically grounded framework to
analyze street-level work under conditions of war.
This article makes four main contributions. First, we show
that war constitutes a distinct form of crisis—one that desta­
bilizes institutional authority, fragments territorial control,
escalates violence, and exposes SLBs to coercion, moral dilem­
mas, and contested identities beyond what existing crisis frame­
works capture. The occupying state instrumentalizes SLBs to

Street-level bureaucrats amid crisis, war, and
armed conflict
Since Lipsky’s seminal work (2010 [1980]), much of the
research on SLBs has focused on understanding the key factors
that shape their daily routines and their interactions with citi­
zens (Gofen et al. 2024). SLBs have discretion to make deci­
sions, interpreting and adapting guidelines to specific encounters
(Brodkin 2011). Their decisions are based on a range of per­
sonal, organizational, and institutional factors (Cohen 2018;
Hupe and Buffat 2014). Still, they work in very critical contexts
characterized by resource scarcity, ambiguity, heavy workload,
and various forms of pressure.
To navigate these challenges systematically, SLBs develop
different coping mechanisms (Lipsky 2010 [1980]). Coping is
conceptualized as the efforts SLBs make to master, tolerate, or
reduce the demands and conflicts they face every day (Edri-Peer
and Cohen 2025; Tummers et al. 2015). Coping strategies influ­
ence how SLBs exercise discretion and respond to the situations
they respond to the situations they encounter (Lipsky 2010),
making them move away from, against, toward or together
with citizens (Edri-Peer and Cohen 2025). Simultaneously,
SLBs operate in ethically and morally demanding environments
(Møller, Pedersen, and Pors 2022) that require discretionary
decisions with real consequences for citizens. To manage these
moral tensions, SLBs develop moral dispositions—relatively
stable orientations cultivated through institutional routines and
professional socialization—which shape how they perceive
their duties and relate to citizens (Zacka 2017).
Zacka (2017) conceptualizes these dynamics through three
archetypal moral dispositions—caregiving, indifference, and
enforcement—each reflecting distinct moral logics (responsive­
ness, impartiality, legality). Institutional settings privilege some
logics over others, leading SLBs to oscillate among dispositions;
overcommitment to any single orientation risks burnout or
moral injury. Thus, moral agency is not only about discretion
or compliance, but about navigating the trade-offs among com­
peting moral demands embedded in bureaucratic work.
However, both the coping literature and Zacka’s framework
are grounded in institutional contexts that, despite constraints,
remain relatively stable. They explain how SLBs respond to
pressure within functioning bureaucratic orders. These models
illuminate routine stressors but offer limited traction for envi­
ronments marked by institutional breakdown, coercion, and
physical danger—conditions typical of war and military occu­
pation. SLBs’ moral orientations may shift or collapse, and
moral reasoning becomes situational and reactive, shaped by
instability and survival pressures.
In crises, war, and territorial conflict, bureaucratic hierarchies
fragment, accountability structures collapse, and the moral
reference points that usually orient public servants—rules, supe­
riors, or norms—become unreliable or contradictory. SLBs do
not merely balance caregiving, indifference, or enforcement,
nor simply cope with pressure. Instead, they face moral disori­
entation, in which habitual dispositions lose traction and moral
reasoning becomes situational, improvised, and often defensive.
This highlights a theoretical blind spot: existing scholarship
explains bureaucratic adaptation under stable or moderately
disrupted conditions but offers limited insight into how SLBs
reconstitute moral agency when institutional order collapses.
Recent literature has begun to examine these dynamics under
crises. Studies show that crises increase SLBs’ discretion, as
existing regulations and prior experiences may not address new
challenges (Gofen and Lotta 2021; Henderson 2014; Hupe and
Buffat 2014). Heightened demand alongside resource scarcity
creates intense pressures and destabilizes governance, weaken­
ing leadership and triggering unpredictable shifts (Farazmand
2007; Lotta et al. 2024). Routine practices may become inef­
fective, requiring SLBs to adapt, resist, or innovate (Brodkin
2021; Cox et al. 2021). Crises also amplify moral dilemmas
and political pressures, especially when they intersect with
institutional and democratic backsliding (Gofen and Lotta
2021; Lotta et al. 2022; Piotrowska 2024).
Crises tend to heighten conflicts between SLBs and politi­
cians. SLBs may respond with divergence, dissent, sabotage,
shirking, or guerrilla strategies (Brehm and Gates 1999; Gofen
2014; O’Leary 2010). Hirschman’s (2004 [1970]) classical
exit-voice-loyalty framework has been used to analyze these
reactions. For example, Piotrowska (2024) shows that SLBs
use discretion to navigate political crises by exiting services,
raising concerns, or implementing political agendas. While
useful, these insights primarily come from COVID-19 or dem­
ocratic backsliding studies and may not fully capture the chal­
lenges of war and territorial conflict, which involve contested
sovereignties and multiple competing authorities. This raises
profound dilemmas regarding identity, representation, and
loyalty—SLBs must decide whom they serve and under which
mandate.
War zones create unique moral demands and normative
spaces that shape SLBs’ moral dispositions (Eikenaar 2023;
Zacka 2017). Moral referents—such as the state, peers, the
population, or even the enemy—may shift significantly, com­
pelling SLBs to navigate competing ethical demands. Decisions
and responses are highly consequential under largely unknown
conditions—SLBs often “normalize the abnormal” to cope
emotionally while continuing to provide services (Ramon et al.
2006). Like soldiers, they may experience moral injuries, jus­
tifying or rationalizing actions by appealing to rules or superi­
ors’ instructions (Molendijk 2024).
War’s disruptions increase bureaucratic discretion and force
SLBs to rely heavily on personal values and judgment, height­
ening the risk of inconsistency, bias, and conflict in
decision-making (Ramon et al. 2006; Strier et al. 2021). Simul­
taneously, wars amplify the stress of making numerous deci­
sions under volatile conditions, including threats such as
bombings, sectarian violence, checkpoint crossings, and lack
of support from higher authorities (Ramon et al. 2006).
Given these specificities, we argue that current scholarship
on SLBs under crisis conditions has yet to theorize adequately
how wars and armed conflict shape street-level work. Existing
models of SLBs’ responses and moral dispositions do not cap­
ture the breakdown of moral and institutional order that

Research design
To examine how war and territorial conflict shape SLBs’ work
and response strategies, we adopted an abductive qualitative
design (Schwartz-Shea and Yanow 2013). The analysis pro­
ceeded iteratively between theory and empirical material. We
began by reviewing scholarship on street-level bureaucracy,
focusing on SLBs’ discretionary decisions in routine and crisis
settings and on the moral dimensions of frontline work. This
engagement generated sensitizing concepts that guided subse­
quent data collection.
Data collection
Our empirical strategy combined document analysis, field­
work, and interviews. Given the centrality of education to the
politics of occupation, we focused on education professionals
as SLBs.
In the first stage, initiated in January 2024, we analyzed
reports, legal norms, media coverage, and prior studies address­
ing education under Russian occupation (see list in Supplemen­
tary Appendix 1). Because many sources reflect polarized
wartime narratives, interviews (including in-person) became
essential to triangulate and contextualize the material. The
preliminary coding also informed the design of the inter­
view guides.
In the second stage, we conducted semi-structured online
interviews (lasting 45 minutes to 1.5 hours) and in-depth inter­
views (lasting 1.5 to 3 hours) with eight SLBs. Both interview
types followed the same set of open-ended questions about the
challenges educators face and the strategies they adopt (see the
interview guides in Supplementary Appendix 2, available in
both English and Ukrainian).
In the third stage, we conducted fieldwork in Ukraine over
three weeks between February and March 2025, visiting six
regions with varying degrees of proximity to the frontline: Lviv,
Ivano-Frankivsk, Kyiv (Bucha), Kyiv City, Mykolaiv (including
areas that experienced 9 months of occupation in 2022), and
Odesa. In total, during this phase, we conducted fifteen
in-person interviews with our target group and two remote
interviews. We also visited educational institutions located just
a few kilometers from the frontline, including some that had
been wholly or partially destroyed and others that had experi­
enced occupation.
Sampling
We used snowball sampling, supported by Ukrainian scholars
and organizations embedded in local networks. Inclusion cri­
teria were: (1) having worked as an education professional
during the 2014 or 2022 Russian invasion, and (2) having lived
under occupation for at least 1 month before relocating to
Ukrainian-controlled territory.
The category “education professionals” in this study includes
school principals, school teachers, university administrators,
university lecturers, and municipal officials responsible for the
education sector. Although these are distinct professional roles,
the occupying forces—as we will discuss later—employed sim­
ilar strategies to recruit them into the new administration and
to instrumentalize both their positions and their social
(street-level) embeddedness in efforts to legitimize the occupa­
tion and enforce cultural assimilation. Three NGO represen­
tatives with expertise on occupation-related pressures were
included to add contextual insight.
Importantly, while the literature distinguishes between SLBs
and street-level managers (such as principals), in this research
we chose to frame all of them as SLBs. This decision stems from
the empirical evidence showing that, during wartime, SLMs
assume dual roles and often perform as SLBs—teaching, inter­
acting with families, and directly engaging with citizens. There­
fore, we include these different types of educational professionals
within the broader category of SLBs, given their interactive roles
with citizens and their discretionary practices in service delivery.
The graphs in Supplementary Appendices 3 and 4 show the
distribution of the 25 interviewees2 by profession and region.
The regions of Kherson and Mykolaiv accounted for the largest
share of respondents—both include localities that experienced
approximately 9 months of occupation before being liberated.
Of all interviewees, 88% experienced occupation, 68% are
women; 16% lived under occupation in 2014, and 76% in
2022. Supplementary Appendices 5 and 6 offer additional
details on researchers’ positionality, case selection, potential
sampling biases, and the strategies adopted to mitigate them.
Data analysis
Using our research questions as analytical anchors, we devel­
oped a coding frame organized into four second-order catego­
ries: (1) occupying state strategies toward SLBs, (2) impacts of
war on street-level work, (3) SLB response strategies, and (4)
moral decisions underpinning these strategies. Codes combined
deductive elements derived from the literature and inductive
elements emerging from the interviews, allowing iterative refine­
ment. Codes and keywords were defined both prior to (deduc­
tive phase) and during (inductive phase) the analysis, allowing
for iterative refinement of the coding scheme. Our integrative
analysis identified 15 first-order codes, organized into four
second-order categories (Supplementary Appendix 7). We then
returned to the literature to examine the similarities and differ­
ences between our findings and existing theories of SLBs.
In line with qualitative standards and our commitment to
faithfully represent the voices and experiences of those who lived
through the war, we included a broad range of extended inter­
view quotes in which SLBs reflected on their decisions in their
own terms. This richer narrative foundation provides a more
nuanced and human-centered basis for the subsequent analysis
of wars’ impacts, SLB response strategies, and moral decisions.

The impacts of war on teachers and education
professionals: political and moral challenges
Our analysis reveals four structural factors that distinguish
wartime street-level work from crises occurring within func­
tioning institutional settings.
Forced interaction with enemy state actors
In military occupations, SLBs must engage directly with enemy
military personnel and administrative agents operating under
the authority of the occupying power. These interactions are
highly asymmetrical and often underpinned by the implicit or
explicit use of force. Due to their prior affiliation with the
legitimate state, SLBs are perceived as potential sources of resis­
tance or disloyalty,
Under military occupation, SLBs are compelled to interact
with enemy personnel who hold coercive power. Due to their
affiliation with the legitimate state, SLBs are perceived as
sources of resistance or disloyalty, which heightens their vul­
nerability and increases threats to their physical safety. They
are caught between the suspicions of the occupiers and the
expectations of the local population, who may see them as
either “intermediaries” or potential “traitors” to the legitimate
state. This dual exposure renders their position both precarious
and politically charged.
In the case of Ukraine, Russian occupying forces employed
simultaneous strategies of coercion and co-optation to recruit
SLBs. School principals were primary targets, viewed as poten­
tial brokers who could persuade teachers to comply with the
new regime. One school principal recalled that meetings with
the occupation administration were held in the presence of
heavily armed officers, reinforcing an atmosphere of
intimidation:
At that time, you had to think for yourself: should I go or
not? But I’m the kind of person who needs to know how
my enemy breathes, to understand what steps I should take
next. So, I had to listen and draw some conclusions. I went
to that meeting. There weren’t many school principals—just
over half showed up. The rest had either already left for
Ukrainian-controlled territory or gone abroad. […] So the
principals came, and *** started talking about how it was
necessary to organize education in [our community]. He

Institutional bellicization and political weaponization
SLBs are essential actors in ensuring state legitimacy, territorial
control, and the interaction between the population and the
higher levels of the state bureaucracy (Sager and Gofen 2022).
Previous studies have shown that they can be politically instru­
mentalized by different actors to increase their legitimacy and
control, especially when democratic institutions are at risk (Lotta
et al. 2024; Piotrowska 2024). This process of politicization also
occurs under war, though it assumes distinctive characteristics;
for this reason, we propose the concept “bellicization.”
Bellicization (from bellum, Latin for “war”) refers to the
transformation of actors, institutions, or practices as they
become integrated into the logics of warfare. It does not require
direct participation in combat; rather, it occurs when civilian
professionals are repositioned within a conflictual order that
assigns them strategic roles in the struggle for territorial control
and political legitimacy. In the context of military occupation,
SLBs undergo this process of institutional bellicization as they
are framed, targeted, or coerced as actors whose decisions can
influence the occupier’s apparatus of rule. Through this
dynamic, they also become politically weaponized—mobilized
as instruments of coercion, indoctrination, or symbolic domi­
nation. Their national affiliations and professional loyalties
become objects of manipulation as the occupier’s identity pol­
itics seek to redefine or suppress them. Amid contested sover­
eignties and blurred identity boundaries, SLBs can thus become
both victims and involuntary instruments of the occupying
state’s agenda.

fined the role of education professionals, turning them from
public service providers into ideological agents and objects of
forced assimilation. After the end of the Ukrainian academic
year in April–May 2022, pressure on education professionals
escalated as the Russian authorities prepared for a September
referendum to annex the occupied territories (Honchar 2022;

as a signal of stability and control; the delivery of public goods

guage and identity, and the coercion against teachers (Amnesty
International 2024; Human Rights Watch 2024; United
Nations Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine 2024).

school activities (in Russian Telegram channels and Rutube) in
occupied areas: social media posts reveal children in military
uniforms, praising Russian military figures, drawing parallels
between the Ukrainian government and Nazism, waving Rus­
sian flags, and singing the Russian national anthem. Ukrainian
authorities and interviewees also reported the removal and
destruction of Ukrainian textbooks and literature—some listed
as “extremist” in official Russian registries (Shynkarenko et al.

educational system in occupied territories is perceived as an
attempt to erase Ukrainian identity, Russian mass media por­

and their inhabitants are Ukrainian (see Krym Inform 2022;
Yemelyanov 2022).3
The political weaponization of education is not exclusive to
the Ukrainian context. Raphael Lemkin (1944), the jurist who
coined the term “genocide”, described how the Nazi regime
systematically instrumentalized schools to carry out cultural
genocide in occupied territories—through political indoctrina­
tion and the repression of languages, identities, and local artis­
tic expression.
In sum, the bellicization of SLBs reconfigures them as stra­
tegic instruments of war, collapsing the normative distinction
between everyday street-level bureaucratic routines and the
high politics of conflict.
Loyalty dilemma and double victimization
In the context of military occupation—marked by contested
sovereignties and competing normative frameworks—SLBs are
compelled to navigate high-stakes trade-offs and complex
moral dilemmas. Although previous SLB studies highlight
moral dilemmas as part of their daily routine (Zacka 2017),
we found that in military occupations, these dilemmas differ
both in degree and content. SLBs in these contexts must either
remain loyal to the de jure state—refusing to cooperate with
the occupying regime, at significant personal risk—or agree to
serve under the de facto authorities/state, thereby exposing
themselves to potential stigmatization and legal consequences
for “treason” or “collaboration.” War, in this sense, compels
SLBs to take sides, pushing their identities, national affiliations,
and discretionary authority into a deeply politicized terrain.
We conceptualize this compelled choice—under extreme
conditions—between allegiance to two competing states as a
loyalty dilemma. Since SLBs are subject to sanctions from both
states, they face a form of double victimization, being penalized
regardless of their course of action—whether they remain loyal
to the de jure state or cooperate with the occupying regime.
The bellicization and political weaponization of the educa­
tion sector place SLBs in particularly vulnerable positions.
According to interviewees, many resisted initially by boycotting
occupation-led meetings. In response, Russian officials and
co-opted local personnel intensified coercive tactics. These
included threats, intimidatory meetings in the presence of heav­
ily armed soldiers, home and workplace searches, and abduc­
tions followed by extrajudicial detention, interrogation,
beatings, and torture in so-called pidvaly (basements)—impro­
vised facilities unfit for human habitation (see Amnesty Inter­
national 2024; Human Rights Watch 2024; United Nations
Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine 2024). A report
by the Zmina Human Rights Center (Okhotnikova et al. 2023)
documented the abduction of at least 30 education profession­
als under such circumstances.

loyalty to the occupiers or to resign and leave the occupied
territory—if permitted. Two school principals recounted being
arrested by masked and heavily armed officers. Held in a pidval

and personal survival.
Bureaucratic rupture and improvised restructuration amid
competing normative frameworks
As in other crises, military occupation produces a marked rup­
ture in bureaucratic stability (Gofen and Lotta 2021). However,
in this case, the lack of stability stems from other sources and
has consequences, as the absence of effective territorial control
by the legitimate state disrupts the ordinary functioning of
administrative structures and procedures, generating institu­
tional discontinuity and operational uncertainty.
In Ukraine, digital technologies have allowed for partial
deterritorialization of communication flows between SLBs and
higher bureaucratic levels. However, without physical access
to territory, such flows were obstructed and yielded limited
outcomes in terms of effective public service delivery. This dis­
connection gave rise to recurring uncertainties regarding the
payment of salaries, the distribution of resources, procedures,
and the exercise of discretion.
Unable to exercise authority directly, the de jure state has to
improvise new channels of communication with local admin­
istrative levels. This restructuring is contingent on the dynamics
of armed conflict, territorial fluidity, and the rupture of insti­
tutional infrastructure. Although SLBs are accustomed to oper­
ating with some functional autonomy, the centralization of
control inherent to wartime governance formally restricts the
“delegated authority” (Hupe and Hill 2007) and reinforces
vertical command structures.
At the same time, the occupying power—despite having de
facto control—must also build its own administrative architec­
ture, often in unfamiliar territory, facing local resistance and
operating outside its sociocultural frame of reference. Institu­
tional adaptation under these conditions is highly improvised
and vulnerable to uncertainty (Hechter and Vidal-Aparicio
2011). Strategies of coercion and co-optation aim to sever the
SLBs’ remaining ties to the legitimate state and place them
under the authority of the occupier.
Regardless of the moral or legal order SLBs choose to follow,
their actions take place in a setting marked by normative ambi­
guity and administrative vacuums. Relations with the legitimate
state become unstable; institutional support fragments; and
state authority, once taken for granted, is eroded. A further
layer of uncertainty stems from strategic indeterminacy: the
unclear trajectory of the war and the uncertain prospect of the
legitimate state’s return. If ambiguity characterizes frontline
work (Matland 1995), this type of ambiguity renders the loy­
alty dilemma even more complex.
In Ukraine, education professionals used to operate within
a coherent political-legal framework that required knowledge
of national, regional, and local bureaucratic systems. Following
the invasion, they were abruptly pressured to engage with the
newly installed de facto authorities. Resource allocation soon
became a critical concern. Russian occupying forces shut down
Ukrainian banks, gradually introduced the Russian currency,
and disrupted communications, partially cutting off access to
Ukrainian digital platforms and financial transactions. Inter­
viewees emphasized that some partially functional online sys­
tems were vital for receiving resources and surviving without
working for the occupying regime. When teaching became
impossible, educators were placed on prostii—a furlough sys­
tem that guaranteed two-thirds of their salary. A former school
teacher (now serving as a school principal) observed that the

infrastructure—with rural and frontline areas being in a worse
situation—and on coordination between the school’s adminis­

As demonstrated above, war reshapes the strategic, institu­
tional, and moral landscape in which SLBs operate. Under
conditions of coercion and occupation, and amid potential
conflicts between authorities’ demands and ideals of commit­
ment, SLBs face moral dilemmas and must decide how to
respond. To capture these responses, we developed a typology
based on the core decisions SLBs have to make to deal with the
war context: (1) whether to remain in or leave the occupied
territory, and (2) whether to engage or not politically against
the occupying forces. These decisions reflect the core dilemmas
SLBs face under occupation, as they directly address both ter­
ritorial and political alignment in a context of contested author­
ity. From this matrix, we identify four primary response
strategies (see fig. 1): exit, accommodation, remote adaptation,
and local defiance. We developed this typology through a com­

evidence from our data.

classic framework on organizational decline, which centers on
the categories of exit, voice, and loyalty. While we also observe
dynamics related to withdrawal, compliance, and protest, we
recognize that its organizational focus fits uneasily with the
coercive dynamics of military occupation. In this way, the key
innovation of our model is the explicit incorporation of
territoriality—linking political positioning to the relationship
with territory.

the habitual orientations, producing a series of situational

occupied and frontline areas. Early in the war, temporary
humanitarian corridors were established. In March 2022, the

teachers and to support displaced students. As discussed earlier,

inantly driven by a moral decision toward self-preservation and
well-being. It involves assessments of personal safety, perceived
risks, and prospects for integration in the new environment.
Accommodation
The accommodation strategy—remaining in the occupied ter­
ritory without engaging in political resistance against the occu­
pying forces—is arguably the most morally complex. It involves
four of the five moral decisions identified earlier. It amplifies
the tensions arising from key wartime impacts, such as the
bellicization and political weaponization of bureaucracy, loy­
alty dilemma, double victimization, and bureaucratic rupture.
Due to the specific nature of SLBs’ interactions with occupation
authorities, accommodation is also a highly controversial
strategy—closely linked to what much of the conflict studies
and historical literature refer to as collaborationism.
The concept of collaborationism is often laden with norma­
tive judgments that obscure the lived complexities of occupied
communities (Buschmeier 2024; Tönsmeyer 2015). To move
beyond normative simplifications, we examine the various
moral decisions that inform SLBs’ decisions to adopt accom­
modation. We distinguish two subtypes: engaged accommoda­
tion and passive accommodation.
Engaged accommodation
Engaged accommodation refers to cases in which SLBs actively
participate in the occupying state’s direct efforts to establish an
administrative structure and to legitimize the new order. This
choice is typically associated with the moral decision of “affiliation
with the de facto state,” whereby SLBs perceive the new status of
the territory positively—either due to prior ideological or
identity-based affinities with the occupying state, or as a response
to dissatisfaction with the pre-occupation political context.
Due to the historical experience of Russian occupation since
2014, educational SLBs were generally aware of the political
weaponization of education as a tool for legitimizing occupa­
tion and promoting the forced assimilation of children. Most
interviewees emphasized that instances of engaged accommo­
dation were rare. While this perception might reflect the under­
reporting of collaboration typical in national historiographies
(see Buschmeier 2024), direct statements from individuals who
adopted this strategy also corroborate its low prevalence. One
teacher who did engage with the occupying administration was
tasked with reorganizing the local education system. Before the
full-scale invasion of 2022, she had publicly supported Mos­
cow’s ideological and identity-based projects and was under

co-optation strategies, offering significantly higher salaries than
those paid by Ukraine, financial bonuses, and administrative

(co-optation) was indeed the dominant moral decision, whether
it coexisted with other dispositions such as “affiliation with the
de facto state,” or whether the decision was primarily driven
by coercion (“the self-preservation” decision). Much of the
resentment stems from the fact that some of these individuals
served as intermediaries for the occupation regime, exerting
pressure on fellow educators to comply—whether through
co-optation or coercion.
Passive accommodation
Passive accommodation refers to the involvement of SLBs
in the functioning of the occupying administration not out
of ideological or identity-based affiliation with the de facto
regime, but rather due to moral decisions such as
self-preservation, search for institutional stability, and
commitment to service provision. While we use the term
“passive,” this should not be understood to imply that
SLBs are merely inert or unreflective actors. On the con­
trary, even in this mode of response, SLBs engage in active
moral reasoning and make consequential decisions in the
face of constrained and risky circumstances. In such cases,
SLBs typically do not participate directly in the new
regime’s political-ideological legitimization efforts, nor do
they hold high administrative positions. Nonetheless, they
contribute indirectly to the consolidation of the occupation
by ensuring the continued functioning of frontline services
and the provision of public goods (Hechter and
Vidal-Aparicio 2011).
As discussed in the previous sections, coercion strategies
included arbitrary detentions, intimidation, and even torture.
In this context, the disposition of self-preservation appears to
have guided some education professionals in their decisions to
accommodate. One teacher described being pressured to coop­
erate and serve as an informant:
They told me that if I didn’t cooperate by the third time [of
inquiry], I’d have problems. […] They knew I was a
teacher—they knew everything. They said: ‘You’re useful to
us. You understand this is now the Russian Federation. We
need to establish order. All you have to do is perform your
duties—just help us. If we need information about certain
people, you provide it, that’s it.’ My friend was taken to a
basement. […] They showed that he was already on the
wanted list. These lists were updated weekly—if someone
said something wrong, even a small thing, they were marked
as someone ‘who did not support the occupiers.’ […] They
went through very severe torture. […] Maybe, over time,
people give in. Because, you know, occupation is constant
pressure—psychological pressure. […] Maybe people break
and start to collaborate.
Although methodological constraints limited the ability to
document the full range of SLBs’ experiences under occupation,
some testimonies hinted at the search for institutional stability
as a moral decision for passive accommodation. One university
staff member asserted that local SLBs and managers were indif­
ferent to who held territorial control, as long as their work and
personal lives remained unchanged. Expressing evident disap­
pointment, she reflected on the power dynamics within her
community:
All the local power-holders stayed—our [local] “officials”.
[…] Their job titles may be different now, but the people are
the same. Everyone who held power under Ukraine’s rule
just changed the flags in their offices […]. So, honestly, I
don’t think much has really changed. […]. Unfortunately,
[my former] school now provides educational services under
what we might call “[local] legislation”—that is, under those
[occupying] authorities.
Finally, the notion of passive accommodation is driven by a
disposition of commitment to service provision, that is, an eth­
ical orientation centered on the recipients of public services. In
such cases, SLBs make suboptimal decisions not out of ideo­
logical alignment, but allegedly to prevent greater harm to their
clients. As an illustrative example, one school principal reported
that the occupying forces exerted various forms of pressure and
intimidation on educators, demanding that all subjects be
taught in Russian, using Russian textbooks and materials dis­
tributed to schools. The principal recalled:
[The occupiers] said that if we didn’t work, they would bring
teachers from Russia. […] It was difficult for all of us to make
that decision. But we decided we would cause less harm to
the children than the teachers who would come from Russia.
These are our children, and we must teach them. […] It was
scary to tell the children: I used to teach Ukrainian, and now
I will also teach Russian. Scary and shameful. But the chil­
dren didn’t ask anything. Probably, their parents explained
everything to them. (Sokolova-Stekh 2022)
The principal also reported receiving disapproving phone
calls from former colleagues who criticized her for “collabo­
rating” and accepting a salary from the occupation authorities.
As this section illustrates, accommodation strategies are
shaped by multiple moral decisions and marked by high levels
of risk and uncertainty. Accommodation also has the potential
to reshape SLBs’ identity: the shift in state allegiance (and
national affiliation) may automatically position them on the
opposite side of the “friend–enemy” dichotomy.

Local defiance
Local defiance—a strategy in which the SLB decides to remain
in the territory while actively engaging in some form of resis­
tance to the occupying forces—is similar to what previous
studies have called sabotage (Brehm and Gates 1999) or diver­
gence (Gofen 2014). However, in this case, this strategy is much
riskier and is undoubtedly the most dangerous of the four strat­
egies in our typology. Similar to other previous studies that
analyzed why SLBs accept taking risks for clients (see Cohen
et al. 2024), our findings reveal that the local defiance strategy
is driven by the moral decision of loyalty to the de jure state
and commitment to service provision and community
well-being. This strategy involves a broad range of actions
opposed to the interests of the occupiers. It may help mitigate
some impacts of war—such as bellicization, political weaponi­
zation, loyalty dilemma, and related moral dilemmas—but in
turn, it significantly heightens the personal security risks for
the SLB in their forced interactions with enemy state actors.
In the case of education professionals, we identified six main
sub-strategies of defiance: (1) improvised service provision, (2)
concealment of working materials, (3) humanitarian functional
innovation, (4) self-concealment, (5) civic engagement, and (6)
military engagement.
(1) Improvised service provision—specifically, the continu­
ation of educational activities—was initially enabled through
remote learning. Drawing on their extensive experience from
the COVID-19 pandemic, educators and students quickly
adapted this strategy at the onset of the invasion (see Spiva­
kovsky et al. 2023). However, in many rural areas, long-term
electricity shortages and internet outages made online teaching
unfeasible. Educators developed alternative methods to main­
tain instruction, such as delivering materials to students’ homes
or teaching small groups in their own residences. Despite the
high risks, these actions reflected a strong commitment to ser­
vice provision and community well-being, aiming to minimize
war-related harm to students. As one school principal explained,
with a resolute and proud demeanor:
The [Ukrainian regional] Department of Education issued
an order for us to shift to family-based learning. We dis­
cussed it with parents and teachers and agreed—just to
make sure the children weren’t left without education. […].
I couldn’t allow them to go uneducated. We set things up
so that teachers would visit students, or sometimes the stu­
dents would come to us. We were risking our lives. I carried
a laptop in my bag. We agreed with the parents that they
would host us in their homes, with wood stoves. […] I’d
work with one group of kids, then go to another house. […]
Teachers had set up classrooms in their own homes—tables,
chairs, a learning space. […] That’s how we finished the
school year. Then came September 1st. It was clear: there
would be no Russian school here. We remained a Ukrainian
school, working under the Ukrainian curriculum. I gathered
the teachers and held a small pedagogical council—we made
our decision. We continued giving assignments to students,
often through their parents. The parents supported us. We
didn’t abandon the students—and we never will.
One university teacher emphasized that some apparent
accommodation strategies may, in fact, reflect local defiance.
This points to the complexities and “gray zones” typical of
military occupation contexts (Buschmeier 2024; Tönsmeyer
2015). In her words:
It’s been ten years [of occupation in Crimea], and [some]
Ukrainian teachers there are still trying—even a decade
later—to tell the truth [in subtle ways]. […] So, the real
question becomes: what does more harm? When a teacher
refuses to work (and is replaced by Russian teachers), or
when they agree to stay—but continue trying to preserve
Ukrainian narratives and tell the truth? That moral dilemma,
for me, is the most difficult challenge teachers face under
the occupation.
(2) The concealment of working materials was another form
of defiance motivated by concerns for student safety and local
communities. Educators feared Russian soldiers would steal
resources or retaliate against children’s relatives who served in
the Donbas war (2014-). Some teachers and principals hid
maps to prevent them from being used for military reconnais­
sance. One school principal recounted the dangers of engaging
in this sub-strategy, speaking with a tone of bitterness and
conviction:
We hid our computers, laptops, TVs, and plasma screens.
Some people informed on us. Later, after I was forced to
write a statement, they took me to the basement. There, we
were made to sign declarations of cooperation with the
occupying forces. Then I was given a sheet of paper and told
to list all the pro-Ukrainian teachers who opposed the Rus­
sian occupation authorities […]. And then came the final
threat: if my teachers didn’t return the laptops, TVs, or
printers, I would not be allowed to go home. Thankfully,
my team—to whom I’m immensely grateful—managed to
gather some of the equipment and hand it over to the col­
laborator school principal.
The third sub-strategy, (3) humanitarian functional innova­
tion, refers to how educators expanded their bureaucratic roles
to meet urgent community needs, including food distribution
and shelter provision. In the absence of effective state struc­
tures, educators became ad hoc coordinators of humanitarian
aid, drawing on their personal and informal resources (Lavee
2022). A teacher and administrative staff from a university
described how the campus was repurposed as an emergency
center (several schools performed the same role) while being
pressured by the new occupying authorities:
Well, I was at my workplace, and I basically lived there. We
had dormitories and the main building. In their basements,
there were bomb shelters. […] I was performing my duties.
And people arrived. […] The children were afraid to go
outside, so they stayed in the basement. […] Kids either had
nowhere to go or thought it was safer to stay here than
where their parents were. […] We cooked for all the
people—fully. […] We had it all set up so that practically
everyone ate together. Because when you cook for everyone,
it’s more economical and easier overall.

Finally, some education professionals adopted the
sub-strategy of (6) military engagement—joining the Ukrainian
military, assisting volunteer units, or transmitting the coordi­

between civilian and military roles and involved extreme per­

suspicion of acting as informants.
As this section demonstrates, local defiance was the most
diverse and dangerous of the response strategies. Motivated by
moral decisions such as loyalty to the de jure state and com­
mitment to service provision and community well-being, many
SLBs reshaped their roles. They risked their lives to resist occu­
pation and uphold their professional and civic responsibilities.
However, it is challenging to sustain defiance over time, and
SLBs may change to other response strategies. As occupying
forces consolidate control, their surveillance and repression
systems grow more sophisticated, with networks of informants
reporting those who resist. Educators mentioned feelings of
shock, betrayal, and frustration when close colleagues joined
the occupying administration. One principal highlighted with
indignation: “I didn’t know whom I could talk to. […] Every
word I said could be used against me.”
Remote adaptation
The final response strategy in our typology, remote adaptation,
refers to efforts to maintain loyalty to the legitimate state and
continue serving clients through geographically and institution­
ally distanced means in repressive contexts, where traditional
channels of defiance are blocked or entail significant risk. It
involves relocating SLBs and their institutions to a safer,
non-occupied region, while continuing their work through
online platforms. In doing so, the delivery of public services to
clients—such as students still under occupation, IDPs, and
refugees abroad—can be maintained. Educational institutions
thereby become a central resilience tool for preserving social
networks and community ties.
This strategy is driven by a combination of moral decisions,
including commitment to service provision and community
well-being, self-preservation/well-being, and loyalty to the de
jure state. As SLBs leave the occupied area, remote adaptation
shares essential features with exit, particularly in its capacity
to mitigate multiple wartime impacts and in the challenges for
resettlement. Unlike exit, though, remote adaptation allows
the SLBs to maintain ties to both their institutions and clients.
This category represents a notable innovation in the litera­
ture on SLBs. While it shares similarities with the experience
of remote teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic, there are
key differences.
First, the territorial restructuring. Unlike pandemic-period
remote education, remote adaptation requires the physical dis­
placement of institutions and personnel. This process involves
extensive logistical efforts, substantial infrastructure invest­
ments, administrative restructuring, digitalization of all educa­
tional processes (see Spivakovsky et al. 2023), and ad hoc
coordination among school principals/university directors,
parents, students, and higher levels of the state bureaucracy.
Second, the degree of politicization and immersion in identity
conflict. While some level of political polarization marked
remote teaching during the pandemic, remote adaptation
acquires a far more pronounced political significance. It func­
tions both as an act of defiance against the occupying state and
as a tool of resilience (see Malchykova and Pylypenko 2025;
Orzhel et al. 2023; Spivakovsky et al. 2023)—preserving loy­
alty to the legitimate state, sustaining national identity, and
maintaining community bonds.
Third, political tension and risk to clients. While remote
learning during the pandemic aimed to protect teachers and
students from a health threat, remote adaptation in wartime
poses substantial danger to students. Attendance in online edu­
cation promoted by the legitimate state may be seen by occu­
pying forces as an act of disloyalty and a threat to their
political agenda.
Fourth, security-centric operation. Given the risk to students
and their families, education professionals must implement
comprehensive safety and confidentiality protocols. They are
also responsible for addressing the psychological impacts of
war on children. Many SLBs have family members and prop­
erty in the occupied territory, necessitating an especially cau­
tious approach.
One of the most successful examples of remote adaptation
occurred at Kherson State University. According to the rector,
after a month of occupation and continued technical difficul­
ties, the academic council agreed to relocate staff to a safer
region. Soon after, the university coordinated with the Ministry
of Education, which issued a decree formalizing the relocation.
In April 2022, a management center was established at Vasyl
Stefanyk Precarpathian National University in Ivano-Frankivsk,
far from the occupation. Later, the university secured an addi­
tional building. This new center handled key bureaucratic func­
tions and occasional in-person activities. The relocation was
essential for maintaining connections with the Ukrainian state
infrastructure and ensuring access to administrative systems.
Following the displacement, the institution also became actively
involved in academic events and publications documenting the

These are our children… children from our community!

continue to educate our children according to the standards
of our country!
One major challenge faced by education professionals has
been providing psychological support to students amid the
trauma and intensity of war. In an improvised and informal
manner, they were compelled to develop strategies beyond their
formal duties. One school principal exemplified how she coped
with this challenge by drawing on her personal experience
within her own family:
At that time, unfortunately, we didn’t have any psycholog­
ical training, so we tried to act simply as human beings—from
the heart. We said the same things to our students that we
said to our own children at home: how we calmed them
down, how we encouraged them—that’s exactly how we
supported our students. […] But now, as the war has lasted
so long, we have already completed training courses—both
in psychological preparation and in psychological stress
relief. And now we are more aware of these things, because
our children—we can even, in an online setting, more or

[…] [During remote teaching] we understood we couldn’t

encouraged [them to dangerous actions]. It was tough to
maintain that balance—giving the children a sense that we
are Ukrainian […], but at the same time not stirring them
to actions that could endanger them.
Participation in Ukrainian online classes remains a risk factor
for children and their families in occupied zones. Teachers must
adapt to this by offering flexible attendance and accommodat­

be someone next to the child who could interfere with the
communication, so this added a huge psychological challenge.”
Some students “disappear” for several days. One school teacher
described this reality with a voice marked by concern, but
also pride:
It is very dangerous and risky—for both the children and
their parents. Children sometimes do this at night, hiding
under the table. Some of them even submitted poems to me
[…] recording them at night, not, you know, in daylight,
but by candlelight or a flashlight. The situation there is
extremely dangerous because the occupiers don’t want the
children to study, not even remotely. They want them to
switch to education according to the Russian curriculum.
That’s why parents are very afraid—afraid of ending up in
a pidval themselves, of having their children taken away,
and then deported somewhere.
Occupation authorities used coercion and co-optation incen­
tives to drive enrollment in Russian schools. According to inter­
viewees, families were offered financial aid in exchange for
attendance and threatened with the loss of parental rights.
Many children simultaneously attend Russian schools in person
and (secretly) Ukrainian online ones.
While remote adaptation has shown promise in the medium
term, its long-term sustainability remains uncertain. Challenges
include new enrollments, degree recognition, unequal digital
access, the delivery of practical courses, and the psychological
strain resulting from the absence of in-person interaction
among children and young students. Frequent blackouts and
infrastructure attacks disrupt learning. Occupation forces
attempt to block Ukrainian sites and signals, forcing reliance
on VPNs. As with exit, displaced educators face difficulties
settling in new areas due to low wages and limited state
assistance.
As shown in this section, remote adaptation represents a
significant innovation in wartime SLB. While it resembles
pandemic-era remote teaching, key distinctions include terri­
torial relocation, political conflict and tensions, security risks
for clients, and the SLBs’ intensified focus on safety. Thanks
to technological innovation, SLBs can leave occupied
territory—as in exit—but simultaneously maintain ties to their
clients and communities.

Discussions and conclusions
This paper analyzed the implications of war and territorial
contributions for street-level work. Our findings make several
theoretical contributions to the literature on SLB in contexts
of crisis—and, most notably, in situations of war and territorial
conflict.
First, the paper develops an analytical framework to analyze
SLBs in wartime, linking: (1) the unique challenges of such
crises, (2) the response strategies that SLBs develop, and (3) the
moral reasoning that underpins these strategies. Our frame­
work identifies four primary response strategies—exit, accom­
modation, local defiance, and remote adaptation—each
reflecting distinct constellations of moral decisions. This frame­
work advances the literature on SLBs during crises by clarifying
the reasons for selecting different response strategies, an under­
explored issue in prior research. Unlike studies of SLBs during
the pandemic or democratic backsliding (e.g., Lotta et al. 2024;
Malandrino and Sager 2021), our findings demonstrate how
these strategies can sustain state continuity and resilience from
below, even under conditions of institutional collapse. Our
analysis also shows that wartime response strategies involve
existential and political decisions—whether to leave, collabo­
rate, resist, or reorganize remotely. In particular, remote adap­
tation emerges as a novel strategy—distinct from pandemic-era
remote work—, in which SLBs maintain public service provi­
sion across territorial divides. This concept provides an inno­
vative lens for theorizing resilience and continuity in public
service delivery under war.
Second, our findings contribute to the theory by demonstrat­
ing that wars and territorial conflicts constitute a distinct and
unique form of crisis, with far-reaching implications for SLBs.
While previous studies analyzed SLBs during crises—especially
pandemic and democratic backsliding (Brodkin 2021; Gofen
and Lotta 2021; Lotta et al. 2024; Piotrowska 2024), war intro­
duces contested sovereignty and legitimacy, violence, and new
types of conflicts that destabilize institutional mandates, author­
ity, and professional identities. These conditions exacerbate
bureaucratic rupture and force SLBs into situations of structural
and moral disorientation that go far beyond classic accounts of
resource scarcity or workload stress (Lipsky 2010 [1980]; Tum­
mers et al. 2015). Moreover, our findings show that, under
conditions of war and armed conflict, SLBs become strategic
targets of a process of institutional bellicization—a concept we
develop in this article. This dynamic goes beyond prior analyses
of bureaucratic politicization and moral pressure (Lotta et al.
2024; Sager and Gofen 2022), as it entails the destabilization
of authority, professional identities, ethical commitments, and
social roles, as well as the emergence of morally ambiguous and
situational decision-making contexts. Through this process,
SLBs become politically weaponized and may turn into both
victims and instruments of the occupying state’s agenda.
In this way, third, our paper contributes to the understanding
of SLBs’ moral agency. Building and advancing on Zacka’s
(2017) model of moral dispositions, our study demonstrates
that wartime conditions fundamentally reshape SLBs’ moral
agency. While Zacka conceptualizes moral dispositions as rel­
atively stable orientations, our data show that in contexts of
war and military occupation new situational moral dilemmas
and decisions arise. We propose that SLBs engage instead in
situational moral decisions, making context-specific judgments
that reinterpret preexisting moral commitments. Our findings
show five moral decisions that co-exist and reflect overlapping
moral referents—the clients, SLBs themselves, and the
state—illustrating the ethical complexity of bureaucratic work
under war. We therefore contribute to theory by showing that
moral agency is not solely about balancing caregiving, enforce­
ment, or indifference: in contexts of institutional collapse, SLBs
navigate moral disorientation and develop situational and reac­
tive ethical reasoning, taking into account the main war impacts
on their work.
Last but not least, by linking moral decisions and response
strategies, our study highlights the potential consequences of
wartime bureaucratic behavior for public service delivery. Each
strategy entails varying degrees of disruption, improvisation,
and risks for SLBs and service users. SLBs become key targets
under conditions of military occupation. Refusal to cooperate
may result in a range of punitive measures by de facto occupa­
tion authorities, while collaboration may expose SLBs to crim­
inal prosecution for treason by the legitimate de jure state.
Therefore, our paper demonstrates that service delivery under
war is inseparable from moral decisions, responses, and terri­
torial dynamics experienced by SLBs, extending prior research
on workload, resource scarcity, and discretion.
Some research limitations, partly acknowledged in the
research design, deserve further reflection here. For ethical and
security reasons, we could not interview education profession­
als currently residing in occupied territories. To partially mit­
igate this gap, we analyzed interviews they had given to Russian
media outlets and collected indirect accounts from colleagues
who had worked with them but are now in Ukrainian-controlled
areas. While this strategy provided valuable insights, such
materials must be interpreted cautiously, given the highly asym­
metrical power relations between the “occupiers” and “occu­
pied” (see Tönsmeyer 2015) and the potential for coercion
affecting the honesty of responses. Moreover, in the polarized
wartime context, peer testimonies may reflect resentment or
social desirability bias, shaping how motivations and actions
are portrayed. Our own positionality as researchers may also
influence how the results are presented; therefore, we included
a statement of positionality in Supplementary Appendix 5.
Finally, the study focused on a specific period—the initial phase
of the occupation. Consequently, our findings are temporally
bounded and do not capture the evolving dynamics of subse­
quent phases. These limitations inevitably affect the descriptive
and interpretive validity of our analysis but, at the same time,
point to promising directions for future research.
Future research could further deepen our understanding of
SLBs in war contexts. It would be especially valuable to inves­
tigate whether the patterns observed among education profes­
sionals also apply to other categories of SLBs, such as healthcare
workers, emergency responders, and law enforcement person­
nel. Further research could also examine how the nature of the
conflict (e.g., interstate versus intrastate war, organized crime,
ethnic violence), the identity of belligerent actors (e.g., state or
non-state forces), and territorial characteristics (e.g., rural ver­
sus urban settings) shape SLBs’ response strategies, as well as
how wars and these response strategies impact public service
users. It is particularly important to develop a longitudinal
perspective to observe how these strategies evolve in prolonged
conflicts—for example, local defiance seems to be a more unsta­
ble response given the risks involved.
Our study opens a critical research agenda at the intersection
of street-level governance in crisis contexts, state legitimacy,
